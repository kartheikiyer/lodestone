## Article

**Clustered star formation** refers to the astrophysical paradigm in which the vast majority of stars—across the entire mass spectrum—originate together within the high-density cores of [[molecular-clouds-and-gas-clumping|Molecular Clouds and Gas Clumping]], rather than in true isolation [Lada et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993prpl.conf..245L/abstract), [Pudritz et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002Sci...295...68P/abstract). Driven by a combination of supersonic turbulence, self-gravity, and rapid gas cooling, the progenitor clouds undergo hierarchical [[fragmentation-astrophysics|Fragmentation (astrophysics)]], collapsing to form [[dense-stellar-systems|Dense stellar systems]] on timescales of merely one to two local crossing times [Elmegreen et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...530..277E/abstract). Within these tightly packed nascent environments, protostars are subjected to strong dynamical interactions and competitive accretion, processes that heavily influence their final mass distribution and early planetary system survival. The recognition of this clustered mode represents a fundamental shift from historical textbook models, which often assumed that typical low-mass stars formed in relative isolation while only massive [[ob-stars|OB stars]] required the dense environments of [[young-massive-clusters|Young massive clusters]] [Efremov et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ssfg.book.....E/abstract), [Evans et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ARA&A..37..311E/abstract). 

The spatial and temporal clustering of stellar births has profound implications for global [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. Because [[massive-star-physics|massive stars]] form in close proximity and evolve on similar timescales, their subsequent deaths as [[clustered-supernovae|Clustered supernovae]] are highly correlated. This overlapping energy injection excavates expansive, low-density superbubbles that are substantially more efficient at driving mass-loaded galactic outflows than randomly distributed supernovae, critically regulating both the gas reservoir and the metal retention of the host galaxy. Furthermore, the fraction of total star formation occurring within gravitationally bound groups—parameterized as the cluster formation efficiency ($\Gamma$)—is highly sensitive to the local galactic environment. While quiescent disks exhibit relatively low efficiencies, the extreme gas pressures encountered during [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] and [[extreme-starbursts|Extreme starbursts]] dramatically enhance the production of robust, bound [[young-massive-clusters|Young massive clusters]].

This article explores the multi-scale physics of the clustered star formation process, tracking the evolution of these systems from their deeply embedded, gas-rich phases through gas expulsion and dynamical dissolution. It examines the competing [[formation-channels|Formation channels]] of [[dense-stellar-systems|Dense stellar systems]], evaluating how hierarchical cluster inspiral versus in-situ gas accretion contributes to the assembly of nuclear star clusters and extragalactic [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] [Agarwal et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...729...35A/abstract), [Fahrion et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...650A.137F/abstract). Finally, the article highlights recent observational breakthroughs from high-redshift facilities like JWST, while addressing major open questions in the field. These ongoing debates include the potential environmental variability of the initial mass function, the precise role of mass-driven versus environment-driven quenching in shaping a galaxy's [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]], and the elusive physical origins of the multiple elemental populations observed within ancient globular clusters.

## Physical Mechanisms of Clustered Star Formation

Star formation is an intrinsically clustered phenomenon; observations suggest that the vast majority of stars form in dense groups rather than in isolation [Lada et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993prpl.conf..245L/abstract), [Pudritz et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002Sci...295...68P/abstract). The physical mechanism governing this process is the hierarchical [[fragmentation-astrophysics|Fragmentation (astrophysics)]] of [[molecular-clouds-and-gas-clumping|giant molecular clouds (GMCs)]]. Driven by the interplay of supersonic turbulence, self-gravity, and gas cooling, this fragmentation produces a scale-free hierarchy of density enhancements [Elmegreen et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph.11282E/abstract). Recent ultraviolet observations of nearby spiral galaxies confirm that young star-forming clumps exhibit a fractal-like distribution with a fractal dimension of $D \approx 1.05 - 1.50$ [Shashank et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.188S/abstract). However, this hierarchy is not infinite; it extends to a maximum correlation scale, $l_{\rm corr}$, which varies between $0.5$ and $3.1 \text{ kpc}$ depending on the galactic environment, such as ambient gas pressure and the absence or presence of strong spiral arms [Shashank et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.188S/abstract). Furthermore, spatial mapping techniques such as Kriging applied to Gaia data for young [[open-clusters|Open clusters]] reveal that clustered star formation imprints hierarchical complexes, disk warps, and corrugations in the local vertical velocity field of the solar neighborhood [Alfaro et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..114A/abstract). Complementary dynamical models of the Galactic disk further demonstrate that clustered star formation produces significant substructure in combined phase and chemical space [Kamdar et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884..173K/abstract).

The fragmentation and subsequent collapse of these clouds proceed extremely rapidly. Observational constraints on embedded [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] indicate that star formation is completed in just one to two crossing times across spatial scales spanning a factor of 1000 [Elmegreen et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...530..277E/abstract). Because the process is so rapid, the spatial distribution of the nascent stars essentially "freezes out," preserving the turbulent structure of the progenitor gas cloud without requiring internal feedback to artificially extend the cloud's lifetime [Elmegreen et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...530..277E/abstract). Furthermore, the structural properties of these resulting clusters are strongly determined at birth; a recent regression analysis of 5,105 clusters demonstrates that cluster mass is the primary driver of cluster radius, with age and [[normalized-star-formation-rate|specific star formation rate]] contributing negligible predictive power [Sebastian et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260200445S/abstract).

Within the densest environments of [[molecular-clouds-and-gas-clumping|Molecular Clouds and Gas Clumping]], protostars are subject to intense dynamical interactions. As described by standard textbook models, low-mass protostars form on timescales shorter than the Kelvin-Helmholtz timescale, $t_{\rm KH} = GM_\star^2 / (RL)$, deriving their luminosity primarily from accretion. Conversely, massive protostars form on timescales longer than $t_{\rm KH}$, meaning they undergo nuclear burning while still accreting gas. In these high-density regions, competitive accretion and close dynamical encounters play critical roles. Gravitational interactions among co-natal protostars alter their orbits and can truncate circumstellar disks, while mass segregation causes the most [[massive-star-physics|massive stars]] to sink deeper into the central potential well of the forming cluster. To systematically understand these embedded environments, the MALT90 survey has provided crucial data by mapping molecular line emission across over 2,000 dense clumps associated with [[massive-star-formation|Massive Star Formation]] [Foster et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASPC..476..127F/abstract).

Despite the classical assumption that massive stars form exclusively at the exact centers of GMCs due to these deep potential wells, recent high-resolution observations reveal a more complex reality. Studies mapping GMCs at parsec resolution (e.g., $\sim 6 \text{ pc}$ scales in the Large Magellanic Cloud) show that massive young stellar objects do not typically form at the exact cloud centers or at the highest column density peaks [Ochsendorf et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...43O/abstract). Instead, [[massive-star-formation|Massive Star Formation]] is clustered over multiple generations on physical scales much smaller than the parent cloud, and is heavily boosted by the presence of slightly older ($\sim 10 \text{ Myr}$) stellar clusters [Ochsendorf et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...43O/abstract). This tension with idealized central-collapse models suggests that triggering and feedback from previous generations heavily modulate local physical conditions, creating localized, off-center pockets of high-pressure star formation. Furthermore, clustered star formation is not strictly confined to dense galactic disks; the discovery of clustered Cepheid variables at 90 kpc from the Galactic center provides stark evidence of recent star formation in distant, potentially accreted, [[stellar-substructure|halo substructures]] [Chakrabarti et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...802L...4C/abstract).

## Historical Development of the Clustered Paradigm

Historically, astrophysical models often distinguished between two primary pathways of stellar genesis: an isolated mode responsible for producing typical low-mass stars, and a clustered mode that yielded high-mass [[ob-stars|OB associations]]. Textbook treatments frequently asserted that while low-mass protostars evolved in relative isolation, the birth of high-mass stars required the dense, highly interactive environments of massive clusters.

This "isolated vs. clustered modes" dichotomy underwent a fundamental shift in the early 1990s, driven by comprehensive near-infrared censuses of nearby [[star-forming-regions-and-cores|Star-Forming Regions and Cores]]. A watershed moment in this transition was the systematic survey of the Orion complex, specifically the L1630 (Orion B) molecular cloud. Observations revealed that rather than forming in isolation, the vast majority of young stellar objects—including typical low-mass stars—were concentrated within a few dense, embedded clusters [Lada et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993prpl.conf..245L/abstract). Extrapolating the spatial distribution found in L1630, researchers concluded that the clustered mode is not merely a special condition for massive stars, but is the dominant mechanism of star formation across the Galaxy [Lada et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993prpl.conf..245L/abstract). 

By the early 2000s, the field had firmly adopted the paradigm that star formation is an intrinsically clustered phenomenon [Pudritz et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002Sci...295...68P/abstract). Theoretical models moved away from the slow, quasi-static collapse of isolated cores toward a dynamic, scale-free hierarchy governed by supersonic turbulence and [[fragmentation-astrophysics|Fragmentation (astrophysics)]]. Within this modern framework, star formation proceeds very rapidly, typically completing within one to two local crossing times ($t_{\rm cross}$) across spatial scales spanning a factor of 1000 [Elmegreen et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...530..277E/abstract). Rather than contracting as independent entities, stars effectively "freeze out" of the turbulent-driven gas structure, preserving the fractal-like hierarchical distributions of their parent clouds up to kiloparsec scales.

As observational resolution improved, further long-held assumptions regarding the internal geometry of these clustered environments were overturned. Earlier models presumed that [[massive-star-formation|Massive Star Formation]] occurred preferentially at the precise gravitational centers and highest column density peaks of giant molecular clouds. However, spatially resolved surveys of large cloud complexes demonstrate that massive stars do not typically form at these central peaks; instead, they form in smaller sub-clusters over multiple generations (separated by timescales up to $\sim 10$ Myr) and are often spatially offset from the global centers of their parent clouds [Ochsendorf et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...43O/abstract). 

Consequently, the contemporary paradigm views the genesis of stars in [[star-clusters-and-complexes|Star Clusters and Complexes]] not as a collection of isolated events, but as a hierarchical, highly dynamic process. The realization that the majority of the galactic stellar mass originates in these dense, clustered environments has profoundly reshaped our understanding of the initial mass function, the early dynamical survival of planetary systems, and the implementation of stellar feedback in models of global galaxy evolution. As the clustered paradigm took root, it also spurred new analytical tools for tracing galactic histories. For instance, the development of the $M_{\rm max}$ method demonstrated that the age and mass of a galaxy's most massive clusters could be utilized as a reliable proxy to reconstruct its overall [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] [Maschberger et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.379...34M/abstract).

## Stellar Feedback and the Lifecycle of Clusters

The lifecycle of a star cluster is intimately tied to the feedback injected by its [[massive-star-physics|massive stellar members]]. Even before the first supernovae (SNe) explode, early feedback mechanisms—primarily photoionization, radiation pressure, and stellar winds—act to rapidly clear the natal gas. This prompt pre-SN feedback restricts [[molecular-clouds-and-gas-clumping|molecular cloud]] lifetimes to approximately one dynamical time ($\sim 10$ Myr) and drives rapid evolutionary cycling, limiting the local [[integrated-star-formation|integrated star formation efficiency]] to just $2\%-3\%$ [Kruijssen et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.569..519K/abstract).

Following this early gas clearing, the deaths of massive stars trigger the supernova feedback phase. Because star formation is spatially and temporally correlated, SNe within [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] do not explode in isolation. Instead, [[clustered-supernovae|Clustered supernovae]] inject kinetic energy into pre-cleared, low-density cavities. This environment drastically reduces radiative energy losses compared to randomly distributed SNe, allowing expanding SN remnants to overlap and form large-scale superbubbles [Fielding et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.3325F/abstract). Early theoretical calculations estimating the global impact of this process found that superbubbles driven by clustered supernovae could account for a Galaxy-wide area filling factor of approximately $0.23$ [Heiles et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ApJ...354..483H/abstract). Theoretical models predict that these superbubbles face one of two primary fates: they either stall and fragment within the interstellar medium (depositing their momentum locally), or they expand beyond the gas scale height to break out of the galactic disk [Orr et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932...88O/abstract). When breakout occurs, it generally happens before the cessation of Type II SN activity ($\lesssim 40$ Myr), enabling a substantial fraction of the injected SN energy ($\gtrsim 0.2$) to vent directly into the galactic halo [Fielding et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.3325F/abstract). 

Superbubble breakout is a primary driver of galactic winds, which are crucial for regulating [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. However, the power and mass loading factors of these winds remain a subject of tension in the literature, particularly in the context of [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]]. Some simulations of solar-neighborhood conditions report strong, primarily atomic outflows with mass loading factors up to $10$, dominated by dense ($\rho \sim 10^{-25}-10^{-24}$ g cm$^{-3}$) and slow ($v \sim 20-40$ km s$^{-1}$) gas [Girichidis et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.3432G/abstract). Conversely, high-resolution models of isolated dwarf galaxies find weaker winds than those often adopted in cosmological models, yielding time-averaged loading factors at the virial radius of approximately $3$ for mass, $1$ for momentum, and $0.05$ for energy [Hu et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.3363H/abstract). The retention of feedback momentum also varies significantly with the host environment; high-redshift starbursts may capture $20\%-50\%$ of their feedback momentum within the interstellar medium, while local galaxies capture $\lesssim 10\%$ [Orr et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...924L..28O/abstract).

The dynamics of superbubbles and their ensuing outflows are further complicated by non-thermal physics. Interstellar magnetic fields constrain superbubble expansion—as evidenced by 3D polarization mapping of the Local Bubble [O'Neill et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988..191O/abstract)—while cosmic ray feedback modulates the burstiness of the outflows. The inclusion of cosmic rays and variable SN energies in high-resolution simulations produces more temperate, multi-phase mass-loaded outflows that allow [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxies]] to retain a higher fraction of their metals than purely hydrodynamical models predict [Gutcke et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.5597G/abstract), [Martin-Alvarez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603245M/abstract).

## Dynamical Evolution and Cluster Dissolution

The transition of [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] from gas-rich, embedded environments to exposed, dynamically evolved systems is governed by a complex interplay of internal dynamics and external environmental forces. Whether a nascent cluster survives as a gravitationally bound system or dissolves into the galactic field depends critically on its initial density, star formation efficiency, and the host galaxy's tidal field.

**Gas Expulsion and Infant Mortality**
Stars form in dense molecular cores with a typically low [[integrated-star-formation|integrated star formation efficiency]]; locally, only $2\%$ to $3\%$ of the gas mass is converted into stars per dynamical time [Kruijssen et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.569..519K/abstract). Once massive stars form, their potent feedback—driven by photoionization, stellar winds, and [[clustered-supernovae|Clustered supernovae]]—rapidly excavates the residual gas. Because the gas is often evacuated on a timescale shorter than the cluster's crossing time, the sudden loss of gravitational potential causes the stellar system to expand [Kroupa et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..9370K/abstract). 

This rapid expansion leads to the "infant mortality" phase, during which a large fraction of young clusters become unbound and disperse. Historically, it was estimated that only $\sim 10\%$ of all clusters survive this phase to reach an old dynamical age [Kroupa et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..9370K/abstract), [Maschberger et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.411.1495M/abstract). Survival is highly dependent on the initial [[stellar-density-profile|stellar density]]: clusters forming with densities $\gtrsim 10^4 M_\odot \text{ pc}^{-3}$ are generally stable against gas expulsion and remain bound at $\sim 10$ Myr with core densities of $\sim 10^{3\pm1} M_\odot \text{ pc}^{-3}$, whereas looser associations with initial densities $\lesssim 100 M_\odot \text{ pc}^{-3}$ disperse into the field within tens of megayears [Gieles et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..266...69G/abstract). However, the literature presents some tension regarding the universality of this rapid disruption; recent comprehensive surveys suggest that a strict, mass-independent infant mortality operating under $100$ Myr is ruled out for most galaxies, and that age distribution steepening is instead a complex function of both disruption and observational incompleteness [Adamo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151108212A/abstract).

**Internal Dynamics: Mass Segregation**
For [[young-massive-clusters|Young massive clusters]] that remain bound after gas expulsion, internal two-body relaxation begins to reshape the stellar distribution. A primary consequence of these dynamical encounters is mass segregation. Through energy equipartition, more massive stars transfer kinetic energy to lower-mass members, causing the massive stars to sink toward the center of the gravitational potential well. This mechanism establishes the [[dense-stellar-systems|Dense stellar systems]] and dense stellar cusps characteristic of evolved [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]]. In sufficiently dense environments, the central crowding of massive protostars and stars can even drive stellar collisions, which has been proposed as a mechanism for forming [[very-massive-stars|Very massive stars]] or intermediate-mass black hole seeds [Katz et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ffbh.book..125K/abstract).

**Tidal Fields and Environmental Destruction**
The long-term survival of a cluster is ultimately dictated by its external environment. Bound clusters are continuously subjected to tidal stripping from the host galaxy's large-scale potential and to impulsive tidal shocks from passing giant molecular clouds [Kruijssen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.1339K/abstract). The efficiency of these destruction mechanisms varies drastically by location. For example, the intense tidal fields and high gas densities in starburst nuclei can destroy clusters extremely rapidly; the nearby starburst galaxy M82 exhibits the shortest known cluster disruption timescale [de Grijs et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...583L..17D/abstract). 

Conversely, clusters that experience "cluster migration"—escaping their dense formation sites and moving into the diffuse galactic halo—suffer much lower disruption rates. This environmental "natural selection" dictates that clusters migrating to low-density halos preferentially survive compared to those that remain near dense starburst regions [Kruijssen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.1339K/abstract). As a population ages and undergoes this dynamical filtering, the varying disruption rates imprint a distinct slope on the cluster age distribution that strongly correlates with the host galaxy's underlying star formation rate density.

## Cluster Formation Efficiency across Galactic Environments

The cluster formation efficiency, denoted as $\Gamma$, quantifies the fraction of a galaxy's total stellar mass that forms within gravitationally bound [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] relative to its global star formation rate. In the quiescent environments of typical local disk galaxies, $\Gamma$ generally ranges between $2\%$ and $10\%$ [Silva-Villa et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...529A..25S/abstract), [Johnson et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827...33J/abstract). However, star formation is highly sensitive to the local galactic environment, and $\Gamma$ is not a universal constant. Observations across diverse galactic environments indicate that the fraction of stars born in bound clusters is intimately linked to the physical conditions and pressures of the interstellar medium [Adamo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151108212A/abstract).

A primary driver of cluster formation efficiency is the star formation rate surface density, $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$. On galaxy-integrated scales, $\Gamma$ exhibits a strong positive correlation with $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ and the ambient mid-plane gas pressure [Bastian et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASPC..470..287B/abstract), [Adamo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151108212A/abstract). On a broader statistical level, the global star formation rate is a significantly better predictor than total stellar mass for both the probability of a galaxy hosting clusters and the total mass contained within its cluster system [Berek et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.1902B/abstract). In high-pressure starburst galaxies and merging systems, extreme gas compression allows a significantly larger fraction of the molecular gas to achieve the densities required to form robust, gravitationally bound [[young-massive-clusters|Young massive clusters]], elevating $\Gamma$ well above the values seen in isolated spirals [de Grijs et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...29D/abstract). Within individual galaxies, spatially resolved studies similarly show that $\Gamma$ can vary by factors of 3 to 4, closely tracking local fluctuations in the molecular gas surface density [Adamo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151108212A/abstract). Taking a broader view of these efficiencies, a continuous upper limit for integrated [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|specific star formation rates]] has been discovered spanning seven orders of magnitude in stellar mass, connecting dwarf galaxies all the way up to massive galaxy clusters [Feulner et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...451L..13F/abstract).

The relationship between $\Gamma$ and $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ is further modulated by the molecular gas depletion time, $\tau_{\rm dep}$. Studies of the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy]] demonstrate that accounting for environmental variations in $\tau_{\rm dep}$ resolves much of the observed scatter in the $\Gamma-\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ relation [Johnson et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827...33J/abstract). Specifically, the efficiency of cluster formation shifts qualitatively when transitioning from the $\text{H}_2$-dominated regions of the inner disk to the $\text{H I}$-dominated, low-intensity star-forming environments of the outer disk. By incorporating $\tau_{\rm dep}$, fiducial $\Gamma$ models can successfully bridge the gap between quiescent $\text{H I}$-dominated environments and dense, high-$\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ starburst systems [Johnson et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827...33J/abstract).

Despite the success of density-driven models at high pressures, the literature is explicitly conflicting regarding the behavior of $\Gamma$ at the low-density extreme. In environments with very low star formation rate surface densities ($\Sigma_{\rm SFR} \sim 10^{-3} - 10^{-2} M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1} \text{ kpc}^{-2}$), some spatially resolved studies find no clear or universal correlation between $\Gamma$ and $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ [Silva-Villa et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...529A..25S/abstract). Instead, the efficiency in these low-density regimes may be more stochastically driven or tied to local properties such as the specific U-band luminosity [Silva-Villa et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...529A..25S/abstract). Moreover, recent findings from the EDGE-CALIFA survey highlight the role of [[inside-out-formation|inside-out quenching]], where star formation efficiency declines rapidly in galaxy centers even before the gas is fully depleted, adding further environmental nuance to clustered star formation rates [Colombo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A.367C/abstract). 

The variability of $\Gamma$ plays a critical role in how galaxies evolve along the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]]. As galaxies undergo cycles of active star formation, gas accretion, and subsequent quenching [Fortun\'e et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320858F/abstract), their trajectory on the main sequence dictates their local gas pressures and $\tau_{\rm dep}$. Consequently, galaxies undergoing merger-induced starbursts that drive them significantly above the main sequence will experience a disproportionate enhancement in bound cluster formation. This high-$\Gamma$ environment leaves a lasting fossil record of a galaxy's assembly history, often manifesting as dense populations of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] in the present-day universe [de Grijs et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...29D/abstract), [Beasley et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020rfma.book..245B/abstract).

## Formation Channels of Nuclear Star Clusters and Globular Clusters

Nuclear star clusters (NSCs) and [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] (GCs) represent the most extreme, gravitationally bound outcomes of [[massive-cluster-formation|Massive cluster formation]]. The assembly of these [[dense-stellar-systems|Dense stellar systems]] is traditionally framed around a dichotomy: the hierarchical merger of pre-existing star clusters driven by dynamical friction, versus the central accumulation of gas followed by in-situ star formation. 

Recent integral-field spectroscopic surveys reveal that the dominant [[formation-channels|Formation channels]] for NSCs transition sharply as a function of host galaxy mass. In low-mass [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxies]] ($M_{\rm gal} < 10^{9} \, M_\odot$), NSCs are generally older and more metal-poor than the surrounding field stars, pointing to an assembly history dominated by the inspiral and subsequent merger of primordial GCs [Fahrion et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...650A.137F/abstract), [Fahrion et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...667A.101F/abstract). Models of this collisionless migration demonstrate that surviving clusters can assemble an NSC containing a small fraction of the host's total stellar mass, provided the initial cluster mass function has a high-mass truncation scale of approximately $10^{5} - 10^{6} \, M_\odot$ [Agarwal et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...729...35A/abstract). This mechanism is a leading explanation for peculiar massive clusters in the local universe; for example, the massive cluster Omega Centauri may have formed from the merger of star cluster complexes within a gas-rich dwarf galaxy that was later [[galaxy-accretion|accreted]] by the Milky Way [Fellhauer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph..1594F/abstract).

However, this purely dissipationless ("dry") merger scenario struggles to explain the complex star formation histories and enriched stellar populations observed in the central regions of more massive galaxies. For host galaxies exceeding stellar masses of $\sim 10^{9} \, M_\odot$, yielding NSCs with $M_{\rm NSC} \gtrsim 10^{7} \, M_\odot$, in-situ star formation fed by central gas accretion becomes the primary driver of mass growth [Fahrion et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...650A.137F/abstract).

Bridging these two extremes is the wet migration scenario. Parsec-resolution hydrodynamical simulations suggest that a massive star cluster can form in a gas-rich galactic disk and migrate to the nucleus while retaining a bound gas reservoir [Guillard et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461.3620G/abstract). As the cluster sinks via dynamical friction, it continues to undergo star formation and can engage in "wet mergers" with other dense clusters and their respective gas reservoirs. This hybrid pathway significantly alters the final mass, morphology, and chemical abundance of the NSC, yielding distinct observational diagnostics compared to purely dry inspiral or purely in-situ formation [Guillard et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461.3620G/abstract).

The physical processes governing NSC formation are inextricably linked to the origin of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] themselves. Once assumed to be simple stellar populations formed in a single coeval burst, GCs are now known to host multiple stellar generations characterized by pronounced abundance variations in elements such as helium, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and sodium [Gratton et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&ARv..20...50G/abstract), [Milone et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Univ....8..359M/abstract). While the exact physical origin of these "multiple populations" remains a subject of intense unresolved debate in the literature, their ubiquity suggests that GCs formed during high-pressure starburst episodes capable of retaining or accreting enriched gas over multiple crossing times. Consequently, whether growing a nuclear star cluster at the center of a galactic potential well or forming a massive GC in a high-redshift disk, the localized retention of gas and clustered feedback plays a critical role in the extended evolution of these systems. From a cosmological standpoint, the oldest [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] likely formed between $z=7$ and $z=3$ within early dwarf galaxies, successfully surviving the tidal disruption of their progenitor galaxies [Gnedin et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003egcs.conf..224G/abstract). To understand the large-scale host environments of these massive clusters over cosmic time, modern cosmological catalogs such as the KiDS-1000 AMICO survey provide high-purity samples essential for calibrating richness-mass scaling relations across $z=0.1-0.9$ [Maturi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.201M/abstract). Crucially, the AMICO algorithm employs an iterative approach to [[clusters-of-galaxies|galaxy cluster detection]] that maximizes signal-to-noise and robustly enables the deblending of close-by structures, significantly improving the identification of these dense systems [Bellagamba et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.5221B/abstract).

## Clustered Star Formation in Mergers and Starbursts

During [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]], the strong compression of the interstellar medium by tidal interactions triggers intense, localized episodes of star formation. In these high-pressure environments, the formation of stars is not merely scaled up; its fundamental mode shifts. Empirical evidence from high-resolution imaging reveals that in the extreme conditions of interacting galaxies, clustered star formation becomes the overwhelmingly dominant mode, driving [[extreme-starbursts|Extreme starbursts]] [de Grijs et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...29D/abstract). Indeed, statistical surveys of nearby galaxies establish that tidal interactions are the dominant trigger for enhanced star formation in the most strongly star-forming systems, particularly among low-mass galaxies [Li et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.385.1903L/abstract). It is important, however, to differentiate between nuclear starbursts that are primarily fueled by massive radial gas inflows, and extended starbursts that are driven by triggered turbulence and subsequent fragmentation within massive cluster complexes [Bournaud et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....51..107B/abstract).

The hallmark of this interaction-driven star formation is the production of [[young-massive-clusters|Young massive clusters]] (YMCs), historically referred to as "super star clusters" or [[luminous-compact-star-clusters|Luminous compact star clusters]]. With initial masses and characteristic radii (typically a few parsecs) comparable to those of ancient Milky Way-type [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]], YMCs are widely considered to be their nascent, metal-rich counterparts forming in the modern universe [de Grijs et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...29D/abstract)[de Grijs et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..235...48D/abstract). This mechanism naturally explains the bimodal color distributions of extragalactic globular cluster systems, where distinct cluster subpopulations are widely observed [Kissler-Patig et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..211...55K/abstract). Standard theoretical models suggest the red (metal-rich) subpopulation assembles directly during these merger-induced starbursts, contrasting with the blue (metal-poor) subpopulation formed earlier in galactic history.

Quantitatively, the fraction of total stellar mass formed within gravitationally bound clusters is parameterized by the cluster formation efficiency, $\Gamma$. Observational studies of [[active-star-forming-regions|Active star-forming regions]] indicate that $\Gamma$ generally increases with the star formation rate surface density, $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ [Johnson et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827...33J/abstract)[Adamo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151108212A/abstract). While the universality of this relation at the lowest $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ remains debated in the literature [Silva-Villa et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...529A..25S/abstract), there is strong consensus that the high $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ conditions inherent to violent mergers yield substantially elevated values of $\Gamma$. While mergers are classic triggers, semi-analytic models clarify that starbursts can also occur without recent mergers, driven instead by high gas surface densities or small disk radii [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.4454W/abstract). Regardless of the trigger, starburst galaxies channel a much larger fraction of their gas reservoir into bound clusters compared to quiescent disks.

However, the extreme environments characterizing [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|Starburst Galaxy Evolution]] are hostile to long-term cluster survival. Numerical simulations demonstrating the interplay between cluster formation and the galactic tidal field show that clusters orbiting near the dense, gas-rich nuclear starburst region face rapid disruption due to tidal shocks and frequent encounters with giant molecular clouds [Kruijssen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.1339K/abstract). Only those clusters born with exceptionally high initial mass densities ($\gtrsim 10^4 M_\odot {\rm pc}^{-3}$) are robust enough to survive the initial "infant mortality" phase of gas expulsion and subsequent tidal harassment [Gieles et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..266...69G/abstract). Clusters that are ejected or migrate into the less dense stellar halo experience lower disruption rates and stand a much greater chance of surviving for a Hubble time.

Finally, the profound spatial and temporal clustering of star formation in these violent events heavily modulates galactic feedback. When thousands of [[massive-star-physics|massive stars]] end their lives as [[clustered-supernovae|Clustered supernovae]] within a confined region, their remnants overlap to form expansive, hot superbubbles. In dense starburst environments, this clustered feedback is substantially more efficient at avoiding radiative cooling than uniformly distributed supernovae, frequently breaking out of the galactic disk before star formation ceases (typically within $\sim 40$ Myr) to drive powerful, mass-loaded galactic winds [Fielding et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.3325F/abstract)[Mills et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919..105M/abstract).

## Recent Advances in High-Redshift Observations

The advent of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has fundamentally transformed the observational study of clustered star formation in the early universe. Notably, recent JWST observations of strongly lensed galaxies at $z > 5$ have revealed dense, bound star clusters with compact radii of $1-10\text{ pc}$. Cosmological radiation-hydrodynamic simulations successfully model the formation of these systems, yielding extreme [[stellar-density-profile|stellar densities]] approaching $\sim 10^5 M_\odot\text{ pc}^{-3}$ that closely resemble the observed objects [Garcia et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025OJAp....8E.146G/abstract). These detections are interpreted as direct snapshots of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] forming in the high-pressure, gas-rich environments of the early universe [Kruijssen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250116438K/abstract). Theoretical models calibrated to these observations suggest that by $z \sim 8.7$, dense nuclear clusters can already seed their host galaxies, growing rapidly through a combination of in-situ star formation and mergers with pre-existing clusters [Garcia et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025OJAp....8E.146G/abstract). Furthermore, JWST data spanning up to $z < 7$ indicate that [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] and the quenching of star formation are fundamentally mass-driven rather than morphologically driven processes [Conselice et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4857C/abstract). Pushing the boundaries of cosmic structure further, algorithms like AMICO applied to JWST COSMOS-Web data have even enabled the detection of ultra-deep galaxy group candidates and [[nascent-galaxy-clusters|protocluster cores]] up to $z = 3.7 [Toni et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A.197T/abstract). To fuel these extreme early environments, models indicate that sustained high star formation rates at $z \sim 2-3$ require constant gas accretion to balance both star formation and outflows [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract). High-resolution simulations (at 20-pc scales) further reveal that during these cosmic phases, [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] can trigger massive starbursts accompanied by a tenfold drop in depletion time, specifically when the gas becomes rotationally dominated [Segovia Otero et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.516.2272S/abstract).

A defining characteristic of high-redshift [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], particularly low-mass dwarfs, is a highly bursty [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]]. Analytic models suggest that this bursty star formation originates when the galactic dynamical timescale is too short for feedback to balance gravitational collapse [Faucher-Giguère et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.3717F/abstract). While purely hydrodynamical models often over-predict the evacuation of gas and metals due to overlapping [[clustered-supernovae|Clustered supernovae]], recent theoretical advancements emphasize the critical role of non-thermal physics in regulating these bursts [Martin-Alvarez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603245M/abstract). Simulations that incorporate cosmic ray feedback, magnetic fields, and radiation demonstrate that cosmic rays drive fast, mass-loaded, multi-phase galactic outflows. Unlike purely thermal supernova feedback, which can violently eject enriched material and suppress the host's metal content, cosmic ray-driven winds entrain a temperate and denser interstellar medium. This non-thermal mechanism successfully reproduces the bursty star formation histories observed by JWST while preserving metals within the galaxy [Martin-Alvarez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603245M/abstract). In more local analogs, cluster population statistics reveal that not all blue compact dwarf galaxies are currently in starburst phases; some maintain steady star formation histories over gigayear timescales [Chandar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...965...95C/abstract). In the context of early-universe analogs in the Local Group, near-field cosmology reveals distinct pathways: contrasting the star formation histories of dwarf spheroidals with stellar halo populations demonstrates that extended star formation episodes in dwarf spheroidals challenge simple reionization-driven quenching models [Grebel et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005nfcd.conf....1G/abstract).

To complement high-redshift discoveries, integral-field spectroscopy from instruments like MUSE has provided detailed fossil records of early cluster formation within the local universe. By resolving the stellar populations and kinematics of central clusters in nearby galaxies, MUSE observations have identified a mass-dependent transition in how massive central clusters assemble. In [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] with stellar masses $M_{\text{gal}} \lesssim 10^9 M_\odot$, central clusters are predominantly old and metal-poor, indicating that they formed primarily via the inspiral and merger of pre-existing [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] through dynamical friction [Fahrion et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...650A.137F/abstract), [Fahrion et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...667A.101F/abstract). Conversely, in more massive galaxies, nuclear clusters exhibit significant contributions from young, enriched stellar populations. This demonstrates that in-situ clustered star formation, fueled by central gas accretion, becomes the dominant growth channel at higher host masses [Fahrion et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...667A.101F/abstract). Beyond cluster scale, spatially-resolved MaNGA data have recently revealed subgroups of early-type galaxies persisting on the star formation main sequence, allowing researchers to distinguish between environmentally-driven quenching and secular evolution pathways in the local universe [Koyama et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250716212K/abstract). Together, these local integral-field data and JWST's direct high-redshift detections paint a cohesive picture of clustered star formation driving early galaxy assembly and generating the dense stellar systems observed today.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

At the stellar level, the clustered nature of star formation offers a physical explanation for the observed H$\alpha$ cut-off in disk galaxies, as the total H$\alpha$ luminosity relates nonlinearly to the star formation rate when massive stars form in discrete [[star-clusters|Star clusters]]. Furthermore, it remains a contested question whether the initial mass function systematically varies within these clustered environments (the integrated galactic IMF, or IGIMF, effect). This potential variability has profound implications, offering a natural mechanism to explain the observed mass-metallicity relation in galaxies: less massive galaxies with lower star formation rates may lack the high-mass star clusters required to heavily populate the upper stellar mass function, thereby severely reducing their overall oxygen yields [Köppen et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375..673K/abstract). Similarly, a central debate continues regarding the universality of the initial cluster mass function (ICMF). While some observational studies suggest the ICMF follows a universal power-law distribution with an index of $\alpha \approx -2$ [Adamo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151108212A/abstract), competing models and observations argue for an environmentally dependent high-mass truncation that scales dynamically with the global star formation rate surface density, $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ [Krumholz et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhR...539...49K/abstract), [Schulz et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...582A..93S/abstract). 

The role of clustered feedback in regulating global galaxy evolution is another area characterized by active tension, particularly regarding how galaxies cease forming stars. Traditional frameworks emphasize environmental quenching, asserting that mechanisms such as ram pressure stripping and tidal fields in [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] suppress star formation from the outside-in [Balogh et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...540..113B/abstract), [Schaefer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.2851S/abstract). The localized effects of this process are currently being mapped by the GASP survey, which provides a spatially resolved view of 'jellyfish' galaxies undergoing active environmental quenching in dense clusters [Poggianti et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IAUS..373..163P/abstract). However, recent observational syntheses conflict with this traditional view, arguing instead that overall quenching is overwhelmingly a mass-dominated process across all epochs, likely driven by central supermassive black holes rather than local environmental densities [Conselice et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4857C/abstract). Reconciling these views requires precise models of a galaxy's [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]]. Currently, standard semi-analytic models struggle to capture this, often predicting nearly flat stellar [[mass-evolution|mass assembly]] histories at $z < 2$, which directly conflicts with observational data favoring rising and then falling trajectories [Mitchell et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.2637M/abstract).

Within dense cluster environments, several localized formational pathways also remain unresolved. For instance, the assembly of nuclear star clusters is highly debated. While low-mass galaxies ($M_* < 10^9 M_\odot$) appear to build their nuclei via the dynamical friction-driven inspiral of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]], more massive galaxies likely rely on in-situ gas accretion. The exact transition mass and the degree of overlap between these two channels remain ambiguous [Fahrion et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...650A.137F/abstract), [Fahrion et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...667A.101F/abstract). Furthermore, the physical origin of the "multiple population" phenomenon within [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] is one of the most prominent unsolved problems in stellar astrophysics. The exact progenitors and mechanisms responsible for distinct elemental abundance variations (e.g., He, C, N, O, Na) across different stellar generations within a single cluster are still heavily disputed [Gratton et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&ARv..20...50G/abstract), [Milone et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Univ....8..359M/abstract). Observationally tracking this embedded protostellar activity across cosmological timescales remains challenging, but water molecular outflows (e.g., the para-H$_2$O $2_{02} - 1_{11}$ transition) offer a promising low-contrast tracer of active star formation [Dutkowska et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...667A.135D/abstract).

Addressing these theoretical bottlenecks requires bridging the gap between small-scale turbulent [[fragmentation-astrophysics|Fragmentation (astrophysics)]] and global galaxy evolution. A highly promising future direction lies in the development of exascale star-by-star simulations [Hirai et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASJ...73.1036H/abstract). By directly resolving individual stars and tracking variable supernova energies explicitly sampled from the initial mass function [Gutcke et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.5597G/abstract), these models bypass the limitations of traditional simple stellar population approximations. Such high-resolution frameworks, utilizing discrete sink particles to model clusters as they dynamically accrete gas and merge [Reina-Campos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240804694R/abstract), will enable the self-consistent modeling of [[clustered-supernovae|Clustered supernovae]] and their ability to launch multi-phase galactic winds [Martin-Alvarez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603245M/abstract). Finally, to bridge microphysics with cosmic evolution, a crucial future direction is the improved integration of massive star cluster populations ($>10^4 M_\odot$) into semi-analytical galaxy formation models, such as L-Galaxies 2020, to accurately track their evolution and differential destruction channels on galactic scales [Hoyer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250412079H/abstract). Ultimately, resolving the interplay between localized feedback and galactic environments holds the key to determining whether the scaling relations governing star formation are truly universal.
