## Article

**Stochastic accretion** is the theoretical framework describing the mass growth of central objects—ranging from protostars to supermassive black holes (SMBHs)—through the capture of discrete, randomly distributed packets of matter, rather than through a continuous, azimuthally symmetric flow. In stark contrast to classical [[accretion-disk-theory|Accretion Disk Theory]], which relies on smooth mass transfer governed by a long [[viscous-timescale|Viscous timescale]], stochastic feeding models the infall of individual molecular clouds, stellar winds, or planetesimals. Because these discrete mass packets possess randomly oriented angular momentum vectors, their collisions in the inner accretion environment lead to rapid angular momentum cancellation. This [[chaotic-accretion|Chaotic accretion]] process allows material to bypass classical [[fragmentation-astrophysics|Fragmentation (astrophysics)]] barriers and fall inward at rates approaching the dynamical timescale, profoundly altering our understanding of [[mass-accretion-and-assembly|Mass Accretion and Assembly]] across multiple astrophysical scales.

In the context of active galactic nuclei (AGN), stochastic feeding fundamentally revises models of SMBH growth and spin evolution. Steady-state disks massive enough to fuel quasars are prone to self-gravitating fragmentation; however, a succession of randomly oriented accretion events circumvents this limit and naturally produces the compact, warped disks observed in galactic nuclei [King et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.377L..25K/abstract). Furthermore, the lack of coherent angular momentum alignment prevents the black hole from spinning up to maximal values, successfully explaining the cosmological spindown of the SMBH population and the occurrence of rapid [[episodic-accretion|Episodic accretion]] flares. On smaller scales, analogous stochastic mechanisms have been explored for compact remnants in dense clusters. In star-forming environments, the exact mode of mass assembly remains actively debated. Models of competitive accretion—which rely on the continuous and chaotic capture of unbound gas from the surrounding environment—face significant theoretical challenges when compared to models favoring discrete gravitational collapse and fragmentation [Krumholz et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.10412K/abstract).

On macroscopic scales, stochastic [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] drives the morphological and chemical evolution of galactic discs. To sustain star formation over Hubble times, galaxies must continuously accrete gas from the cosmic web [Bouché et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Sci...341...50B/abstract). Within the "regulator" or quasi-equilibrium model, galaxies maintain a dynamic balance between this stochastic gas inflow, internal star formation, and feedback-driven outflows [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). The discrete nature of the gas supply introduces an [[inherent-stochasticity|Inherent stochasticity]] to the galaxy's star formation history (SFH). By utilizing mathematical tools like the Power Spectral Density (PSD) and [[damped-random-walk-process|Damped random walk process]], modern statistical frameworks demonstrate that galaxies exhibit varying degrees of star formation "burstiness," effectively losing memory of their previous starburst activity on correlation timescales of roughly 200 million years [Caplar et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.3845C/abstract), building on the foundational concepts of early stochastic models [Gerola et al. (1978)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1978ApJ...223..129G/abstract).

This article explores the physical mechanisms and mathematical models underlying stochastic accretion, tracing its historical development from deterministic steady-state paradigms to modern chaotic frameworks. It examines the role of stochasticity in feeding SMBHs, sustaining galactic star formation, and triggering extreme transient phenomena such as tidal disruption events and "changing-look" AGN. Finally, the article addresses persistent open questions in the field. Chief among these are the profound computational challenges of isolating genuine physical variability from the numerical noise of sub-grid prescriptions in cosmological simulations, and the ongoing observational effort to definitively untangle the signatures of internal bursty feedback from those of external stochastic mass supply.

## Introduction to Stochastic Accretion Mechanisms

Stochastic accretion represents a fundamental departure from classical, steady-state [[accretion-disk-theory|Accretion Disk Theory]]. While standard models often assume a continuous, azimuthally symmetric flow of material that slowly spirals inward over a long [[viscous-timescale|Viscous timescale]], stochastic accretion posits that mass growth is driven by a succession of **discrete events**. In this paradigm, central objects grow via the **clump capture** of randomly distributed molecular clouds, stellar winds, or planetesimals. 

The defining physical characteristic of **stochastic feeding** is the random orientation of the accreted angular momentum. When a discrete mass packet is captured, it forms a localized, transient accretion structure. If successive **mass deposition** events possess misaligned angular momentum vectors, the resulting fluid interactions lead to the partial cancellation of angular momentum within the inner accretion environment. Consequently, material is driven inward at rates much faster than standard viscous transport allows, occasionally approaching near-dynamical accretion timescales [Nayakshin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0705.1686N/abstract), [King et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013CQGra..30x4006K/abstract). 

This theoretical framework has been particularly transformative for our understanding of SMBH growth and the triggering of Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN). The classical assumption of a long-lived, aligned accretion disc is challenged by the fact that self-gravity in the outer parts of steady-state discs leads to gravitational instabilities and [[fragmentation-astrophysics|Fragmentation (astrophysics)]] at radii $R \gtrsim 0.1 \text{ pc}$, preventing continuous fueling at the high rates required to power quasars [Lodato et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007NCimR..30..293L/abstract). Modeling AGN fueling as a series of randomly oriented stochastic events successfully circumvents this fragmentation barrier, producing less massive, warped discs whose orientations perform a random walk over time [Nayakshin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0705.1686N/abstract). 

Beyond supermassive scales, the coupling between accretion and feedback is remarkably similar across different mass regimes, suggesting common physics governing both stellar-mass and supermassive black holes [Fender et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016LNP...905...65F/abstract). Furthermore, stochastic processes are essential for modeling global galactic evolution. The macroscopic time-variability of a whole galaxy's star formation history can be modeled as a stochastic process governed by the internal life-cycles, finite lifetimes, and discrete nature of molecular clouds across the galaxy [Tacchella et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497..698T/abstract). Whether describing the episodic fueling of black holes or the fluctuating star formation histories of entire galaxies, the shift toward stochastic models highlights that many [[accretion-processes|Accretion Processes]] are fundamentally chaotic, bridging the gap between steady-state idealizations and the extreme variability observed in nature.

## Mathematical Frameworks and Statistical Modeling

To formally quantify the "burstiness" and temporal variability of [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] and [[unsteady-accretion|Unsteady accretion]] processes, researchers rely heavily on statistical frameworks, most notably the Power Spectral Density (PSD). In the context of star-formation histories (SFHs), the PSD evaluates the fluctuation strength of the star-formation rate across different frequencies. Observational and theoretical constraints typically model the PSD using a broken power law [Caplar et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.3845C/abstract). A comprehensive model for the PSD of a galaxy's SFH exhibits three characteristic breaks corresponding to distinct physical timescales: the correlation time of the gas inflow rate, the equilibrium timescale of the galactic gas reservoir, and the average lifetime of individual molecular clouds [Tacchella et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497..698T/abstract).

On short timescales, this variability is well-described by a [[damped-random-walk-process|Damped random walk process]] (DRW), which is mathematically equivalent to an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. At high frequencies, this DRW yields a power-law slope of $\beta \approx 2$, with a break occurring near the average molecular cloud lifetime [Tacchella et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497..698T/abstract). By assuming a high-frequency slope of $\alpha = 2$, stochastic models of the [[log-normal-distribution|Log-normal distribution]] scatter in the star-forming main sequence indicate a break timescale of $\tau_{\rm break} \approx 170^{+169}_{-85} \text{ Myr}$, though this result remains highly degenerate depending on the assumed slope $\alpha$ [Caplar et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.3845C/abstract). This quantitatively implies that galaxies lose "memory" of their previous star-formation activity on characteristic timescales approaching $200 \text{ Myr}$. Observations further validate these mathematical assumptions; for example, the distributions of ultraviolet (UV) slopes across all redshifts (up to $z \approx 16$) are consistent with theoretical models of stochastic star formation histories governed by a memory parameter near unity ($H = 1$) [Kelson et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RNAAS...6..162K/abstract). Stochastic mathematical modeling also naturally explains structural properties of galaxies. Ubiquitous exponential radial profiles observed in galactic disks may arise fundamentally from random stellar scattering, provided there is a slight inward bias in the scattering probability [Elmegreen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830..115E/abstract). Furthermore, the mathematical connection between the PSD in Fourier space and its corresponding autocovariance function in the time domain permits the advanced modeling of SFHs using physically motivated Gaussian processes operating in $\log(\text{SFR})$ space [Iyer et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961...53I/abstract).

While standard [[accretion-disk-theory|Accretion Disk Theory]] often assumes steady viscous flows, statistical modeling is equally critical in the regime of supermassive black hole growth. Historically, early applications of stochastic dynamics to model quasi-periodicities in AGN X-ray light curves demonstrated that assuming random variations in the accretion rate could successfully reproduce complex temporal behaviors [Vio et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...405..163V/abstract). Modern approaches build upon this foundation. Under the paradigm of stochastic feeding, accretion onto AGNs can vary on timescales vastly shorter than the cooling times of the surrounding X-ray emitting gas. The Langevin approach—a framework utilizing stochastic differential equations to capture random fluctuations—demonstrates that short-timescale variability in the instantaneous accretion rate can induce fluctuations in the AGN power output that are both larger in amplitude and longer-lived than the initial perturbations [Pope et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.381..741P/abstract). 

These mathematical frameworks highlight the [[inherent-stochasticity|Inherent stochasticity]] of astrophysical processes, which must be carefully accounted for in numerical models. In broader cosmological contexts, observations such as those from the Las Campanas Redshift Survey demonstrate that simple linear biasing is insufficient to describe galaxy clustering, necessitating a stochastic biasing framework for large-scale structure [Tegmark et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518L..69T/abstract). On smaller, non-point mass scales, mathematical models describing the mechanism of external mass accumulation onto core potentials—such as star clusters and dwarf galaxies moving through a background medium—further emphasize the ubiquity of stochastic mass capture [Naiman et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...735...25N/abstract). Moreover, standard analytical frameworks like Bondi accretion have been explicitly generalized to incorporate complex host galaxy potentials utilizing fully analytical solutions via the Lambert-Euler W-function, providing a rigorous mathematical basis for predicting stochastic gas capture in realistic galactic environments [Ciotti et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848...29C/abstract).

## Stochastic Feeding of Supermassive Black Holes

Historically, the growth of supermassive black holes (SMBHs) was modeled via large-scale, steady-state accretion disks aligned with the host galaxy's rotation. However, standard [[accretion-disk-theory|Accretion Disk Theory]] dictates that coherent disks extending beyond $\sim 0.01 \text{--} 0.1$ pc become gravitationally unstable at the mass supply rates required to power active galactic nuclei (AGN) [Nayakshin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0705.1686N/abstract). To resolve this, the stochastic feeding model proposes that SMBHs are primarily fueled by a sequence of discrete, independently oriented mass deposition events, such as the infall of molecular clouds or the capture of stellar winds [King et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.377L..25K/abstract). 

A prominent, localized example of this stochastic fueling is our own galactic center, Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*). Here, the accretion of stellar winds from massive stars on realistic orbits creates cold gas clumps and filaments. The intermittent capture of these discrete clumps leads to an accretion rate onto Sgr A* that is highly variable on timescales of mere decades [Cuadra et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006JPhCS..54..436C/abstract). To explain Sgr A*'s multi-waveband spectrum and observed variability, theorists frequently incorporate the advection-dominated accretion flow (ADAF) model, which natively accounts for this radiatively inefficient and inherently intermittent feeding [Yuan et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006JPhCS..54..427Y/abstract).

A defining feature of this [[chaotic-accretion|Chaotic accretion]] framework is the mechanism of angular momentum cancellation. In a steady disk, the radial transport of matter is strictly limited by the [[viscous-timescale|Viscous timescale]]. When an SMBH is instead fed by stochastically oriented gas parcels, the vector sum of the accreted angular momentum is much smaller than in a coherent disk. This incomplete angular momentum cancellation leads to the formation of compact, warped accretion structures in the inner parsecs, whose orientation undergoes a random walk over time [Nayakshin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0705.1686N/abstract). By circumventing the classical angular momentum barrier, stochastic feeding allows matter to proceed inward at rates approaching the dynamical timescale, enabling extremely rapid black hole mass assembly [King et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013CQGra..30x4006K/abstract).

The orientation of the accreting material also governs BH spin evolution. Because the angular momentum vectors of successive accretion episodes are uncorrelated, chaotic accretion generally prevents the SMBH from spinning up to maximal values, driving the population toward low to moderate spins [King et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.377L..25K/abstract). This theoretical expectation is supported by the cosmological evolution of the SMBH radiative efficiency, $\eta$. Observations suggest that $\eta$ decreases from $\eta \approx 0.3$ at $z \approx 2$ down to $\eta \approx 0.03$ at $z \approx 0$, a trend interpreted as a progressive spin-down driven by [[episodic-accretion|Episodic accretion]] events [Wang et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697L.141W/abstract). Maintaining a low spin has critical implications for binary SMBH mergers, as it significantly suppresses the gravitational wave recoil kick, allowing the merged remnant to remain bound to its host galaxy [King et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013CQGra..30x4006K/abstract). Note that the superposition of gravitational waves from these extragalactic accretion and massive merger events creates a stochastic background of gravitational waves that will be detectable by future observatories [Schneider et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010CQGra..27s4007S/abstract). 

Finally, stochastic feeding naturally drives extreme AGN variability. Random fluctuations in the mass deposition rate inject energy across a wide range of frequencies, inducing large-amplitude, persistent variations in AGN power output [Pope et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.381..741P/abstract). Extreme observational signatures, such as "changing-look" AGN—which rapidly transition between Seyfert 1 and Seyfert 2 classifications—operate on timescales far shorter than standard disk dynamics allow. These abrupt transitions can be successfully modeled as manifestations of instabilities and rapid depletion within these geometrically compact, stochastically fed flows [Śniegowska et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AN....34310065S/abstract).

## Gas Accretion and Galaxy Disc Evolution

To sustain observed star formation rates over the Hubble time, spiral galaxies require continuous [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]]; without replenishment, existing galactic gas reservoirs would be depleted in $0.5\text{--}2\text{ Gyr}$ [Fraternali et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.2166F/abstract)[Bouché et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..355B/abstract). Cosmological simulations indicate that galaxies acquire the majority of their baryonic mass through [[smooth-accretion|Smooth accretion]] from the cosmic web, which accounts for approximately $77\%$ of mass assembly, compared to $23\%$ from discrete mergers [L'Huillier et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...544A..68L/abstract). This inflow primarily occurs along cosmic filaments and is bimodal, governed by different [[gas-accretion-modes|Gas accretion modes]]: transitioning from a cold mode at high redshift ($z > 2$) to a shock-heated [[hot-mode-accretion|Hot Mode Accretion]] in more massive halos at lower redshifts [Dave et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..219D/abstract)[Woods et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442..732W/abstract). During these assembly phases, virialization heating naturally occurs as a consequence of mass accretion, where the infalling gravitational potential energy is transformed into internal thermal energy through shocks and viscous dissipation [Wang et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007astro.ph..1363W/abstract).

The dynamics of this process are frequently described by an equilibrium or "regulator" model, wherein galaxies exist in a slowly evolving balance between stochastic gas inflow, star formation, and feedback-driven outflows [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). Fluctuations in the accretion rate introduce scatter in galactic scaling relations, such as the fundamental metallicity relation, as galaxies are temporarily perturbed before relaxing back to equilibrium [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443..168F/abstract). Particularly at high redshifts ($z \sim 2$), theoretical models require that gas accretion rates strictly balance both vigorous star formation and powerful outflows to reproduce observed mass-metallicity trends [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract). Quantitatively, the [[net-accretion-rates|Net accretion rates]] for Milky Way-like galaxies peak at $\sim 10\ M_\odot\text{ yr}^{-1}$ at early epochs before undergoing an exponential decay with a typical timescale of $7.2\text{ Gyr}$ [Iza et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517..832I/abstract). 

Stochastic gas accretion dictates not only mass assembly but also the morphological and [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]. The traditional view of galactic morphology as a permanent trait has shifted to a paradigm where morphology is a transient state governed by accretion history; galactic discs can be destroyed by major mergers and subsequently rebuilt by the steady accretion of high-angular-momentum gas [Steinmetz et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002NewA....7..155S/abstract). These flips tend to reduce overall disc sizes and centrally concentrate gas, accelerating star formation and allowing galaxies to reach higher stellar masses more rapidly [Padilla et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.2801P/abstract). The interplay between smooth vs. clumpy accretion is critical here. At high redshifts, massive, gas-rich discs are prone to [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]], leading to the formation of giant clumps and manifesting as [[clumpy-disks|Clumpy disks]] and [[clumpy-star-formation|Clumpy star formation]] [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.1552F/abstract)[Elmegreen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ASPC..419...23E/abstract). Over time, as direct accretion is balanced by radial transport and feedback, these systems transition into quiescent, stellar-dominated discs driven by secular processes and central bar instabilities [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.1552F/abstract)[Combes et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..8616C/abstract).

## Stochastic Star Formation and the Regulator Model

The evolution of star-forming galaxies is heavily regulated by the interplay between cosmological gas accretion, internal star formation, and feedback-driven outflows. In the widely adopted "regulator model" (or quasi-equilibrium model), galaxies exist in a slowly evolving equilibrium state where the mass inflow rate $\dot{M}_{\rm in}$ balances the sum of the [[instantaneous-star-formation-rate|Instantaneous star formation rate]] $\dot{M}_\star$ and the outflow rate $\dot{M}_{\rm out}$ [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). However, because cosmic gas accretion is not perfectly smooth, galaxies experience stochastic perturbations in their gas supply. These fluctuations drive systems away from their equilibrium states, and the finite time required to re-equilibrate naturally produces the observed scatter in scaling relations, such as the galaxy main sequence and the fundamental metallicity relation [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443..168F/abstract), [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract).

To quantify this "burstiness" in the star formation history (SFH), researchers map the physical timescales of the regulator model into Fourier space using the PSD [Caplar et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.3845C/abstract), [Tacchella et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497..698T/abstract). On intermediate scales, the finite time required for the system to re-equilibrate after an accretion event produces the observed [[log-normal-distribution|Log-normal distribution]] of scatter about the star-forming main sequence. Through statistical analysis of this scatter, it has been shown that galaxies lose "memory" of their previous star formation activity on a decorrelation timescale of approximately $200 \text{ Myr}$ [Caplar et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.3845C/abstract). 

While analytical [[toy-models-in-astrophysics|Toy models in astrophysics]] provide a robust framework for capturing this [[inherent-stochasticity|Inherent stochasticity]], distinguishing whether SFH variability is primarily driven by external stochastic accretion or internal, feedback-driven "bursty" physics remains an ongoing challenge. In cosmological hydrodynamic simulations, the discrete, random nature of sub-grid star formation and feedback recipes can strongly impact the macroscopic evolution of individual systems. Although ensemble-averaged scaling relations remain robust, random variability between different realizations of the same simulated galaxy can alter individual final stellar masses by up to $25\%$, even at high numerical resolutions [Borrow et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.2441B/abstract).

## Extreme Accretion Events and Episodic Phenomena

The most dramatic manifestations of inherent stochasticity in galactic nuclei occur when a sudden, massive influx of material is deposited into the central potential. These high-amplitude transient events challenge steady-state models of supermassive black hole (SMBH) growth, highlighting the importance of [[episodic-accretion|Episodic accretion]] and violently variable inflows.

When a star strays too close to an SMBH, it is torn apart by tidal forces in a Tidal Disruption Event (TDE). Following the disruption, the fallback rate of the stellar debris, $\dot{M}_{\rm fb}$, can initially exceed the Eddington accretion limit, $\dot{M}_{\rm Edd}$, by orders of magnitude, driving [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion and Feedback]]: 
$$ \dot{M}_{\rm fb} \gg \dot{M}_{\rm Edd} $$
Because the angular momentum of the infalling stellar gas is relatively low, it cannot immediately settle into a standard thin Keplerian disk. Instead, the trapped accretion energy inflates the gas into a weakly bound, [[quasi-spherical-accretion|Quasi-spherical accretion]] "zero-Bernoulli accretion" flow. In this state, the flow cannot arrest the highly super-Eddington accretion, and the excess energy and angular momentum are expected to escape via powerful polar jets [Coughlin et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...781...82C/abstract). 

Another severe manifestation of unsteady feeding is the "changing-look" AGN, in which a galactic nucleus undergoes rapid, order-of-magnitude variations in luminosity and spectral classification. The timescales for these episodic outbursts are dramatically shorter than the standard [[viscous-timescale|Viscous timescale]] predicted by conventional [[accretion-disk-theory|Accretion Disk Theory]]. Such rapid variability can be modeled as time-dependent instabilities driven by [[radiation-pressure-instability|Radiation-pressure instability]] within the disk. These transitions often involve a complex coupling between an optically thick accretion disk, a hot coronal layer, and an inner advection-dominated accretion flow (ADAF) [Śniegowska et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AN....34310065S/abstract). 

These extreme, short-lived events are symptomatic of a broader paradigm in which SMBH growth is driven by episodic, randomly oriented mass deposition rather than large-scale, persistent gas disks. When discrete packets of gas—such as molecular clouds—fall into the nucleus with misaligned angular momenta, they form warped disks. The subsequent collision and cancellation of angular momentum among misaligned flows enables infall rates far exceeding viscous predictions [Nayakshin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0705.1686N/abstract), [King et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013CQGra..30x4006K/abstract). 

## Historical Development: From Steady State to Chaos

Historically, astrophysical accretion was modeled through highly idealized, deterministic frameworks. At the scale of compact objects and supermassive black holes (SMBHs), the standard paradigm relied on spherically symmetric Bondi accretion or steady-state, aligned Shakura-Sunyaev disks. Within these deterministic models, disk evolution was strictly governed by the [[viscous-timescale|Viscous timescale]], assuming that mass transfer occurred smoothly and continuously. Early statistical explorations of variable feeding included studies of stochastic interstellar accretion onto populations of slow-moving neutron stars in the Galaxy [Blaes et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...381..210B/abstract). Similarly, early models of [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] and [[stellar-accretion|Stellar accretion]] presumed that gas smoothly cooled and condensed into idealized rotationally supported disks.

The rigid determinism of these models began to fracture as observations revealed structural complexities and temporal variability that steady states could not explain. At the galactic scale, the introduction of stochastic [[self-propagating-star-formation|Self-propagating star formation]] models demonstrated that introducing finite, random probabilities for local star-forming events—rather than purely continuous, deterministic triggers—could naturally generate the quasi-rigid spiral structures of galaxies [Gerola et al. (1978)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1978ApJ...223..129G/abstract). Concurrently, the realization that spiral galaxies must continuously replace depleted gas reservoirs over $0.5 - 2 \text{ Gyr}$ timescales [Fraternali et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.2166F/abstract), [Bouché et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..355B/abstract) highlighted that large-scale feeding mechanisms from the cosmic web are inherently episodic and variable. 

The most drastic paradigm shift occurred in the theoretical modeling of active galactic nuclei (AGN). Assuming standard radiative efficiency for the Bondi accretion of the hot interstellar medium significantly overpredicted the luminosities of nearby SMBHs, forcing the adoption of radiatively inefficient, advection-dominated accretion flows (ADAFs). Furthermore, because the standard [[viscous-timescale|Viscous timescale]] is prohibitively long for large, steady disks to fuel rapid AGN outbursts, theorists pivoted toward chaotic, event-driven feeding mechanisms. Seminal work established that SMBHs are likely fueled by successive, discrete mass deposition events with randomly oriented angular momenta. This [[chaotic-accretion|Chaotic accretion]] leads to warped inner disks and rapid angular momentum cancellation, driving mass inflow rates that vastly exceed standard viscous transport limits [King et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013CQGra..30x4006K/abstract). 

Today, [[inherent-stochasticity|Inherent stochasticity]] is recognized not merely as observational noise, but as a fundamental physical feature of the universe. The "burstiness" of star formation and the observed scatter in the star-forming main sequence are now explicitly modeled using power spectral density frameworks, which reveal that galaxies "forget" their previous star formation activity on correlation timescales of $\sim 200 \text{ Myr}$ [Caplar et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.3845C/abstract). 

## Computational Challenges in Stochastic Modeling

Modern cosmological hydrodynamical simulations must span an immense range of spatial and temporal scales to model [[mass-accretion-and-assembly|Mass Accretion and Assembly]]. To capture physical processes that occur below the achievable numerical resolution—such as star formation, black hole accretion, and energetic feedback—simulations rely on sub-grid prescriptions. In contemporary codes, these sub-grid models heavily utilize stochastic elements to discretize continuous equations across the time domain [Borrow et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.2441B/abstract). While mathematically necessary, this approach introduces numerical noise that can have profound consequences on galactic evolution.

A primary computational challenge is the back-reaction of this small-scale ($< 1$ kpc) stochasticity onto macroscopic galaxy properties over cosmological ($> 1$ Gyr) timescales. Because galaxy formation is a highly non-linear process regulated by complex feedback loops, small random perturbations in the timing or location of a sub-grid event can rapidly amplify. Studies utilizing the EAGLE model implemented in the SWIFT code have demonstrated that re-simulating the exact same initial conditions with different random numerical seeds leads to significant divergence in the final state of the system [Borrow et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.2441B/abstract). 

While the properties of individual galaxies are highly sensitive to this stochasticity, properties aggregated cumulatively over many objects are much more robust. The median loci of global scaling relations remain relatively robust against random numerical variations [Borrow et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.2441B/abstract). However, analyzing the scatter in these relations presents a theoretical tension: it remains an active debate whether the observed scatter is a fundamental physical feature driven by the [[inherent-stochasticity|Inherent stochasticity]] of gas inflows and molecular cloud lifecycles [Caplar et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.3845C/abstract), [Tacchella et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497..698T/abstract), or if it is artificially inflated by the numerical noise of sub-grid prescriptions.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the macroscopic effects of stochastic [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] are increasingly well-characterized, the underlying microphysics remains a major source of uncertainty. In the regime of large-scale structure formation, the exact nature of the turbulent cascade driven by accretion shocks depends sensitively on the elusive microphysics of the intracluster medium. Specifically, uncertainties in plasma collisionality and the dissipation physics of weak shocks fundamentally limit our ability to predict stochastic particle acceleration rates and the resulting diffuse radio emission in clusters [Miniati et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800...60M/abstract). On galactic scales, the stochastic feeding of supermassive black holes via randomly oriented accretion events permits mass assembly at rates much faster than the standard [[viscous-timescale|Viscous timescale]] [King et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013CQGra..30x4006K/abstract), but the exact triggers for phenomena operating on these rapid timescales—such as "changing-look" active galactic nuclei—remain debated [Śniegowska et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AN....34310065S/abstract).

Furthermore, the literature exhibits explicit tension regarding how stochasticity should be treated in cosmological simulations. It remains unresolved whether the observed variance in galaxy scaling relations is primarily driven by internal bursty feedback or by the external [[inherent-stochasticity|Inherent stochasticity]] of gas inflows. Some authors argue that stochastic sub-grid prescriptions introduce a fundamental limit to predictive power, noting that random variability between identical re-simulations can alter the predicted stellar mass of individual, well-resolved galaxies by up to $25\%$ [Borrow et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.2441B/abstract). 

To formalize and measure this variability, recent theoretical efforts model star formation histories as stochastic processes, seeking to extract physical timescales from the power spectral density (PSD) of observed galaxies [Caplar et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.3845C/abstract), [Tacchella et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497..698T/abstract). Moving forward, tightly constraining these stochastic hyperparameters requires shifting from modeling individual objects to population-level hierarchical inference [Wan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.4002W/abstract). Because each galaxy is observed as a single-epoch snapshot, disentangling the duty cycles, burstiness, and inflow rates of stochastic accretion is fundamentally an inverse statistical problem. Next-generation wide-field spectroscopic facilities are expected to be transformative in resolving these degeneracies.
