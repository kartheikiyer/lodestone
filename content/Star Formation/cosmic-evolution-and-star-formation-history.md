## Article

The study of **Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History** characterizes the global trajectory of baryonic matter as it condenses into stars across Hubble time. A central metric in this field is the comoving cosmic star formation rate density (SFRD), which quantifies the total mass of gas converted into stars per unit volume per unit time [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Observations across the electromagnetic spectrum have established a definitive overarching narrative for the [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]]. Starting from the epoch of [[first-light-galaxies|First-light galaxies]], the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] density rose steadily over the first few billion years of cosmic history. This activity culminated in a pronounced epoch known as "cosmic noon" ($z \approx 1-3$), where both the global rate and central supermassive black hole accretion reached their [[peak-star-formation-history|Peak star formation history]]. Following this peak, the SFRD has undergone an exponential decline of nearly an order of magnitude down to the present day [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract).

Reconstructing this history requires synthesizing a variety of multi-wavelength [[star-formation-rate-tracers|Star formation rate tracers]] to account for both unobscured [[fuv-star-formation-rate|ultraviolet emission]] and heavily dust-reprocessed [[infrared-galaxy-evolution|infrared radiation]], as the vast majority of stellar mass assembly at cosmic noon occurred in heavily obscured environments [Kennicutt et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ARA&A..36..189K/abstract), [Somerville et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph.11001S/abstract). Analyses of these [[integrated-galaxy-properties|integrated observations]] yield several fundamental scaling relations that govern galaxy growth. Most notably, [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] across cosmic time exhibit a tight correlation between their [[instantaneous-star-formation-rate|Instantaneous star formation rate]] and total stellar mass, known as the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]]. Furthermore, the integral of this star formation reveals a "downsizing" paradigm: contrary to naive expectations of hierarchical structure formation, the most [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] assembled their stellar mass and subsequently transitioned into [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] much earlier and more rapidly than their low-mass counterparts.

Despite a robust consensus on the general shape of the cosmic [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]], several critical open questions remain at the frontiers of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. In the early Universe, recent observations by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have uncovered an unexpected abundance of UV-bright [[very-young-galaxies|Very Young Galaxies]] at $z > 10$. This challenges standard theoretical models and suggests that early star formation may have been highly stochastic or governed by top-heavy initial mass functions. At intermediate redshifts, the precise physical mechanisms responsible for quenching star formation—whether driven internally by active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback or externally by environmental stripping—remain heavily debated. Finally, accurately quantifying the SFRD is continuously challenged by methodological biases, such as the "outshining" of older [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]] by bursty star formation and the persistent difficulty of conducting a complete census of deeply hidden starbursts within [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|Dust-Obscured Star Formation and Galaxies]].

## The Global Cosmic Star Formation Rate Density (SFRD)

The global cosmic star formation rate density (SFRD), often denoted $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$, measures the comoving mass of gas converted into stars per unit time and unit volume as a function of redshift [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). The visualization of this evolutionary trajectory across cosmic time is universally known as the 'Madau-Dickinson' plot. It characterizes the overarching history of [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] from the epoch of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] to the present day.

Tracing this trajectory forward from the Big Bang, the earliest epochs of stellar assembly remain a dynamic frontier. Recent observations with the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have revealed an unexpected abundance of UV-bright [[very-young-galaxies|galaxy candidates]] at $z > 10$. This challenges standard theoretical models and suggests that the first few hundred million years may have been characterized by highly bursty star formation, elevated [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|star formation efficiencies]], a lack of dust attenuation, or top-heavy initial mass functions [Finkelstein et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946L..13F/abstract), [Mitsuhashi et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260116284M/abstract). Over the next several billion years, as larger dark matter halos assembled, the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] density rose steadily. This steady rise is characterized by the evolution of the star formation rate function; the characteristic SFR derived from Schechter fits grew linearly from $\approx 5\ M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$ at $z \sim 8$ to $\approx 100\ M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$ at $z \sim 2$, providing strong evidence for a uniform buildup of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] during the first 3 Gyr of cosmic time [Smit et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...756...14S/abstract). This activity culminated in a pronounced epoch of [[vigorous-star-formation|Vigorous star formation]] known as "cosmic noon", reaching its [[peak-star-formation-history|Peak star formation history]] approximately 3.5 Gyr after the Big Bang at $z \approx 1.9$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). However, there is an active debate in the literature regarding the exact shape of this maximum; while some multi-wavelength calibrations point to a sharply defined peak [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract), recent observational studies [Katsianis et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919...88K/abstract) and earlier theoretical simulations [Nagamine et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...541...25N/abstract) argue that the SFRD took the form of a broader plateau spanning $z \approx 1-4$.

A comprehensive census of the SFRD peak requires careful treatment of dust, as a majority of the star formation at cosmic noon occurred in heavily obscured environments. [[infrared-galaxy-evolution|Mid-infrared observations]] mapping emission out to $z = 1.2$ [Rujopakarn et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...718.1171R/abstract), alongside predictions from early $\Lambda$CDM hydrodynamical simulations [Nagamine et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...541...25N/abstract), indicate that up to two-thirds of the stellar emission at $z \approx 1-3$ is absorbed and re-radiated by dust. Even at $z > 4$, hidden star formation within [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|High-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies]] can contribute up to 40% of the total SFRD [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract), underscoring the critical role of [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|Dust-Obscured Star Formation and Galaxies]] in tracking the true comoving rate.

Following cosmic noon, the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] density underwent a precipitous decline, dropping by roughly an order of magnitude down to the local Universe. Furthermore, robust constraints from the "cosmic spectrum"—the volume-averaged spectrum of local galaxies—independently limit the allowed increase in the global SFR density back to $z \approx 1$, corroborating this steep post-peak decline [Baldry et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...569..582B/abstract). This post-peak decay is well-modeled by an exponential decline with an e-folding timescale of roughly 3.9 Gyr [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract), ultimately reaching a present-day value of $\dot{\rho}_\star(0) \approx 1.09 \times 10^{-2} \, M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-3}$ [Rujopakarn et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...718.1171R/abstract). Integrating this cosmic [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] over time yields the cumulative stellar mass density. This integration reveals that approximately half of the stellar mass observed in the present-day Universe was already formed before $z = 1.3$, with 25% forming before the peak of cosmic noon and the remaining 25% forming after $z = 0.7$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract).

Finally, the shape of the Madau-Dickinson relation is not unique to stellar mass assembly. The global history of supermassive black hole accretion closely mirrors the rise and fall of the SFRD around cosmic noon, exhibiting a proportional growth curve that peaks contemporaneously at $z \approx 2$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). However, recent evidence from JWST indicates that at very high redshifts ($z > 5.5$), the growth of supermassive black holes tends to outpace the assembly of stellar mass [D'Silva et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250303431D/abstract). This striking similarity at later epochs serves as foundational evidence for the co-evolution of central supermassive black holes and their [[host-galaxies|Host galaxies]] throughout cosmic history.

## Multi-Wavelength Tracers and Observational Methodologies

Accurate measurement of the cosmic star formation history relies on a suite of [[star-formation-rate-tracers|Star formation rate tracers]] spanning the electromagnetic spectrum to capture both unobscured and dust-enshrouded stellar growth. 

Rest-frame [[fuv-star-formation-rate|ultraviolet (UV) continuum emission]] directly traces the photospheric output of young, massive [[ob-stars|OB stars]], providing a measure of the [[instantaneous-star-formation-rate|Instantaneous star formation rate]]. Surveys routinely identify high-redshift [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] via the Lyman-break technique, exploiting the prominent opacity break at $912 \ \text{\AA}$ caused by neutral hydrogen. However, UV photons are highly susceptible to dust attenuation. Furthermore, comparing these UV observations directly to rest-frame optical structures requires a "morphological k-correction," as galaxies systematically appear more late-type at shorter wavelengths [Mager et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...864..123M/abstract). Optical recombination lines, notably H$\alpha$ and H$\beta$, provide an alternative and physically robust measure of the ionizing photon flux from [[h-ii-galaxies|H II regions]] [Kennicutt et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ARA&A..36..189K/abstract). By measuring the Balmer decrement (the H$\alpha$/H$\beta$ line ratio), observers can quantify and correct for nebular dust attenuation directly, avoiding the assumptions inherent in purely continuum-based corrections [Shivaei et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...815...98S/abstract).

A critical challenge in galaxy evolution is that a substantial fraction of cosmic star formation is [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|obscured by dust]], which absorbs UV/optical photons and re-radiates the energy in the far-infrared (FIR). At the peak of the cosmic star formation rate density ($z \approx 1.9-2.0$), theoretical hydrodynamical simulations predict that approximately two-thirds of all stellar emission is dust-obscured [Nagamine et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...541...25N/abstract). Consequently, mid-IR (e.g., $24 \ \mu\text{m}$) and sub-millimeter observations are essential for compiling a complete census of galaxy assembly [Rujopakarn et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...718.1171R/abstract). Empirical correlations between the FIR-to-UV flux ratio and the UV continuum slope ($\beta$) are frequently used to estimate total SFRs, although these relations can severely underpredict the infrared luminosity in [[heavily-dust-obscured-starbursts|Heavily dust-obscured starbursts]] and ultraluminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs) [Meurer et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.11201M/abstract). Recent radio-selected samples have further demonstrated that deeply obscured systems completely missed by optical and UV surveys still constitute a substantial fraction of the total SFR density even at $z > 4$ [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract).

To circumvent dust biases, astronomers employ radio and X-ray observations. Deep radio continuum surveys (e.g., utilizing 3 GHz observations) trace non-thermal synchrotron radiation from [[core-collapse-supernova-rate|core-collapse supernova]] remnants, providing a dust-independent SFR indicator out to $z \sim 5$ [Leslie et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...58L/abstract). Similarly, the X-ray luminosities of high-mass X-ray binary populations have been calibrated as effective probes of recent star formation histories on galactic scales [Ghosh et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...559L..97G/abstract), [Lehmer et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...851...11L/abstract).

To synthesize these varied diagnostics, researchers typically rely on [[integrated-galaxy-properties|spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting]]. By comparing broadband multi-wavelength photometry to theoretical templates (e.g., [[bruzual-charlot-stellar-population-synthesis-models|Bruzual-Charlot stellar population synthesis models]]), observers can simultaneously derive [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar masses]], ages, and SFRs [Salim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...619L..39S/abstract), [Thorne et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..540T/abstract). However, SED fitting is subject to notable systematic uncertainties. Assumptions regarding the initial mass function, dust attenuation laws, and simplified [[sfr-calibrations|SFR calibrations]] can introduce variations of $\sim 0.1-0.3$ dex in derived SFRs [Kobayashi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763....3K/abstract). Furthermore, in galaxies with highly bursty star formation, the phenomenon of "outshining"—where the immense luminosity of young stars masks the underlying older [[galactic-stellar-populations|stellar populations]]—can lead to systematic underestimations of total stellar masses, highlighting the ongoing methodological challenges in accurately reconstructing early galaxy assembly [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250415255W/abstract). To improve these reconstructions, researchers are developing advanced Bayesian methods. For instance, the introduction of the $\Delta_{\text{age}, n}$ parameter provides a robust metric to quantify the duration and extension of reconstructed star formation histories, mitigating some degeneracies in traditional SED fitting [Rossi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250706006R/abstract).

## Stellar Mass Assembly and the Downsizing Paradigm

The buildup of stellar mass over cosmic time represents the integral of the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] density, modulated by the return of enriched gas to the interstellar medium by dying stars. Observations indicate that the global [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass density]] inferred at any epoch matches the time integral of preceding star-formation activity reasonably well, effectively bridging instantaneous measurements with the present-day cosmic stellar mass budget [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). 

The evolving distribution of these masses is quantified by the galaxy stellar mass function, which is typically parameterized by a Schechter function. Estimates based on near-infrared photometry yield a characteristic stellar mass of $M^* \sim 5 \times 10^{10} h^{-2} M_\odot$ with a low-mass slope of $\alpha \sim -1.1$ [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Over the redshift interval $0 < z < 4$, the evolution of the stellar mass function is driven primarily by an increase in the characteristic density parameter, with relatively little evolution in the characteristic mass or the low-mass slope [Thorne et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..540T/abstract).

Measuring these masses relies on converting observed luminosities via the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]] ($\Upsilon_*$). Because rest-frame blue and ultraviolet bands are highly sensitive to recent star formation and dust attenuation, near-infrared bands (e.g., the $K$-band) are the preferred diagnostic. In the near-infrared, $\Upsilon_*$ varies by only a factor of roughly two across a wide range of star formation histories, compared to a factor of ten in the optical [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Nevertheless, population synthesis models inherently retain uncertainties of roughly $\pm 40\%$ ($\sim 0.15$ dex) for a fixed initial mass function due to age-metallicity-extinction degeneracies.

A foundational result of modern [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] is the "downsizing" paradigm, which stands in contrast to naive expectations of pure hierarchical assembly where the largest structures should form their stars last. Instead, observations show that the epoch of peak star formation is mass-dependent: the most [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] formed their stars earlier and more rapidly. Galaxies with $M_* > 10^{10.8} M_\odot$ formed the bulk of their stellar mass in the first $\sim 3$ Gyr of cosmic history and dropped to their present-day star formation rates by $z \sim 1$ [Juneau et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...619L.135J/abstract). Intermediate-mass systems ($10^{10.2} < M_* / M_\odot < 10^{10.8}$) continued active mass assembly for an additional $\sim 2$ Gyr, whereas low-mass galaxies have experienced extended, continuous star formation across the Hubble time [Juneau et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...619L.135J/abstract).

This [[morphological-downsizing|mass-dependent evolution]] is clearly reflected in the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]], where the [[normalized-star-formation-rate|specific star formation rate]] ($\mathrm{sSFR} \equiv \mathrm{SFR}/M_*$) is inversely correlated with stellar mass at any given redshift [Damen et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...690..937D/abstract). Furthermore, the average sSFRs of the galaxy population increase steeply with redshift, scaling approximately as $\mathrm{sSFR} \propto (1+z)^n$ with $n \approx 5.0$, though the exact slope depends on stellar mass [Damen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...690..937D/abstract), [Ilbert et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...579A...2I/abstract).

As massive galaxies rapidly assembled their mass, their star formation truncated. They transitioned from the actively star-forming [[blue-star-populations|blue cloud]], traversing the "green valley" at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 0.8$), and joined the red sequence of [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] [Gonçalves et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...67G/abstract), [Fang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...858..100F/abstract). While the phenomenological picture of downsizing is well-established, the exact physical drivers remain heavily debated. The literature is currently divided on whether this mass-driven quenching is primarily regulated by internal mechanisms—such as [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback]] or morphological stabilization [Conselice et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4857C/abstract), [Davies et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250521948D/abstract)—or whether external environmental factors, such as strangulation and tidal stripping in dense groups and clusters, dominate the shutdown of star formation [Sheth et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...650L..25S/abstract), [Trudeau et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...972...27T/abstract).

## Cosmic Noon: The Peak of Galaxy Activity

The epoch spanning redshifts $z \approx 1$ to $3$, colloquially known as "cosmic noon", represents the most active phase of galaxy assembly in the Universe. During this period, which corresponds to approximately 3.5 to 6 billion years after the Big Bang, the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] density ($\dot{\rho}_\star$) reached its absolute maximum [[peak-star-formation-history|Peak star formation history]] before undergoing an exponential decline to the present day [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). While standard models and observations generally agree on a peak at $z \approx 1.9$, some recent observational multi-wavelength studies [Katsianis et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919...88K/abstract) and foundational hydrodynamical simulations [Nagamine et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...541...25N/abstract) suggest the maximum may instead resemble a broad plateau stretching across $z \approx 1-4$ rather than a sharp peak. Regardless of its exact topology, this extended period of elevated activity is responsible for producing the bulk of the stellar mass in the local Universe.

A defining characteristic of cosmic noon is the tight correlation between a galaxy's instantaneous star formation rate (SFR) and its [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|total stellar mass]] ($M_\star$), known as the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] (SFMS). Extensive photometric and spectroscopic campaigns, such as the [[candels-survey|CANDELS survey]] and the [[mosdef-survey|MOSDEF survey]], have robustly quantified this relation. Using rest-frame optical emission lines (H$\alpha$ and H$\beta$) to bypass the biases of ultraviolet-based [[star-formation-rate-tracers|Star formation rate tracers]], the [[mosdef-survey|MOSDEF survey]] confirmed a log-linear SFMS with a slope of $\approx 0.65$ at $1.4 < z < 2.6$ and an intrinsic scatter of merely $\approx 0.31$ dex for star-forming galaxies at $2.1 < z < 2.6$ [Shivaei et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...815...98S/abstract). The persistence and low scatter of this relation indicate that galaxy growth during cosmic noon was primarily driven by steady gas accretion, rather than being entirely dominated by stochastic major mergers.

Accurately charting the CSFRD at cosmic noon is heavily complicated by dust attenuation. At $z \approx 2$, the Universe experienced an era of highly [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|Dust-Obscured Star Formation and Galaxies]]. Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations predict that up to two-thirds of the stellar emission at these redshifts is absorbed by interstellar dust and re-radiated at longer wavelengths [Nagamine et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...541...25N/abstract). Consequently, rest-frame ultraviolet studies alone can severely undercount the total star formation budget during cosmic noon if deeply obscured, infrared-luminous systems are neglected [Meurer et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.11201M/abstract), [Talia et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract).

Crucially, the assembly of stellar mass during this epoch was mirrored by the rapid growth of supermassive black holes (SMBHs). The comoving accretion rate density of [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] closely tracks the rise and fall of the CSFRD, peaking in the same redshift window [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract), [D'Silva et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250303431D/abstract). Averaged over large galaxy populations, the SMBH accretion rate is linearly correlated with the [[host-galaxies|host galaxy's]] SFR, strongly implying that black hole coevolution and stellar mass assembly are regulated by the same underlying cold gas supplies [Chen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1401.2179C/abstract). As cosmic noon waned at $z < 1.5$, mass-driven quenching processes—likely linked to this AGN feedback alongside internal morphological stabilization—began to increasingly suppress star formation, transitioning massive systems out of the blue cloud and onto the red sequence [Gonçalves et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...67G/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4857C/abstract).

## Scaling Relations and the Star-Forming Main Sequence

Across cosmic time, the vast majority of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] reside on a tight locus in the parameter space connecting their star formation rate (SFR) and total stellar mass ($M_*$). This scaling relation is known as the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] (SFMS) and persists from the local universe up to at least $z \sim 5$ [Leslie et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...58L/abstract). The sequence is commonly parameterized as a power law:
$$ \log \text{SFR} = \alpha \log M_* + \beta(z) $$

The normalization of the SFMS, $\beta(z)$, scales strongly with redshift. This is often expressed via the [[normalized-star-formation-rate|specific star formation rate]] ($\text{sSFR} \equiv \text{SFR}/M_*$), which measures the fractional rate of stellar mass growth. Galaxies at a fixed mass had significantly higher sSFRs in the past, with the median sSFR evolving approximately as $\text{sSFR} \propto (1+z)^b$ [Ilbert et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...579A...2I/abstract). In the sSFR-mass plane, this manifests as a steady upward shift of the main sequence ridgeline toward earlier cosmic epochs, reflecting the higher cosmological gas accretion rates at high redshift. Beyond the standard SFR-$M_*$ relation, recent frameworks introduce the Fundamental Formation Relation (FFR), which links sSFR to the molecular [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]] ($\mu$) and [[average-star-formation-efficiency|star formation efficiency]] (SFE). The FFR identifies a tighter physical connection between a galaxy's gas content and its star formation efficiency than standard two-dimensional scaling laws, providing a more fundamental metric for galaxy growth and quenching [Dou et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...907..114D/abstract).

While the relation is robust, the exact slope $\alpha$ remains debated. For low- and intermediate-mass galaxies, the slope is generally found to be near unity or slightly sub-linear ($\alpha \approx 0.65-0.8$) [Shivaei et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...815...98S/abstract). However, there is substantial evidence for a mass-dependent [[morphological-downsizing|bending or flattening]] of the SFMS at high stellar masses ($M_* \gtrsim 10^{10.5} M_\odot$), particularly at $z < 1.5$ [Leslie et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...58L/abstract), [Thorne et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..540T/abstract). Recent observations from the Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1) confirm this bending of the star-forming main sequence at the high-mass end ($\log M > 11$) across a wide redshift range from $z = 0.2$ to $3.0$ [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315314E/abstract). This flattening strongly correlates with galaxy morphology; galaxies with high Sérsic indices ($n > 2$) exhibit lower $\text{SFR}/M_*$ ratios. This indicates that the buildup of [[galactic-bulge-giants|central bulges]] and subsequent mass-quenching drive massive galaxies off the upper main sequence, transitioning them toward populations of [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] [Whitaker et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...811L..12W/abstract). Spatially resolved studies further suggest that this flattening is a signature of inside-out quenching, where the suppression of star formation proceeds from the dense inner regions outward [Hemmati et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...896L..17H/abstract). Evidence for this "inside-out" quenching is already active at $z \sim 4$, where massive galaxies exhibit reduced specific star formation rates in their central regions compared to their outskirts [Jung et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...834...81J/abstract). Interestingly, a diversity exists among [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|early-type galaxies]] that remain on the main sequence. Spatially resolved observations, such as those from the MaNGA survey, distinguish between early-type main sequence galaxies with centrally concentrated star formation and those exhibiting suppressed centers but extended, disk-like star formation patterns, revealing complex environmental and internal origins for their structural evolution [Koyama et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250716212K/abstract).

The intrinsic SFMS scatter is remarkably tight, typically ranging from $0.3$ to $0.4$ dex across a wide range of redshifts [Salmon et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799..183S/abstract), [He et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250506868H/abstract). Studies utilizing robust dust-corrected Balmer emission lines from the [[mosdef-survey|MOSDEF survey]] find a scatter of $\sim 0.31$ dex at $z \sim 2$ [Shivaei et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...815...98S/abstract). A significant portion of this dispersion can be attributed to structural variations at fixed mass: more compact galaxies with higher stellar surface densities exhibit greater variations in their SFRs. This is theorized to result from fluctuations in cosmic accretion flows interacting with shorter gas depletion timescales [Whitaker et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...811L..12W/abstract), [He et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250506868H/abstract).

The interplay between stellar mass and SFR is further modulated by chemical enrichment, extending the SFMS into a three-dimensional parameter space known as the [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]]. While the standard mass-metallicity relation dictates that massive galaxies are more metal-rich, at a fixed stellar mass, galaxies with higher SFRs tend to have lower gas-phase metallicities. This anti-correlation is widely interpreted as the result of pristine gas inflows that simultaneously trigger star formation and dilute existing metal abundances. Whether this fundamental relation evolves significantly with redshift remains a topic of active debate; current literature notes that observational biases and the degeneracy between $M_*$/SFR and redshift complicate efforts to disentangle true chemical evolution from selection effects [Niino et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761..126N/abstract).

## Star Formation at Cosmic Dawn and the Reionization Era

The exploration of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] during the cosmic dawn and the epoch of reionization ($z > 6$) has been fundamentally transformed by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). Prior to JWST, standard hierarchical models predicted a steep decline in the abundance of massive, star-forming galaxies at $z > 10$. However, recent deep-field observations have revealed a striking overabundance of UV-bright [[very-young-galaxies|nascent systems]] between $z \approx 9$ and $z \approx 16$ [Finkelstein et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946L..13F/abstract). Estimations of the rest-frame UV luminosity function at $z \sim 11$ demonstrate that the comoving number density of galaxies at $M_{\text{UV}} \approx -20$ exhibits surprisingly little evolution from $z \sim 9$ to $z \sim 11$ [Finkelstein et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946L..13F/abstract), a finding that directly challenges theoretical expectations of early [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]].

To explain the unexpectedly high UV luminosities of these systems, researchers have proposed significant deviations from the steady-state [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] paradigms established at lower redshifts. Spectroscopic and photometric analyses suggest that star formation at $z > 7$ is highly stochastic, characterized by long-duration, large-amplitude bursts [Mitsuhashi et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260116284M/abstract). Such "bursty" star formation models can transiently boost a galaxy's UV brightness by up to $\Delta M_{\text{UV}} \sim -2.0$ mag relative to a constant star formation model. This variability can naturally reproduce the observed abundance of $z > 10$ galaxies without requiring an anomalously high underlying dark matter halo mass [Mitsuhashi et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260116284M/abstract). Furthermore, systems at $z \simeq 7$ frequently exhibit steeply rising star formation histories. Assuming a constant star formation rate for these rapidly growing objects can lead to overestimating their 100-Myr-averaged star formation rate by a factor of roughly three [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110741F/abstract). Alongside extreme burstiness, these early galaxies operate with an intrinsically [[high-star-formation-efficiency|High star formation efficiency]] and are remarkably compact, often featuring half-light radii of $r_e \sim 0.5$ kpc [Finkelstein et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946L..13F/abstract). Additionally, the star formation rate surface density ($\Sigma_{\text{SFR}}$) is observed to increase mildly with redshift out to $z = 10$, acting as a critical diagnostic for early mass accumulation in these compact systems [Calabrò et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240217829C/abstract).

An alternative—or potentially complementary—solution to the tension between $\Lambda$CDM predictions and the observed UV luminosity density involves an evolving Initial Mass Function (IMF). If the early universe harbored environments conducive to a top-heavy IMF, potentially linked to the metal-poor conditions characteristic of [[first-star-formation|First star formation]] and Population III stars, early galaxies would produce significantly more UV light per unit stellar mass [Finkelstein et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946L..13F/abstract). Models incorporating a top-heavy IMF or elevated star formation efficiencies can successfully reproduce the bright end of the $z \sim 13$ UV luminosity function [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954L..48W/abstract). However, the literature remains currently conflicted on whether variations in the IMF, extreme temporal burstiness, or a lack of dust attenuation are the primary drivers of the observed UV excesses.

While the [[early-galaxy-formation|cosmic dawn]] is often associated with [[very-young-galaxies|Very Young Galaxies]] undergoing their initial formative bursts, JWST has also identified surprisingly mature stellar populations at these epochs. Some galaxies at $z \sim 10$ display low specific star formation rates and high [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|stellar mass-to-light (M/L) ratios]], suggesting they formed the bulk of their stellar mass at least 500 Myr prior (at $z > 20$) and have since evolved into more quiescent states [Santini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209139S/abstract). Measuring these stellar populations accurately relies heavily on breaking degeneracies in SED fitting, which has been recently aided by JWST/MIRI imaging at $7.7\ \mu\text{m}$, allowing for the first rest-frame $I$-band measurements of galaxies at $z \approx 8$ [Helton et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250602099H/abstract). Additionally, deep multi-wavelength observations reveal that dust obscuration remains a critical factor even deep into the epoch of reionization. In massive ($10^9 - 10^{10} M_\odot$) [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|Lyman-break galaxies]] at $z \simeq 7$, 50% to 80% of the star formation is heavily obscured by dust [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110741F/abstract). This substantial obscuration underscores that the assembly of stellar mass and the [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|chemical enrichment]] of the interstellar medium progressed exceptionally rapidly during the first billion years of cosmic history. Bridging the gap between cosmic dawn and cosmic noon, the ALPINE survey has provided crucial insights into "teenage" galaxies at $z = 4-6$. ALPINE's large-scale census of [C II] $158\ \mu\text{m}$ emission highlights the demographics and kinematics of normal star-forming galaxies during this transitional epoch, revealing a substantial presence of early mergers, extended gas halos, and significant dust obscuration already well-established by $z \sim 5$ [Faisst et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Univ....8..314F/abstract).

## The Role of Dust-Obscured Star Formation

A complete census of the cosmic [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] requires accounting for the substantial fraction of stellar emission that is absorbed by interstellar dust. In star-forming regions, ultraviolet (UV) photons from massive young stars are readily absorbed by dust grains and thermally re-radiated at mid- to far-infrared (FIR) wavelengths. Consequently, rest-frame UV surveys systematically undercount the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] unless large, and sometimes uncertain, extinction corrections are applied [Kobayashi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763....3K/abstract). Both cosmological models and observations indicate that approximately two-thirds of the total stellar emission in the Universe has been reprocessed by dust, making [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|obscured star formation]] the dominant mode of stellar mass assembly near the peak of the cosmic star formation history at $z \approx 1.9-2.0$; an obscured fraction of roughly two-thirds is required in early hydrodynamical simulations to match local data [Nagamine et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...541...25N/abstract), and is supported by mid-infrared observations [Rujopakarn et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...718.1171R/abstract).

The most intense epochs of obscured star formation are driven by populations of [[infrared-galaxy-evolution|infrared-luminous galaxies]], such as Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs, $L_{\rm FIR} > 10^{11} L_\odot$) and ULIRGs ($L_{\rm FIR} > 10^{12} L_\odot$). At high redshifts, these systems are frequently observed as [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|sub-millimeter galaxies]]. Initially discovered in large numbers by the SCUBA instrument and now routinely characterized with high spatial resolution by ALMA, these galaxies exist at a median redshift of $z \sim 2.5$ and often exhibit extreme star formation rates reaching several $100\, M_\odot \rm yr^{-1}$. The integrated emission from these dusty, rapidly evolving systems forms the bulk of the cosmic FIR background, strongly constraining the fraction of early star formation that occurred within optically thick gas clouds [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

The fractional contribution of obscured star formation evolves strongly with both cosmic time and galaxy stellar mass. Cosmological hydrodynamic simulations predict that the dust-obscured fraction of the star formation rate density peaks around $z \sim 2-3$ before gradually becoming subdominant at $z \gtrsim 5$. At any given epoch, more [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] systematically exhibit higher degrees of obscuration due to their enhanced dust mass fractions and column densities [Zimmerman et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240106719Z/abstract). However, observational constraints in the early Universe reveal that heavily obscured systems remain crucial even at very high redshifts. Recent radio and ALMA surveys targeting optically dark [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|High-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies]] at $z > 4.5$ indicate that these elusive systems can contribute up to $40\%$ of the total star formation rate density, representing a population largely missed by standard UV-dropout techniques [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract).

Quantifying this hidden activity poses persistent methodological challenges. Standard empirical calibrations used to correct UV luminosities for dust attenuation—such as the local IRX-$\beta$ relation—can drastically underpredict the FIR fluxes of extreme high-redshift ULIRGs by factors ranging from 7 to 70 [Meurer et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.11201M/abstract). Furthermore, the literature reflects active tensions between UV-corrected and IR-based [[star-formation-rate-tracers|Star formation rate tracers]]. While both wavelength regimes agree on a broad peak or plateau in the global star formation rate density at $1 < z < 4$, they yield conflicting shapes for the high-mass end of the star formation rate function [Katsianis et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919...88K/abstract). This ambiguity highlights the ongoing difficulty in reconciling UV and IR censuses of the most vigorously star-forming galaxies across cosmic time.

## Quenching, Morphological Transformation, and the Green Valley

The local galaxy population exhibits a pronounced color bimodality, separating into a star-forming "blue cloud" and a red sequence dominated by [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]]. The sparsely populated intermediate region, known as the "green valley," consists of galaxies actively transitioning between these two states. Observations indicate that the total stellar mass density residing in the red sequence has roughly doubled since $z \sim 1$, implying a continuous flux of galaxies undergoing the cessation of star formation, a process known as [[mini-quenching-events|quenching]] [Ruhland et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...695.1058R/abstract).

A primary driver of this transition is "mass quenching," which operates largely independently of the local environment. This process is characterized by "[[morphological-downsizing|downsizing]]," wherein the most massive galaxies quench their star formation earlier and more rapidly than lower-mass systems [Gonçalves et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...67G/abstract). At the high-mass end of the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]], the injection of energy from central supermassive black holes—[[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|AGN feedback]]—is strongly associated with rapidly declining star formation histories (SFHs) [Davies et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250521948D/abstract). Conversely, models of galaxy formation, such as the FEGA framework, also highlight the role of "positive AGN feedback," where AGN activity can transiently trigger star formation bursts from cooling gas before permanent quenching takes hold [Contini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240510544C/abstract). Recent observations out to $z < 7$ demonstrate that the specific star formation rate depends predominantly on stellar mass and internal mass density rather than visual morphology, confirming that early galaxy assembly and subsequent quenching are fundamentally mass-driven processes [Conselice et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4857C/abstract).

Despite the dominance of mass in regulating star formation, quenching is often accompanied by distinct morphological transformations. At $z \sim 1-2$, highly concentrated, star-forming spheroids (often termed "blue nuggets") contribute approximately 15% of the cosmic star formation budget. This suggests that structural [[compaction-events|Compaction events]] frequently precede the quenching phase [Osborne et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902...77O/abstract). Following the shutdown of star formation, spheroids with extended stellar disks (S0-like galaxies) become a dominant morphological class within the [[red-sequence-transition-galaxies|green valley]] and the red sequence at $z < 1$ [Osborne et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902...77O/abstract).

In parallel to internal mass-driven mechanisms, environmental effects play a crucial role in suppressing star formation, particularly in dense cluster and group environments. External mechanisms such as strangulation (the removal of the hot circumgalactic gas halo), ram-pressure stripping, and tidal stripping systematically deprive galaxies of their cold gas reservoirs. Stacking analyses of distant clusters reveal that environmental quenching becomes dramatically more effective at $z \sim 1.5$, operating on a rapid timescale as galaxies accrete into dense overdensities [Trudeau et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...972...27T/abstract). Furthermore, interactions and mergers can precipitate quenching through violent tidal gas removal; for example, observations of intermediate-redshift [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] show that mergers can strip up to $\sim 50\%$ of a galaxy's molecular gas into extended tidal tails [D'Onofrio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250721249D/abstract).

The literature remains divided on the relative dominance of internal versus external quenching agents, and it is likely that both operate in tandem depending on the galaxy's mass and epoch. What is unambiguous is that the timescale required to traverse the green valley evolves significantly with redshift. While stellar population synthesis models suggest a typical transition time of $\sim 2$ Gyr in the local Universe, high-redshift observations from the [[mosdef-survey|MOSDEF survey]] indicate that massive transitional galaxies at $1.4 < z < 2.6$ quench much more rapidly, exhibiting exponential decline timescales of $\tau \sim 0.1-0.2$ Gyr [Zick et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...867L..16Z/abstract). This points to an early epoch of catastrophic, internally driven quenching for massive spheroids, transitioning to a regime where more gradual, environmentally driven quenching becomes prevalent at late cosmic times.

## Chemical Evolution and the History of Metallicity Enrichment

The chemical composition of a galaxy serves as a fossil record of its past assembly, linking its [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] to the continuous cycling of gas through stars and the interstellar medium (ISM). As successive [[stellar-generations|Stellar generations]] evolve, they return heavy elements to the ISM via stellar winds and supernova explosions. By $z = 6$, roughly one billion years after the Big Bang, the mean metallicity of the Universe had already risen to approximately $0.001 Z_\odot$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). 

The timescale of [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] enrichment is strongly dependent on the nucleosynthetic channels involved. [[core-collapse-supernova-rate|Core-collapse supernovae]] (Type II) inject $\alpha$-elements (e.g., O, Mg, Si) into the ISM on short timescales ($\lesssim 10^7$ yr), whereas Type Ia supernovae contribute iron-peak elements over much longer, extended timescales ($\gtrsim 1$ Gyr). Consequently, the [$\alpha$/Fe] ratio, or $\alpha$-enhancement, acts as a cosmic clock for galaxy evolution. A short, intense burst of star formation yields a highly $\alpha$-enhanced stellar population, while prolonged, continuous star formation gradually decreases [$\alpha$/Fe]. However, interpreting these ratios can be complex; variations in the stellar initial mass function, such as a top-heavy IMF at high redshift, can also drive elevated $\alpha$-enhancement by increasing the relative fraction of massive stars [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

Globally, galaxies exhibit a tight [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|mass-metallicity relation]] (MZR), wherein both gas-phase and stellar metallicities scale tightly with stellar mass before flattening at $M_* > 10^{10.5} M_\odot$. This relation evolves significantly with cosmic time: at a fixed stellar mass, galaxies were systematically less metal-enriched in the past [Niino et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761..126N/abstract). Recent JWST spectroscopic measurements at $z \approx 10$ reveal that metallicities were approximately a factor of three lower than those observed at $z \approx 0-3$. This offset in the early Universe is likely caused by massive, pristine gas inflows that heavily diluted the observed metal abundances during the earliest phases of galaxy assembly [Pollock et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250615779P/abstract). 

Theoretical models explicitly tie the [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|chemical enrichment history]] to the star formation rate and mass-dependent gas outflows. SFH-regulated chemical evolution models have successfully reproduced the metallicity distributions of local [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxies]], demonstrating that chemical enrichment histories can be directly mapped to structural assembly histories [Yin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958...34Y/abstract). Nevertheless, modeling the chemical evolution of low-mass systems remains challenging; there is ongoing tension regarding the role of stochastic sampling of the IMF in low-SFR environments, which introduces significant scatter in observed abundance ratios (such as N/O) that deterministic models struggle to fully capture [Cerviño et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...394..525C/abstract).

Furthermore, chemical enrichment inherently drives [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|dust production]]. Heavy elements produced by massive stars and asymptotic giant branch stars rapidly condense into dust grains. This dust accumulation fundamentally alters the observability of galaxy evolution, resulting in a peak of obscured star formation at $z \approx 2-3$ before becoming subdominant at $z \gtrsim 5$ [Zimmerman et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240106719Z/abstract). Observationally, the presence of dust and metals severely complicates the extraction of SFHs from integrated photometry due to the [[age-extinction-degeneracy|age-metallicity degeneracy]], wherein older stellar populations and more metal-rich populations both yield redder broadband colors. Breaking this degeneracy requires high-resolution spectral synthesis—utilizing absorption features like $D_n(4000)$, Balmer lines, and [MgFe]—or the application of SFH-regulated priors to accurately disentangle the chemical and stellar age components of unresolved galaxies [Sextl et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...949...60S/abstract), [Yin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958...34Y/abstract).

## Resolved Star Formation Histories: The Fossil Record

The study of [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|Local Volume and Nearby Galaxy Studies]] provides a unique opportunity to reconstruct the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] of galaxies through the "fossil record" of their [[spatially-resolved-stellar-populations|Spatially resolved stellar populations]]. Unlike the integrated light of high-redshift galaxies, which relies on global scaling relations and broad-band spectral energy distributions, high-resolution observations of nearby galaxies allow for the analysis of individual stars and [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] to map mass assembly with high temporal and spatial precision.

The primary tool for extracting this fossil record is the color-magnitude diagram (CMD). By comparing deep, multi-band photometry of resolved stars against theoretical stellar evolutionary models (isochrones), astronomers can infer the distribution of stellar ages and metallicities. This method yields a highly precise, temporally resolved star formation history. For instance, CMD-based studies of [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxies]] in the Local Group reveal diverse evolutionary paths: dwarf irregulars tend to show a gradually increasing star formation rate toward the present, whereas dwarf ellipticals exhibit a gradual decrease, though some retained star formation activity until as recently as $30$ Myr ago [Ren et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966...25R/abstract).

Scaling these techniques to slightly larger distances, the [[angst-survey|ANGST survey]] analyzed the CMDs of 36 dwarf galaxies within $4$ Mpc. This study uncovered a surprising degree of synchronized evolution: a median period of quiescence at lookback times of $3$--$6$ Gyr, followed by a rejuvenated, synchronized epoch of star formation over the past $3$ Gyr that remains active today [Olsen et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913...45O/abstract). Despite this synchronization, there remains an active debate in the literature over whether the predominant mode of star formation in low-mass dwarfs is continuous or driven by global, stochastic starburst episodes [Lee et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..109L/abstract). It is widely agreed, however, that the massive diversity in dwarf galaxy histories is strongly regulated by environmental factors such as ram pressure and tidal stripping [Grebel et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ESASP.445...87G/abstract). Furthermore, studies in the low surface brightness regime reveal that such diffuse galaxies host [[h-i-rich-galaxies|large H I reservoirs]] but remain highly inefficient at converting gas to stars, with depletion times reaching $\sim 18$ Gyr [Christie et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250920080C/abstract). The extreme nearby galaxy [[i-zw-18|I Zw 18]] provides another key puzzle in low-mass evolution, where recent JWST/NIRCam observations have resolved its old stellar populations to finally unveil its historical age and evolutionary state [Bortolini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250204965B/abstract).

In addition to individual stars, [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] act as vital tracers of the chemical and star formation history, effectively providing snapshots of a galaxy's evolution at different epochs. Because clusters can be studied in integrated light, they extend the reach of fossil record analyses to greater distances. For example, the integrated optical spectra of star clusters in the [[notable-astrophysical-objects-j-l|Large Magellanic Cloud]] have been shown to reliably trace the galaxy's global chemical enrichment history derived from resolved stellar populations over the past $\sim 3.5$ Gyr [Chilingarian et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...858...63C/abstract). Similarly, the cluster population of the [[post-starburst-galaxies|post-starburst galaxy]] S12 demonstrates an intense burst of star formation ending $\sim 70$ Myr ago, reaching peak rates of $500_{-250}^{+500}\ M_\odot\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ [Chandar et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...920..105C/abstract).

The fossil record also enables the reconstruction of spatially resolved SFH, unveiling the internal dynamics of galaxy assembly. The PHATTER survey mapped the disk of M33, finding an average star formation rate of $0.32 \pm 0.02\ M_\odot\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ over the past $100$ Myr and demonstrating that young stellar populations ($< 80$ Myr) exhibit a flocculent morphology, whereas older populations are dominated by a two-arm spiral and central bar [Lazzarini et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934...76L/abstract). In the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] system, studies of variable asymptotic giant branch stars in Andromeda IX suggest an outside-in formation scenario, with quenching occurring approximately $3.65_{-0.13}^{+1.52}$ Gyr ago [Abdollahi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...948...63A/abstract). These granular, resolved studies bridge the gap between small-scale star formation physics and the global cosmic star formation rate density.

## Open Questions and Future Directions in Cosmic Evolution

While the broad paradigm of cosmic evolution—characterized by a peak in the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] at $z \approx 2$ followed by a steady decline—is well established, several fundamental mechanisms governing galaxy assembly remain hotly debated. Recent high-redshift observations have challenged long-standing theoretical frameworks, introducing new complexities to our understanding of the early Universe.

One of the most pressing open questions concerns the assembly histories of [[first-light-galaxies|First-light galaxies]] at $z > 6$. Prior to recent James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observations, star formation at high redshift was often modeled as a relatively uniform, steady buildup of stellar mass [Smit et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...756...14S/abstract). However, current spectral analyses strongly suggest that early star formation was characterized by high degrees of stochasticity and burstiness [Firestone et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250108568F/abstract), [Mitsuhashi et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260116284M/abstract). This shift toward bursty star formation complicates the interpretation of photometric [[star-formation-rate-tracers|Star formation rate tracers]]. During a rapid starburst, the phenomenon of outshining occurs: the intense ultraviolet luminosity of newly formed, massive stars completely dominates the spectral energy distribution, obscuring the fainter continuum of older, underlying stellar populations. If not properly accounted for with specialized population-level priors, outshining can lead to systematic offsets in the inferred [[instantaneous-star-formation-rate|Instantaneous star formation rate]] [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250415255W/abstract). Furthermore, the unexpected abundance of UV-bright [[very-young-galaxies|Very Young Galaxies]] at $z > 10$ remains in tension with standard halo mass functions. Reconciling these observations may require profound shifts in early-universe physics, such as the assumption of a top-heavy initial mass function (IMF) or negligible dust attenuation [Finkelstein et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946L..13F/abstract), [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954L..48W/abstract).

At later epochs, the exact physical drivers that halt star formation and move galaxies from the blue cloud through the green valley remain ambiguous. While observational data universally support "[[morphological-downsizing|downsizing]]"—wherein massive spheroids quench earlier and more rapidly than low-mass systems [Juneau et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...619L.135J/abstract), [Gonçalves et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...67G/abstract)—the relative dominance of internal versus external quenching mechanisms is unresolved. It remains actively debated whether quenching is primarily driven by internal AGN feedback and morphological stabilization [Davies et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250521948D/abstract), or by environmental factors such as tidal gas removal and strangulation [D'Onofrio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250721249D/abstract). 

Finally, constructing a complete census of [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|Dust-Obscured Star Formation and Galaxies]] represents a major observational frontier. A substantial fraction of cosmic star formation is obscured by dust, leading to significant discrepancies between UV-corrected and infrared-derived [[sfr-calibrations|SFR calibrations]] [Kobayashi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763....3K/abstract). Heavily obscured starbursts at $z > 4$ alone may contribute up to 40% of the total cosmic star formation rate density [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract). 

Resolving these tensions relies heavily on the capabilities of next-generation observatories. Proposed infrared missions like SPICA aim to directly measure star formation and black hole accretion histories through rest-frame mid-to-far infrared spectroscopy, piercing the dust to probe the peak of cosmic star formation at $z = 1-4$ without the biases inherent to UV tracers [Spinoglio et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021IAUS..356...17S/abstract). At radio wavelengths, the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) will revolutionize our understanding of the interstellar medium by mapping H I 21-cm absorption across cosmic time. By tracing the transition from atomic to molecular gas, SKA will provide the critical missing link between the global cosmic [[gas-fraction|gas reservoir]] and the local astrophysical processes that dictate star formation [Takeuchi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160301938T/abstract).
