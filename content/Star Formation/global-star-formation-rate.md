The global star formation rate density (SFRD), often denoted as $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$, is a fundamental astrophysical metric that quantifies the [[integrated-star-formation|integrated mass of stars]] formed per unit time per unit comoving volume across [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|cosmic time]]. Serving as the primary observable for tracing the luminous assembly of the Universe, the [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|redshift evolution]] of the SFRD is canonically visualized in the Madau-Lilly plot. Observational consensus demonstrates that the global rate of star formation rose steadily during the early epochs of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], peaking approximately 3.5 billion years after the Big Bang at $z \approx 1.9$—an epoch colloquially known as "cosmic noon" [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Following this period of [[vigorous-star-formation|Vigorous star formation]], the SFRD has undergone an exponential decline by roughly an order of magnitude toward the present day [Connolly et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...486L..11C/abstract). Consequently, integration of the SFRD indicates that roughly half of the universe's current [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass]] was already formed by $z = 1.3$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract).

The physical drivers underlying this cosmic "rise and fall" are governed by the complex interplay between cosmological gas accretion, [[galaxy-structure|internal galactic structure]], and self-regulating feedback. Analytically, this evolution is frequently described by quasi-equilibrium or [[bathtub-models|Bathtub models]], which posit that galaxies exist in a steady state balancing the inflow of cold gas from the cosmic web with consumption via star formation and ejection via galactic winds [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). On macroscopic scales, the efficiency with which this gas is converted into stars is empirically described by the [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]] [Kennicutt et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ARA&A..36..189K/abstract). On a global scale, the bulk of the universe's star formation occurs within systems residing on the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]], a tight correlation between a galaxy's [[star-formation-rate-indicators-and-metrics|star formation rate]] and its total stellar mass. The peak of the cosmic SFRD is fundamentally linked to the epoch when cosmological accretion was highest, just before mass-driven quenching processes, such as active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback, began truncating star formation in [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] [Behroozi et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...762L..31B/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4857C/abstract).

Despite a robust phenomenological framework, accurately calibrating the global SFRD presents significant observational and theoretical challenges. Astronomers rely on a variety of wavelength-dependent [[star-formation-rate-tracers|Star formation rate tracers]], from rest-frame ultraviolet to far-infrared emission, each requiring careful calibrations to account for interstellar dust attenuation. A persistent tension in modern astrophysics is the discrepancy wherein the time integral of the empirical SFRD systematically overestimates the directly observed cosmic stellar mass density detailed in [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]]. The origin of this disparity remains an active debate, centering on unaccounted populations of [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|High-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies]], populations of faint [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], and systematic uncertainties arising from the assumed universality of the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. Today, the frontier of SFRD research is driven by observations from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), which is mapping [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] and probing whether the remarkably [[average-star-formation-efficiency|high star formation efficiencies]] observed in the first billion years of the universe ($z > 10$) require fundamental revisions to the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm.

## Definition, History, and the Madau-Lilly Plot

The cosmic star formation rate density (SFRD), typically denoted as $\dot{\rho}_{\star}(z)$, is a fundamental metric characterizing the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]] of the universe. It is defined as the total mass of gas converted into stars per unit time per unit comoving volume at a given redshift $z$. By capturing the aggregated [[instantaneous-star-formation-rate|Instantaneous star formation rate]] of all galaxies within a representative volume, the SFRD serves as the primary observable for tracing the luminous assembly of the universe.

The canonical visualization of $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$ as a function of redshift is known as the Madau-Lilly plot. It originated in the mid-1990s when deep-field observations first allowed astronomers to bridge the local and distant Universe. Early surveys by Lilly et al. mapped the decline of star formation at $z < 1$, while Madau et al. utilized Lyman-break galaxies to constrain the [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|high-redshift]] ($z > 2$) regime [Madau et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997AIPC..393..481M/abstract). Note that the redshift interval $1.4 < z < 3$, where star formation culminates, is historically known as the "redshift desert" due to the difficulty of observing galaxies at these distances [Renzini et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Msngr.137...41R/abstract). This observational gap was subsequently bridged using [[redshift-distribution|photometric redshifts]] from the Hubble Deep Field, establishing a continuous "rise and fall" history of global star formation [Connolly et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...486L..11C/abstract). 

Modern multi-wavelength campaigns have solidified this paradigm. Observational constraints outline a consistent evolutionary trajectory: the global SFRD rose steadily during the early phases of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], culminating in a broad peak approximately 3.5 Gyr after the Big Bang at "cosmic noon" ($z \approx 1.9$). While canonical estimates place the peak around $z \approx 1.9$, some recent multi-wavelength surveys (e.g., the HELP survey leveraging Herschel data) find peak SFRD values at this epoch that are up to 1.4 times higher than standard estimates [Duivenvoorden et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..277D/abstract). Following this maximum, the SFRD has declined exponentially by roughly an order of magnitude toward the present day, characterized by an $e$-folding timescale of $3.9 \text{ Gyr}$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Integration of the Madau-Lilly plot over cosmic time indicates that 50% of the local [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass density]] was assembled before $z = 1.3$, while less than 1% formed during the epoch of reionization [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract).

Despite the general consensus on the shape of the Madau-Lilly plot, a critical test of the standard cosmological paradigm involves comparing the time integral of the SFRD with direct measurements of the cosmic stellar mass density. Under the assumption of a universal [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], the global stellar mass density inferred at any epoch matches reasonably well the time integral of all the preceding star-formation activity [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). However, many empirical calibrations reveal a persistent tension where the time integral yields a value that systematically overestimates the directly observed mass budget [Choi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.419.1280C/abstract), [Kobayashi et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763....3K/abstract). The literature is currently divided on the primary cause of this discrepancy. Some studies argue this tension arises from flux-limited surveys missing populations of faint, [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], which steepen the faint-end slope of [[luminosity-functions-and-scaling-relations|Luminosity Functions and Scaling Relations]] at high redshift [Choi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.419.1280C/abstract). Others suggest the inconsistency is driven by systematic uncertainties in empirical star formation rate conversions, particularly regarding dust obscuration corrections and UV-to-SFR conversion factors, which are highly sensitive to assumptions regarding the IMF.

Furthermore, the high-redshift tail of the Madau-Lilly plot ($z > 4$) is undergoing significant revision. Historically, the SFRD at early epochs was calibrated almost exclusively using rest-frame ultraviolet observations of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]]. However, recent surveys utilizing far-infrared and radio wavelengths have uncovered a substantial population of heavily dust-obscured [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|High-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies]] at $z > 4.5$ that were entirely missed by UV/optical selections. These elusive systems may contribute an amount equal to as much as 40% of the previously known UV-based SFRD at these early epochs, suggesting that the [[nascent-galaxies|nascent universe]] was significantly more dusty and actively star-forming than previously assumed from optical data alone [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract).

## Observational Tracers and Star Formation Diagnostics

To determine the global star formation rate (SFR) of a galaxy, astronomers rely on wavelength-dependent [[star-formation-rate-tracers|Star formation rate tracers]] that map observed luminosities to physical mass conversion rates. The calibration of these SFR indicators requires robust models of the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], stellar evolution timescales, and interstellar dust attenuation.

The [[fuv-star-formation-rate|FUV star formation rate]] is a primary diagnostic derived from the ultraviolet continuum, which is dominated by the photospheres of O and early B-type stars. The FUV continuum is generally sensitive to star formation averaged over a timescale of $\sim 10$ to $100$ Myr, depending on the burstiness of the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] [Flores Velázquez et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.4812F/abstract). In contrast, nebular Hα emission arises from the recombination of hydrogen ionized by the most massive, short-lived stars, thereby probing [[instantaneous-star-formation-rate|instantaneous star formation]] on much shorter timescales of $\sim 5$ Myr [Flores Velázquez et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.4812F/abstract). In systems with extremely low star formation rates ($< 0.001 \, M_\odot \text{yr}^{-1}$), both Hα emission and UV indicators become subject to significant stochastic fluctuations because the upper mass end of the stellar mass distribution is not fully populated [Tremonti et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AAS...211.9503T/abstract). In addition to UV and optical diagnostics, X-ray emission also serves as a quantitative [[star-formation-tracers|star formation tracer]], which shows linear correlations with radio and infrared luminosities up to $z \sim 1$ [Ranalli et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PhDT.......134R/abstract). In post-merger systems and interacting galaxies, the luminosity of the brightest star cluster can serve as an effective tracer for estimating both current and peak historical star formation rates [Bastian et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.390..759B/abstract). Furthermore, within our own Galaxy, the radioactive decay of $^{26}\text{Al}$ resulting in characteristic gamma-ray emission acts as a unique tracer for the Milky Way's massive star formation and [[core-collapse-supernova-rate|Core-collapse supernova rate]] over the last million years [Timmes et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...479..760T/abstract).

A critical challenge in estimating global SFRs is the presence of interstellar dust, which strongly absorbs UV and optical photons. Observational uncertainties in dust attenuation corrections and UV-to-SFR conversion factors can systematically skew global cosmic SFR density estimates by $\sim 0.1-0.3$ dex and $\sim 0.1-0.2$ dex, respectively [Kobayashi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763....3K/abstract). Multi-wavelength approaches are often required to construct a complete census of galaxy growth, as observations leveraging radio and FIR data have revealed that dust-obscured star formation may contribute an amount equal to up to $40\%$ of the previously known UV-based cosmic star formation rate density estimates at $z > 4.5$ [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract).

The conversion of any of these luminosities to a total SFR heavily depends on the assumed stellar mass distribution. Standard relations typically assume a universal IMF. However, there is persistent debate in the literature regarding this universality. Several studies present strong evidence for an SFR-dependent IMF, suggesting that highly star-forming galaxies produce proportionally more massive stars, resulting in a flatter IMF slope [Weidner et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...625..754W/abstract), [Gunawardhana et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.1647G/abstract), [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...39H/abstract). If the IMF systematically varies with environment or starburst intensity, the scaling factors used in standard star formation tracers could introduce significant, systematic errors into global SFR calculations. This ambiguity remains a central tension in reconciling the integral of the cosmic star formation rate density with the observed total stellar mass density across cosmic time.

## The Epoch of Cosmic Noon

The epoch affectionately termed "Cosmic Noon" marks the pinnacle of the universe's [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]]. Occurring roughly 3.5 billion years after the Big Bang at a redshift of $z \approx 1.9$, this period is characterized by the absolute maximum of the global star formation rate density (SFRD) [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). The sheer productivity of this era was staggering: approximately 25% of the local universe's stellar mass was assembled prior to the $z \approx 1.9$ peak, and another 25% formed after $z = 0.7$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). 

During Cosmic Noon, the universe was dominated by [[vigorous-star-formation|Vigorous star formation]] within gas-rich disks. The vast majority of these [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] resided on a tight [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]], a fundamental scaling relation between the ongoing star formation rate and total stellar mass [Förster Schreiber et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ARA&A..58..661F/abstract), [He et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250506868H/abstract). This epoch was particularly critical for the assembly of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]]. Observational evidence indicates that the most massive systems rapidly assembled the bulk of their stellar mass before $z = 1$ [Feulner et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.358L...1F/abstract), a phenomenon generally referred to as [[morphological-downsizing|Morphological downsizing]]. Furthermore, the comoving rate of central supermassive black hole accretion—specifically, the QSO luminosity density—shows a striking similarity to the global star formation history, staying approximately 1/40th of the magnitude of the star formation rate throughout the last 11 Gyr [Boyle et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.293L..49B/abstract), [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract), [Chen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1401.2179C/abstract). This strongly implies a coevolutionary process regulated by shared gas accretion from the cosmic web and internal feedback mechanisms.

Resolved views of galaxies at this epoch reveal that gravitational instabilities and efficient secular processes within gas- and baryon-rich galaxies played an important role in the early buildup of galactic structure [Förster Schreiber et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ARA&A..58..661F/abstract). Massive [[galaxy-nuclei-and-complex-morphologies|bulges]], dense cores, and powerful active galactic nuclei (AGN) driven outflows became more prevalent toward the higher-mass end of the galaxy population, setting the stage for the quenching mechanisms that would ultimately drive the post-peak decline in global star formation [Förster Schreiber et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ARA&A..58..661F/abstract). Furthermore, the discovery of heavily obscured star-forming cores (e.g., GOODS-N-774) at $z = 2.3$ provides direct evidence of systems in their core formation phase, exhibiting the gas dynamics and structure of forming [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|massive elliptical galaxies]] [Nelson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.513..394N/abstract).

## Empirical Scaling Relations and the Main Sequence

The global star formation activity of a galaxy is tightly coupled to its stellar mass, forming one of the most robust [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] in astrophysics. This empirical relationship, known as the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] (SFMS), is characterized by a nearly linear correlation between the star formation rate (SFR) and stellar mass ($M_*$), exhibiting a surprisingly narrow intrinsic dispersion of $\sim 0.3$ dex across cosmic time [Förster Schreiber et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ARA&A..58..661F/abstract), [He et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250506868H/abstract). The normalization of the SFMS evolves predictably with redshift. The specific star formation rate, defined as
$$ \mathrm{sSFR} \equiv \frac{\mathrm{SFR}}{M_*} $$
increases systematically at higher redshifts, reflecting the elevated global gas accretion rates present in the early Universe [Feulner et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.358L...1F/abstract), [Förster Schreiber et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ARA&A..58..661F/abstract). 

While the SFMS is well-defined for low- to intermediate-mass [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], the sequence exhibits a distinct bending or turnover at high stellar masses ($\log M_* > 11$) [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315314E/abstract). This turnover is primarily associated with mass-driven quenching processes, where mechanisms such as active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback and structural stabilization by high [[stellar-surface-density|stellar surface densities]] ($\Sigma_* \propto M_*/R_e^2$) suppress further star formation [Conselice et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4857C/abstract). Furthermore, the growth of a stellar bulge can reduce star formation efficiency by stabilizing the gaseous disk against fragmentation, an effect that likely acts alongside other mass-driven quenching processes [Eales et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491...69E/abstract). Detailed spatially resolved studies, such as the EDGE-CALIFA survey, reveal that star formation efficiency (SFE) remains constant during the initial quenching stages but declines rapidly as galaxies move toward retirement, particularly in their galactic centers [Colombo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A.367C/abstract). However, the literature remains divided on how to interpret populations that fall below the SFMS. While galaxies are traditionally separated into a star-forming [[blue-star-populations|"blue cloud"]] and a quenched [[red-sequence|"red sequence"]], some recent studies argue for a continuous "Galaxy End Sequence" wherein there is no distinct phenomenological break between actively star-forming and [[passive-galaxies|passive galaxies]] [Leslie et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455L..82L/abstract), [Eales et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.3125E/abstract). 

The persistence and tightness of the SFMS lend strong support to quasi-equilibrium "bathtub" models of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. In these frameworks, a galaxy's SFR is regulated by a steady-state balance between smooth gas inflows from the cosmic web, star formation, and feedback-driven outflows [Davé et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract), [Mitra et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1184M/abstract). The intrinsic dispersion of 0.3-0.4 dex along the SFMS is interpreted as the natural response to stochastic fluctuations in cosmic accretion flows over a galaxy's gas consumption timescale. Furthermore, galaxies that scatter above or below the mean SFMS share highly symmetric structural properties, suggesting that these variations in SFR are transient oscillations rather than permanent evolutionary divergences [He et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250506868H/abstract).

The SFMS is also intimately linked to galactic chemical evolution, as detailed in [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]]. Together, stellar mass, SFR, and [[stellar-populations-by-metallicity|gas-phase metallicity]] form a three-dimensional plane often referred to as the Fundamental Metallicity Relation (FMR) [Torrey et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477L..16T/abstract). Hydrodynamical simulations and observations indicate that at a fixed stellar mass, offsets in the SFR are anticorrelated with offsets in metallicity. A galaxy experiencing a surge in metal-poor gas accretion will temporarily shift above the SFMS and below the mean mass-metallicity relation, before returning to equilibrium as the gas is consumed and enriched [Torrey et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477L..16T/abstract). Moreover, spatially resolved studies demonstrate that younger galaxies tend to be more metal-poor at a fixed [[age-velocity-dispersion-relation|velocity dispersion]], and that metallicity gradients become increasingly negative in faster-rotating galaxies [Lu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.1022L/abstract). This interconnected behavior underscores that the SFMS is not a static sequence, but a dynamic attractor state governing the integrated evolution of [[galaxy-populations|Galaxy populations]]. As a result of this continuous baryonic cycling and star formation, the global mean metal abundance of the Universe has steadily grown over cosmic time, reaching approximately solar metallicity at the present day [Edmunds et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997MNRAS.292..733E/abstract).

## The Kennicutt-Schmidt Law and Gas Regulation

The empirical relationship between the surface density of star formation, $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$, and the surface density of cold gas, $\Sigma_{\rm gas}$, is universally encapsulated by the [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]]. Based on global averages over galactic disks, this fundamental scaling relation is typically parametrized as a power law:

$$ \Sigma_{\rm SFR} \propto \Sigma_{\rm gas}^N $$

For total gas (atomic plus molecular), the index is consistently found to be $N \approx 1.4$ across a wide range of environments [Kennicutt et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ARA&A..36..189K/abstract). Alternatively, the relation can be formulated by incorporating the dynamical timescale of the disk, $\tau_{\rm dyn}$, such that $\Sigma_{\rm SFR} \propto \Sigma_{\rm gas} / \tau_{\rm dyn}$ [Kennicutt et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ARA&A..36..189K/abstract). 

While total gas surface density provides a robust macroscopic scaling, modern multi-wavelength observations reveal that star formation is more directly governed by the dense gas phase. Observations tracing dense molecular gas via HCN emission demonstrate a tight, linear correlation ($N \approx 1.0$) with the global star formation rate across nine orders of magnitude in infrared luminosity, spanning from local dense cloud cores to [[extreme-starbursts|extreme high-redshift starbursts]] [Gao et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..237..331G/abstract). This contrasts with the total molecular gas traced by CO, which exhibits a non-linear relationship. This suggests that the global star formation rate is fundamentally regulated by the specific [[gas-fraction|availability of dense molecular gas]] rather than the total molecular or atomic gas content [Gao et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..237..331G/abstract).

Theoretical efforts to explain the physical origin of the [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]] suggest it emerges naturally from the density probability distribution function (PDF) of a multifractal interstellar medium driven by supersonic turbulence [Kravtsov et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...590L...1K/abstract), [Tassis et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.382.1317T/abstract). In these frameworks, the global star formation rate is determined by the mass fraction of gas populating the high-density tail of a lognormal density PDF. The mass in this tail naturally scales as $\Sigma_{\rm gas}^{1.4}$, rendering the macroscopic law largely independent of local, small-scale stellar feedback prescriptions [Kravtsov et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...590L...1K/abstract). On smaller scales, star formation is inherently clustered. Bound [[star-clusters|stellar clusters]] represent roughly 10% of total star formation in most galaxies, with evidence suggesting that this fraction increases in environments characterized by high star formation rate surface densities [Bastian et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASPC..470..287B/abstract). Indeed, the global star formation rate serves as a primary statistical predictor for both the probability of a galaxy hosting young clusters and the total mass contained within its cluster system [Berek et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.1902B/abstract).

The efficiency of converting this gas into stars is parameterized by the depletion timescale, defined as:

$$ \tau_{\rm dep} = \frac{\Sigma_{\rm gas}}{\Sigma_{\rm SFR}} $$

In local star-forming disks, $\tau_{\rm dep}$ is typically on the order of a few gigayears. Because this timescale is often much shorter than the Hubble time, maintaining the observed star formation histories requires continuous gas replenishment from the [[galaxy-and-igm-evolution|intergalactic medium]] [Fraternali et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.2166F/abstract). This continuous infall is a core tenet of quasi-equilibrium [[bathtub-models|Bathtub models]], wherein galaxies exist in a regulated steady-state balance between cosmic gas accretion, star formation, and feedback-driven outflows [Davé et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract), [Mitra et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1184M/abstract). 

Within this paradigm, a galaxy's [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|specific star formation rate]] closely tracks its cosmological accretion rate [Furlanetto et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.3394F/abstract). The global gas fraction and depletion timescale evolve significantly with redshift and dictate a galaxy's vertical position relative to the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] [Tacconi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ARA&A..58..157T/abstract). Variations in galactic wind strengths and halo mass concentrations further modulate this equilibrium, maintaining the tight dispersion of the main sequence by regulating the available gas reservoir as a function of the galaxy's structural size [Lin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...93L/abstract).

## Drivers of Evolution: Accretion, Mergers, and Feedback

The global decline of the cosmic star formation rate density from its peak at $z \approx 1.9$ down to the present day is primarily governed by the complex interplay between cosmological gas accretion, internal feedback, and environmental processing [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). In the modern paradigm of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], this evolution is frequently described using quasi-equilibrium or [[bathtub-models|Bathtub models]]. In these frameworks, galaxies exist in a self-regulated state where the infall of fresh gas from the cosmic web is balanced by star formation and feedback-driven outflows [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract).

Observations and simulations indicate that the specific star formation rate (sSFR) is fundamentally linked to the cosmological [[galaxy-accretion|dark matter accretion]] rate [Furlanetto et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.3394F/abstract). Because gas accretion rates closely track the star formation rate, continuous gas replenishment has been necessary to maintain the observed scaling relations since $z \approx 1$ [Fraternali et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.2166F/abstract). As the universe expands and the global density of the cosmic web decreases, the rate of cold gas accretion naturally drops, precipitating a universal decline in star formation activity. Recent findings also demonstrate that star formation activity is significantly modulated by proximity to [[primeval-large-scale-structures|cosmic web filaments]], exhibiting enhanced accretion at high redshifts ($z > 2$) and complex 'V-shaped' trends in sSFR mapped to distance from the filament backbone at $z=0$ [Jego et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250918077J/abstract).

However, declining accretion alone cannot fully explain the sharp reduction in star formation efficiencies and the truncation of the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] at high masses. Self-regulating feedback mechanisms are strictly required. Cosmological simulations demonstrate a distinct division in feedback roles: supernova feedback regulates the abundance of low- to moderate-star-forming galaxies across all redshifts, whereas AGN feedback primarily quenches high-SFR systems at lower redshifts [Katsianis et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472..919K/abstract). In low- to intermediate-mass halos, supernova feedback is the dominant regulator. The energy and momentum injected by [[core-collapse-supernova-rate|core-collapse supernovae]] drive massive [[galaxy-structure|galactic winds]] that heat or eject interstellar gas, effectively removing fuel for future star formation. Variations in the strength of these winds may explain the dispersion in star formation rates at a fixed stellar mass, as smaller galaxies may require weaker winds to maintain structural scaling relations [Lin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...93L/abstract).

At the high-mass end ($M_* \gtrsim 10^{11} M_\odot$), the Star-Forming Main Sequence exhibits a characteristic turnover. Here, AGN feedback becomes the critical quenching mechanism. Energy released by supermassive black hole accretion prevents the cooling of halo gas, while high stellar surface densities and morphological stabilization further suppress star formation [Förster Schreiber et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ARA&A..58..661F/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4857C/abstract).

The role of major mergers, once thought to be the primary driver of galaxy growth in hierarchical models, is now considered secondary to smooth accretion and the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] for the bulk of cosmic star formation [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). However, early hierarchical models fundamentally required a bursting mode of star formation triggered by galaxy-galaxy mergers to accurately reproduce the high global star formation rate densities observed at $z \sim 3$ [Somerville et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph.11001S/abstract). Nonetheless, the broader environment plays a pivotal role in the quenching process. In the local universe ($z < 0.075$), approximately 65% of star formation occurs in field spiral galaxies, with the total star formation budget partitioned among field, groups, and clusters in a roughly 21:3:1 ratio [Lofthouse et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.4910L/abstract). As galaxies migrate into denser environments, such as [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|groups and clusters]], they become subject to external processes like [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|ram-pressure stripping]] and "strangulation" (the removal of the extended hot gas halo), which violently halt gas replenishment [Balogh et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...504L..75B/abstract), [Cen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741...99C/abstract). In these distant clusters, the tidal truncation of halo gas reservoirs acts as another distinct environmental mechanism that halts gas replenishment, leading to a global suppression of galactic star formation .

Despite the success of the quasi-equilibrium paradigm, tensions remain in the literature. Simple analytical models capture the mean global trends but often fail to reproduce the steep increase in galaxy growth rates observed between $z=0$ and $z=1$ [Damen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705..617D/abstract). Furthermore, a persistent debate continues regarding whether star formation quenching is primarily a mass-driven phenomenon regulated by internal AGN and supernova feedback, or if external environmental processing is the dominant driver of galaxy quenching [Balogh et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...504L..75B/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4857C/abstract).

## Theoretical Frameworks: Equilibrium and Bathtub Models

Over the past decade, the theoretical paradigm for [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] has shifted away from a framework dominated by stochastic, merger-driven growth toward one governed by smooth, continuous processes. At the heart of this shift is the "equilibrium" or "bathtub" model of galaxy evolution. In this analytic framework, galaxies are treated as mass reservoirs in a quasi-steady state, where the global star formation rate is regulated by a continuous balance between the accretion of gas from the cosmic web, the consumption of gas via star formation, and the ejection of mass through feedback-driven outflows [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). 

In this continuity framework, the cold gas mass in the interstellar medium reflects the balance between the cosmological [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] rate, the galactic outflow rate, and the star formation rate. By applying an equilibrium condition where the gas reservoir remains roughly constant over time, the global star formation rate is fundamentally dictated by the cosmological accretion rate of [[transition-halo-mass-scale|dark matter halos]] [Furlanetto et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.3394F/abstract), [Kudritzki et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910...87K/abstract). In particular, analytical models indicate that to maintain the sustained high star formation rates observed at $z \sim 2-3$, gas accretion rates must be roughly equal to the combined star formation and outflow rates [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract).

The efficiency of this baryon cycling is heavily dependent on the mass of the host dark matter halo. Reconstructed [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] profiles reveal that the instantaneous star formation efficiency—defined as the star formation rate divided by the baryon accretion rate—peaks sharply in halos with a characteristic mass of $\sim 10^{11.7} \, M_\odot$ [Behroozi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...762L..31B/abstract). This scale coincides with theoretical predictions for the mass threshold required for the virial shock heating of accreted gas. Consequently, the mass dependence of accretion and feedback restricts where the bulk of cosmic stellar mass is assembled: approximately two-thirds of all star formation in the Universe has occurred in halos within a factor of three of a characteristic mass of $10^{11.7} \, M_\odot$, a range that includes the mass of the Milky Way [Behroozi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...762L..31B/abstract).

The equilibrium model naturally produces several observed [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]]. When galaxies are perturbed from their equilibrium state due to stochastic variations in the inflow rate, restoring forces drive them back. An influx of fresh gas increases the gas fraction and dilutes the metallicity, subsequently triggering a proportional increase in star formation that exhausts the excess gas. This restorative behavior explains why deviations in the star formation rate at a fixed stellar mass are positively correlated with gas fractions and anti-correlated with gas-phase metallicities [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). Furthermore, to maintain the observed lack of correlation between a galaxy's star formation rate and its physical radius at a fixed stellar mass, bathtub models require that smaller, more [[red-nuggets|compact galaxies]] possess correspondingly weaker galactic winds [Lin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...93L/abstract).

While the bathtub model successfully captures the mean evolution of stars and metals across cosmic time, it remains an idealized approximation and faces certain phenomenological tensions. Simple formulations that assume a time-independent star formation efficiency accurately map dark matter accretion to star formation at $z > 4$, but they can struggle to fully reproduce the steep decline in specific growth rates observed between $z=1$ and the present day [Damen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705..617D/abstract). Conversely, comprehensive analytic frameworks have shown that simple equilibrium models can successfully reproduce the mean cosmic evolution of stars and metals out to $z=2$. These frameworks utilize an evolving quenching mass scale and intermediate outflow scalings, without strictly requiring delayed gas consumption or environmental starvation to fit the data [Mitra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1184M/abstract). Complementing these analytic solutions, flexible empirical frameworks such as the EMERGE model have been developed to assign star formation rates to halos across a wide redshift range by mapping them to dark matter growth rates and baryon conversion efficiencies, successfully matching observed scaling relations from $z \sim 10$ to the present day [Moster et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.1822M/abstract).

## Impact of the Initial Mass Function (IMF) on Global Estimates

The conversion of observed galactic luminosities into a physical mass of newly formed stars relies fundamentally on the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], which describes the mass distribution of stars at birth. Because extragalactic [[star-formation-rate-tracers|Star formation rate tracers]]—such as ultraviolet, H$\alpha$, and infrared emission—are overwhelmingly dominated by the light of massive, short-lived stars, extrapolating these observed luminosities to a total [[stellar-birthrate|Stellar birthrate]] requires rigid assumptions about the relative abundance of unseen low-mass stars.

Historically, galaxy evolution models and observational calibrations have operated on the assumption of a universal and invariant stellar mass distribution, such as the classic [[salpeter-initial-mass-function|Salpeter initial mass function]]. Under the assumption of a universal IMF, the time integral of the cosmic star formation rate density matches the observed local stellar mass density reasonably well [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Despite this, the IMF remains the greatest single source of systematic uncertainty in measuring global star formation rates and stellar masses [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...39H/abstract). 

A significant complication in empirically constraining the IMF is its degeneracy with a galaxy's star formation history. Variations in a galaxy's star formation rate, such as episodic bursts or lulls, can imprint artificial plateaus or declines on the observed present-day mass function. If a constant star formation history is incorrectly assumed, these temporal fluctuations can be misinterpreted as intrinsic variations in the IMF, yielding "false" IMF structures where the slope appears artificially steepened or flattened [Elmegreen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...636..149E/abstract).

Beyond these observational biases, there is an active and unresolved tension in the literature regarding whether the IMF is genuinely invariant. A growing body of evidence supports a [[non-universal-initial-mass-function|Non-universal initial mass function]] that varies systematically with the local environment and the galaxy's overall star formation rate. For instance, observations from the [[gama-survey|GAMA survey]] demonstrate a strong dependence between the high-mass IMF slope and the star formation rate: highly star-forming galaxies produce proportionally more massive stars, resulting in a flatter (or "top-heavy") IMF slope compared to more quiescent galaxies [Gunawardhana et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.1647G/abstract).

Theoretical models of clustered star formation further predict global variations through the integrated galaxial initial mass function (IGIMF). In this framework, all stars form in embedded clusters, and the galaxy-wide IMF is the integral of the local stellar IMF over the embedded cluster mass function. Because the mass of the most massive star cluster is limited by the total star formation rate of the galaxy, the resulting [[non-universal-initial-mass-function|integrated galaxial initial mass function (IGIMF)]] is generally steeper—containing fewer massive stars per unit of stellar mass—than the local stellar IMF [Weidner et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...625..754W/abstract). However, in massive starbursts with high star formation rates, the IGIMF flattens, producing a relatively higher fraction of massive stars. 

The conflict between "universal IMF" models and the top-heavy signatures observed in high-SFR systems remains a central debate in astrophysics [Krumholz et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhR...539...49K/abstract), [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...39H/abstract). If the IMF systematically flattens in highly star-forming environments, applying a standard universal calibration will systematically overestimate the true star formation rates of high-redshift and starbursting galaxies. Consequently, resolving the universality of the IMF is critical for accurately mapping the cosmic star formation history and understanding the true efficiency of galaxy assembly across cosmic time.

## Open Questions and Future Frontiers with JWST

The advent of JWST has pushed the observational frontier of the global star formation rate deep into the first billion years of the Universe ($z > 10$), allowing astrophysicists to directly probe the Epoch of Reionization. A central focus of this new era of observations is resolving tensions between theoretical models of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] and the empirical assembly histories of the first galaxies.

A long-standing puzzle in high-redshift studies is the apparent plateau in the specific star formation rate (sSFR). Prior to JWST, observations indicated that the sSFR flattens to a roughly constant value of $\sim 2 \text{ Gyr}^{-1}$ between $z = 2$ and $z = 7$ [Weinmann et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.417.2737W/abstract). This behavior strongly conflicts with standard quasi-equilibrium ("bathtub") models, which predict that the sSFR should closely track the cosmological specific accretion rate. Because dark matter halo accretion scales rapidly at $z \ge 6$, the observed low sSFR at these epochs implies a missing regulatory mechanism. Theoretical solutions to this plateau require ad hoc modifications to standard physical recipes, such as a delayed consumption of cold gas or a severe suppression of the star formation rate at $z \ge 4$ via enhanced feedback, allowing gas to form stars later in more massive halos [Weinmann et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.417.2737W/abstract).

Recent JWST observations are beginning to clarify these dynamics. Early release data indicate a rapidly rising cosmic star formation rate and stellar mass density at $z \ge 8$, successfully establishing that a well-defined [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] was already in place at $z < 10$ [Di Cesare et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4632D/abstract). Furthermore, JWST CEERS data at $z < 7$ demonstrate that the sSFR does not vary with visual morphology, but instead depends strongly on stellar mass and internal mass density. This confirms that early galaxy formation and the quenching of star formation are fundamentally mass-driven processes rather than morphologically driven ones [Conselice et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4857C/abstract). Complementing these high-redshift insights, recent spatially resolved star formation history reconstructions at $z < 1$ reveal that some galaxies exhibit early signs of quenching in their outskirts before total global suppression occurs, suggesting an outside-in pathway for some systems [Olsen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251102828O/abstract).

At the extreme high-redshift frontier, the discovery of remarkably bright galaxy candidates at $15 \lesssim z \lesssim 30$ initially raised questions regarding potential tensions with the $\Lambda\text{CDM}$ cosmological paradigm. However, recent empirical models mapped to $N$-body simulations demonstrate that these nascent populations can be fully accommodated without invoking exotic physics, provided that [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|High-redshift cosmic star formation efficiencies]] reached peak values of $20\%$ to $65\%$ [Yung et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250418618Y/abstract). If the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] was top-heavy in these low-metallicity environments, or if early star formation was highly stochastic and bursty, the required baryonic conversion efficiencies to match the observed rest-frame ultraviolet luminosities would be even lower [Yung et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250418618Y/abstract). In parallel with JWST discoveries, observations of the global 21-cm signal serve as a complementary probe at these extreme early epochs ($z > 15$). This signal provides critical constraints on parameter estimation for early galaxy formation models, particularly in defining halo mass thresholds for star formation and evaluating the X-ray luminosity to star formation rate ($L_X$-SFR) relation [Mirocha et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...813...11M/abstract).

Finally, open questions remain regarding how these ultra-high-redshift systems chemically enriched the intergalactic medium and regulated their own growth. The mass-metallicity relation during the Epoch of Reionization ($z = 5-10$) serves as a crucial diagnostic for these processes. Cosmological simulations often struggle to reproduce the expected chemical scaling, showing artificial flattening at high stellar masses due to over-suppressed star formation and inaccurate stellar feedback modeling [Noel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221016750N/abstract). Calibrating these feedback prescriptions against incoming JWST [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|metallicity and star formation rate measurements]] remains a primary frontier in understanding the global baryonic cycle before cosmic noon. Looking ahead, this multi-wavelength frontier will be further revolutionized by facilities such as the Large Synoptic Survey Telescope (LSST), which will provide massive statistical samples necessary to identify and decouple the fundamental physical mechanisms governing galaxy evolution across cosmic time [Robertson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NatRP...1..450R/abstract).
