In extragalactic astrophysics, **extreme starbursts** are galaxies or localized galactic regions undergoing exceptionally intense, rapid episodes of stellar assembly. Deviating significantly from the steady-state growth of typical [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] on the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]], extreme starbursts are characterized by dramatically elevated specific star formation rates and total star formation rates (SFRs) that can exceed $1000 \, M_\odot \text{yr}^{-1}$ [Riechers et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.496..329R/abstract). These violent episodes are predominantly triggered by dynamical instabilities—most notably gas-rich [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] and severe radial gas inflows—that funnel vast reservoirs of cold molecular gas into highly concentrated nuclear or disk environments. Because the immense ultraviolet radiation produced by newly formed [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] is almost entirely absorbed by surrounding interstellar dust, these systems typically manifest observationally as [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]], ultra-luminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs), or submillimeter galaxies (SMGs), emitting the bulk of their luminosity at far-infrared wavelengths.

The physical conditions within extreme starbursts represent a fundamentally distinct, highly efficient mode of star formation compared to quiescent galactic disks. Observations of dense gas tracers, such as HCN, initially suggested a universally linear relationship between dense gas mass and star formation rate across all scales, seemingly connecting local dense cores directly to extreme starbursts [Wu et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...635L.173W/abstract), [Gao et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..237..331G/abstract). However, more recent data demonstrate that extreme starbursts frequently operate under modified, bimodal laws, exhibiting substantially higher star formation efficiencies in their dense gas phases. Furthermore, the star formation efficiency per free-fall time in these compact molecular environments can reach 50% to 100% [Fisher et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..169F/abstract). The extreme cosmic ray energy densities and elevated gas temperatures in these regions also strongly influence the resulting stellar populations. Compelling isotopic evidence suggests that the stellar initial mass function (IMF) in [[heavily-dust-obscured-starbursts|Heavily dust-obscured starbursts]] is significantly "top-heavy," preferentially forming [[very-massive-stars|Very massive stars]] rather than following the universal IMF typical of local environments [Zhang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.558..260Z/abstract).

On a cosmological scale, extreme starbursts play a vital role in [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]] and the rapid assembly of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] in the early universe. The highly concentrated populations of massive stars drive powerful, mass-loaded galactic superwinds that transport heavy elements and mechanical energy deep into the circumgalactic medium [Heckman et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999mdrg.conf..227H/abstract), [Heckman et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...822....9H/abstract). This severe energetic feedback, combined with the rapid exhaustion of molecular gas, abruptly quenches star formation. This propels the system through a brief transitional phase as [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] before settling as compact [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] or [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. Despite their recognized importance in galactic evolution, several fundamental questions remain the subject of active research, including the precise role of active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback in permanently quenching these bursts, the systematic dependencies of the top-heavy IMF, and whether the unexpectedly high abundance of optically invisible, dust-obscured starbursts at $z > 3$ requires novel physical paradigms to reconcile with standard hierarchical assembly models.

## Definitions and Phenomenological Classification

Extreme starbursts represent a mode of galactic evolution fundamentally distinct from the steady-state growth of typical [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]]. While normal star-forming galaxies form stars on timescales comparable to the age of the Universe, extreme starbursts are characterized by dramatically elevated star formation rates (SFR) that, if sustained, would exhaust their available cold gas reservoirs in a mere fraction of that time. 

Quantitatively, a starburst is classically defined by its specific star formation rate ($\mathrm{sSFR} = \dot{M}_\star / M_\star$). Normal disk galaxies typically exhibit $\mathrm{sSFR} \sim 0.1 \mathrm{\, Gyr}^{-1}$. Extreme starbursts, however, display sSFRs 10 to 100 times higher. This evolutionary state is frequently parameterized by the [[stellar-birthrate|Stellar birthrate]] ($b$), defined as the ratio of the instantaneous star formation rate to the time-averaged [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]]:
$$b = \frac{\mathrm{SFR}}{\langle \mathrm{SFR} \rangle}$$
While normal star-forming disk galaxies have $b \sim 1$, extreme starbursts possess $b \gg 1$. Consequently, the gas consumption time, or depletion timescale ($\tau_{\mathrm{SF}} = M_{\mathrm{gas}} / \dot{M}_\star$), plunges from typical values of $\sim 2 \times 10^9$ years in normal spirals down to $10^7 - 10^8$ years.

Phenomenologically, extreme starbursts are not merely scaling up the global SFR; the activity is highly concentrated, leading to extraordinary star formation intensity ($\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}$). While normal galactic disks exhibit $\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}$ values of a fraction of a solar mass per year per square kiloparsec, extreme starbursts feature heavily centralized, compact regions of star formation. High-resolution submillimeter observations have revealed dense clumps within dust-obscured galaxies at high redshift exhibiting $\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}} \sim 1200 - 3000 \, M_\odot \, \mathrm{yr}^{-1} \, \mathrm{kpc}^{-2}$, and occasionally approaching $10,000 \, M_\odot \, \mathrm{yr}^{-1} \, \mathrm{kpc}^{-2}$ when corrected for gravitational lensing [Oteo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837..182O/abstract). 

At such extreme surface densities, galaxies approach the theoretical regime of "maximal starbursts." In this limit, the radiation pressure from young, massive stars balances the self-gravity of the gas disk, effectively establishing an Eddington-limited surface density for star formation. Classical studies note that starbursts appear to obey a limiting infrared surface brightness of approximately $10^{11} L_\odot \text{ kpc}^{-2}$, which corresponds to a maximum star formation rate of about $20 M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1} \text{ kpc}^{-2}$ for a standard IMF [Lehnert et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...472..546L/abstract). Interestingly, multi-wavelength studies demonstrate that the starburst intensity limit—or maximum surface brightness—remains remarkably constant from the local universe all the way up to $z \sim 6$ [Hathi et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...673..686H/abstract). While some high-redshift systems exhibit extreme total SFRs exceeding $2000 \, M_\odot \, \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ [Riechers et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.496..329R/abstract), the literature remains divided on whether these represent true Eddington-limited systems globally, or if their vigorous star formation is distributed widely enough to remain locally sub-Eddington. To investigate these dense environments in detail, astronomers look to local major mergers—such as the Antennae Galaxies—where individual giant molecular clouds exhibit some of the highest luminosities, surface densities, and turbulent pressures observed [Krahm et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..166K/abstract). Similar extreme conditions and physical limits are investigated at high redshift in strongly gravitationally lensed, dusty star-forming galaxies [Kamieneski et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961....2K/abstract).

In the context of the broader galaxy population, extreme starbursts are phenomenologically classified as outliers situated significantly above the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]]. Typical star-forming galaxies reside on a fundamental "star-forming plane", whereas dynamical events such as mergers or gas instabilities trigger dramatic shifts in gas conversion efficiency, driving galaxies out of this plane and into the extreme starburst regime [Feldmann et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020CmPhy...3..226F/abstract). Multi-wavelength studies indicate that rather than simply possessing higher initial gas fractions, these extreme systems exhibit a fundamentally higher star formation efficiency (SFE) compared to main-sequence galaxies [Silverman et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812L..23S/abstract). In extreme cases, the efficiency of converting gas into stars per free-fall time can reach 50-100% within compact starburst disks [Fisher et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..169F/abstract). Because the immense ultraviolet flux produced by these extreme events is heavily reprocessed by associated dust, these objects are almost exclusively classified phenomenologically as [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] or ultra-luminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs). Beyond traditional infrared classifications, researchers also employ X-ray selection to identify a wide range of star-forming systems—from quiescent galaxies to extreme (U)LIRGs—at intermediate redshifts [Georgakakis et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.377..203G/abstract). Observations additionally identify extremely strong emission line galaxies (EELGs) as a specific population of low-mass outliers that can double their stellar mass in less than 100 Myr during intense bursts [Atek et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...789...96A/abstract). Furthermore, the onset of a burst can sometimes be signaled by extreme luminosity transients, such as the source observed in [[galaxy-sdss-j130408-52|Galaxy SDSS J130408.52]], which are indicative of massive supernova events triggered early in the starburst phase [Kiyota et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ATel.6853....1K/abstract).

## Historical Development: From IRAS to JWST

The study of extreme starbursts underwent a paradigm shift with the launch of the Infrared Astronomical Satellite (IRAS) in the 1980s. IRAS revealed that many of the most intensely star-forming galaxies in the local Universe are heavily obscured by dust, re-emitting their ultraviolet radiation in the far-infrared. These objects were classified as [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] ($L_{\rm IR} > 10^{11} L_\odot$) and Ultra-luminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs) ($L_{\rm IR} > 10^{12} L_\odot$). Initially, ULIRGs were viewed as rare local curiosities, almost exclusively triggered by gas-rich [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]]. Following IRAS, the Spitzer Space Telescope played a critical role in measuring the power sources of these extreme systems, effectively disentangling active galactic nucleus (AGN) contributions from pure starburst activity and identifying early signs of energetic feedback in (U)LIRGs [Armus et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..467A/abstract).

The landscape changed dramatically in the late 1990s with the commissioning of the Submillimeter Common-User Bolometer Array (SCUBA) on the James Clerk Maxwell Telescope. Deep surveys at $450\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and $850\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ uncovered a large population of Submillimeter galaxies (SMGs) at median redshifts of $z \sim 2.5$. With inferred star formation rates often exceeding several hundred to $10^3\, M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ [Baugh et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1191B/abstract), it became evident that extreme starbursts were a dominant mode of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. Historically, constraints on the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]] relied on rest-frame UV selections, such as Lyman-break galaxies (LBGs). However, the discovery of SMGs demonstrated that up to 40% of early cosmic star formation is hidden by dust, forming a substantial cosmic infrared background [Heckman et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999mdrg.conf..227H/abstract), [Meurer et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.11201M/abstract), [Talia et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract). Furthermore, deep surveys differentiate between these 'classical', ultra-luminous SMGs and a population of fainter submillimeter galaxies that generally follow the star-forming main sequence, yet still show a high fraction of starburst activity at $z \sim 2-3$ [Cardona-Torres et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.5446C/abstract).

In the 2010s, the Herschel Space Observatory resolved the bulk of the cosmic infrared background into individual sources [Lutz et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..373L/abstract). Following this, the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) provided the spatial resolution necessary to map these distant, dust-enshrouded systems. ALMA observations confirmed that high-redshift SMGs are the likely progenitors of compact [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] and massive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] [Toft et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782...68T/abstract). ALMA also discovered "maximum starbursts" during the first billion years of the Universe, converting gas into stars at unprecedented rates [Riechers et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.496..329R/abstract). Furthermore, high-resolution ($\sim 20\,\mathrm{mas}$) submillimeter imaging revealed that star formation in these systems is often remarkably concentrated, with star formation rate surface densities reaching $\Sigma_{\rm SFR} \sim 1200\text{--}3000\, M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\,\mathrm{kpc}^{-2}$ [Oteo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837..182O/abstract). ALMA also identified a vast population of optically invisible [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] at $z > 3$, boasting space densities two orders of magnitude higher than previously known extreme starbursts [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.572..211W/abstract).

The launch of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) extended the study of intense, dust-obscured star formation into the epoch of reionization. Spectroscopic observations with JWST have confirmed the existence of exceptionally luminous galaxies at $z > 11$ [Arrabal Haro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Natur.622..707A/abstract). The sheer abundance of these massive, early starbursts has sparked active debate in the literature. It remains unresolved whether these early systems can be accommodated by standard hierarchical assembly models, or if they necessitate novel physical paradigms—such as feedback-free starbursts—where traditional stellar feedback fails to suppress star formation at cosmic dawn [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A.108L/abstract).

## Dynamical Triggering and Physical Drivers

The extraordinary star formation rates characteristic of extreme starbursts require the rapid accumulation and compression of vast reservoirs of cold molecular gas. In both the local Universe and at high redshift, this concentration is predominantly driven by [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] and strong tidal interactions between gas-rich galaxies. These gravitational encounters efficiently strip angular momentum from the interstellar medium, driving massive gas inflows toward the central regions [Combes et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASSL..329..167C/abstract). As a result, star formation rates (SFRs) are typically enhanced by factors of 10 to 1000 compared to quiescent [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] [Schweizer et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASSL..329..143S/abstract). However, demographic surveys caution that for most local interacting galaxies, the SFR enhancement is actually moderate (a factor of $\sim 2$) rather than extreme, with truly extreme bursts occurring in only a minority of cases [Knapen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.1742K/abstract). Internally, large-scale stellar bars can also play a pivotal role, driving mass-transfer that funnels gas inward to trigger and fuel concentrated nuclear starbursts [Shlosman et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994mtia.conf.....S/abstract). 

Rather than being ignited merely by enhanced cloud-cloud collision rates, the primary trigger for the burst is the rapid escalation of interstellar medium pressure within extended shock regions [Schweizer et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASSL..329..143S/abstract). While the classical paradigm often assumes these events are strictly confined to compact nuclear regions (typically $\sim 1$ kpc in radius), observational evidence indicates that merger-induced starbursts can be spatially extended over scales of 10 to 20 kpc [Schweizer et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASSL..329..143S/abstract). In merging systems, there is an important physical distinction between compact nuclear starbursts fueled by these radial inflows and more spatially extended starbursts, which are instead driven by triggered gas turbulence and large-scale fragmentation in massive clusters [Bournaud et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....51..107B/abstract). Based on the concentration of H$\alpha$ emission, morphological classifications divide [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] starbursts into four structural types, ranging from compact 100 pc nuclear bursts to extended 20 kpc starbursts [Hattori et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127..736H/abstract). At high redshift, gas-rich major mergers are capable of converting these reservoirs into stars at extreme rates. Theoretical models demonstrate that these interaction-triggered starbursts are essential for explaining the early hierarchical formation of galaxies, revealing why many massive galaxies already possessed significant stellar mass at redshifts of $z \sim 1.5 - 2$ . For example, observations of merging submillimeter galaxies at $z \sim 2.3$ reveal SFRs exceeding $2000\ M_\odot\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, a process capable of rapidly assembling early-type ellipticals with stellar masses up to $4 \times 10^{11}\ M_\odot$ by $z \sim 1.5$ [Fu et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.498..338F/abstract).

Extreme starbursts can also be ignited without major mergers through violent disk instabilities. In compact, massive, and highly gas-rich disks, high gas velocity dispersions and surface densities can drive the Toomre Q instability below the critical threshold for stability. Observations of compact starburst disks reveal Toomre parameters of $Q \approx 0.5 - 0.7$, which trigger rapid radial gas inflows where the inflow timescale is shorter than the gas depletion time [Fisher et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..169F/abstract). Under these dynamically unstable conditions, the local star formation efficiency per free-fall time can surge to $50\% - 100\%$, operating in a highly efficient regime that pushes the galaxy well above the standard main sequence of star formation [Fisher et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..169F/abstract).

Beyond major mergers and violent disk instabilities, localized starbursts can also be ignited by the direct accretion of primordial material. For instance, localized starbursts in [[low-metallicity-galaxies|Low-metallicity galaxies]] can be triggered by the recent impact of low-metallicity cosmic gas clouds from the cosmic web [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...810L..15S/abstract). Extreme environmental interactions act as distinct physical drivers in dense regions as well. In massive galaxy clusters at $z > 0.3$, extreme ram-pressure stripping by the hot intracluster medium can severely compress the gas of infalling galaxies. This violent shock compression triggers vigorous, localized starbursts along the galaxy-gas interface and within the stripped debris tails. Such interactions temporarily create highly luminous "jellyfish" galaxies, highlighting that external hydrodynamic forces are also capable of driving the extreme starburst phenomenon [Ebeling et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...781L..40E/abstract). These highly efficient, extreme starbursts stand in stark contrast to ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs), which represent an opposite extreme: environments where star formation occurs at very low efficiencies relative to the atomic gas content [Kado-Fong et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...11K/abstract).

## The Interstellar Medium in Extreme Environments

The interstellar medium (ISM) in extreme starburst environments is characterized by exceptionally high gas densities and profound dust obscuration. In these intense star-forming regions, the copious ultraviolet radiation produced by young, [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] is almost entirely absorbed by interstellar dust grains and re-radiated at far-infrared wavelengths. Consequently, these systems often manifest as [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] or ultra-luminous infrared galaxies with infrared luminosities exceeding $10^{11}$ to $10^{12} L_\odot$. At high redshifts ($z > 3$), heavily dust-obscured starbursts represent a dominant population of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] that are largely invisible in rest-frame ultraviolet surveys, yet they can contribute up to 40% of the cosmic star formation rate density [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.572..211W/abstract), [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract). Additionally, the exceptionally high molecular gas densities and star formation rates in heavily obscured systems like Arp 220 predict significant high-energy gamma-ray emission due to neutral pion decay and bremsstrahlung processes [Torres et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...617..966T/abstract).

To better understand these conditions without the ambiguity of cosmological distances, astronomers use the Milky Way's Galactic Centre as a unique local laboratory. Its extreme gas densities and strong magnetic fields provide a readily accessible analog for studying star formation in environments that closely resemble high-redshift starbursts [Schoedel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240604022S/abstract). The relationship between the total gas surface density ($\Sigma_{\mathrm{gas}}$) and the star formation rate surface density ($\Sigma_{\dot{\star}}$) is empirically parameterized by the Kennicutt-Schmidt law, 
$$\Sigma_{\dot{\star}} \propto \Sigma_{\mathrm{gas}}^N$$
While typical spiral galaxies exhibit gas consumption times on the order of several gigayears, extreme starbursts feature a dramatically reduced molecular gas depletion time, often falling below $10^8$ years. In compact starburst disks, the star formation efficiency per freefall time can reach 50% to 100%, representing an enhancement of two orders of magnitude compared to normal star-forming disks [Fisher et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..169F/abstract). 

To probe the densest phases of the molecular ISM ($n_{\mathrm{H}_2} \gtrsim 10^4 \text{ cm}^{-3}$) where star formation actively occurs, astronomers rely on dense gas tracers (HCN) rather than standard CO emission. Observations reveal a tight correlation between the HCN luminosity ($L_{\mathrm{HCN}}$) and infrared luminosity ($L_{\mathrm{IR}}$) that spans over eight orders of magnitude, connecting individual Galactic dense cores to high-redshift hyper-luminous starbursts [Wu et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...635L.173W/abstract), [Gao et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..237..331G/abstract). While early studies indicated a linear law across all scales for dense gas, more recent high-quality HCN observations reveal that dense gas also exhibits a bimodal behavior. The star formation efficiency of the dense molecular gas in extreme starbursts is intrinsically 3 to 4 times higher than that of galaxies on the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] [García-Burillo et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...539A...8G/abstract). This implies that extreme starbursts are not simply scaled-up normal galaxies with proportionally higher gas fractions, but rather operate via a distinct, highly efficient star-forming mode.

The extreme conditions of the starburst ISM—such as heightened cosmic ray energy densities and extreme pressures—may also fundamentally alter the [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]], boosting the Jeans mass of the gas [Papadopoulos et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.1705P/abstract). Because severe dust obscuration precludes classical optical and ultraviolet measurements of the IMF in these environments, observers utilize the rotational transitions of CO isotopologues to trace chemical abundances. Specifically, anomalously low $^{13}\mathrm{CO}/\mathrm{C}^{18}\mathrm{O}$ isotope ratios provide unambiguous evidence for a "top-heavy" IMF that systematically favors the production of massive stars in extreme environments [Zhang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.558..260Z/abstract).

## Extreme Starbursts at Cosmic Dawn

The advent of ALMA and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has fundamentally reshaped our understanding of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]], revealing that the rapid build-up of stellar mass began much earlier than previously thought. Spectroscopic observations indicate that the onset of star formation occurred as early as 250 Myr after the Big Bang, corresponding to $z \approx 15$ [Hashimoto et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.557..392H/abstract). Even at redshift $z > 6$, the universe already hosted extremely luminous star-forming systems. JWST has confirmed an unexpectedly high abundance of UV-bright galaxies at $z > 11$ [Arrabal Haro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Natur.622..707A/abstract), while ALMA has unveiled a massive population of optically dark, dust-obscured galaxies that were entirely missed by earlier rest-frame ultraviolet surveys [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.572..211W/abstract). These heavily obscured systems are significant contributors to the overall [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]], accounting for up to 40% of the cosmic star formation rate density in the early universe [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract). Recent early results, such as the Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1), have further identified candidates for incredibly massive galaxies (up to $10^{11} M_\odot$) existing at the end of the Epoch of Reionization ($z=5-7$); while a fraction ($\sim 12\%$) reside in the extreme starburst region of the SFR-stellar mass plane, the vast majority lie on the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251111943E/abstract).

At cosmic dawn, extreme starbursts operate on scales that dwarf their local counterparts. A prime example is the "maximum starburst" discovered at $z = 6.34$, which converts a $\sim 10^{11}\ M_\odot$ interstellar medium into [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] at a rate exceeding $2000\ M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ [Riechers et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.496..329R/abstract). Such galaxies are often situated in the highest-density peaks of the early dark matter distribution, acting as the central engines of [[nascent-galaxy-clusters|Nascent galaxy clusters]]. A vivid illustration of this occurs around [[high-redshift-radio-galaxies|High-redshift radio galaxies]], which are often found accompanied by multiple submillimeter-luminous companion galaxies. This clustering indicates synchronous, starburst-driven elliptical galaxy formation occurring actively within dense proto-cluster cores [Stevens et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Natur.425..264S/abstract). Within these dense environments, continuous gas accretion and [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] drive star formation to near the Eddington limit, rapidly assembling the progenitors of today's massive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] [Cen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...775..112C/abstract). This violent mode of protocluster core assembly continues through to "Cosmic Noon" at $z \sim 2-3$ [Foo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250405617F/abstract).

The unexpected abundance of these highly luminous galaxies at $z \gtrsim 10$ has created tension with standard hierarchical models of galaxy formation, sparking debate over whether the underlying physics of star formation differs in the early universe. One proposed solution is the occurrence of feedback-free starbursts [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A.108L/abstract). In this regime, the extreme densities of early galactic disks allow star formation to proceed with an efficiency per free-fall time approaching $\epsilon_{\max} = 0.2 - 1.0$. Because these compact starbursts—with effective radii $R_e \sim 0.3\ \mathrm{kpc}$—evolve on timescales of $\sim 10\ \mathrm{Myr}$, they convert gas into stars efficiently before supernova feedback has time to disrupt the molecular clouds and expel the gas [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A.108L/abstract). However, the literature remains actively divided on whether such modified efficiencies are strictly necessary, or if variations in the stellar initial mass function toward a more top-heavy distribution can fully explain these extraordinary luminosities [Arrabal Haro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Natur.622..707A/abstract).

Despite their heavy dust attenuation, the sheer intensity of these early bursts plays a complex role in cosmic reionization. While the most evolved, dust-enshrouded maximum starbursts likely trap the majority of their Lyman-continuum photons [Riechers et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.496..329R/abstract), their less massive, metal-poor precursors drive exceptionally fast, highly ionized outflows into the circumgalactic medium. In addition to these massive progenitors, [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|Star-forming dwarf galaxies]] are of critical importance. During their extreme starburst phases, these low-mass systems generate intense, hard ionizing radiation fields, potentially playing a dominant role in driving cosmic reionization [Bergvall et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASSP...28..175B/abstract). These violent winds (reaching velocities over $1000\ \mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}$) expel gas and inject metals into the intergalactic medium, clearing pathways for ionizing radiation to escape and significantly contributing to the reionization of the universe [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A.108L/abstract).

## Stellar Populations and the Initial Mass Function

The extreme physical conditions within starburst galaxies profoundly influence their resulting stellar demographic, driving star formation modes that differ significantly from those in quiescent galactic disks. In these environments, star formation is highly clustered, heavily biased toward massive stars, and provides unique testing grounds for the universality of the stellar initial mass function (IMF).

In extreme starbursts, the dominant mode of star formation is the creation of [[super-star-clusters|Super star clusters]] (SSCs) [de Grijs et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASSL..329..157D/abstract). These are extraordinarily luminous, compact stellar systems with masses often exceeding $10^4 M_\odot$. Because their physical properties—such as mass, size, and density—mirror the expected initial conditions of ancient [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]], SSCs are widely considered to be their present-day progenitors, forming in the high-pressure environments typical of galaxy interactions and [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] [de Grijs et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph..8232D/abstract). A prominent local example is the interacting galaxy Arp 299 (NGC 3690). A classic study of this system established it as a premier example of interaction-induced, widespread star formation occurring simultaneously across multiple distinct galactic subregions [Gehrz et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...267..551G/abstract), [Alonso-Herrero et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...532..845A/abstract). In such cases, these galaxies host massive supergiant H II regions that are physically similar to 30 Doradus, but operating on an unprecedented, galactic scale.

The [[young-stellar-populations|Young Stellar Populations]] within these clusters are exceptionally rich in [[massive-stars|Massive stars]]. [[very-massive-stars|Very massive stars]] ($>100 M_\odot$) dominate the ultraviolet continuum of starbursts and are the primary drivers of rapid chemical enrichment [Martins et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250703371M/abstract). Observationally, this is manifested in the high prevalence of [[wolf-rayet-star-populations|Wolf-Rayet star populations]]. For instance, the local starburst cluster NGC 3125-1 exhibits one of the most extreme Wolf-Rayet signatures known, characterized by a highly broadened He II $\lambda 1640$ emission feature ($\sim 1000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$) alongside strong N IV and N V emission lines [Chandar et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...604..153C/abstract), [Conti et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000PASP..112.1413C/abstract).

The sheer abundance of these massive stars in starbursts has sparked a major debate regarding the universality of the IMF. While the IMF is broadly consistent across local, quiescent environments, extreme starbursts exhibit compelling evidence for a "top-heavy" IMF, which preferentially forms higher-mass stars. Locally, extreme star-forming regions within the Milky Way, such as W49A, exhibit top-heavy young stellar object luminosity distributions, mirroring conditions found in extragalactic starbursts [Eden et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.3369E/abstract). Historically, validating extragalactic IMF variations was complicated by severe dust obscuration and the degenerate effects of starburst duration or extinction laws [Sextl et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...949...60S/abstract). However, recent observations of isotope abundance ratios in high-redshift ($z \sim 2-3$) dust-enshrouded starbursts have provided unambiguous, dust-independent evidence for a top-heavy IMF. Specifically, the low $^{13}\mathrm{C}/^{18}\mathrm{O}$ ratios measured via rotational transitions of $^{13}\mathrm{CO}$ and $\mathrm{C}^{18}\mathrm{O}$ require a significantly enhanced population of massive stars compared to ordinary star-forming spiral galaxies [Zhang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.558..260Z/abstract).

Theoretical frameworks suggest that this top-heavy IMF is a natural consequence of the extreme interstellar medium conditions in starbursts. Intense star formation generates extreme cosmic ray dominated regions (CRDRs), where the cosmic ray energy density, $U_{\mathrm{CR}}$, can reach values $10^3$ to $10^4$ times higher than the Galactic average. In these UV-shielded dense gas regions, cosmic ray heating decouples the gas kinetic temperature from the dust temperature ($T_{\mathrm{kin}} \gg T_{\mathrm{dust}}$), raising the minimum gas temperature to $\sim 50-100\text{ K}$ [Papadopoulos et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.1705P/abstract). This elevated thermal floor boosts the Jeans mass of the molecular clouds by factors of $10$ to $100$. Consequently, the characteristic mass of forming stars is fundamentally shifted upward, dictating that extreme starbursts operate under a distinctly bimodal, top-heavy [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] regime [Papadopoulos et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.1705P/abstract).

## Feedback, Galactic Superwinds, and the CGM

The intense concentration of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] in extreme starbursts results in a remarkably high rate of core-collapse supernovae and powerful stellar winds. The mechanical energy and momentum injected into the interstellar medium (ISM) by these processes drive large-scale, multiphase galactic outflows, commonly referred to as **galactic superwinds**. These winds are highly pressurized and strongly mass-loaded flows containing hot, X-ray emitting plasma that entrains cooler atomic and molecular gas [Heckman et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999AIPC..470..322H/abstract). The launch of these immense outflows is heralded by the abrupt deaths of massive stars. For example, extremely luminous X-ray outbursts resulting from supernova shock 'break-outs' directly signal the onset of these powerful stellar explosions, which collectively inject the mechanical energy required to drive the feedback [Soderberg et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.453..469S/abstract).

Observations of UV interstellar absorption lines reveal that superwinds routinely eject gas at velocities of hundreds to thousands of kilometers per second. The mechanics of these outflows correlate with host galaxy properties; the outflow velocity scales tightly with the galaxy's stellar mass, circular velocity, global star formation rate (SFR), and SFR surface density ($\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$) [Heckman et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...822....9H/abstract). While the exact scaling of outflow velocity for extreme velocity outliers has historically been a point of debate, recent analyses demonstrate that extreme starbursts lie along the direct extrapolation of the trends defined by normal [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] [Heckman et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...822....9H/abstract). In the most violent cases, outflow velocities can exceed the host halo's virial velocity by a factor of two ($v_{\rm out} \gtrsim 2 v_{\rm vir}$), allowing the enriched material to escape the galaxy's gravitational potential entirely [Heckman et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846..151H/abstract).

Superwinds act as the primary mechanism for transporting heavy elements and mechanical energy into the **circumgalactic medium (CGM)** and the broader intergalactic medium. Spectroscopic observations utilizing background quasar sightlines have directly probed the CGM of low-redshift starbursts, revealing high detection rates of highly ionized species—such as C IV, Si III, and O VI—at impact parameters extending out to $200\text{ kpc}$ [Borthakur et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...768...18B/abstract), [Heckman et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846..151H/abstract). The equivalent widths and velocity offsets of these absorption features are significantly larger than those found in the halos of typical [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], providing direct evidence that starburst-driven winds dynamically heat and chemically pollute the bulk of the extended gaseous halo [Borthakur et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...768...18B/abstract), [Heckman et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846..151H/abstract). While these winds are capable of sweeping up significant material, Herschel/SPIRE observations of the local starburst M 82 reveal that although superwinds entrain dust, a majority of the extraplanar cool dust is likely removed via tidal interactions rather than the wind itself [Roussel et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518L..66R/abstract).

By ejecting vast quantities of gas at rates often comparable to or exceeding the global SFR ($\dot{M}_{\rm out} \gtrsim {\rm SFR}$), superwinds rapidly deplete the cold gas reservoirs necessary to sustain star formation [Heckman et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999AIPC..470..322H/abstract). This severe energetic feedback is a critical mechanism for **star formation quenching**. The rapid shutdown of star formation transforms these systems into [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] (often exhibiting characteristic [[e-a-galaxies|E+A galaxies]] optical spectra), facilitating their evolutionary migration from the active blue cloud to the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] of [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] [Pattarakijwanich et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833...19P/abstract), [French et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASP..133g2001F/abstract). However, the literature remains divided on the exact interplay of quenching mechanisms; while starburst-driven gas exhaustion acts rapidly, the extent to which subsequent AGN feedback is required to permanently suppress star formation and maintain the quenched state remains an active area of research.

## Evolutionary Transitions: The Post-starburst Phase

Extreme starbursts represent a pivotal, albeit transient, stage in cosmic assembly, serving as the evolutionary bridge between gas-rich progenitors and massive, dead elliptical galaxies. At high redshift, dust-obscured extreme starbursts—often identified as submillimeter galaxies at $z=3-6$—exhibit stellar masses, sizes, and internal kinematics consistent with being the direct precursors of compact [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] observed at $z \sim 2$ [Toft et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782...68T/abstract). Because of their profoundly elevated star formation rates, these extreme systems deplete their gas reservoirs on very short timescales. For example, a [[major-mergers|major merger]]-induced starburst at $z=2.3$ forming stars at $\sim 2000 M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$ is projected to exhaust its molecular gas within roughly $200 \text{ Myr}$, rapidly assembling a passive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|elliptical galaxy]] of $4 \times 10^{11} M_\odot$ by $z \sim 1.5$ [Fu et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.498..338F/abstract). However, while the star formation rates of high-redshift ULIRGs are extraordinarily massive, their overall evolutionary impact is nuanced; studies suggest they may ultimately account for only a few percent of the total stellar mass locked in present-epoch spheroidal populations [Trentham et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.305...61T/abstract). To better understand the structural legacy of these violent events, recent weak-lensing studies of post-merger galaxies investigate how dark matter halos and their associated stellar content evolve and stabilize following extreme, major merger-induced bursts [Cheng et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250200584C/abstract).

As an extreme starburst ceases, the galaxy enters a brief transitional period known as the post-starburst phase. These systems, historically referred to as [[e-a-galaxies|E+A galaxies]] or K+A galaxies, are identified spectroscopically by strong Balmer absorption lines (e.g., H$\delta$) superimposed on an older stellar continuum, coupled with a lack of nebular emission lines (such as H$\alpha$) that would indicate ongoing star formation. This distinct spectral signature signifies that a major burst of star formation occurred but was abruptly quenched within the last $1-2 \text{ Gyr}$. Spectroscopic sequences suggest an evolutionary pathway from the active "blue cloud" through a composite or [[seyfert-galaxies|Seyfert galaxies]] phase, and finally onto the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] [Leslie et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455L..82L/abstract). Detailed spectroscopic studies track this evolutionary progress through specific transition markers. As galaxies evolve from active starburst to quiescence over their final billion years of star formation, an increase in Mg II absorption lines uniquely indicates the emergence of intermediate-mass F- and G-type stars dominating the optical spectrum [Wu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..192W/abstract). At lower masses, "transition" dwarf galaxies represent a similarly crucial phase, hosting significant starbursts embedded within smooth, older elliptical envelopes that serve as a clear evolutionary bridge toward quiescent dwarf ellipticals [Dellenbusch et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....135..326D/abstract).

The precise physical mechanisms driving this rapid shutdown—often termed "quenching"—remain a subject of active research. While rapid gas exhaustion plays a significant role, observational evidence increasingly implicates active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback in expelling residual gas and preventing further cooling to finalize the transition to quiescence [Leslie et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455L..82L/abstract). Interestingly, environmental studies of local and intermediate-redshift [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] reveal that they occupy ambient density distributions remarkably similar to those of normal [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]]. This lack of an environmental preference implies that the quenching of these extreme starbursts is primarily driven by internal impulses (such as major mergers triggering exhaustion or AGN feedback) rather than external environmental effects like ram-pressure or tidal stripping in dense clusters [Hogg et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...650..763H/abstract).

Despite their theoretical importance as an evolutionary link, highly luminous [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] are exceedingly rare in the modern universe. Demographic surveys indicate that only $\sim 1\%$ of all star formation quenching in the redshift range $z = 0.2-0.7$ results in a luminous post-starburst system [Pattarakijwanich et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833...19P/abstract). This finding indicates that while the post-starburst phase effectively traces the dramatic evolution of the most extreme starbursts, the vast majority of normal galaxies migrating to the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] do so via slower, less violent evolutionary pathways.

## Open Questions and Future Research Directions

Despite recent observational and theoretical advances, several fundamental questions regarding the physical properties and cosmic impact of extreme starbursts remain unresolved. Current research is primarily focused on characterizing their evolutionary lifecycles, the nature of their [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]], and their complex interplay with central supermassive black holes. Another area of investigation involves evidence for repetitive periods of nuclear star formation in bulgeless, late-type spiral galaxies, fueled by central reservoirs of molecular gas [Böker et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...39B/abstract).

A major unresolved issue is the validity of **IMF universality** in extreme environments. Standard galaxy evolution models often assume a universal stellar initial mass function (IMF) across all environments. However, recent isotopic and chemical abundance studies strongly challenge this assumption, offering evidence for a top-heavy IMF in extreme star-forming environments [Zhang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.558..260Z/abstract). Theoretical models suggest that extreme cosmic ray energy densities in these compact regions decouple the gas and dust temperatures, maintaining $T_{\rm kin} \gg T_{\rm dust}$ and boosting the Jeans mass by factors of 10 to 100 [Papadopoulos et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.1705P/abstract). Whether this top-heavy IMF is a ubiquitous feature of all extreme starbursts or depends systematically on local star formation density remains an active area of debate.

The characteristic duration and **burst duty cycle** of extreme star formation events are also highly debated, particularly for [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] and [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|Star-forming dwarf galaxies]]. Older models characterized dwarf starbursts as brief, "flickering" events lasting only $3-10 \text{ Myr}$. However, resolved stellar population data indicate that these bursts can be sustained for much longer periods, typically $450-650 \text{ Myr}$, and sometimes exceeding $1 \text{ Gyr}$ [McQuinn et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...695..561M/abstract), [McQuinn et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724...49M/abstract). Adding to the complexity, reconstructed star formation histories of nearby dwarf starbursts reveal elevated rates that are often sustained for hundreds of millions of years, effectively ruling out rapid self-quenching on global scales for these low-mass systems [McQuinn et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...721..297M/abstract). Statistically, local dwarf galaxies appear to spend approximately $10\%$ of their lifetimes in a starburst state, during which they assemble about half of their total stellar mass [Lee et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhDT........12L/abstract). However, across the overall dwarf population in the local universe, these intense bursts are responsible for only about a quarter of the total star formation, indicating that the majority of stars in low-mass systems today do not form in this extreme mode [Lee et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...692.1305L/abstract). Furthermore, these episodic starbursts in dwarf galaxies leave distinct imprints on their long-term chemical evolution, specifically creating observable metallicity gaps in the alpha-element abundance plane that models must now strive to reproduce [Ting et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025OJAp....8E...7T/abstract). In contrast, the most massive submillimeter galaxies at high redshift exhibit shorter mean duty cycles of $\sim 42 \text{ Myr}$ [Toft et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782...68T/abstract). Resolving these timescales is critical for accurately mapping the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]].

Another critical frontier is the **AGN-starburst connection** and its role in galaxy quenching. [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] (characterized by strong Balmer absorption lines) represent a brief transitional phase between active star-forming systems and [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] [Pattarakijwanich et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833...19P/abstract), [French et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASP..133g2001F/abstract). The exact mechanism that abruptly halts extreme starbursts is unclear, with competing theories pointing toward rapid gas exhaustion due to exceptionally high star formation efficiencies [Fu et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.498..338F/abstract) versus violent active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback [Leslie et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455L..82L/abstract). Theoretical simulations of extreme [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] predict that mid-infrared color selections should reveal a high fraction of obscured dual AGNs accompanying nuclear starbursts, complicating observational efforts to isolate the primary quenching driver [Blecha et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.3056B/abstract). Another emerging area of study is the link between episodic accretion events and extreme environments in galactic nuclei. Recent discoveries highlight an intriguing connection between quasi-periodic eruptions (QPEs) and tidal disruption events in low-mass galaxy nuclei that simultaneously exhibit extreme coronal line emission, pointing toward complex interactions between massive black holes and localized, intense starburst or accretion environments [Chakraborty et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250319013C/abstract).

Finally, the discovery of highly luminous, dust-obscured galaxies at $z > 3$ that were entirely missed by UV surveys challenges hierarchical assembly models and necessitates further study into early-universe starbursts [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.572..211W/abstract), [Talia et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract). At even higher redshifts, extreme primordial starbursts may be inextricably linked to the formation of massive black holes. Theoretical models suggest that the intense Lyman-Werner radiation fields generated by nearby high-redshift quasars can suppress low-mass halo star formation, accumulating massive reservoirs of primordial gas that subsequently undergo catastrophic cooling to form massive Population III starbursts or direct collapse black holes [Johnson et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...879...18J/abstract). Future observations utilizing the James Webb Space Telescope and ultra-high-resolution ALMA imaging will be essential to untangle these exotic high-redshift phenomena [Oteo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837..182O/abstract).
