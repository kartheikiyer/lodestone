## Article

**Episodic accretion** refers to the non-steady, intermittent accumulation of mass onto a central compact object or protostar, characterized by high-magnitude bursts separated by prolonged periods of quiescence. In contrast to classical textbook models that assume continuous, steady-state inflows—such as Bondi spherical accretion [McCrea et al. (1953)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1953MNRAS.113..162M/abstract) or the standard [[accretion-disk-theory|Shakura-Sunyaev $\alpha$-disk]]—most astrophysical systems exhibit severe [[rapid-variability|Rapid variability]] and [[unsteady-accretion|Unsteady accretion]]. This intermittent behavior is driven by a variety of internal and external triggers, including thermal and viscous [[accretion-disk-theory|disk instabilities]], [[radiation-pressure-instability|Radiation-pressure instability]], magnetic barrier transitions, and the [[stochastic-accretion|stochastic capture]] of external gas clouds or discrete stellar disruptions. Operating across all mass scales, from the formation of infant stars to the fueling of supermassive black holes (SMBHs), episodic [[mass-transfer|Mass transfer]] is now recognized as a fundamental mode of cosmic assembly.

The theoretical shift toward episodic models was largely necessitated by the widespread "luminosity problem" encountered in astrophysics. Early steady-state models consistently over-predicted the persistent luminosities of nearby galactic nuclei, such as Sgr A* and M60, which appeared drastically underluminous compared to their available gas reservoirs [Di Matteo et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997MNRAS.286L..50D/abstract), [Yuan et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006JPhCS..54..427Y/abstract). To reconcile these tensions, theoretical frameworks evolved to show that [[accretion-processes|Accretion Processes]] are inherently stochastic rather than continuous. For instance, the fueling of Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) is now understood to be driven by short-lived, randomly oriented mass injection events—yielding duty cycles of just ~1%—rather than steady flows [Sanders et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981Natur.294..427S/abstract). Similar episodic paradigms were developed to explain the extreme, transient outbursts observed in compact binary systems and X-ray novae [Remillard et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005tsra.conf...79R/abstract).

This article explores the physical mechanisms and widespread consequences of episodic accretion across varied astrophysical environments. It begins by detailing the fundamental disk instabilities that trigger these bursts, before examining their manifestation in stellar systems, including the high-magnitude outbursts of young stellar objects (such as FUors and [[exors|EXors]]) and the extreme state transitions of X-ray binaries. On larger scales, the text investigates the [[stochastic-accretion|Stochastic accretion]] governing AGN activity, as well as extreme nuclear transients driven by [[fallback-accretion|Fallback accretion]], such as Tidal Disruption Events (TDEs) and Quasi-Periodic Eruptions (QPEs). Finally, it discusses the role of distinct [[gas-accretion-modes|Gas accretion modes]] in galactic assembly, illustrating how episodic inflows from the cosmic web regulate star formation and chemical evolution over Hubble timescales [Firmani et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992A&A...264...37F/abstract).

While the episodic paradigm successfully unifies a diverse array of transient phenomena, several critical open questions remain at the forefront of modern research. Chief among these are timescale tensions: the spectral shifts in "changing-look" AGN occur much faster than the standard [[viscous-timescale|Viscous timescale]], challenging current instability models. Furthermore, recent James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) discoveries of overmassive black holes at high redshifts raise questions about whether these objects grew via widespread [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion and Feedback]], or if current mass estimates are simply biased. By leveraging next-generation time-domain surveys and gravitational wave observatories, researchers aim to resolve the physical nature of these extreme accretors and refine our understanding of how intermittent mass accumulation shapes the universe.

## Fundamental Mechanisms and Disk Instabilities

The canonical [[accretion-disk-theory|Shakura-Sunyaev $\alpha$-disk model]] assumes a continuous and steady-state mass inflow, yet observational evidence across varying mass scales frequently demonstrates [[rapid-variability|Rapid variability]]. This episodic behavior arises from physical mechanisms that temporarily alter the balance of angular momentum transport or the thermal stability of the disk, driving bursts of accretion that evolve much faster than the standard [[viscous-timescale|Viscous timescale]].

**Radiation-Pressure and Thermal Instabilities**
In the inner regions of disks accreting at high Eddington fractions, the dominant source of internal pressure shifts from gas to radiation. This [[radiation-pressure-instability|Radiation-pressure instability]] renders the accretion flow both thermally and secularly unstable, generating sharp, high-amplitude luminosity flares [Grzędzielski et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...603A.110G/abstract). To accurately model the rapid state transitions observed in phenomena such as "changing-look" active galactic nuclei (AGN), theoretical frameworks often employ a modified viscosity prescription. For instance, parameterizing the viscous stress tensor $\tau_{r\phi}$ as proportional to the geometric mean of the gas and total pressure yields episodic [[outburst-duration|burst durations]] of $\sim 10^3 - 10^4$ years, successfully reproducing the intermittent activity timescales of compact radio sources [Czerny et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...698..840C/abstract), [Śniegowska et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AN....34310065S/abstract). 

**Magnetic Disk Accretion and the MRI**
While the magneto-rotational instability (MRI) is widely accepted as the primary driver of turbulent viscosity in ionized accretion flows, the global magnetic topology is critical to governing episodic outbursts. Recent cosmological magneto-hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate that circum-nuclear quasar disks can become highly magnetically dominated, maintaining midplane plasma betas of $\beta \sim 10^{-4}$ [Hopkins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..19H/abstract). This intense magnetization supports trans-Alfvénic turbulence and profoundly alters the disk's thermodynamic stability, effectively preventing the catastrophic fragmentation that is otherwise predicted for purely hydrodynamic super-Eddington flows [Hopkins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..19H/abstract). In these massive, highly magnetized quasar accretion disks, local fragmentation can even lead to in-situ star formation; owing to the extreme tidal environment, this process is predicted to produce a distinctly top-heavy Initial Mass Function (IMF) [Hopkins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..71H/abstract). In 1D phenomenological models of [[magnetic-disk-accretion|Magnetic disk accretion]], the degree of disk magnetization is often parameterized via a modified $\mu$-prescription, which correlates directly with the duration and amplitude of flares across scales ranging from microquasars to supermassive black holes [Grzędzielski et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...603A.110G/abstract).

**Gravitational Instability in Outer Disks**
At larger radii, where the disk is cooler and less influenced by the central object's radiation field, self-gravity becomes the dominant mechanism for angular momentum redistribution. Gravitational instability generates spiral density waves that exert long-range torques, driving robust inward gas flows [Lodato et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007NCimR..30..293L/abstract). The dynamic balance between this instability-driven transport, fresh accretion from the external environment, and local stellar feedback dictates the overall structural evolution of the disk [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.1552F/abstract). However, if the local cooling timescale becomes sufficiently short, the instability transitions from driving a steady effective viscosity to inducing complete disk fragmentation, leading to the formation of localized bound clumps rather than smooth central accretion [Lodato et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007NCimR..30..293L/abstract).

**Tensions: Intrinsic Instabilities vs. External Triggers**
Despite significant advancements in [[accretion-disk-theory|Accretion Disk Theory]], there remains an active debate in the literature regarding the root cause of the most extreme episodic variability. While models of modified viscosity and [[radiation-pressure-instability|Radiation-pressure instability]] successfully match many light-curve morphologies, there is tension over whether intrinsic disk instabilities alone can fully explain phenomena like Quasi-Periodic Eruptions (QPEs) or changing-look AGN. Conflicting models suggest that such [[rapid-variability|Rapid variability]] may instead require external triggers, such as [[stochastic-accretion|Stochastic accretion]] of dense molecular clouds [Sanders et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981Natur.294..427S/abstract), or the periodic impacts of a secondary body (e.g., a white dwarf) in an extreme mass-ratio inspiral (EMRI) piercing the primary's accretion disk [King et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523L..26K/abstract), [Miniutti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241113460M/abstract). As such, the relative dominance of secular disk instabilities versus stochastic external disruptions remains unresolved.

## Historical Development and the Luminosity Problem

Historically, [[accretion-processes|Accretion Processes]] were modeled under the assumption of continuous, steady-state flows. The foundational [[accretion-disk-theory|Shakura-Sunyaev model]] described a geometrically thin, optically thick accretion disk where angular momentum transport is parameterized by an isotropic viscosity $\alpha$ [Grzędzielski et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...603A.110G/abstract). For roughly spherical inflows from a hot interstellar medium, standard Bondi accretion provided a baseline estimate for the mass accretion rate $\dot{M}$. Under these steady-state accretion frameworks, the predicted bolometric luminosity $L$ is expected to scale with the accretion rate as:
$$ L = \eta \dot{M} c^2 $$
where $\eta$ is the radiative efficiency [Wang et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697L.141W/abstract). 

However, multi-wavelength surveys soon revealed severe discrepancies between predicted and observed luminosities—a tension collectively known as the luminosity problem. Given the dense, hot gas surrounding nearby supermassive black holes (SMBHs), standard Bondi accretion models predicted X-ray luminosities orders of magnitude higher than those actually observed in systems like M60 [Di Matteo et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997MNRAS.286L..50D/abstract) and the Galactic Center's Sgr A* [Yuan et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006JPhCS..54..427Y/abstract). A similar "missing source" problem plagued stellar-mass objects: the anticipated $\sim 10^9$ isolated neutron stars in the Galaxy were mysteriously absent in ROSAT X-ray surveys, implying that the standard Bondi formula vastly overestimates the true accretion efficiency [Perna et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...594..936P/abstract). Furthermore, theoretical refinements indicated that adiabatic heating in magnetized collisionless accretion flows—such as those expected around isolated black holes—is predicted to be 25% more efficient than in non-magnetized gas models, complicating standard flux predictions [Beskin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AdSpR..42..523B/abstract).

To resolve the luminosity deficit in persistent sources, theoretical attention shifted to the Advection-dominated accretion flow (ADAF) model [Abramowicz et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ComAp..18..141A/abstract). In this regime, the inner flow becomes virially hot, optically thin, and radiatively inefficient. The plasma develops a two-temperature structure where protons are significantly hotter than electrons, causing the bulk of the gravitational potential energy to be advected across the event horizon rather than radiated away [Mahadevan et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.394..651M/abstract), [Yuan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..529Y/abstract). ADAF models successfully reconciled the spectral energy distributions of underluminous sources like Sgr A*, whose mass is tightly constrained to $M_h \approx 4.1 \times 10^6 M_\odot$ [Dokuchaev et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhyU...58..772D/abstract), formally establishing the existence of multiple distinct [[gas-accretion-modes|Gas accretion modes]].

While ADAFs explain low-luminosity states, they do not account for the [[rapid-variability|Rapid variability]] and extreme energetic transients observed across the universe. It became increasingly clear that resolving the luminosity problem over cosmic time required abandoning the assumption of steady-state accretion entirely. Models pivoted to episodic and stochastic fueling. In active galactic nuclei (AGN), activity is now understood to be driven by short-lived, randomly oriented accretion events lasting $\sim 10^5$ years, yielding duty cycles on the order of $1\%$ [Sanders et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981Natur.294..427S/abstract), [Sanders et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984A&A...140...52S/abstract), [King et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.377L..25K/abstract). Observational evidence supports this extreme intermittency; for example, the nearby quasar in IC 2497 experienced a catastrophic shutdown, dropping its radiative output by over four orders of magnitude in $<70,000$ years [Schawinski et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724L..30S/abstract).

The necessity of [[unsteady-accretion|Unsteady accretion]] extends from SMBHs down to protostellar systems. Both AGN and protostellar disks are subject to profound instabilities governed by [[accretion-disk-theory|Accretion Disk Theory]]. Outer disks can become self-gravitating, leading to fragmentation and the episodic delivery of bound clumps that feed both young stars and supermassive black holes [Lodato et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007NCimR..30..293L/abstract). Meanwhile, inner disks operating at high Eddington ratios are prone to radiation-pressure instabilities that trigger sharp, violent flares [Grzędzielski et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...603A.110G/abstract), [Czerny et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...698..840C/abstract). This conceptual evolution from steady-state uniform flows to dynamic, episodic accretion has fundamentally reshaped our understanding of mass assembly across all astrophysical scales.

## Episodic Accretion in Stellar and Binary Systems

In the context of [[stellar-accretion|Stellar accretion]], protostars and young stellar objects grow through phases of [[unsteady-accretion|Unsteady accretion]]. High-magnitude outbursts in young stellar systems—empirically classified as FUors and [[exors|EXors]]—are largely driven by thermal and viscous instabilities in the circumstellar disk. While classical textbook models of self-regulated accretion suggest that the final mass of a star is limited by the velocity dispersion of its natal gas clump, recent observations and multi-scale simulations strongly favor formation via the gravitational collapse of fragmented cores, rejecting models of continuous competitive accretion from a common reservoir [Krumholz et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005Natur.438..332K/abstract), [Krumholz et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.10412K/abstract). During this episodic growth, protostellar feedback plays a critical role in modulating or abruptly halting mass accumulation. For example, radiation-hydrodynamics simulations show that ultraviolet radiation from massive primordial stars can entirely evaporate the accretion disk once the protostar reaches a mass of $\sim 43 M_\odot$ [Hosokawa et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...334.1250H/abstract). 

Beyond protostars, episodic behavior is also a defining feature of evolved massive stars. Historically, it was even hypothesized that the extreme luminosities of Hubble-Sandage variables and the famous 19th-century outburst of Eta Carinae were driven by runaway accretion events in binary systems [Bath et al. (1979)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1979Natur.282..274B/abstract). Modern multi-wavelength surveys continue to reveal this volatility; for instance, the ASSESS project has cataloged numerous dusty, evolved massive stars (such as red supergiants) in nearby southern galaxies that exhibit significant infrared excess, identifying them as prime candidates for ongoing episodic [[mass-loss|Mass loss]] and [[mass-transfer|Mass transfer]] events [Bonanos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..77B/abstract). 

In stellar-mass compact binaries, episodic accretion manifests through extreme luminosity variations and spectral state transitions. High-mass X-ray binaries, such as Supergiant Fast X-ray Transients (SFXTs), exhibit immense volatility due to the interaction between the accreting neutron star and the clumpy stellar wind of its companion. Based on long-term monitoring by observatories like INTEGRAL, supergiant high-mass X-ray binaries can be classified into classical, eccentric, and fast transient systems, reflecting differing regimes of wind-driven episodic accretion [Walter et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&ARv..23....2W/abstract). Transitions across the magnetic and centrifugal barriers of the neutron star can induce luminosity swings by factors of $10^4$ or more on timescales as short as a few hours [Bozzo et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...683.1031B/abstract). Additionally, in accreting neutron stars reaching extreme luminosities, the radiation-dominated boundary layer between the accretion disk and the stellar surface can form a "levitating spreading layer," which serves as a key mechanism driving high-energy spectral transitions [Gilfanov et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhyU...57..377G/abstract). Similarly, in black hole binaries and Microquasars, state transitions are closely coupled with [[episodic-jet-activity|Episodic jet activity]]. During the radiatively inefficient "hard" state, sources like Cygnus X-1 produce 1-2 day transient $\gamma$-ray flares ($100\text{ MeV}$-$3\text{ GeV}$) that punctuate the [[persistent-x-ray-emission|Persistent X-ray emission]] [Sabatini et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...712L..10S/abstract). The behavior of these [[hard-x-ray-transients|Hard X-ray transients]] and the ejection of highly relativistic plasmoids can be successfully modeled via the toroidal instability of current-carrying magnetic flux ropes anchored in the accretion disk corona [Shende et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877..130S/abstract).

At the most extreme end of the accretion scale are Ultraluminous X-ray sources (ULXs), which often undergo [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion and Feedback]]. Spectroscopic studies reveal that many bona-fide ULXs possess supercritical accretion disks that launch massive, hot winds, showing optical spectra remarkably similar to the Galactic supercritical accretor SS 433 [Fabrika et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASPC..510..395F/abstract). The discovery of pulsating ULXs further demonstrates that some of these systems host highly magnetized neutron stars rather than black holes. In these specific systems, the accretion column becomes heavily advection-dominated. At extreme mass accretion rates ($\dot{M} > 10^{21}\text{ g s}^{-1}$), the thermal energy at the base of the column is so immense that neutrino emission begins to dominate the energy losses. These "neutrino pulsars" can reach observed photon luminosities of $L \sim 10^{41}\text{ erg s}^{-1}$, exceeding the standard Eddington limit by a factor of up to 500 [Mushtukov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.2867M/abstract). 

The underlying mechanisms driving these outbursts share theoretical grounding with [[accretion-disk-theory|Accretion Disk Theory]] across mass scales. Time-dependent models of radiation-pressure dominated disks show that modified viscosity prescriptions can reproduce the sharp, episodic flares seen in intermediate-mass black hole candidates like HLX-1 [Grzędzielski et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...603A.110G/abstract), while analogous thermal-viscous instabilities are responsible for the shorter $\sim 1$-week outbursts observed in dwarf novae [Zsidi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532..592Z/abstract).

## Stochastic Fueling of Supermassive Black Holes

The growth and activity of active galactic nuclei (AGN) are fundamentally characterized by [[stochastic-accretion|Stochastic accretion]] driven by discrete, episodic mass injections rather than steady, continuous inflows. The mechanisms responsible for funneling gas into the deep potential well of a supermassive black hole (SMBH) vary strongly with cosmic time and galaxy environment.

At high redshifts ($z \sim 2$), major galaxy mergers are thought to dominate the fueling process, driving massive gas inflows that can spin up SMBHs to near-maximal values [Li et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...710..878L/abstract). Recent observations of unexpectedly massive black holes in the early universe are well-described by models invoking short, episodic bursts of [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion and Feedback]] triggered by such major mergers. These bursts are estimated to last $0.5-3$ Myr, yielding an active duty cycle of $1-4\%$ for high-$z$ quasars [Trinca et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241214248T/abstract).

In the local universe, fueling shifts toward a series of randomly oriented, small-scale accretion events, such as minor mergers and the capture of giant molecular clouds [King et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.377L..25K/abstract). Moreover, internal galactic dynamics play a crucial role; barred structures within spiral galaxies can drive the episodic accretion of gas clumps into the central region on roughly $10\text{ Myr}$ timescales, a dynamical process that strongly depends on the host galaxy's bulge-to-disk mass ratio [Yutani et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250914487Y/abstract). The stochastic capture of molecular clouds with low net angular momentum can ignite Seyfert activity lasting $\sim 10^5$ yr. If these capture events recur roughly every $10^7$ yr, the resulting $\sim 1\%$ duty cycle naturally explains the observed local fraction of Seyfert galaxies [Sanders et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981Natur.294..427S/abstract), [Sanders et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984A&A...140...52S/abstract). Because these sequential discrete events lack a preferred orbital plane, they cause the black hole spin to "random walk" and decrease over cosmic time. This is reflected in the cosmological evolution of the AGN radiative efficiency parameter, $\eta$, which is inferred to drop from $\eta \approx 0.3$ at $z \approx 2$ to $\eta \approx 0.03$ at $z \approx 0$ [Wang et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697L.141W/abstract). This framework of episodic, [[chaotic-accretion|Chaotic accretion]] versus steady major mergers also provides a natural explanation for the radio-loudness dichotomy in active galaxies, as the resulting black hole spin evolution dictates the efficiency of jet production [Sikora et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008NewAR..51..891S/abstract).

The episodic nature of AGN is starkly illustrated by "changing-look" AGN, which exhibit dramatic transitions in spectral type (e.g., between Seyfert 1 and Seyfert 2) accompanied by massive luminosity swings. These transitions pose a theoretical challenge because they occur much faster than the standard [[viscous-timescale|Viscous timescale]] of the accretion disk [Śniegowska et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AN....34310065S/abstract). The literature currently reflects a tension regarding the exact physical mechanism driving these extreme variations. Some models suggest they are driven by intrinsic thermal-viscous instabilities operating in radiation-pressure dominated disks [Śniegowska et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AN....34310065S/abstract). Other models argue for external stochastic triggers, such as the sudden infall of an individual molecular cloud [Sanders et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981Natur.294..427S/abstract), or stellar disruptions of an accretion disc [Pandey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250903593P/abstract). This rapid variability regime has also been highlighted by the recent discovery of a new class of extreme, long-lived optical/UV flares (e.g., AT 2017bgt) which represent sudden, intensified accretion episodes completely distinct from standard stellar tidal disruption events [Trakhtenbrot et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NatAs...3..242T/abstract).

Our own Galactic Center provides a uniquely resolved laboratory for studying stochastic fueling in a highly quiescent state. Sgr A*, with a measured mass of $M \approx 4.1 \times 10^6 M_\odot$, currently operates as a radiatively inefficient, advection-dominated accretion flow (ADAF) [Abramowicz et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ComAp..18..141A/abstract), [Yuan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..529Y/abstract), [Dokuchaev et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhyU...58..772D/abstract). Three-dimensional numerical simulations reveal that even this low-level accretion is highly episodic: shocked stellar winds from nearby stars cool to form cold gas clumps and filaments, causing the mass accretion rate onto Sgr A* to fluctuate significantly on timescales of tens to hundreds of years [Cuadra et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006JPhCS..54..436C/abstract).

## Extreme Nuclear Transients: TDEs and QPEs

When a star is perturbed into the loss cone of a supermassive black hole (SMBH) and crosses its tidal radius, it is violently shredded in a process known as a Tidal disruption event (TDE). The subsequent return of the bound stellar debris dictates a highly transient phase of [[fallback-accretion|Fallback accretion]], where the mass return rate follows a power-law decline expected for the fallback rate of the stellar debris [Gezari et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ARA&A..59...21G/abstract). The initial mass accumulation rate frequently drives [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion]], powering highly luminous and [[energetic-transients|Energetic transients]]. In rare, extreme cases involving high-mass ($\gtrsim 3 M_\odot$) stellar progenitors, such as the transient Gaia18cdj, the peak luminosities can reach $7 \times 10^{45} {\rm erg\,s^{-1}}$ with total radiated energies exceeding $10^{53} {\rm erg}$ [Hinkle et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025SciA...11...74H/abstract). Beyond stellar disruptions by isolated black holes, extreme transients can also arise in more complex environments. When such disruptions occur in binary supermassive black hole systems, the resulting gas streams can chaotically form accretion disks around both compact objects. The resulting light curves, modulated by relativistic beaming, can serve as electromagnetic tracers for the gravitational wave-driven orbital decay of the binary pair [Hayasaki et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016NatSR...635629H/abstract). Furthermore, extreme, high-magnitude transient accretion and outflow events can also signal the death of massive stars; for example, the birth of a supernova can be accompanied by an extremely luminous X-ray outburst marking the shock breakout [Soderberg et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.453..469S/abstract).

The exact physical geometry of the accretion flow during this super-Eddington phase remains a subject of active debate. Some models posit that the circularizing debris forms a compact, standard accretion disc that drives modest, sub-escape velocity outflows [Miller et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.526..542M/abstract). Conversely, other theoretical frameworks argue that the low angular momentum of the infalling gas traps radiation, causing the flow to inflate into a weakly bound, [[quasi-spherical-accretion|Quasi-spherical accretion]] envelope [Coughlin et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...781...82C/abstract). This envelope scenario provides a natural funneling mechanism to collimate and launch the relativistic jets observed in events like Swift J1644+57 [Zauderer et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Natur.476..425Z/abstract), [Coughlin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...781...82C/abstract). However, constraints from extensive radio surveys indicate that the rate of relativistic jet production in TDEs is quite low, implying that such energetic, collimated outflows are intrinsically much rarer than typical thermal TDE flares [Bower et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732L..12B/abstract). Furthermore, while most TDE flares decay over timescales of months, rare events exhibit decade-long sustained super-Eddington X-ray emission, indicating prolonged, steady feeding of the SMBH [Lin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017NatAs...1E..33L/abstract).

An emerging, related class of episodic nuclear variability is Quasi-periodic eruptions (QPEs). These events are characterized by intense soft X-ray bursts from low-mass galactic nuclei that last for roughly an hour and repeat every few hours [Miniutti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.179M/abstract). The leading physical model for QPEs involves Extreme mass-ratio inspirals (EMRIs), wherein a secondary compact object—such as a $\sim 0.21 M_\odot$ white dwarf or the stripped core of a red giant—resides in a highly eccentric orbit around the primary SMBH. The periodic eruptions of QPEs are thought to be triggered by the secondary either undergoing partial tidal stripping at pericenter or repeatedly impacting the primary's pre-existing accretion disc [King et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493L.120K/abstract), [King et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523L..26K/abstract). 

However, the EMRI-impact paradigm faces unresolved tensions due to complex timing anomalies. In GSN 069, the first recognized QPE-emitting galaxy, the observed-minus-calculated (O-C) arrival times of the flares exhibit a super-orbital modulation on timescales of tens of days. Reconciling this modulation with standard EMRI models requires invoking either the rigid precession of a misaligned accretion disc or the presence of a sub-milliparsec SMBH binary that gravitationally perturbs the inspiraling body [Miniutti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241113460M/abstract), [Miniutti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.179M/abstract). Alternatively, such binary systems can generate "cavity accretion flares", where gas leakage into a central cavity once per orbit triggers recurring, luminous outbursts that might mimic isolated disruption events [Tanaka et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.434.2275T/abstract). As the EMRI orbit decays due to gravitational wave emission and the eccentricity drops below a critical threshold ($e_{\rm crit} \simeq 0.97$), theoretical models predict that QPEs will eventually transition into smoother quasi-periodic oscillations [King et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523L..26K/abstract).

Finally, the observational boundary between TDEs and intrinsic active galactic nucleus (AGN) variability is increasingly blurred by "ambiguous nuclear transients." Sources such as AT2020adpi display a mixture of TDE-like multi-wavelength properties—including delayed infrared dust echoes tracking the thermal emission of surrounding material, a general phenomenon increasingly recognized across flare populations [Necker et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240701039N/abstract)—and AGN-like spectral features specifically detailed for sources like AT2020adpi [Pandey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250903593P/abstract). Such objects suggest that some extreme episodic flares may result from star-disc collisions or massive disc instabilities in already active nuclei, rather than isolated stellar disruptions in dormant galaxies [Pandey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250903593P/abstract), [Pandey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025OJAp....851453P/abstract).

## Galactic Assembly and Cosmological Gas Inflow

The mass assembly of galaxies over cosmic time is governed by an interplay of distinct [[gas-accretion-modes|Gas accretion modes]]. While early paradigms emphasized hierarchical galaxy mergers as the primary driver of galactic growth, modern consensus highlights the dominant role of gas inflows from the cosmic web. On the largest scales, accretion shocks at the boundaries of large-scale structures (such as cosmic filaments and galaxy clusters) provide direct evidence of cosmic gas assembly; these shocks can accelerate cosmic rays, manifesting observationally as massive radio relics [Ensslin et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..5367E/abstract). Recent observations have further cemented this picture, such as the detection of a new GeV gamma-ray source in the outer regions of the Coma cluster, which serves as direct evidence of an [[external-accretion|external accretion shock]] driven by gas falling from the cosmic web [Chen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241202436C/abstract). Observational constraints on the gas fractions of star-forming galaxies on the "main sequence" indicate that their gas reservoirs would be depleted by star formation in $\lesssim 2$ Gyr without continuous replenishment from the intergalactic medium [Bouché et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..355B/abstract). Consequently, ongoing gas infall is strictly required to sustain the observed star formation history of spiral galaxies over Hubble timescales. Within the galactic disc itself, the continuous supply of gas from the circumgalactic medium can induce large-scale Kelvin-Helmholtz turbulence, which acts as an indirect observational signature of this steady gas accretion [Goldman et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.2949G/abstract).

Cosmological simulations demonstrate that for most galaxies, [[smooth-accretion|Smooth accretion]] from cosmic filaments accounts for the majority of their mass assembly. On average, galaxies acquire $\approx 77\%$ of their mass via smooth accretion, with discrete mergers contributing the remaining $23\%$, though the relative importance of mergers increases for the most massive systems [L'Huillier et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...544A..68L/abstract). In the local universe, spiral galaxies are modeled as open systems that double their mass roughly every $10$ Gyr through this continuous infall [Block et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...394L..35B/abstract). In the case of our own galaxy, empirical estimates utilizing the Kennicutt-Schmidt law indicate that the Milky Way likely formed two-thirds of its stars after $z=1$, with cosmic gas accretion rates closely tracking its star formation history [Fraternali et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.2166F/abstract). However, there remains an active debate in the literature regarding the relative importance of smooth accretion versus mass assembly via discrete galaxy mergers at high redshifts ($z > 2$), where merger rates are significantly higher and large-scale structures are still rapidly assembling.

The coupling between cosmological inflow and galactic disk evolution is tightly regulated. In simulated Milky Way-analogues, net accretion rates exhibit an exponential decay over time, settling into an equilibrium where the star formation rate (SFR) is governed directly by the gas accretion rate on a Gyr timescale [Iza et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517..832I/abstract), [Pflamm-Altenburg et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..440..403P/abstract). Simulations such as TNG100 reveal that the resulting temporal fluctuations in the star formation rate (SFR) within individual galaxies can naturally account for the observed scatter in the star-forming main sequence [Gui et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251200151G/abstract). This self-regulation yields typical mass-loading ratios where the outflow-to-inflow rate is $\dot{M}_\mathrm{out}/ \dot{M}_\mathrm{in} \sim 0.75$, and the SFR-to-inflow efficiency is $10\%-30\%$ [Iza et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517..832I/abstract). Furthermore, the gravitational energy released by this ongoing mass assembly is significant; prior to the dominance of stellar feedback, accretion energy can drive fast turbulent motions within high-redshift galaxy disks, profoundly influencing their early structural evolution [Elmegreen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...712..294E/abstract).

Although accretion from the cosmic web is broadly continuous on cosmological scales, its delivery to the inner star-forming regions of the galactic disk is frequently episodic. Gas accreted from the halo can pool in the outer disk until gravitational instabilities, secular bar-driven torques, or minor interactions drive it inward, triggering episodic [[violent-starbursts|bursts of star formation]] [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.1552F/abstract), [Combes et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..8616C/abstract). Empirical evidence of this intermittency is well-documented in the Local Group; for example, the Carina dwarf spheroidal galaxy exhibits distinct episodes of star formation separated by several gigayears rather than a single brief burst [Smecker-Hane et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ASPC...98..328S/abstract). In more massive galaxies, spatially resolved Balmer absorption lines provide further evidence that star formation in outer discs is predominantly driven by short-lived episodic bursts linked directly to recent [[external-accretion|External accretion]] events [Huang et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431.2622H/abstract). One of the clearest observational signatures of these discrete external accretion events is the presence of metallicity drops in star-forming galaxies. When pristine, metal-poor gas from the cosmic web penetrates the disk and fuels a sudden starburst, the local gas-phase metallicity sharply decreases, creating a distinct chemical signature of cosmic inflow [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430...67S/abstract), [Elmegreen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..317..204E/abstract).

Direct observational evidence of this infalling material has historically been elusive but is now robustly probed using background quasars. Absorption line studies combined with integral field unit (IFU) observations reveal extended, co-rotating gaseous structures at impact parameters of $10-30$ kpc from host galaxies. These structures confirm that the accreting cosmic gas delivers not only the necessary fuel for [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|Starburst Galaxy Evolution]] but also the specific angular momentum required to support and maintain extended spiral and bar structures over cosmic time [Bouché et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..355B/abstract), [Bouché et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Sci...341...50B/abstract).

## Feedback Processes and Environmental Regulation

Episodic accretion onto supermassive black holes (SMBHs) plays a pivotal role in galactic evolution by periodically injecting massive amounts of energy and momentum into the interstellar medium (ISM) and circumgalactic medium (CGM). This intermittent AGN feedback regulates both the growth of the central black hole and the star formation history of the host galaxy. During periods of active fueling, radiation pressure and magnetic instabilities can launch powerful disc winds and drive [[episodic-jet-activity|Episodic jet activity]] [Shende et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877..130S/abstract), [Yuan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..529Y/abstract). Crucially, these accretion-disk winds exhibit striking physical similarities across all mass scales, from stellar binaries to quasars, and can evolve on timescales as short as minutes. This rapid evolution necessitates high-resolution time-domain spectroscopy to fully disentangle their feedback impact in future studies [Castro Segura et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260102028C/abstract). Observations of "ultrafast outflows" (UFOs) reveal that these winds can reach relativistic velocities exceeding $0.2c$ (e.g., $v \approx 71,000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$) and respond to changes in the inner X-ray emitting region on exceptionally short timescales [Parker et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017Natur.543...83P/abstract).

Intense X-ray flares originating from episodic accretion episodes also serve as powerful probes of the strong gravity regime. For instance, observations of extreme flares in nearby galaxies, such as I Zwicky 1, have allowed astronomers to detect photons "echoing" off the far side of the accretion disk, directly confirming general relativistic light bending around the supermassive black hole [Wilkins et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Natur.595..657W/abstract).

Momentum-driven winds launched by radiatively efficient episodic accretion exert both "ejective" and "preventative" feedback on their environments. Cosmological simulations demonstrate that these outflows, characterized by velocities of $500 - 1000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, can expel up to 80% of the affected galactic gas and suppress subsequent large-scale gas inflow by factors of up to 30 [Brennan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...860...14B/abstract). The kinetic power of these outflows is fundamentally governed by the mass outflow rate $\dot{M}_{\rm out}$ and the outflow velocity $v_{\rm out}$:

$$ \dot{E}_{\rm out} = \frac{1}{2} \dot{M}_{\rm out} v_{\rm out}^2 $$

Observations of merging systems reveal that this kinetic power can range from $\dot{E}_{\rm out} \sim 10^{40}$ to $10^{42} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$, which is sufficient to clear circumnuclear gas and rapidly quench local accretion [Xu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250920716X/abstract). 

Despite these massive energy ejections, the literature reflects a nuanced view regarding the immediate quenching of galaxy-wide star formation. High-resolution simulations of idealized $z \sim 2$ disc galaxies suggest that thermally driven AGN winds often escape perpendicular to the galactic plane, bypassing the densest star-forming gas. Thus, while mass outflow rates can match or exceed the star formation rate (reaching tens of $M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$ ), the feedback primarily starves the galaxy of future fuel over extended timescales of $\gtrsim 1 \text{ Gyr}$ rather than causing an abrupt, immediate halt to star formation [Gabor et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441.1615G/abstract). 

The stochastic and highly variable nature of these accretion episodes is critical for maintaining the long-term thermal stability of the host galaxy's halo. Theoretical models indicate that rapid, large-amplitude fluctuations in the accretion rate inject energy across a broad continuum of timescales. This stochastic energy injection is significantly more effective at balancing the radiative cooling of the X-ray emitting CGM than idealized, strictly periodic outbursts, allowing the AGN to maintain a close thermodynamic equilibrium with its surroundings [Pope et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.381..741P/abstract). On even larger scales, the episodic infall of cosmic gas into the halo generates accretion shocks that heat the diffuse circumgalactic and intracluster medium. These shocks shape the entropy profile of the halo and establish the underlying thermal boundary conditions against which AGN feedback must continuously operate [Shi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461.1804S/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the foundational mechanisms of episodic accretion are well established, recent observational breakthroughs have exposed critical gaps in standard [[accretion-disk-theory|Accretion Disk Theory]]. Current literature highlights several unresolved tensions, ranging from the characteristic timescales of local "changing-look" Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) to the rapid assembly of the first supermassive black holes (SMBHs). 

**High-Redshift Overmassive Black Holes**
Recent observations by JWST have revealed a population of AGN at $z \gtrsim 4$ hosting overmassive black holes that appear excessively large compared to the stellar mass of their host galaxies [Lupi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240617847L/abstract). The literature is currently divided on how to interpret these objects. One leading hypothesis posits that these BHs grow via repeated, short-lived episodes ($0.5-3\ {\rm Myr}$) of [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion and Feedback]] triggered by major galaxy mergers, with duty cycles of $1-4\%$ [Trinca et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241214248T/abstract). Such violent bursts could also explain the peculiar spectra of the "Little Red Dots" observed in the early universe [Quadri et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250505556Q/abstract). Alternatively, some early universe quasars may not originate from standard low-mass seeds at all; theoretical models of galaxy merger-driven direct collapse suggest that high-metallicity accretion disks could lead to the formation of $10^6 M_\odot$ supermassive stars, which would then collapse directly into massive black hole seeds [Haemmerlé et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250301970H/abstract). Conversely, some models argue that SMBH mass estimates derived from emerging spectra near the Eddington limit are highly uncertain—potentially by up to an order of magnitude. This suggests that if the true masses are lower, the required accretion rates must be even higher, potentially indicating that we are witnessing a widespread phase of very rapid, super-Eddington accretion [Lupi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240617847L/abstract).

**Quasi-Periodic Eruptions and Gravitational Waves**
Another major theoretical challenge is identifying the physical mechanism driving Quasi-Periodic Eruptions (QPEs) in low-mass galaxy nuclei. While often modeled as Extreme Mass-Ratio Inspirals (EMRIs) where a secondary body, such as a white dwarf, periodically impacts the primary SMBH's accretion disc [King et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493L.120K/abstract), timing anomalies in systems like GSN 069 conflict with simple impact models. Reconciling these observations may require invoking rigid disc precession or perturbations from sub-milliparsec binaries composed of two SMBHs [Miniutti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241113460M/abstract), [Miniutti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.179M/abstract). 

Future space-based observatories will be critical in resolving these EMRI and QPE models through the detection of gravitational waves. Such detections promise to inaugurate a new era of multi-messenger astronomy, potentially allowing simultaneous independent measurements of the central black hole's mass accretion rate and the accretion disc's viscosity to a relative precision of $\sim 10\%$ [Duque et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.111h4006D/abstract).

**Timescale Tensions and Extreme Accretors**
In local AGN, the "changing-look" phenomenon—where sources rapidly transition between Seyfert types—remains physically ambiguous. These spectral shifts occur much faster than the standard [[viscous-timescale|Viscous timescale]] of a thin disc, requiring alternative explanations such as radiation-pressure instabilities or rapid transitions to advection-dominated flows [Śniegowska et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AN....34310065S/abstract). 

Furthermore, the nature of the compact objects powering Ultraluminous X-ray sources (ULXs) is still heavily debated. While some ULXs exhibit coherent pulsations indicating highly supercritical accretion onto magnetized neutron stars (where neutrino cooling may dominate the energy budget at mass transfer rates $>10^{21}\ {\rm g\ s^{-1}}$ [Mushtukov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.2867M/abstract)), others remain strong candidates for intermediate-mass black holes [Amato et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220265A/abstract). Resolving whether these represent distinct accretion regimes or a continuum of [[unsteady-accretion|Unsteady accretion]] will heavily depend on next-generation time-domain X-ray surveys and continued multi-wavelength monitoring.
