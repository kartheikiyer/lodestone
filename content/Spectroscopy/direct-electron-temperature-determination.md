## Article

**Direct electron temperature** ($T_e$) **determination** is a fundamental observational technique used to establish the thermal state and chemical composition of ionized gas. The method primarily relies on measuring the flux ratio of collisionally excited emission lines originating from the same ionic species but possessing significantly different excitation energies. By taking the ratio of a faint, high-excitation "auroral" line to a strong, low-excitation "nebular" line—such as the prominent [O III] $\lambda$4363/$\lambda$5007 diagnostic—the dependence on elemental abundance and the region's [[emission-measure|Emission measure]] cancels out entirely. The resulting ratio is highly sensitive to the [[kinetic-temperature|Kinetic temperature]] of the free electrons and only weakly dependent on the [[galactic-electron-density-distribution|Galactic electron density distribution]]. Historically, the development of empirical thermometry dismantled early assumptions that all H II regions were uniformly hot ($\sim 10^4$ K) plasmas, revealing complex thermal structures governed by heavy-element cooling [Burbidge et al. (1963)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1963ApJ...138..945B/abstract), [Churchwell et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975A&A....38..451C/abstract).

Widely regarded as the "gold standard" benchmark for [[direct-abundance-methods|Direct-abundance methods]], direct $T_e$ measurements are essential for anchoring the absolute gas-phase metallicity scale of the universe. However, a major, unresolved tension in extragalactic astrophysics is the "abundance discrepancy factor": direct methods historically yield oxygen abundances 0.2 to 0.5 dex lower than those derived from photoionization-based strong-line empirical calibrations [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591..801K/abstract). This discrepancy is largely attributed to systematic observational biases. Because auroral line emissivity scales exponentially with temperature, unresolved [[temperature-fluctuations-astrophysics|Temperature fluctuations (astrophysics)]] (often parameterized as $t^2$) and contamination from diffuse ionized gas (DIG) disproportionately weight integrated spectra toward the hottest gas. This effect systematically inflates the inferred global temperature, subsequently causing an underestimation of the true absolute metallicity. 

In recent years, the landscape of nebular thermometry has been transformed by advanced spatially resolved and high-redshift observations [Kewley et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ARA&A..57..511K/abstract). Modern integral field unit (IFU) surveys map detailed 2D temperature gradients across galactic disks, tracing the thermodynamics of the interstellar medium and isolating localized chemical feedback. Simultaneously, the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has pushed direct auroral line detections into "Cosmic Noon" and beyond ($z > 3$), providing the first direct constraints on chemical enrichment patterns in the early universe. In environments where heavy dust extinction obscures optical auroral lines, astronomers increasingly utilize multi-wavelength alternatives, pairing far-infrared fine-structure lines with radio recombination lines to probe heavily embedded star-forming regions. 

Beyond star-forming regions, the principles of direct temperature measurement extend to extreme astrophysical plasmas, such as the intracluster medium (ICM) of galaxy clusters. In these environments, thermal states are mapped using [[x-ray-temperature|X-ray temperature]] diagnostics and relativistic corrections to the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect. Unifying these diverse regimes drives several open questions in modern astrophysics. Future research utilizing next-generation Extremely Large Telescopes and deep SZ surveys aims to robustly resolve the abundance discrepancy, quantify the impact of stochastic star formation on line diagnostics, explain anomalous high-redshift abundance ratios, and determine whether high-energy plasmas are better described by standard Maxwellian or suprathermal electron velocity distributions.

## Theoretical Foundations and Physical Principles

The theoretical foundation of the direct electron temperature ($T_e$) method relies on the physics of collisionally excited lines (CELs) in ionized gas. In typical H II regions, heating by stellar photoionization is balanced by radiative cooling, which is dominated by the nebular emission of CELs from trace heavy elements (e.g., oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur). 

The volume emissivity $j$ of a collisionally excited transition depends heavily on the [[kinetic-temperature|Kinetic temperature]] of the free electrons. For a Maxwell-Boltzmann electron velocity distribution, the emissivity of a transition with excitation energy $\Delta E$ scales approximately as:
$$ j \propto \frac{n_e n_{\rm ion}}{\sqrt{T_e}} \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta E}{k_B T_e}\right) $$
where $n_e$ is the electron density and $n_{\rm ion}$ is the number density of the emitting ion. 

The direct method isolates $T_e$ by comparing the fluxes of two emission lines from the *same* ionic species but with significantly different excitation energies. By taking the ratio of a high-excitation "auroral" line to a low-excitation "nebular" line, the [[emission-measure|Emission measure]] and elemental abundance terms cancel out entirely. The resulting line ratio becomes a direct, highly sensitive probe of the electron temperature, with only a weak dependence on electron density. 

Because ionized nebulae are stratified into different ionization zones by the local radiation field, multi-wavelength observations of different ions are required to fully characterize the thermal structure of the gas [Kewley et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ARA&A..57..511K/abstract). High-excitation auroral lines, such as [O III] $\lambda4363$ or [S III] $\lambda6312$, trace the hotter, highly ionized inner regions of the nebula. Conversely, low-excitation auroral lines like [N II] $\lambda5755$ and [O II] $\lambda\lambda7320, 7330$ probe the cooler, outer low-ionization zones [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591..801K/abstract), [Welch et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...33W/abstract). Relying on a single temperature measurement (e.g., only $T_e$([O III])) and using empirical relationships to infer the temperatures of unobserved ionization zones can introduce significant systematic errors in [[direct-abundance-methods|Direct-abundance methods]], sometimes inflating the measured abundance dispersion by up to 0.11 dex [Cameron et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.3695C/abstract), [Rogers et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...939...44R/abstract).

Furthermore, the directly measured "ratio temperature" ($T_{\rm ratio}$) may diverge from the true emissivity-weighted "line temperature" ($T_{\rm line}$) if there are unresolved [[temperature-fluctuations-astrophysics|Temperature fluctuations (astrophysics)]] within the observational aperture. Because the auroral line emissivity increases exponentially with temperature, temperature inhomogeneities will disproportionately weight the integrated spectrum toward the hottest gas. This effect systematically biases the inferred global $T_e$ high, subsequently driving the derived metallicity down [Cameron et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522L..89C/abstract), [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591..801K/abstract). The exact magnitude of this bias remains a subject of debate in the literature, with global galaxy simulations predicting significant fluctuation-driven offsets, while resolved integral field observations of some isolated H II regions exhibit high spatial homogeneity [Sattler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251202802S/abstract).

Beyond classical H II regions, the thermal structures of higher-temperature interstellar gas are mapped using all-sky far-ultraviolet cooling lines, such as C IV and O VI. These lines are used to characterize the temperature (approximately $1.9 \times 10^5$ K) and the filling fraction of transition-temperature gas permeating the Galactic disk [Jo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..243....9J/abstract).

## Historical Evolution of Nebular Thermometry

Early nebular astrophysics often modeled H II regions as isothermal plasmas with an assumed [[kinetic-temperature|Kinetic temperature]] of $\sim 10,000$ K. However, theoretical reassessments of heating and cooling balance demonstrated that temperatures could be significantly lower—often $\sim 6,000$ K in spiral arms—due to efficient fine-structure cooling by heavy elements [Burbidge et al. (1963)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1963ApJ...138..945B/abstract). This realization highlighted the need for empirical thermometry to accurately constrain chemical abundances. Early Fabry-Perot spectrophotometry successfully derived electron temperature distributions in diffuse nebulae like M8 and M20, finding core temperatures typically between 7,000 K and 12,000 K [Foukal et al. (1969)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1969Ap&SS...5..469F/abstract). Additionally, early spectroscopic studies extending into the outer Galaxy (up to 18 kpc) utilized both direct temperature measurements and photoionization models to identify a substantially flat nitrogen abundance gradient [Vilchez et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.280..720V/abstract).

Because optical observations of the inner Milky Way are heavily obscured by dust, early systematic mappings of the Galactic temperature distribution relied on *radio recombination lines* (RRLs) and broadband radio continuum surveys. By measuring the ratio of an RRL to the adjacent free-free continuum, observers could derive an *LTE temperature* ($T_e^*$) without the complication of interstellar extinction. Using this technique, early surveys identified a systematic increase in $T_e^*$ with increasing *Galactic radius*, which was interpreted as a reflection of a radial metallicity gradient where heavy-element cooling becomes less efficient in the metal-poor outer Galaxy [Churchwell et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975A&A....38..451C/abstract), [Churchwell et al. (1978)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1978A&A....70..719C/abstract). However, the methodology was not without controversy; analytical models of the era frequently assumed that H II regions were plane-parallel, isothermal, and homogeneous. It was soon shown that applying these simplifying assumptions to RRL emission could introduce artificial gradients in temperature and density, complicating the interpretation of early radio data [Lockman et al. (1978)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1978ApJ...222..153L/abstract). Additionally, surveys such as the GEM project provide high-sensitivity 2.3 GHz continuum maps to characterize large-scale synchrotron emission and transition-region properties in the Galactic interstellar medium [Tello et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...556A...1T/abstract). Complementary low-frequency absorption mapping at 74 MHz has been used to detect discrete H II regions against the Galactic synchrotron background, directly constraining the brightness temperature of cosmic-ray emission [Nord et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....132..242N/abstract). Note that upcoming space missions aim to reconstruct the [[galactic-electron-density-distribution|Galactic electron density distribution]] using ultralong-wavelength radio observations (<10 MHz), where free-free absorption becomes highly significant [Cong et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...940..180C/abstract).

As optical detector sensitivity improved, the focus shifted toward high-signal-to-noise spectroscopy and the measurement of faint, temperature-sensitive auroral lines, such as [O III] $\lambda4363$ and [N II] $\lambda5755$. This approach bypassed the model dependencies inherent in empirical strong-line diagnostics and quickly became the "gold standard" for [[direct-abundance-methods|Direct-abundance methods]] in both local and extragalactic H II regions [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591..801K/abstract). 

Crucially, the widespread application of this direct $T_e$ method forced a major revision of the historical abundance scale. Previously, empirical strong-line calibrations had suggested that the inner disks of spiral galaxies could reach extreme metallicities of $3$ to $4$ times the solar value. Direct $T_e$ measurements using [N II] $\lambda5755$ in metal-rich environments like the disk of M51 revealed that temperatures were significantly higher than photoionization models predicted, meaning the actual oxygen abundances were substantially lower than earlier estimates [Garnett et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...607L..21G/abstract), [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591..801K/abstract). 

Today, modern studies routinely combine RRLs and optical auroral lines to construct unified, multi-wavelength temperature gradients across the Milky Way and nearby galaxies [Deharveng et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000MNRAS.311..329D/abstract), [Balser et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...738...27B/abstract). While the direct method remains the benchmark, the literature still reflects ongoing tension regarding systematic uncertainties, particularly the extent to which unresolved [[temperature-fluctuations-astrophysics|Temperature fluctuations (astrophysics)]] within discrete H II regions might bias auroral-line temperatures upward, thereby artificially suppressing the derived metallicities [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591..801K/abstract).

## Diagnostic Ratios and Ionic Species

The direct determination of electron temperature ($T_e$) relies on the flux ratio of collisionally excited emission lines originating from a single ionic species. The classic diagnostic compares the intensity of a weak, high-excitation auroral line to that of lower-excitation strong nebular lines. While the total luminosity of these lines depends on the [[emission-measure|Emission measure]] of the region, their ratio is governed by the differential collisional excitation rates to their respective upper energy levels. Because these rates scale differently with the Maxwellian electron velocity distribution, the line ratio is highly sensitive to the [[kinetic-temperature|Kinetic temperature]] of the gas and largely independent of the [[galactic-electron-density-distribution|Galactic electron density distribution]] (provided the gas is below the critical density of the transitions). This multi-line technique is widely considered the "gold standard" for [[direct-abundance-methods|Direct-abundance methods]] [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591..801K/abstract), [Cameron et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522L..89C/abstract).

Because H II regions and planetary nebulae are highly stratified by radiation field intensity, no single temperature characterizes an entire nebula. To reconstruct a complete thermal profile, observers target specific ions that dominate in different ionization zones:
*   **High-ionization zone:** Traced by the [O III] $\lambda 4363$ auroral line measured relative to the $\lambda\lambda 4959, 5007$ nebular lines. Theoretical R-matrix calculations have established several ultraviolet and optical [O III] ratios (e.g., $2322\mathrm{\AA} / (1661\mathrm{\AA} + 1667\mathrm{\AA})$) as highly sensitive diagnostics for temperatures spanning 7,500 to 40,000 K [Keenan et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJ...319..403K/abstract).
*   **Intermediate-ionization zone:** Traced by the [S III] $\lambda 6312$ auroral line.
*   **Low-ionization zone:** Probed primarily by the [N II] $\lambda 5755$ auroral line relative to $\lambda\lambda 6548, 6584$, as well as [O II] auroral transitions at $\lambda\lambda 7320, 7330$.

Beyond standard H II regions, these line diagnostics are crucial in active galactic nuclei. In LINER galaxies like NGC 1052, [S III] line strengths have been used to distinguish between photoionization and shock-heating models as the primary excitation mechanism [Diaz et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985MNRAS.214P..41D/abstract). Furthermore, X-ray narrow-line regions in Seyfert galaxies like NGC 1068 exhibit emission measure distributions across a wide range of ionization states, from nitrogen to iron, consistent with photoionization originating from a hidden central source [Ogle et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003A&A...402..849O/abstract). In the same galaxy, the electron-scattering region is modeled as a dynamic wind driven by continuum heating from the central source, consistent with observed temperature and optical depth values [Krolik et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...308L..55K/abstract). Moreover, detailed spectroscopy of the powerful radio galaxy 3C 321 suggests that a mixture of optically thin and thick clouds best fits observed physical conditions, yielding a mean electron temperature of approximately 11,500 K [Robinson et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000MNRAS.317..922R/abstract).

Recently, the advent of JWST has extended this multi-species direct method to "Cosmic Noon" ($z > 2$), allowing the simultaneous mapping of temperatures across low, intermediate, and high ionization zones in the early universe [Welch et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240506631W/abstract), [Welch et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...33W/abstract).

In many observational campaigns, detecting auroral lines across all ionization zones is unfeasible due to their intrinsic faintness. Consequently, empirical relationships between different ionic temperatures are frequently employed. By organizing planetary nebulae and H II regions into a specific excitation class—often defined by strong-line ratios such as [O II]/[O III] and He II/He I—investigators can derive linear calibrations linking temperatures across zones, most notably between $T_e$([O III]) and $T_e$([N II]) [Martins et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...387.1074M/abstract). 

Despite the theoretical underpinning of photoionization models anchoring these relationships, significant tensions remain in the literature. Temperatures derived from [O III], [N II], and [S III] generally exhibit tight correlations with relative offsets consistent with theoretical expectations. However, $T_e$ derived from the [O II] $\lambda 7325$ complex often displays large scatter and correlates poorly with the temperatures of other species, pointing to unresolved physical or observational systematic errors [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591..801K/abstract). 

Furthermore, assumptions regarding the homogeneity of the thermal structure within these zones remain hotly debated. Disagreements over which internal temperature structure assumptions (and which combinations of ionic species) best represent the gas can radically alter the slopes of derived metallicity gradients in spatially resolved studies of galaxies, highlighting a major systematic uncertainty in extragalactic chemical abundance mapping [Cameron et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.3695C/abstract).

## The Direct Method vs. Strong-Line Calibrations

The precise calibration of the absolute gas-phase metallicity scale is one of the most prominent unresolved debates in extragalactic astrophysics. Direct electron temperature ($T_e$) measurements—often referred to as [[direct-abundance-methods|Direct-abundance methods]]—serve as the benchmark for determining the oxygen abundance ($\text{O/H}$) of H II regions and star-forming galaxies. However, a longstanding systematic discrepancy exists: the direct method yields metallicities that have historically been $0.2$ to $0.5$ dex lower than those derived from strong-line diagnostics calibrated via photoionization models [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591..801K/abstract), though recent comprehensive analyses of SDSS-IV MaNGA data measure a smaller median discrepancy of 0.09 dex [Peng et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250918770P/abstract).

Strong-line diagnostics are extensively utilized to infer abundances when temperature-sensitive auroral lines are too faint to detect. Because empirical strong-line calibrations heavily rely on photoionization models, they are sensitive to the underlying assumptions of those models. Historically, strong-line calibrations indicated that the inner disks of spiral galaxies could reach metallicities three to four times the solar value. However, direct $T_e$ measurements of low-excitation, metal-rich H II regions in galaxies such as M51 have revealed absolute abundances approximately a factor of 3 lower than model predictions [Garnett et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...607L..21G/abstract). Furthermore, integrated global galaxy spectra are subject to severe flux-weighting effects and contamination from diffuse ionized gas (DIG). The inclusion of DIG can systematically alter strong-line ratios and artificially elevate strong-line metallicity estimates by more than $0.3$ dex compared to the resolved H II regions within the same galaxy [Sanders et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...850..136S/abstract). Despite these offsets, comparison studies in galaxies like M101 have shown that specific strong-line techniques, such as the P-method, can provide closer agreement with direct electron temperature abundances than older empirical calibrations [Cedrés et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004A&A...422..511C/abstract).

Conversely, the direct method is not immune to systematic biases that could artificially depress abundance measurements. The most significant source of error is the potential presence of unresolved [[temperature-fluctuations-astrophysics|Temperature fluctuations (astrophysics)]] within the emitting volume. Because the emissivity of collisionally excited auroral lines scales exponentially with temperature, internal temperature inhomogeneities cause the observed $T_e$ to be heavily weighted toward the hottest, most highly ionized zones. Adopting this inflated temperature for the entire nebula leads to a systematic underestimation of the ionic abundances. Recent simulations suggest that correcting for these internal temperature structures could shift the normalization of the $T_e$-based mass-metallicity relation upward [Cameron et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522L..89C/abstract).

While neither method is perfectly calibrated, they possess different sensitivities to the physical conditions of the interstellar medium. The direct method robustly accounts for varying ionization states across different environments, whereas strong-line methods that rely on single-ionization diagnostics are highly susceptible to stochastic variations in the ionizing stellar continuum, which can introduce scatter of up to $\sim 0.4$ dex [Arora et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508.3290A/abstract). Ultimately, idealized one-dimensional photoionization models cannot fully capture the complexity of real star-forming regions [Peng et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250918770P/abstract), leaving the true absolute oxygen abundance scale likely situated between the direct $T_e$ values and the strong-line model predictions. Furthermore, benchmarking these nebular direct abundances against the Sun requires re-evaluating photospheric values using 3D granulation models and non-LTE effects to ensure consistent chemical evolution standards [Holweger et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AIPC..598...23H/abstract).

## Systematic Biases: Temperature Fluctuations and DIG

The derivation of gas-phase metallicities via [[direct-abundance-methods|Direct-abundance methods]] classically relies on the assumption of a homogeneous, isothermal emitting volume. In practice, direct electron temperature measurements can be subject to systematic biases driven by unresolved thermal inhomogeneities and multi-component emission, most notably [[temperature-fluctuations-astrophysics|Temperature fluctuations (astrophysics)]].

Because the emissivity of auroral transitions (such as [O III] $\lambda4363$) scales exponentially with the [[kinetic-temperature|Kinetic temperature]], hotter parcels of gas disproportionately dominate the integrated auroral line flux within an unresolved observation. Consequently, the observed *ratio temperature* ($T_{\rm ratio}$)—derived directly from the integrated auroral-to-nebular line ratios—systematically overestimates the volume- and density-weighted *line temperature* ($T_{\rm line}$) of the region [Cameron et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522L..89C/abstract). These unresolved temperature fluctuations, parameterized in the literature by the fractional mean-square temperature variation ($t^2$), cause metallicities derived from $T_{\rm ratio}$ to be systematically underestimated. Theoretical calibrations utilizing high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations suggest that correcting for these internal temperature fluctuations can shift the normalization of the derived mass-metallicity relation upwards by approximately $0.18$ dex in low-mass galaxies [Cameron et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522L..89C/abstract).

At galactic scales, direct temperature measurements are further complicated by DIG contamination. Extragalactic global or wide-aperture spectra capture an emission-weighted ensemble of both dense, star-forming H II regions and the widespread, low-density DIG. Because the DIG exhibits distinct strong-line ratios and physical conditions compared to classical Strömgren spheres, its inclusion alters the integrated spectrum. Due to *flux weighting* across a mixed population of varying metallicities and ionization states, integrated global spectra can yield effective electron temperatures $1000$--$3000$ K higher than the average of their constituent individual H II regions [Kobulnicky et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514..544K/abstract). Left uncorrected, the combination of DIG contamination and flux-weighting effects can bias global metallicity estimates low by $0.1$ dex to over $0.3$ dex [Kobulnicky et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514..544K/abstract), [Sanders et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...850..136S/abstract).

The literature remains conflicting regarding the scale and ubiquity of these biases. While global galaxy models and simulations predict substantial systematic offsets, highly resolved observations of local isolated H II regions challenge the assumption that strong internal fluctuations are universal. For instance, spatial mapping of the Trifid Nebula reveals a highly homogeneous electron temperature distribution, demonstrating no significant differences between the integrated and spatially resolved physical conditions [Sattler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251202802S/abstract). Furthermore, large-scale resolved surveys of nearby galaxies (such as the CHAOS survey in M33) show that the interstellar medium can be chemically well-mixed with minimal intrinsic dispersion. In such well-resolved studies, artificially inflated abundance dispersions are often not driven by $t^2$ fluctuations, but rather by systematic biases introduced when studies rely on a single $T_e$ measurement and empirical $T_e-T_e$ scaling relations to infer the temperatures of unmeasured ionization zones, an effect that can inflate abundance dispersions by up to $0.11$ dex [Rogers et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...939...44R/abstract).

## Spatially Resolved Mapping with Integral Field Spectroscopy

Modern integral field unit (IFU) spectroscopy, provided by instruments such as MUSE and the SDSS-V Local Volume Mapper (LVM), has revolutionized the application of [[direct-abundance-methods|Direct-abundance methods]] by enabling contiguous 2D mapping of electron temperatures ($T_e$) across entire galactic disks. These surveys have robustly measured negative radial metallicity gradients in nearby galaxies, though the literature is currently conflicting regarding the intrinsic scatter of these gradients. While some studies report significant local abundance variations of up to $0.3$ dex on sub-kiloparsec scales in galaxies like M31 [Sanders et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...758..133S/abstract), recent highly multiplexed observations of M33 suggest a remarkably well-mixed interstellar medium with a small intrinsic dispersion of just $\sim 0.04$ dex [Rogers et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...939...44R/abstract). Robustness tests of these spatial maps in galaxies like M81 indicate that while the direct method can introduce larger local dispersions due to the challenges of measuring faint lines, it remains the primary anchor for validating strong-line gradients [Arellano-Córdova et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2627A/abstract). It has been demonstrated that relying on empirical $T_e-T_e$ scaling relations for unmeasured ionization zones can artificially inflate the measured dispersion by up to $0.11$ dex [Rogers et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...939...44R/abstract), and overlooking internal temperature structures can drastically alter the inferred spatial gradient slopes entirely [Cameron et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.3695C/abstract).

On the scale of individual nebulae, ultra-high resolution IFU mapping of the Trifid Nebula indicates that isolated H II regions can be highly homogeneous, lacking significant internal gradients in $T_e$. This casts doubt on whether internal [[temperature-fluctuations-astrophysics|Temperature fluctuations (astrophysics)]] severely bias integrated measurements in such environments [Sattler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251202802S/abstract). Furthermore, deep echelle spectroscopy of the Magellanic Clouds has utilized multiple line ratios to determine that iron depletion levels in these H II regions are comparable to those found in the Milky Way, underscoring the method's precision in capturing local chemical variations [Domínguez-Guzmán et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018cagn.conf..151D/abstract). The effects of stochastic sampling of the stellar initial mass function in small IFU spaxels primarily degrade single-ionization empirical diagnostics; direct $T_e$-based methods utilizing multiple ionization states remain highly robust to these fluctuations, showing variations of $<0.1$ dex [Arora et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508.3290A/abstract). 

Beyond radial trends, IFU mapping reveals complex azimuthal variations. MUSE observations of NGC 1672 demonstrate systematic electron temperature variations across spiral arms, where $T_e$ reaches a minimum on the arm itself and peaks on the downstream side—a consequence of localized metallicity enhancements rather than artifactual strong-line calibration errors [Ho et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...885L..31H/abstract). To leverage vast IFU data sets where faint auroral lines remain undetected, novel approaches like the "charge-exchange method" have been developed. By modeling the $\mathrm{[O\,I]}\,\lambda 6300/\mathrm{H}\alpha$ ratio, this technique has allowed $T_e$ inference across thousands of H II regions in the PHANGS-MUSE survey, corroborating both radial gradients and azimuthal substructures [Kreckel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...667A..16K/abstract). However, recent analyses using stacked [N II] auroral lines found no systematic metallicity offsets between arm and interarm environments [Kreckel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250720744K/abstract).

Finally, spatially resolved $T_e$ mapping provides direct constraints on the galactic baryon cycle by simultaneously resolving the thermodynamics of the interstellar medium, metal-poor inflows, and metal-enriched outflows. For example, direct $T_e$-based mapping of the edge-on galaxy Markarian 1486 (from the DUVET survey) has revealed a distinct minor-axis temperature gradient. The outflowing gas is observed to be $\sim 0.20$ dex more metal-rich than the average disk interstellar medium, and over $0.80$ dex more enriched than the inflowing gas, offering a direct, quantitative trace of chemical feedback and mass assembly [Cameron et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...918L..16C/abstract).

## Direct Abundances at Cosmic Noon and Beyond

Historically, [[direct-abundance-methods|Direct-abundance methods]] utilizing the electron temperature ($T_e$) were strictly confined to the local universe. The extreme faintness of temperature-sensitive auroral lines, such as [O III] $\lambda4363$, rendered them largely undetectable in distant star-forming galaxies, forcing astronomers to rely on locally calibrated, empirical strong-line ratios. However, the advent of the *James Webb Space Telescope* (JWST), frequently combined with the magnification provided by gravitational lensing, has pushed direct $T_e$ measurements into "Cosmic Noon" ($z \sim 1-3$) and beyond.

Using JWST's NIRSpec instrument, astrophysicists can now detect an array of rest-frame optical and ultraviolet auroral lines in high-redshift galaxies. For instance, observations of the gravitationally lensed galaxy SGAS1723+34 at $z=1.33$ successfully detected [O III] $\lambda4363$, [S III] $\lambda6312$, and [O II] $\lambda\lambda7320, 7330$, enabling the simultaneous constraint of $T_e$ across multiple ionization zones [Welch et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240113046W/abstract). Similarly, deep spectroscopy of the Sunburst Arc, a highly magnified Lyman-continuum emitting galaxy at $z=2.37$, yielded detections of an unprecedented suite of auroral transitions, including [S II] $\lambda\lambda4070, 4076$ and [Ne III] $\lambda3343$ [Welch et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240506631W/abstract), [Welch et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...33W/abstract).

These robust temperature constraints allow for the derivation of gas-phase metallicities with a precision previously reserved for local H II regions. In the Sunburst Arc, researchers measured an absolute oxygen abundance of $12+\log(\text{O}/\text{H}) = 7.97 \pm 0.05$. Crucially, access to direct abundances across multiple species permits the evaluation of detailed chemical enrichment patterns in the early universe. While the relative abundances of sulfur, argon, neon, and iron in the Sunburst Arc closely match those of local low-metallicity environments, the galaxy exhibits an enhanced nitrogen abundance of $\log(\text{N}/\text{O}) = -0.65^{+0.16}_{-0.25}$, consistent with rapid, localized enrichment from Wolf-Rapet stars [Welch et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240506631W/abstract), [Welch et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...33W/abstract). Nitrogen-to-oxygen ratios in other lensed systems at $z \sim 1.3$ also appear broadly consistent with local scaling relations [Welch et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240113046W/abstract).

As sample sizes grow, direct $T_e$ measurements are beginning to reveal tensions between local chemical evolution models and the extreme environments of the very early universe. A recent survey of star-forming galaxies out to $z \sim 7.7$ utilized JWST and Keck spectroscopy to directly measure oxygen and argon abundances. While galaxies at $z \sim 1.3-3.4$ display chemical enrichment patterns consistent with models driven by core-collapse and Type Ia supernovae in the Milky Way, galaxies at $z > 3.6$ exhibit unexpectedly low $\log(\text{O}/\text{Ar})$ ratios [Bhattacharya et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240813396B/abstract). The literature suggests this high-redshift anomaly may be driven by rapid, intermittent star formation histories or alternative nucleosynthetic pathways that differ fundamentally from local paradigms [Bhattacharya et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240813396B/abstract). 

Ultimately, these high-redshift measurements represent a major paradigm shift in extragalactic astrophysics. The standard practice of relying on empirical strong-line calibrations—which often systematically overestimate local abundances compared to direct methods—can now be bypassed or directly recalibrated using the physical conditions of the early universe itself.

## Multi-wavelength Alternatives: Far-Infrared and Radio

The traditional optical direct method relies on auroral transitions, such as [O III] $\lambda 4363$, which are highly sensitive to the electron temperature $T_e$ and easily obscured by interstellar dust. This limits their utility in heavily extinguished environments, such as the inner Milky Way or dusty star-forming galaxies. To overcome these limitations, multi-wavelength approaches utilizing far-infrared (FIR) fine-structure lines and radio observations have been developed as temperature-insensitive alternatives.

Far-infrared fine-structure transitions, such as [O III] $88\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, [O III] $52\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and [N III] $57\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, arise from low-lying energy levels. Because their excitation potentials $\Delta E$ are significantly smaller than the characteristic thermal energy of the ionized gas ($k_B T_e \approx 0.86\,\mathrm{eV}$ for a $10^4\,\mathrm{K}$ plasma), the exponential Boltzmann factor in their collisional excitation rate approaches unity:
$$ j \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{T_e}} \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta E}{k_B T_e}\right) \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{T_e}} $$
Consequently, the emissivities of these FIR lines are practically independent of the [[kinetic-temperature|Kinetic temperature]] of the gas. This lack of temperature sensitivity allows FIR lines to accurately trace ionic abundances while avoiding the systematic underestimations caused by unresolved [[temperature-fluctuations-astrophysics|Temperature fluctuations (astrophysics)]] [Croxall et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777...96C/abstract). The FLAMES atlas provides a comprehensive catalog of far-infrared fine-structure lines, validating their widespread use as temperature-insensitive tracers of metallicity and radiation field hardness across cosmic time [Peng et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250710702P/abstract). Line ratios such as [N III] $57\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ / [O III] $52\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ serve as robust diagnostics for the N/O abundance ratio, provided that corrections for the radiation field hardness (e.g., using [Ne III]/[Ne II] ratios) are applied [Peng et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908..166P/abstract).

At longer wavelengths, Radio Recombination Lines (RRLs) and free-free continuum emission are entirely immune to dust extinction. The ratio of an RRL flux to the underlying free-free continuum provides a direct, LTE-based measurement of $T_e$, a technique extensively used to map temperature and metallicity gradients across the Galactic disk [Balser et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...738...27B/abstract). At very low radio frequencies ($\nu < 200$ MHz), the thermal electron content can also be probed by analyzing the absorption of background point sources, providing a cloud-based model of the warm interstellar medium [Jones et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.3298J/abstract).

By combining FIR and radio diagnostics, it is possible to derive absolute gas-phase abundances in completely dust-obscured regions. The Far-Infrared Abundance (FIRA) method normalizes temperature-insensitive FIR fine-structure fluxes against radio free-free emission or hydrogen RRLs to compute absolute oxygen abundances. When applied to local spiral galaxies such as M101, the FIRA method yields abundance gradients consistent with those derived from optical direct methods [Lamarche et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...925..194L/abstract). Similarly, coupling the [N II] $205\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ fine-structure line with RRLs allows researchers to map electron densities and nitrogen abundances deep into the inner Galaxy, where optical studies are impossible [Pineda et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...886....1P/abstract).

Despite their advantages, these multi-wavelength alternatives may probe systematically different physical environments than their optical counterparts. The literature suggests a potential tension wherein FIR methods are biased toward younger, denser H II regions, whereas optical strong-line and auroral methods are more strongly weighted toward older H II regions and the surrounding diffuse ionized gas [Peng et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908..166P/abstract).

## Thermal States in Galaxy Clusters and Hot Plasmas

The intracluster medium (ICM) is a hot, fully ionized plasma where electron temperatures are exceptionally high, typically ranging from $10^7$ to $10^8$ K. The determination of the electron temperature, $T_e$, in these environments is conventionally performed via X-ray bremsstrahlung continuum and line emission, yielding the [[x-ray-temperature|X-ray temperature]]. However, an independent and increasingly precise probe of the ICM thermal state is provided by the thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect. 

Because electrons in the hot ICM are mildly relativistic, the standard SZ spectrum must be modified with temperature-dependent relativistic corrections. Exploiting the frequency dependence of these corrections across millimeter and submillimeter wavelengths (e.g., 30 to 545 GHz) provides a direct, "spectroscopic" measurement of the electron temperature [Colafrancesco et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...520A..31C/abstract), [Coulton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241019046C/abstract). Using this methodology, the Atacama Cosmology Telescope (ACT) measured an average temperature for a sample of Compton-$y$ selected clusters of:
$$ \langle T_e \rangle = 8.5 \pm 2.4 \text{ keV} $$
with additional systematic uncertainties dominated by passband calibration [Coulton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241019046C/abstract). Moreover, the one-point probability distribution function (PDF) of the thermal SZ decrement, as measured by the Atacama Cosmology Telescope, provides a sensitive probe of cluster gas physics and the amplitude of matter density perturbations [Hill et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1411.8004H/abstract).

Temperatures derived from the SZ effect are systematically hotter than corresponding X-ray temperatures. Because the SZ signal is weighted by electron pressure, it is less sensitive to the dense, cool-gas substructures that strongly bias X-ray emission measurements. Consequently, the SZ temperature tracks the global properties of the cluster more faithfully, remaining within roughly $10\%$ of the cluster's virial temperature [Kay et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.386.2110K/abstract). Joint fitting of X-ray and SZ data can map the full thermodynamic profile of the cluster [Castagna et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...639A..73C/abstract). Separately, independent X-ray studies have revealed phenomena such as thermal conduction balancing radiative cooling at approximately $30\%$ of the Spitzer conductivity limit [Zakamska et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...582..162Z/abstract). Indeed, analysis of Chandra data suggests that in even hotter clusters ($>5$ keV), the energy balance between radiative cooling and electron conduction requires plasma conductivity close to the full Spitzer value [Voigt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.347.1130V/abstract).

Extracting precise X-ray temperatures requires advanced modeling; fast temperature mapping techniques for X-ray clusters utilize Taylor expansions to estimate thermal states even in regions with low photon statistics [Churazov et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...471..673C/abstract). In large-scale surveys where cluster redshifts are unavailable, apparent temperatures derived from multi-band X-ray data can be combined with other X-ray information to constrain cluster redshifts rather than the temperature function itself [Liddle et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.325..875L/abstract). Additionally, model-independent deprojection routines, such as DSDEPROJ, are used to reconstruct radial temperature and abundance profiles in the hot ICM without introducing the oscillating artifacts common to model-dependent methods [Russell et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.390.1207R/abstract). Furthermore, failure to account for radial temperature profiles in SZ observations can systematically shift derived cluster peculiar velocities by $10\%$--$20\%$ if not corrected through deprojection techniques [Hansen et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.351L...5H/abstract).

While macroscopic X-ray and SZ analyses conventionally assume that the electron velocity distribution follows a standard relativistic Maxwellian (the Jüttner distribution) to define the [[kinetic-temperature|Kinetic temperature]], there is an active debate regarding the presence of non-thermal or modified Jüttner distributions in the ICM [Molnar et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..143M/abstract). High-precision SZ measurements with errors on the order of $1\%$ are required to robustly distinguish between these competing relativistic kinetic theories [Molnar et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..143M/abstract).

The presence of suprathermal electron populations in the ICM, as well as in active galactic nuclei (AGN) and the solar corona, significantly alters spectral line emissivities. When a purely thermal Maxwellian distribution is incorrectly assumed in such environments, it generally leads to a systematic overestimation of metal abundances. This effect has been documented for argon in the HCG 62 group and iron in the Centaurus cluster [Prokhorov et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...508...69P/abstract). The flux ratio of highly ionized iron lines, specifically Fe XXV to Fe XXVI, serves as a highly sensitive diagnostic of these suprathermal tails, particularly in lower-temperature clusters like Abell 2199 [Prokhorov et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...496...25P/abstract). Similarly, in extreme environments such as the accretion flows and jets around supermassive black holes (e.g., M87), electron temperatures cannot be accurately described by simple thermal prescriptions. Instead, accurate modeling requires self-consistent microscopic plasma physics that accounts for turbulent magnetic reconnection and non-thermal particle acceleration [Cruz-Osorio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251214835C/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While direct electron temperature ($T_e$) measurements are widely considered the "gold standard" for determining gas-phase metallicities, several unresolved physical and observational challenges remain. Addressing these issues is a primary focus for upcoming campaigns utilizing the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and next-generation Extremely Large Telescopes (ELTs).

**The Abundance Discrepancy**
A persistent open question in nebular astrophysics is the origin and universality of the abundance discrepancy. As outlined above, oxygen abundances derived via [[direct-abundance-methods|Direct-abundance methods]] are systematically lower than those inferred from empirical strong-line calibrations [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591..801K/abstract). While theoretical models invoking unresolved [[temperature-fluctuations-astrophysics|Temperature fluctuations (astrophysics)]] ($t^2$) within H II regions can mathematically reconcile some of these differences by shifting the inferred mass-metallicity relation upwards [Cameron et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522L..89C/abstract), conflicting evidence from highly resolved spatial studies suggests that thermal homogeneity may hold on small scales [Sattler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251202802S/abstract). Determining whether this discrepancy is dominated by true internal temperature fluctuations or alternative observational biases remains a major goal.

**Stochasticity at Small Scales**
As integral field unit (IFU) surveys achieve spatial resolutions of $\lesssim 100$ pc, the impact of stellar population stochasticity on emission line diagnostics has emerged as a novel challenge. At these scales, the incomplete sampling of the stellar initial mass function—particularly the discrete presence or absence of hot Wolf-Rayet stars—creates stochastic fluctuations in the local ionizing continuum. While this stochasticity can induce scatter of up to $\sim 0.4$ dex in metallicities derived from single-ionization state diagnostics, $T_e$-based methods that measure multiple ionization states are highly robust, with stochastic variations limited to $< 0.1$ dex [Arora et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508.3290A/abstract). Quantifying these effects is necessary for optimizing the observing strategies of future ELT surveys, which will map heavily resolved H II regions in distant galaxies.

**High-Redshift Chemical Evolution**
The extension of direct $T_e$ measurements to the early universe via JWST is opening new avenues for testing chemical evolution models. Observations of gravitationally lensed galaxies are revealing enrichment patterns at "Cosmic Dawn" that challenge local paradigms. For example, while star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 1$--$3.4$ exhibit chemical patterns consistent with Milky Way-like enrichment, systems at $z > 3.6$ show anomalously low $\log(\text{O/Ar})$ abundance ratios [Bhattacharya et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240813396B/abstract). Future theoretical work must reconcile these direct measurements with chemical evolution models by exploring whether these ratios are driven by rapid, intermittent star formation or unique high-redshift nucleosynthetic yields.

**Non-Thermal Distributions in the ICM**
Beyond star-forming galaxies, open questions remain regarding the precise [[kinetic-temperature|Kinetic temperature]] and [[x-ray-temperature|X-ray temperature]] of the intracluster medium (ICM). Relativistic corrections to the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect provide an independent measure of the ICM temperature, but the derived values depend heavily on the assumed electron velocity distribution. It is currently debated whether the standard Jüttner distribution or modified non-thermal distributions (which account for suprathermal electron tails) best describe the plasma. Upcoming high-precision SZ surveys aim to reach the $\sim 1\%$ precision required to empirically distinguish between these competing relativistic kinetic theories [Molnar et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..143M/abstract).
