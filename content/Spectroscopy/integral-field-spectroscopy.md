**Integral field spectroscopy (IFS)** is a transformative observational technique in [[astrophysical-spectroscopy|Astrophysical spectroscopy]] that captures both spatial and spectral information simultaneously. By dividing a continuous two-dimensional field of view into discrete spatial pixels—known as [[spaxel|Spaxel]]s—an integral field unit (IFU) records an independent spectrum for each localized region of an extended source. This produces a three-dimensional data cube containing two spatial dimensions and one spectral dimension ($x, y, \lambda$). Historically, astronomers studying extended objects relied heavily on [[long-slit-spectroscopy|Long-slit spectroscopy]], which maps only a single one-dimensional slice across a target, or [[integrated-light-spectroscopy|Integrated light spectroscopy]], which captures the total light within a single fiber but frequently introduces severe aperture biases [Sánchez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...538A...8S/abstract). IFS overcomes these limitations by providing a continuous, spatially resolved map, allowing researchers to accurately trace the internal dynamics, chemical environments, and asymmetric structures of galaxies and nebulae without conflating distinct physical regions.

Over the past two decades, the deployment of highly multiplexed IFU instruments has revolutionized [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]]. Major [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Galaxy Surveys and Catalogs]] in the local Universe, such as the [[califa-survey|CALIFA survey]], the SAMI survey, and the [[manga-survey|MaNGA survey]], have transitioned the field from single-fiber observations to statistically robust population studies of thousands of fully resolved galaxies [Allen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.1567A/abstract). By decomposing the spectrum within each spaxel, researchers can extract maps of [[spatially-resolved-stellar-populations|Spatially resolved stellar populations]] and chemical abundance gradients. This has yielded definitive evidence that most massive disk galaxies assemble via "inside-out" growth, where older, metal-rich stars populate the central bulge while younger populations dominate the extended disk [Delgado et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..300D/abstract), a process vividly mapped in individual targets like NGC 628 [Sánchez et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410..313S/abstract). Furthermore, detailed IFS kinematic mapping has fundamentally redefined the classification of early-type galaxies. Moving beyond simple morphological distinctions, early-type systems are now primarily categorized by their internal stellar kinematics into "fast rotators"—quenched, rotation-supported disks—and "slow rotators"—massive, triaxial systems forged through gas-poor mergers [McDermid et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006NewAR..49..521M/abstract), [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract).

Today, the frontiers of 3D spectroscopy are expanding into the distant Universe. Advanced instruments like the [[muse-instrument|MUSE (instrument)]] and the space-based [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] are driving a new era of [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|High-Redshift Galaxy Observations]], enabling astronomers to resolve the kinematic structures and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback mechanisms of assembling galaxies at "cosmic noon." Despite these advances, significant open questions remain. Researchers are actively debating the physical origins of the highly elevated gas turbulence observed in high-redshift star-forming disks [Glazebrook et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PASA...30...56G/abstract), the precise mechanisms responsible for creating kinematically distinct cores, and the complex interplay between sub-kiloparsec local scaling relations and global galaxy properties. Addressing these tensions relies heavily on the development of sophisticated machine learning algorithms for spectral decomposition, as well as the anticipated arrival of next-generation wide-field spectroscopic facilities.

## Fundamental Principles and the Integral Field Data Cube

Integral field spectroscopy overcomes the spatial limitations of traditional 1D slits and single-fiber surveys by simultaneously capturing spectral information across a continuous two-dimensional plane. This enables the direct mapping of extended sources, allowing observers to resolve asymmetric structures, complex large-scale flows, and decoupled kinematic components that would otherwise be missed or conflated [de Zeeuw et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASPC..291..205D/abstract), [Becker et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AN....325..155B/abstract).

The fundamental output of an integral field unit (IFU) is a three-dimensional data structure known as a data cube. This structure maps the two spatial dimensions (typically denoted as $x$ and $y$) against one spectral dimension ($\lambda$). The spatial plane is divided into discrete spatial pixels, or [[spaxel|Spaxel]]s. Each spaxel within the data cube contains a complete, independent spectrum corresponding to a specific line of sight through the observed target [Scott et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.2299S/abstract). Depending on the sampling strategy of the instrument, multiple exposures can be dithered and combined to ensure a 100% spatial filling factor, generating a continuous and finely sampled data cube across the targeted area [Sánchez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...538A...8S/abstract). 

The physical capabilities of any IFS observation are strictly governed by the instrument's field of view and its spectral resolution, $R = \lambda / \Delta\lambda$. The field of view dictates the spatial extent over which an extended source can be mapped in a single pointing. For example, the PPAK IFU utilized in the [[califa-survey|CALIFA survey]] employs a hexagonal field of view of $\sim 1.3 \text{ arcmin}^2$ to capture the full optical extent of nearby galaxies [Sánchez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...538A...8S/abstract). Meanwhile, the spectral resolution determines the ability to distinguish closely spaced spectral features and measure the internal velocity dispersion, $\sigma$, of kinematic components. High spectral resolution is critical for applying spectral decomposition techniques that separate multiple overlapping components within a single line of sight—such as disentangling broad, shock-excited gas outflows from narrow star-forming emission within individual spaxels [Ho et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.3894H/abstract), [Rhea et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923..169R/abstract).

From the assembled data cube, observers can collapse the spectral dimension over specific wavelength intervals to synthesize broad-band or narrow-band images perfectly aligned with the spectral data. More importantly, robust fitting algorithms can be applied spaxel-by-spaxel to extract two-dimensional maps of physical parameters. By analyzing the continuum and emission lines across the entire data cube, researchers can reconstruct spatially resolved maps of stellar kinematics, gas ionization conditions, and chemical abundance gradients [Sánchez et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...538A...8S/abstract), [Sánchez et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...594A..36S/abstract). This multi-dimensional approach effectively bridges the gap between spatial imaging and spectroscopy, providing the foundational data structure required for resolved studies of astrophysical sources.

## Historical Development: From Long-Slit to 3D Spectroscopy

Historically, the standard method for extracting spatially resolved kinematics and abundances was [[long-slit-spectroscopy|Long-slit spectroscopy]]. While effective for measuring large-scale flows such as a symmetric disk's rotation curve, $V_{\text{rot}}(R)$, 1D slits struggle to separate closely packed point sources from complex, non-uniform galaxy backgrounds [Becker et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AN....325..155B/abstract). To observe at large galactocentric radii beyond the typically small fields of view of early IFUs, researchers also developed hybrid multislit techniques like SuperSKiMS to reconstruct 2D kinematics [Pastorello et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PASA...33...35P/abstract).

Before the widespread adoption of dense fiber arrays and image slicers, 2D spatial mapping often relied on [[fabry-perot-spectroscopy|Fabry-Perot spectroscopy]]. Fabry-Perot interferometers successfully mapped local variations in gas velocity dispersion, allowing observers to measure the expansion velocities of ionized-gas superbubbles across entire galactic disks [Smirnov-Pinchukov et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AstBu..76..367S/abstract). However, this technique required stepping through discrete wavelengths sequentially, significantly limiting the simultaneous spectral range.

The true paradigm shift arrived with the technological evolution of IFUs, which allowed for the simultaneous capture of a complete 3D data cube $(x, y, \lambda)$. This 2D spatial coverage proved essential for unraveling complex internal dynamics, such as decoupled kinematic components, asymmetric structures, and kinematically distinct cores [de Zeeuw et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASPC..291..205D/abstract), [McDermid et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006NewAR..49..521M/abstract). Early pioneers in the field demonstrated the necessity of such instruments; for instance, the TIGER integral field spectrograph was used in early efforts to disentangle the low- and high-velocity emission line systems in the cD galaxy NGC 1275 at 0.8-arcsecond resolution [Ferruit et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997NewA....2..345F/abstract). A foundational effort in later spectroscopic mosaicking was the PINGS project, which provided continuous coverage of the entire optical disks of nearby galaxies [Rosales-Ortega et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.405..735R/abstract). Extending from this foundation, wide-field monolithic IFUs enabled large baseline studies like the [[califa-survey|CALIFA survey]] using the PPAK instrument, which definitively confirmed the "inside-out" growth model for massive disks [Sánchez et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...538A...8S/abstract), [Delgado et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..300D/abstract). 

As IFU technology matured, the field's focus shifted toward massive multiplexing. By coupling deployable IFU fiber bundles with multi-object spectrographs, astronomers could observe dozens of spatially resolved galaxies simultaneously, an approach pioneered by the SAMI Galaxy Survey [Allen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.1567A/abstract). This technological evolution is culminating in proposed next-generation facilities like the [[wst|WST]], which intends to pair a giant $3 \times 3$ arcminute monolithic IFU with a multi-object spectrograph capable of a 20,000-target multiplex [Mainieri et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240305398M/abstract), [Bacon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240512518B/abstract).

Today, state-of-the-art instruments like the [[muse-instrument|MUSE (instrument)]] on the Very Large Telescope provide continuous 1 arcminute fields that dominate ground-based resolved studies [Walsh et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Galax...8...31W/abstract). In space, the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] has expanded 3D spectroscopy into the infrared. Beyond its dedicated IFU hardware, JWST observers have developed innovative multiplexing strategies, such as the MSA-3D program. By employing a "slit-stepping" technique with the [[nirspec|NIRSpec]] microshutter array, astronomers can synthesize IFU-like data cubes for up to 43 galaxies simultaneously at $z \sim 1$, achieving an observational efficiency $>15$ times greater than JWST's standard IFU mode [Barišić et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240808350B/abstract), [Barišić et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983..139B/abstract).

## Spatially Resolved Stellar Populations and Star Formation Histories

The transition from single-fiber or [[integrated-light-spectroscopy|Integrated light spectroscopy]] to IFS has fundamentally resolved the aperture biases that previously limited galaxy evolution studies. By spatially resolving continuum emission and stellar absorption features, IFS enables the detailed mapping of [[spatially-resolved-stellar-populations|Spatially resolved stellar populations]] across complex galactic structures [Sánchez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...538A...8S/abstract). Moreover, mapping chemical signatures such as metallicity and alpha-enhancement enables astronomers to serendipitously identify co-spatial dwarf satellites hidden within the luminous disks of massive host galaxies [Somawanshi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250303242S/abstract).

By applying stellar population synthesis models to 3D datacubes, astronomers can reconstruct a spatially resolved star formation history for a given galaxy. Data from extensive local programs demonstrate that most massive disk galaxies assemble via inside-out growth [Delgado et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..300D/abstract). In this paradigm, older, more metal-rich stars are concentrated in the central bulge, while younger stellar populations and active star formation dominate the extended disk [Sánchez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410..313S/abstract), [Sánchez-Blázquez et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.437.1534S/abstract). 

IFS is particularly adept at decoupling the evolutionary trajectories of individual galactic components, such as bulges, bars, and disks. Observations indicate that galactic bars frequently drive gas into the nuclear regions, rejuvenating the center and triggering the growth of pseudobulges. This is evidenced by a central "turnover" in age tracers, such as a drop in the $4000 \ \mathrm{\AA}$ break and an upturn in $\mathrm{H}\delta$ absorption, indicating recent star formation within the past $1-2 \text{ Gyr}$ [Lin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838..105L/abstract). However, the literature reveals complexity in this process: only about half of the barred galaxies in the sample exhibit these younger central populations, although the mechanism remains an important channel for pseudobulge growth [Lin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838..105L/abstract). Conversely, as galaxies transition below the star-forming main sequence, IFS maps reveal that quenching also proceeds inside-out, progressively suppressing star formation in massive bulges and disks alike [Catalán-Torrecilla et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848...87C/abstract), [Mun et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.5072M/abstract).

## Gas Kinematics, Chemical Enrichment, and the Interstellar Medium

By mapping the two-dimensional velocity fields and emission line ratios of galaxies, integral field spectroscopy provides a comprehensive view of the physical conditions within the interstellar medium. Spectral decomposition techniques applied to individual [[spaxel|Spaxel]] data allow astronomers to isolate distinct kinematic components, separating quiescent rotation from turbulent flows and shock excitation [Ho et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Ap&SS.361..280H/abstract), [Ho et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.3894H/abstract).

**Gas Kinematics and Turbulence**
Observations of ionized gas kinematics reveal a stark contrast between the local Universe and earlier cosmic epochs. At $z \sim 1$, star-forming galaxies are generally rotationally supported but possess significantly higher gas fractions ($f_{\rm gas} \sim 35\%$) and exhibit much higher velocity dispersions than local disks [Stott et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457.1888S/abstract). Understanding the origin of this elevated turbulence—whether driven by stellar feedback or by gravitational instabilities—has become a central pursuit of high-redshift IFS studies [Glazebrook et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PASA...30...56G/abstract), [Stott et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457.1888S/abstract). Within more complex local systems, high-resolution kinematic mapping can directly peel back an object's dynamical history. For example, observations from the SOSIMPLE survey of the galaxy NGC 7135 reveal counter-rotation of gas and asymmetric chemical distributions, allowing researchers to accurately distinguish native stars from mergers occurring 6 to 10 Gyr ago [Davison et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502.2296D/abstract).

**Chemical Abundances and Metallicity Gradients**
Mapping oxygen and nitrogen abundances across galaxy disks yields crucial insights into their chemical enrichment histories. Broadly, large local surveys such as the [[califa-survey|CALIFA survey]] demonstrate that most massive disk galaxies exhibit a negative radial metallicity gradient, which is a hallmark of the "inside-out" mass assembly framework [Sánchez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410..313S/abstract), [Valé et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250708633V/abstract). 

However, high-resolution IFS reveals that these gradients are frequently more complex than a simple linear decline. The steepness is mass-dependent, with a transition in enrichment behavior identified at a stellar mass of $\log(M_*/M_{\odot}) \approx 9.5$ [Valé et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250708633V/abstract). Multi-modal abundance gradients have been well-documented; for instance, NGC 628 displays a flat central metallicity, a steep decline across the main disk, and a shallow gradient in the outer regions [Rosales-Ortega et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.2439R/abstract), [Sánchez-Blázquez et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.437.1534S/abstract). Anomalous flattening in the outer disk ($r > 4.4$ kpc) of NGC 5668 directly challenges standard chemical evolution models, suggesting that secondary mechanisms—such as radial gas transfer induced by a galactic bar—can actively reshape a galaxy's enrichment profile [Marino et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...754...61M/abstract). On sub-kiloparsec scales, variations in local metal abundances correlate strongly with the local ionization parameter, demonstrating that localized star-formation-driven self-enrichment actively competes with the homogenizing effects of spiral density waves [Grasha et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221106005G/abstract), [Grasha et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IAUS..373..234G/abstract). Robustly measuring these abundances requires separating emission from discrete H II regions from the pervasive diffuse ionized gas, a process recently applied to catalogs of tens of thousands of extragalactic star-forming regions using the [[muse-instrument|MUSE (instrument)]] [Lugo-Aranda et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.6099L/abstract).

**Outflows and Feedback**
IFS is exceptionally sensitive to large-scale outflows driven by active galactic nuclei (AGN) and starbursts. IFS has also been applied to compact, vigorously star-forming 'Green Pea' galaxies to carefully disentangle disc-like rotation from strong outflows and turbulent mixing layers [Bosch et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.1787B/abstract). Furthermore, in the cometary starburst galaxy NGC 4861, PMAS IFU observations identify broad He II emission consistent with several hundred Wolf-Rayet stars and evidence of a bimodal star formation history fuelled by massive gas inflows [Roche et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523..270R/abstract). By isolating broad, highly blueshifted emission lines, researchers can trace multi-phase gas outflows extending up to $15$ kpc in luminous quasars like PDS 456 [Seebeck et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976..240S/abstract). In the narrow-line regions of nearby AGN, mass outflow rates can exceed the central black hole's accretion rate by factors of 10 to 100, indicating that significant amounts of gas are entrained from the galaxy plane [Storchi-Bergmann et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AIPC.1201...88S/abstract). 

It often remains difficult, however, to distinguish cleanly between starburst-driven and AGN-driven outflows. Many dusty systems previously classified as pure starbursts (e.g., OH Megamaser galaxies) have been shown by IFS to harbor faint, heavily obscured AGN that actively drive plasma ejections [Hekatelyne et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.2632H/abstract). Regardless of the driving mechanism, the interplay between these feedback processes and the available molecular gas dictates the efficiency of future star formation, with recent optical and millimeter synergies demonstrating that the correlation between ${\rm H_2}$ surface density and star formation rate surface density is the tightest scaling relation in the local interstellar medium [Wong et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271...35W/abstract). Combining IFS from CALIFA and CO maps from the EDGE survey confirms that local scaling relations between stellar mass, molecular gas, and star formation rate hold across a wide range of galactic scales [Sánchez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IAUS..373....3S/abstract), allowing for the precise characterization of the local molecular gas environments of different supernova types [Galbany et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468..628G/abstract).

## Nuclear Environments and AGN Feedback

Integral field spectroscopy has fundamentally transformed the study of galactic nuclei by spatially resolving the complex interplay of gas and stellar dynamics in these dense environments. By combining high-resolution spatial mapping—often aided by adaptive optics or space-based platforms like the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]]—with detailed spectral coverage, IFS provides a comprehensive view of the feeding and feedback mechanisms associated with an active galactic nucleus. Historically, early observations with the SPIFFI IFU on the Very Large Telescope pioneered this field by enabling the study of the stellar dynamics within the Galactic Centre and the molecular emission of ultraluminous infrared mergers like NGC 6240 [Eisenhauer et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Msngr.113...17E/abstract).

A primary application of nuclear IFS is the determination of central black hole masses. By mapping the kinematics of stars and gas within the gravitational sphere of influence of the nucleus, IFS allows for the derivation of intrinsic dynamical structures and the precise measurement of supermassive black hole masses [de Zeeuw et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASPC..291..205D/abstract). For example, near-infrared IFS observations of dust-obscured galaxies at $z \sim 1.5$ have been used to place lower limits on black hole masses of $(1-9) \times 10^8 M_{\odot}$ by isolating the nuclear emission from extended star-forming regions [Melbourne et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AJ....141..141M/abstract). Additionally, IFS has been instrumental in characterizing dual supermassive black hole systems; while spatially resolved double-peaked broad emission lines can be a signature of potential binary AGNs separated by just hundreds of parsecs, they are frequently degenerate with other kinematic structures like rotating disk emitters, requiring careful interpretation [Kim et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...894..126K/abstract).

To trigger and sustain AGN activity, gas must be transported from the host galaxy into the nuclear region. High-resolution IFS tracks these feeding and feedback cycles by mapping multi-phase gas kinematics. In many nearby active systems, near-infrared IFS reveals that the hot molecular gas (H$_2$) is dominated by circular rotation within a nuclear disk, tracing the reservoir that actively feeds the AGN [Murphy et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AJ....120.1675M/abstract), [Storchi-Bergmann et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AIPC.1201...88S/abstract). Conversely, ionized gas kinematics frequently exhibit decoupled, non-circular motions indicative of AGN feedback. Within the narrow-line region (NLR), mass outflow rates are measured between $10^{-2}$ and $1 M_{\odot} \text{ yr}^{-1}$. Remarkably, these outflow rates are 10 to 100 times higher than the concurrent AGN accretion rates, demonstrating that the bulk of the outflowing mass consists of interstellar gas entrained by the AGN wind, carrying an average kinetic power of approximately $10^{-4}$ times the bolometric luminosity [Storchi-Bergmann et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AIPC.1201...88S/abstract). Multi-epoch MEGARA and MaNGA IFU observations of the 'changing-look' AGN Mrk 883 show broad-line variability that is disconnected from the continuum, potentially driven by these powerful polar ionized winds rather than variations in accretion alone [Benítez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Univ...10...21B/abstract).

In extreme systems, the spatial extent of AGN feedback is immense. Combined optical ([[muse-instrument|MUSE (instrument)]]) and mid-infrared IFS of the highly luminous local quasar PDS 456 ($L_{\text{bol}} \sim 10^{47} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$) has mapped multiphase gas outflows extending up to 15 kpc from the central source, with blueshifted velocities reaching $-500 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Seebeck et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976..240S/abstract). Similar high-velocity gas flows, with peak-to-peak amplitudes exceeding $2.4 \times 10^3 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ , have been observed in local ultraluminous infrared galaxies [Colina et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...527L..13C/abstract).

Despite these observational successes, interpreting nuclear outflows remains subject to active debate in the literature. In systems lacking a dominant, unobscured AGN, it is notoriously difficult to unambiguously distinguish between AGN-driven outflows and those driven by intense central starbursts [Ho et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.3894H/abstract). This tension is particularly acute at higher redshifts where spatial resolution is limited and spectral blending occurs. However, local IFS studies have helped resolve this ambiguity in several nearby galaxies by utilizing diagnostic line ratios and precise kinematic mapping to uncover faint, heavily obscured AGNs embedded within regions previously classified as pure starbursts [Hekatelyne et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.2632H/abstract).

## High-Redshift Observations and Gravitational Lensing

The application of integral field spectroscopy to [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|High-Redshift Galaxy Observations]] has transformed our understanding of galaxy assembly during 'cosmic noon' ($z \sim 1-3$), the epoch corresponding to the peak of the cosmic star formation history. Taking advantage of 8-to-10-meter class telescopes and adaptive optics, modern surveys have resolved the 2D kinematics and chemical properties of young galaxies, revealing a population dominated by highly gas-rich, turbulent disks [Glazebrook et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PASA...30...56G/abstract). To further combine datasets, the MUSE Ultra Deep Field (MUDF) provides a stereoscopic view of gas and galaxies across 10 billion years by combining deep MUSE spectroscopy with HST grism data for thousands of sources, allowing for cross-epoch investigations [Revalski et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...40R/abstract).

At $z \sim 1$, observations indicate that while the majority of star-forming galaxies are already supported by ordered rotation, they exhibit significantly higher turbulence and molecular gas fractions ($f_{\rm gas} \sim 35\%$) than their local counterparts. The [[kross-survey|KROSS survey]] finds no strong correlation between galaxy-averaged velocity dispersion and total star formation rate, suggesting that gravitational instabilities—driven by the ubiquity of high gas fractions—may be the primary source of turbulence in these early disks, rather than feedback from intense star formation [Stott et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457.1888S/abstract). Furthermore, dynamical modeling of these assembling galaxies yields an average dark matter to total mass fraction of $65 \pm 12\%$ within $2.2 r_e$ (roughly $9.5$ kpc) at $z \sim 1$ [Stott et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457.1888S/abstract).

To push spatial resolution down to sub-kiloparsec scales at high redshift, observers frequently rely on gravitational lensing or specifically targeting dense galaxy cluster cores. For example, integral field observations of the Abell 2218 cluster core with the PPAK module detected dozens of cluster-member galaxies, allowing for a combined spectroscopic and morphological study of their evolution [Sanchez et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.12550S/abstract). Observing strongly lensed galaxies with integral field units provides highly magnified views of internal galaxy structures, though it requires complex modeling to disentangle intrinsic kinematics from the lensing distortion. Recent advancements in pixel-based reconstruction demonstrate that applying 3D regularization across an entire spectral data cube—rather than standard 2D regularization on separate velocity channels—yields significantly more accurate recoveries of both intensity and velocity structures, with more uniform noise properties [Young et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929....6Y/abstract). Lensed targets at $1.3 < z < 4.2$ are currently a primary focus of the [[templates|TEMPLATES]] program using the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] to map the spatial variation of star formation and attenuation with unprecedented sensitivity [Rigby et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231210465R/abstract).

The advent of the JWST has further expanded the horizons of high-redshift integral field spectroscopy. In addition to traditional IFU modes, multiplexed "slit-stepping" techniques using the microshutter assembly can produce IFS-like data cubes with over $15$ times the efficiency of standard modes [Barišić et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983..139B/abstract). Such observations at $z \sim 1.1$ have already revealed mature spiral galaxies exhibiting moderate velocity dispersions ($\sigma \approx 36$ km s$^{-1}$), exponentially decreasing star formation rate density profiles, and negative radial metallicity gradients of $-0.020$ dex kpc$^{-1}$, confirming that the foundational properties of the modern Hubble sequence were already falling into place at cosmic noon [Barišić et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983..139B/abstract).

## Methodologies for Data Reduction and Spectral Analysis

The transformation of raw integral field observations into science-grade 3D datacubes presents significant computational challenges. A standard data reduction pipeline must perform complex spatial and spectral calibrations, including the correction of differential atmospheric refraction, spatial re-sampling, and the removal of instrumental artifacts. For example, the pipeline developed for the SAMI survey achieved atmospheric dispersion removal with an accuracy of $0.09$ arcseconds before drizzling the spectra onto a regular output grid [Allen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ascl.soft07006A/abstract), [Allen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.1567A/abstract). More recently, frameworks like the new [[galaxy-integral-field-spectroscopy-tool|Galaxy Integral-field Spectroscopy Tool]] (nGIST) have optimized memory management and parallelization to handle expansive optical wavelength ranges, specifically addressing problematic sky residuals at redder wavelengths ($>7000$ Å) [Fraser-McKelvie et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ascl.soft07015F/abstract). Moreover, prototypes of the SDSS-V Local Volume Mapper (LVM) data analysis pipeline are being applied to massive MaNGA mosaics of nearby galaxies like IC 342 to resolve H II regions at sub-kiloparsec scales [Barrera-Ballesteros et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250705414B/abstract). In near-infrared observations, techniques such as Butterworth spatial filtering and Richardson-Lucy deconvolution are often applied to remove high spatial-frequency noise and resolve individual ionized-gas clouds that would otherwise remain undetected [Menezes et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.2597M/abstract).

Extracting physical parameters from these calibrated datacubes relies heavily on automated emission-line fitting and spectral decomposition. Because multiple kinematic components frequently overlap along the line of sight, robust spectral separation is necessary to distinguish, for instance, narrow photoionized gas in H II regions from broad, shock-excited outflows [Ho et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.3894H/abstract). The [[lzifu|LZIFU]] (LaZy-IFU) toolkit, an IDL-based fitting engine, has been widely adopted across large programs like the [[califa-survey|CALIFA survey]] and instruments like the [[muse-instrument|MUSE (instrument)]] to simultaneously fit multiple emission lines and extract distinct kinematic components [Ho et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Ap&SS.361..280H/abstract). Additionally, specialized tools such as BUDDI have been developed for spectroscopic decomposition. This allows researchers to extract separate spectra for the central galaxy and the extended stellar halo in massive cD galaxies (such as NGC 3311), enabling the study of their distinct chemical histories [Johnston et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.4255J/abstract). Furthermore, 3D image reconstruction algorithms are employed in crowded fields to reliably separate [[discrete-astronomical-sources|Discrete astronomical sources]] (such as planetary nebulae or individual star clusters) from complex, bright galaxy backgrounds [Becker et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AN....325..155B/abstract).

Advanced modeling techniques are also required for specialized cases, such as the reconstruction of strongly lensed galaxies. Research indicates that applying 3D regularization to an entire datacube reconstructs a source's intrinsic intensity and velocity structures more accurately than 2D regularization applied to individual velocity channels, resulting in more uniform noise properties [Young et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929....6Y/abstract). 

To manage the escalating computational costs of these complex multicomponent fits, machine learning approaches are increasingly being utilized. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on synthetic spectra have been successfully deployed to determine the number of line-of-sight components present within a single spatial pixel (spaxel). When applied to specific optical spectral ranges, these CNNs have demonstrated higher accuracy and lower computational resource demands compared to traditional Bayesian inference methods. This machine learning methodology has proven particularly effective for disentangling complex kinematics and multiple overlapping components in merging galaxy systems [Rhea et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923..169R/abstract).

## Major Legacy Surveys and Statistical Population Studies

The advent of multi-object and wide-field IFUs initiated a paradigm shift in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], transitioning the field from 1D single-aperture spectroscopy to the spatially resolved 2D mapping of large, statistically significant galaxy samples [Sánchez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...538A...8S/abstract). This transition has been driven by a generation of [[legacy-surveys|Legacy surveys]] that have systematically mapped the kinematics, ionized gas, and [[spatially-resolved-stellar-populations|Spatially resolved stellar populations]] of thousands of nearby galaxies.

Among the foundational programs is the [[califa-survey|CALIFA survey]] (Calar Alto Legacy Integral Field Area), which acquired spatially resolved optical spectra for a diameter-selected sample of roughly 600 galaxies in the local Universe ($0.005 < z < 0.03$) [Sánchez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...538A...8S/abstract). Following CALIFA, highly multiplexed IFU instruments enabled even larger statistical samples. The SAMI survey (Sydney-AAO Multi-object Integral field spectrograph) obtained science-grade data cubes for over 3,000 unique galaxies across a broad redshift range of $0.004 < z < 0.113$ and stellar masses spanning $7.5 < \log(M_\star/M_\odot) < 11.6$ [Scott et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.2299S/abstract), [Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..991C/abstract). Concurrently, the [[manga-survey|MaNGA survey]] (Mapping Nearby Galaxies at Apache Point Observatory) further expanded the statistical backbone of local galaxy studies, targeting an even larger sample of nearby galaxies and facilitating robust comparisons between local baseline populations and higher-redshift systems [Bland-Hawthorn et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..309...21B/abstract), [Foster et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASA...38...31F/abstract).

These [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Galaxy Surveys and Catalogs]] have resolved several long-standing questions regarding galaxy assembly and structure. By extracting age and metallicity gradients across the optical extent of massive disk galaxies, these surveys cemented the inside-out growth paradigm [Sánchez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410..313S/abstract), [Delgado et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..300D/abstract). However, the literature notes that this assembly mode is not perfectly universal; lower-mass galaxies frequently deviate, and local metallicity and star-formation variations within individual spatial regions show significant scatter that global scaling laws cannot fully explain [Delgado et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..300D/abstract), [Grasha et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221106005G/abstract). Other programs have mapped the impact of dense environments; for instance, the MUSE GASP survey has extensively studied 'jellyfish' galaxies, revealing how cluster environments drive ram-pressure gas stripping and trigger star formation within the stripped tails [Poggianti et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IAUS..373..163P/abstract). In massive cluster environments, a VIMOS IFU survey of 73 galaxy clusters indicates that ionized gas in the core typically possesses ordered kinematics that are decoupled from the stellar motions of the brightest cluster galaxy [Hamer et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.1758H/abstract). Similarly, results from the [[magpi-survey|MAGPI survey]] at $z \sim 0.3$ indicate that cluster galaxies possess less centrally concentrated mass density slopes compared to isolated field galaxies, underscoring the role of the environment in reshaping total internal mass distributions [Derkenne et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.3602D/abstract).

Statistical population studies utilizing IFUs have also fundamentally redefined the classification of early-type galaxies (ETGs). Driven by observations from the SAURON instrument and the [[atlas3d-survey|Atlas3D survey]], the traditional morphological dichotomy of ETGs has been largely superseded by a kinematic framework. ETGs are now primarily divided into "fast" and "slow" rotators [McDermid et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006NewAR..49..521M/abstract), [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract). Fast rotators start as star-forming disks and evolve through a channel dominated by gas accretion, bulge growth, and quenching, while slow rotators are weakly triaxial systems that assemble through gas-poor mergers and dominate at the highest masses [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract). 

Furthermore, the extensive spatial coverage of these surveys allows for the robust measurement of dynamical transitions. For instance, SAMI survey data covering $\sim 350,000$ individual [[spaxel|Spaxel]] velocity dispersion measurements demonstrate a clear dynamical transition mass. At stellar masses above $\log(M_\star/M_\odot) \sim 10$, galaxies exhibit strongly increasing central velocity dispersions indicative of dispersion-dominated bulges, a structural feature that is notably absent in lower-mass systems [Scott et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.2299S/abstract). Ultimately, the combination of these massive IFU campaigns has demonstrated that global, integrated galaxy properties are intricately and inevitably tied to their spatially resolved structural components.

## Open Questions and Future Frontiers in 3D Spectroscopy

While integral field spectroscopy has fundamentally transformed [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] by resolving the internal kinematics and stellar populations of galaxies, several critical mysteries regarding galaxy assembly remain unresolved. At the forefront is the precise physical driver of the elevated gas turbulence observed in high-redshift ($z \sim 1$) star-forming disks. The literature remains divided on whether this turbulence is predominantly driven by feedback from intense star formation [Glazebrook et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PASA...30...56G/abstract) or by gravitational instabilities intrinsic to highly gas-rich disks [Stott et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457.1888S/abstract). This dynamical state indicates that the disks are marginally unstable, which is parameterized as having a Toomre stability criterion of $Q \sim 1$.

Similarly ambiguous is the exact formation mechanism of kinematically distinct cores (KDCs) within early-type slow rotators. While traditionally associated with gas-poor major mergers or in-situ star formation following gas accretion [McDermid et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006NewAR..49..521M/abstract), recent cosmological simulations suggest that late-stage minor mergers (stellar mass ratios of $\sim 1:10$) on retrograde orbits are sufficient to produce KDCs [Taylor et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479..141T/abstract). Furthermore, interpreting spatially resolved attenuation maps remains challenging due to strong degeneracies between intrinsic extinction laws and complex line-of-sight dust geometries [Viaene et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.1286V/abstract).

Resolving these tensions requires pushing observational frontiers toward wider fields of view, higher spectral multiplexing, and greater spatial resolution. For [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|High-Redshift Galaxy Observations]], the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] has recently pioneered a highly efficient "slit-stepping" technique using its microshutter assembly. This strategy produces IFS-like data cubes with $>15\times$ the efficiency of standard IFS modes, enabling detailed kinematic mapping of galaxies well into the Epoch of Reionization [Barišić et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240808350B/abstract), [Barišić et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983..139B/abstract), [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315587S/abstract). Early results from this technique have already demonstrated that mature, rotationally supported spiral structures with established negative metallicity gradients existed as early as $z \sim 1.1$ [Barišić et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240808350B/abstract). Additionally, the JWST PASSAGE program is utilizing [[niriss|NIRISS]] [[slitless-spectroscopy|Slitless spectroscopy]] to obtain unbiased samples of over ten thousand emission-line galaxies without prior photometric selection, further expanding the frontier of multi-object spectral mapping [Malkan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250900596M/abstract).

In the ground-based domain, the deployment of extremely large telescopes (ELTs) will mark the next major revolution. Equipped with advanced adaptive optics, next-generation instrumentation like the proposed [[mosaic-instrument|MOSAIC (instrument)]] and EAGLE spectrographs will achieve the angular resolution necessary to perform [[spatially-resolved-stellar-populations|Spatially resolved stellar populations]] studies—extracting robust chemical abundances and kinematics—well beyond the Local Group [Evans et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010aoel.confE1004E/abstract), [Evans et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150601611E/abstract). Looking to space, the development of sophisticated simulation tools like PyISH is currently underway to model the performance of future ultraviolet IFUs proposed for the Habitable Worlds Observatory [Sweetak et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260114432S/abstract).

Simultaneously, the drive for unprecedented statistical significance in galaxy evolution studies is fueling the development of dedicated, highly multiplexed survey facilities. To map the complete optical extent of extremely close targets, the upcoming SDSS-V Local Volume Mapper (LVM) will serve as a major facility for panoptic spectroscopic mapping of the Milky Way and nearby galaxies [Kollmeier et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019BAAS...51g.274K/abstract). Among [[future-astronomical-facilities-and-surveys|Future Astronomical Facilities and Surveys]], the proposed [[wst|WST]] (Wide-field Spectroscopic Telescope) represents a massive leap forward. WST is conceptualized as a 12-meter telescope featuring simultaneous operation of a high-multiplex multi-object spectrograph (20,000 fibers over a 3 square degree field) and a giant $3 \times 3$ arcmin$^2$ integral field spectrograph [Mainieri et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240305398M/abstract), [Bacon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240512518B/abstract). By executing contiguous, pre-imaging-free surveys, the WST's integral field unit is expected to deliver billions of serendipitous spectra. This panoramic 3D view will definitively link sub-kiloparsec galactic physics—such as star formation quenching, gas accretion, and the genesis of KDCs—to the macro-scale environment of the cosmic web.
