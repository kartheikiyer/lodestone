## Article

Redshift ($z$) is the fractional displacement of electromagnetic radiation toward longer wavelengths between the rest frame of the emitter and the observer. In modern astrophysics, this shift arises from three primary physical mechanisms: the expansion of spacetime governed by the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric ([[cosmological-redshift|Cosmological redshift]]), the kinematic Doppler effect driven by the local [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]], and, to a lesser extent, gravitational time dilation within massive potential wells ([[gravitational-redshift|Gravitational redshift]]). Because the scale factor of an expanding universe monotonically increases, redshift serves as a fundamental proxy for both distance and [[look-back-time|Look-back time]], providing a direct, observable window into the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] of the cosmos.

The historic discovery that extragalactic [[receding-velocity|recession velocities]] increase linearly with distance permanently revolutionized cosmology, overturning static universe models and establishing the redshift-distance relation as a pillar of modern astrophysics [Hubble et al. (1936)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1936rene.book.....H/abstract). Today, massive observational campaigns—such as the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey and the Sloan Digital Sky Survey—rely on automated [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]] and photometric estimations to map the three-dimensional large-scale structure of the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]] [Colless et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004mmu..symp..196C/abstract). By utilizing redshift catalogs to trace the cosmic web, these surveys provide the statistically representative volumes necessary to constrain the matter density parameter, trace galaxy evolution, and probe the nature of the dark energy driving [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]]. 

Because an object's observed redshift synthesizes both the universal expansion and its local kinematic motion, three-dimensional galaxy maps exhibit [[relativistic-observational-effects|apparent line-of-sight anisotropies]] known as [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]] (RSD). These distortions encode critical dynamical information: large-scale coherent infall into overdense regions compresses structures (the Kaiser effect), while non-linear virialized motions within galaxy clusters elongate them into "Fingers of God" [McGill et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990MNRAS.242..428M/abstract), [Hamilton et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...385L...5H/abstract). By analyzing the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] and higher-order statistics in redshift space, cosmologists can isolate the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] of structure from the underlying galaxy bias, offering rigorous, background-independent tests of General Relativity and the cosmic [[growth-index|Growth index]] [Cole et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994MNRAS.267..785C/abstract).

Despite the robust framework of the $\Lambda$CDM concordance model, high-precision redshift measurements have revealed ongoing theoretical tensions and open questions. Most notably, structural growth rates inferred from modern RSD surveys are consistently lower than those extrapolated from Planck cosmic microwave background data, a discrepancy that may point to new physics or highlight systematic biases in modeling non-linear velocity dispersions at small scales. Looking forward, the next major observational frontier involves using extreme-precision interferometry to directly observe [[redshift-drift|Redshift drift]]—the real-time kinematic measurement of cosmic acceleration over a multi-year baseline. 

## Physical Mechanisms and Definitions

Redshift, denoted $z$, is formally defined as the fractional wavelength displacement of electromagnetic radiation between the rest frame of the emitter ($\lambda_{\rm em}$) and the observer ($\lambda_{\rm obs}$):

$$z = \frac{\lambda_{\rm obs} - \lambda_{\rm em}}{\lambda_{\rm em}}$$

In astrophysical and cosmological contexts, this wavelength displacement arises from three primary physical mechanisms:

**1. Cosmological Expansion:** In the standard model of cosmology, governed by the [[metric-perturbations|FLRW metric]], spacetime itself is expanding. Photons propagating through the Universe are stretched over the [[look-back-time|Look-back time]] between emission and observation. The resulting [[cosmological-redshift|Cosmological redshift]] relates directly to the scale factor $a(t)$ of the Universe:

$$1 + z_{\rm cosmo} = \frac{a(t_0)}{a(t)}$$

where $t_0$ is the present epoch and $t$ is the time of emission. Because $a(t)$ is a monotonically increasing function in an expanding universe, cosmological redshift is uniquely related to time and serves as a fundamental proxy for distance and the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]]. 

**2. Kinematic Doppler Effect:** Galaxies do not perfectly co-move with the expanding background; they exhibit local motions driven by the gravitational pull of large-scale structure, forming a [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]]. This local motion introduces a Doppler shift based on the [[relative-velocity|Relative velocity]] of the source. For a line-of-sight peculiar velocity $v_{\rm pec}$, the non-relativistic contribution is approximately $z_{\rm pec} \approx v_{\rm pec}/c$. Historically, systematic local motions driven by large-scale streaming flows of elliptical galaxies led to the proposition of the 'Great Attractor', a massive overdensity located at a [[receding-velocity|Receding velocity]] of $V \sim 4000 \text{ km/s}$ that induces large-scale streaming motions in local structures [Dressler et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ApJ...329..519D/abstract). Today, modern catalogs provide a complete census of baryonic mass concentrations; data from 2MASS, for example, successfully matches the predicted gravitational [[cosmic-velocity-field|Cosmic velocity field]] of the Local Group to the observed cosmic microwave background dipole [Huchra et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IAUS..216..170H/abstract). This precise alignment of the Local Group's CMB motion with the net optical flux dipole determined from galaxy catalogs had been previously deduced from earlier surveys [Lynden-Bell et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989MNRAS.241..325L/abstract). Furthermore, the observed $21^\circ$ misalignment between the Local Group's velocity and the 2MASS dipole is entirely consistent with standard $\Lambda$CDM halo models without needing to invoke nearby hidden galaxies [Erdoǧdu et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvD..80d3005E/abstract).

**3. Gravitational Time Dilation:** Photons emitted from deep within a massive potential well lose energy as they escape to the observer, resulting in a [[gravitational-redshift|Gravitational redshift]] as dictated by general relativity. 

The total observed redshift of an extragalactic object is a multiplicative synthesis of these [[relativistic-observational-effects|Relativistic observational effects]]. Neglecting the generally small gravitational component, the observed redshift is given by:

$$1 + z_{\rm obs} = (1 + z_{\rm cosmo})(1 + z_{\rm pec})$$

Because the multiplicative nature can complicate the separation of cosmic expansion from local kinematics, it has been proposed to use a logarithmic wavelength shift, $\zeta = \ln(1+z)$, which renders the cosmological and peculiar velocity terms strictly additive at any comoving distance [Baldry et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181205135B/abstract). Accurately separating the expansion component from the peculiar velocity component remains a central challenge—and tool—in modern cosmology.

## Historical Development: From Static to Expanding Universe

Prior to the 1920s, the prevailing cosmological consensus favored a static universe. Albert Einstein famously introduced the cosmological constant ($\Lambda$) into his equations of General Relativity to counter gravitational collapse and maintain a static spacetime. However, this paradigm was permanently dismantled by the discovery of the linear relationship between distance and redshift.

In 1929, Edwin Hubble published observations that revolutionized cosmology. By measuring the spectra of extragalactic nebulae, he discovered that almost all galaxies appear to be moving away from the Milky Way, and that their [[receding-velocity|Receding velocity]] increases in direct proportion to their distance from us ($v_r \propto r$) [Hubble et al. (1936)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1936rene.book.....H/abstract). This linear relationship, now known as Hubble's Law, provided the first definitive observational confirmation that the universe is expanding. 

Hubble summarized his profound findings—originally delivered as the 1935 Silliman lectures at Yale University—in his seminal 1936 work, *Realm of the Nebulae* [Hubble et al. (1936)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1936rene.book.....H/abstract). In it, he framed the observed redshift as a Doppler-like effect resulting from the rapid kinematic motion of celestial objects. While his fundamental law remains a pillar of modern astrophysics, Hubble's initial calculation of the [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] (the Hubble constant) was vastly overestimated because he incorrectly assumed that neighboring galaxies were much closer to Earth than they actually are [Hubble et al. (1936)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1936rene.book.....H/abstract). 

Theoretically, Hubble's Law is most naturally explained if the universe as a whole is expanding homogeneously and isotropically. Under these conditions, the distance between any two objects comoving with the expanding background is governed by a time-dependent scale factor, $a(t)$:
$$r(t) = a(t) \frac{r(t')}{a(t')}$$
Because $a(t)$ is a monotonically increasing function in an expanding universe, photons emitted at an earlier time $t$ will be stretched by the time they are observed at $t_0$, resulting in an observed wavelength $\lambda_{\rm obs} = \lambda_{\rm em} [a(t_0) / a(t)]$. This stretching of light manifests as the [[cosmological-redshift|Cosmological redshift]]. This overarching framework of an expanding [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] provided the foundation for subsequent foundational analyses of the thermodynamic and kinetic processes in the [[radiation-dominated-era|Radiation-dominated era]] that underlie galaxy formation and cosmological instability [Zeldovich et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983reas.book.....Z/abstract). While the homogeneous FLRW metric became the standard, theorists have also thoroughly surveyed inhomogeneous cosmological models and exact solutions of Einstein's equations that explain structure formation without relying on such approximations [Krasinski et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997icm..book.....K/abstract).

The observational triumph of the expanding universe led Einstein to abandon the cosmological constant, a decision he allegedly termed his "biggest blunder." However, late-time observations of the redshift-distance relation in Type Ia supernovae in 1998 demonstrated that the expansion of the universe is actually accelerating. This discovery necessitated the reintroduction of an energy component observationally identical to Einstein's $\Lambda$ to drive this [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]]. 

As observational tools matured, multi-wavelength imaging surveys provided the basis for modern massive redshift campaigns [Colless et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000PASA...17..215C/abstract). The resulting accumulation of enormous redshift datasets prompted new sophisticated approaches to large-scale structure analysis [Colless et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph.11326C/abstract). These immense redshift surveys opened new frontiers, including the exploration of the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]] and the determination of rigorous links between star formation and large-scale galaxy distribution [Geller et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001PASP..113..405G/abstract).

## Redshift as a Distance and Time Proxy

In an expanding universe governed by the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric, the stretching of photon wavelengths directly links an object's redshift $z$ to the scale factor of the universe at the time of emission, $a(t)$, relative to the present-day scale factor, $a(t_0)$. This fundamental relationship is defined as:
$$ 1 + z = \frac{a(t_0)}{a(t)} $$
Because $a(t)$ is a monotonically increasing function in an expanding universe, redshift serves as a unique, observable proxy for both the age of the universe at the time of emission and the object's distance [Dodelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020moco.book.....D/abstract).

### Redshift as a Time Proxy

Redshift is intimately connected to the [[look-back-time|Look-back time]], defined as $t_0 - t(z)$. Deriving a precise physical time interval from a measured redshift requires integrating the cosmic [[expansion-history|Expansion history]], which is dictated by the underlying matter and energy densities of the universe. 

Furthermore, the real-time temporal evolution of an object's redshift—known as [[redshift-drift|Redshift drift]]—provides a direct, purely kinematic measurement of [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]]. However, because the drift occurs on the timescale of a Hubble time, detecting it requires an extreme spectroscopic precision of $\Delta z \sim 10^{-9}$ over a decade-long baseline. Future implementations of differential interferometry or spatial heterodyne spectroscopy aim to achieve this sensitivity [Kim et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015APh....62..195K/abstract).

### Redshift as a Distance Proxy

In the local universe, redshift scales linearly with distance according to Hubble's law ($v \approx cz = H_0 d$), where $H_0$ is the [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]]. At cosmological distances, however, the non-Euclidean geometry of spacetime causes the definition of "distance" to bifurcate depending on the physical observable in question:

*   **Comoving distance:** The distance between two objects that remains constant if they move purely with the Hubble flow. To simplify calculations of large-scale structure, some authors advocate using the logarithmic wavelength shift, $\zeta = \ln(1+z)$, which scales proportionally with [[proper-distance-and-motion|comoving distance]] in a non-accelerating universe and allows [[peculiar-velocity-field|peculiar]] and cosmological velocities to become cleanly additive [Baldry et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181205135B/abstract).
*   **Luminosity distance ($d_L$):** Relates an object's intrinsic luminosity $L$ to its observed flux $f$ via:
$$ L = 4\pi d_L^2 f $$
*   **Angular-diameter distance ($d_A$):** Relates an object's physical transverse size $D$ to its observed angular size $\theta$ via:
$$ D = d_A \theta $$

### Cosmological Tests

The geometric interdependence of these distances allows for stringent tests of global cosmology. The Alcock-Paczynski test, for instance, isolates the true [[geometric-redshifts|cosmic geometry]] by comparing transverse clustering sizes (dependent on $d_A$) to line-of-sight clustering (dependent on the Hubble parameter and redshift) [Montanari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvD..86f3503M/abstract). By and large, utilizing these geometric distance proxies in massive galaxy surveys firmly supports a flat $\Lambda$CDM concordance model [Colless et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004mmu..symp..196C/abstract).

## Galaxy Redshift Surveys and Large-Scale Structure

Systematic observations of the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]] have fundamentally transitioned cosmology from two-dimensional sky projections to precise three-dimensional maps of the cosmic web. Historically, the transition to proper three-dimensional mapping of voids, filaments, and bubbles was exemplified by the early CfA redshift surveys [Huchra et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ASPC....5...41H/abstract). Another significant precursor, the Stromlo-APM Redshift Survey, advanced this effort by randomly sampling galaxies across a massive 1.3 sr area [Loveday et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJS..107..201L/abstract).

Subsequently, massive observational campaigns—most notably the 2dFGRS (Two-Degree Field Galaxy Redshift Survey) and the SDSS (Sloan Digital Sky Survey)—mapped the spatial distribution of hundreds of thousands of galaxies. These surveys establish the statistically representative volumes necessary for testing the $\Lambda$CDM concordance model via the [[redshift-distribution|Redshift distribution]] [da Costa et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph.12258D/abstract), [Colless et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004mmu..symp..196C/abstract). The 2dFGRS utilized the unprecedented capabilities of a 400-fibre multi-object spectrograph designed for quasar and [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]] mapping [Colless et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998HiA....11..473C/abstract). Its initial goals aimed at measuring redshifts for 250,000 galaxies to thoroughly characterize low-redshift galaxy populations [Colless et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RSPTA.357..105C/abstract). Early measurements drawn from the first 10,000 redshifts obtained with the 2dF facility validated the concept [Colless et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998wfsc.conf...77C/abstract), rapidly yielding precise measures of galaxy clustering and redshift-space anisotropy [Peacock et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001defi.conf..221P/abstract). By reaching depths of $z \approx 0.3$, the 2dFGRS mapped over 221,000 galaxies, providing direct observational evidence that large-scale structure formed via gravitational instability [Colless et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004mmu..symp..196C/abstract). Furthermore, the 2M++ redshift compilation utilized 2MASS photometry to assemble 69,160 galaxies, allowing researchers to seamlessly map large-scale structures and [[peculiar-velocity-field|peculiar velocities]] out to depths of $200\ h^{-1}\text{Mpc}$ [Lavaux et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2840L/abstract).

In these surveys, the three-dimensional map is constructed using redshift as a proxy for line-of-sight distance. However, local peculiar velocities introduce predictable anisotropies along the line of sight known as [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]] (RSD). The statistical properties of galaxy clustering in redshift space are primarily quantified using the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]]. Redshift surveys successfully measured the shape of the power spectrum, including its expected turnover near $160 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Schuecker et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...472..485S/abstract). Nevertheless, interpreting these measurements requires caution; reviews of factors complicating power spectrum interpretation note that issues such as relative bias and non-linear effects must be carefully navigated [Strauss et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996astro.ph.10033S/abstract). 

Because galaxies are biased tracers of the underlying dark matter, different galaxy populations cluster differently. The 2dFGRS provided robust evidence for this bias bimodality, demonstrating that at scales smaller than $10 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$, early-type (red) galaxies cluster much more strongly than late-type (blue) galaxies [Lahav et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ntto.conf..145L/abstract), [Lahav et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASA...21..404L/abstract). 2dFGRS data further revealed important dependencies, mapping luminosity functions based on surface brightness and local density [Colless et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASA...21..352C/abstract), and demonstrating clear variations in star formation activity with environment within clusters and galaxy groups [Couch et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004cgpc.symp...42C/abstract). Beyond optical regimes, 2dFGRS spectral analysis has also been vital in clarifying the nature and physical properties of local mJy-level radio sources [Magliocchetti et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12286M/abstract). Additionally, analyses of near-infrared selected 2MASS samples indicate a steeper relationship between galaxy bias and luminosity compared to these optical samples [Westover et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007PhDT.........3W/abstract).

## Redshift-Space Distortions (RSD)

In observational cosmology, a galaxy's measured redshift includes both the cosmological expansion and its local kinematic motion. In the non-relativistic limit, this is approximated as $z_{\text{obs}} \approx z_{\text{cosm}} + v_{\text{pec}}/c$, where $v_{\text{pec}}$ is the line-of-sight component of the [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]]. Because these peculiar velocities correlate with the underlying matter density field, their inclusion breaks the translational symmetry of the universe from the observer's perspective, producing an apparent anisotropy in clustering measurements known as [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]] (RSD). Within the Local Group, models of these local kinematics often employ modified variational principles to recover physical distances directly from measured redshifts [Schmoldt et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998AJ....115.2231S/abstract).

RSD manifests differently depending on the physical scale being observed. On large, linear scales, galaxies falling into overdense regions produce a coherent compression of clustering along the line of sight, an artifact known as the Kaiser effect, which is sensitive to the [[growth-function|Growth function]] [Gramann et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...425..382G/abstract). This distortion introduces characteristic quadrupole and hexadecapole moments into the [[redshift-space-correlation-function|Redshift-space correlation function]] [Hamilton et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...385L...5H/abstract). The amplitude of this linear distortion is parameterized by $\beta = f(\Omega)/b \approx \Omega^{0.6}/b$, where $f$ is the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]], $\Omega$ is the matter density parameter, and $b$ is the linear galaxy bias [Cole et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994MNRAS.267..785C/abstract). The extension of Kaiser's initial formalism to the general all-sky case has been achieved via an exact linear calculation of the full covariance matrix, which serves as a sensitive probe of both the density parameter and galaxy biasing [Zaroubi et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993astro.ph.11013Z/abstract), [Zaroubi et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462...25Z/abstract). 

Modeling redshift-space clustering accurately across large volumes must account for 'wide-angle effects' and apparent violations of statistical homogeneity, which can be properly addressed by expanding the density field using spherical Fourier-Bessel coefficients rather than relying on the traditional plane-parallel approximation [Castorina et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.4403C/abstract). Furthermore, modern analytical methods have expanded to use rank-2 Minkowski Tensors to analyze anisotropic signals in redshift space, providing a topological approach for connecting structure geometry to underlying [[expansion-history|cosmological parameters]] [Appleby et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250710091A/abstract). Alternatively, cosmologists have proposed methods for deriving the linear growth rate $\beta$ that are independent of clustering anisotropies altogether, such as minimizing the spread of estimated absolute magnitudes to isolate the kinematic peculiar velocity signature [Nusser et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...744..193N/abstract).

Conversely, on small, non-linear scales (typically inside virialized halos), large random [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|velocity dispersions]] cause an apparent stretching of structures along the line of sight. This distinctive elongation, known as the "Finger-of-God" effect, is dominated by galaxies in dynamically non-linear regions [McGill et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990MNRAS.242..428M/abstract). Extracting cosmological parameters from RSD requires carefully separating these linear and non-linear effects. To better isolate small-scale kinematics, an object-weighted measure of the small-scale velocity dispersion has been proposed, which is less sensitive to the overwhelming influence of rich clusters than traditional pair-weighted measures [Baker et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..201..186B/abstract). Non-linearities can obscure baryonic acoustic features and severely bias estimations of the growth rate if linear models are applied at wavenumbers $k > 0.07 \ h\text{Mpc}^{-1}$ [Kwan et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748...78K/abstract). To overcome the parameter degeneracy between the bias $b$ and the density $\Omega$ present in the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]], researchers analyze higher-order statistics like the bispectrum. The redshift-space bispectrum isolates non-linear mode-coupling effects, allowing for an independent determination of the galaxy bias and establishing a more accurate mapping between luminous matter and dark matter halos [Scoccimarro et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...517..531S/abstract).

Measurements of RSD serve as a robust test of General Relativity and the cosmic [[growth-index|Growth index]], offering constraints that are independent of the background [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] [Linder et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008APh....29..336L/abstract). Theoretical models identify the epoch around $z \sim 0.5$ as the optimal redshift range for these tests, balancing the available survey volume against the strength of the RSD signal [Simpson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvD..81d3513S/abstract).

However, the current literature reveals a notable tension in these measurements. Metastudies of RSD results from major redshift surveys (such as BOSS and WiggleZ) have consistently found structure growth rates that are lower than those expected from Planck cosmic microwave background data assuming a standard $\Lambda$CDM framework [Macaulay et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PhRvL.111p1301M/abstract). It remains an open question whether this discrepancy points to new physics, or merely reflects the theoretical challenges of accurately modeling small-scale non-linear Finger-of-God velocity dispersions.

## Cosmological Parameter Estimation and Dark Energy

Large-scale three-dimensional maps of the universe, constructed via extensive [[redshift-distribution|redshift surveys]] such as the 2dFGRS and SDSS, provide foundational constraints on the $\Lambda$CDM concordance model [Peacock et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph..9240P/abstract), [Colless et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004mmu..symp..196C/abstract). By analyzing the redshift-distance relation and the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]], these surveys—often combined with cosmic microwave background (CMB) data—yield precise estimates of the matter density $\Omega_m$ and the [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] [Lahav et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ntto.conf..145L/abstract). Crucially, redshift clustering data in the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]] provide strong constraints on the equation of state for Dark Energy, consistently supporting a value near $w \approx -1$ [Calvão et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..283..127C/abstract), [Lahav et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002astro.ph.12358L/abstract).

Redshift distributions are also essential for leveraging Baryonic Acoustic Oscillations (BAO) as a standard ruler. The presence of the BAO peak in the galaxy distribution enables the Alcock-Paczyński test, which evaluates the ratio of angular to spatial/redshift dimensions of spherically symmetric source distributions [Melia et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IJMPD..2650055M/abstract). Extensive simulations have demonstrated the robustness of these BAO peaks, showing that they survive and provide reliable [[coherence-length|standard rulers]] despite the smearing effects of non-linearity and redshift distortions in the quasi-linear regime [Seo et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...633..575S/abstract). This breaks geometric degeneracies, allowing for separate measurements of the Hubble parameter $H(z)$ and the angular diameter distance $D_A(z)$ to further constrain the mechanisms driving [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]]. 

Finally, precision parameter estimation requires careful accounting of redshift-induced systematics. For instance, redshift distortions in the Integrated Sachs-Wolfe (ISW) signal add power at all redshifts. If this effect is neglected, it leads to an artificial overestimation of the dark energy density $\Omega_\Lambda$ and an underestimation of statistical errors [Rassat et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0902.1759R/abstract).

## Galaxy Evolution and the Redshift Desert

Because the scale factor of the universe $a(t)$ is a monotonically increasing function of time, the observed redshift $z$ of an object is uniquely related to the time its light was emitted. This relationship allows astronomers to calculate the [[look-back-time|Look-back time]], defined as $t_0 - t(z)$, which precisely maps the redshift of a source to a specific epoch in the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] of the universe. 

Observing populations of galaxies at progressively higher redshifts provides a direct window into galaxy evolution, as these distant objects act as the statistical progenitors of galaxies in the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]]. By analyzing how galaxy number densities and intrinsic properties change with $z$, researchers can systematically trace the universe's [[metagalactic-radiation-field|star formation history]] and the dynamics of mass assembly over cosmic time [da Costa et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph.12258D/abstract). The physical processes required to accurately model these evolutionary changes are highly complex, incorporating general relativity, hydrodynamics, and the dynamics of collisionless dark matter systems.

At low to intermediate redshifts ($z \lesssim 1$), extensive spectroscopic surveys such as DEEP2 have deeply probed the relationship between galaxy properties and their environments, mapping the evolution of star formation and active galactic nuclei (AGN) activity [Cooper et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..235..167C/abstract). Furthermore, determinations of SEDs and luminosity functions for galaxies in the Caltech Faint Galaxy Redshift Survey near HDF-North have detailed the evolutionary properties of these intermediate populations [Cohen et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001defi.conf...49C/abstract). Researchers have similarly leveraged the construction of the two-point angular correlation function for faint galaxies to derive the [[coherence-length|clustering length]] and its structural evolution across this epoch [Hudon et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...469..519H/abstract). Intriguingly, analyses of the conditional luminosity function using these datasets indicate surprisingly little evolution in the way galaxies occupy their host dark matter halos from $z=0$ out to $z=0.8$ [Yan et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...598..848Y/abstract). This lack of evolution suggests that while individual galaxies assemble mass and form stars, the statistical relationship between luminous matter and dark matter halos remains relatively stable over the latter half of the universe's age.

Further back in time, the epoch peaking around $z \sim 2$ represents a critical phase in cosmic history. During this period, the global cosmic star formation rate, AGN activity, mass assembly, and morphological differentiation all reach their culmination [Renzini et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Msngr.137...41R/abstract). However, mapping the galaxy population in the interval $1.4 < z < 3$ presents severe observational challenges. Because this bustling epoch of cosmic assembly is paradoxically much harder to explore than both the closer universe and the more distant universe, it has historically been dubbed the "redshift desert" [Renzini et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Msngr.137...41R/abstract). Despite the intense physical action taking place within this spacetime volume, the observational difficulties in properly mapping these populations have required dedicated multi-wavelength approaches and photometric advances to bridge the gap in our empirical understanding of the universe's peak growth phase. To push past traditional observational boundaries, astronomers frequently measure high-redshift structure by analyzing foreground absorption in quasar spectra and mapping gravitationally induced distortions [White et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997euvl.conf..219W/abstract). Furthermore, multi-wavelength approaches searching for high-redshift galaxy clusters in deep optical/NIR and X-ray surveys serve as essential diagnostics of global cosmology at these immense distances [Sommer-Larsen et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..253..195S/abstract).

## Methodologies in Redshift Determination

The shift toward mapping the three-dimensional large-scale structure of the universe using massive datasets has necessitated the development of highly automated methodologies for redshift determination. Historically, the standard technique for obtaining a [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]] involved the cross-correlation of an observed galaxy spectrum with a library of empirical or synthetic templates. However, to handle the sheer volume and spectral diversity of modern surveys, automated techniques based on Principal Component Analysis (PCA) were introduced. 

PCA generalizes the traditional cross-correlation approach by replacing individual, discrete templates with a simultaneous linear combination of orthogonal eigenspectra [Glazebrook et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...492...98G/abstract). This mathematical framework effectively eliminates the mismatch between fixed templates and observed data, yielding more robust error estimates. Furthermore, it allows galactic spectra to be parameterized continuously based on emission and absorption strengths. For example, the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey utilized PCA projections to define a continuous spectral parameter, $\eta = 0.44pc_1 - pc_2$, which proved to be highly robust to instrumental uncertainties [Madgwick et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001misk.conf..331M/abstract). Complementary automated routines, such as matched filters and wavelet transforms, have also been employed to identify morphologically complex or rare targets, such as quasars, from large spectroscopic pipelines [Madgwick et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.334..209M/abstract).

While spectroscopy provides precise kinematics, the required integration times make it observationally expensive for the deepest and widest all-sky surveys. Consequently, researchers frequently rely on photometric redshift estimation. By cross-matching multi-wavelength photometric datasets—such as optical, near-infrared, and mid-infrared observations—astronomers can efficiently estimate redshifts for tens of millions of sources. The 2MPZ catalog, for instance, utilized these multi-wavelength techniques to map the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]] out to $z \sim 0.2 - 0.3$ over unprecedented angular scales [Bilicki et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..308..143B/abstract). These catalogs also serve a crucial role in multi-messenger astrophysics; for instance, 2MASS photometric redshift data have been utilized to optimize the targeted electromagnetic follow-up of gravitational-wave events from LIGO [Antolini et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.2212A/abstract).

Despite its efficiency, photometric redshift estimation introduces significant statistical uncertainties that must be rigorously modeled. Attempts to reconstruct the true [[redshift-distribution|Redshift distribution]] of galaxies binned by photometric redshift using angular two-point correlation functions have shown that uncertainties depend critically on the number of free parameters and the angular scales analyzed. If not properly constrained—for example, by subdividing galaxies by spectral type or reducing the number of parameters to achieve mean redshift precision of $\sim 0.01$—these uncertainties can dominate the error budgets for derived cosmological parameters [Schneider et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...651...14S/abstract). 

Recent methodological advances also extend to how redshifts are mathematically represented. When evaluating photometric redshifts and extracting the [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]] over large distances, it can be advantageous to use a logarithmic wavelength shift formulation:
$$ \zeta = \ln(1+z) = \ln \lambda_{\rm obs} - \ln \lambda_{\rm em} $$
Because $\zeta$ scales linearly with comoving distance in a comparative non-accelerating universe, this definition allows cosmological expansion and local peculiar velocity terms to be treated additively. This logarithmic approach improves the accuracy of line-of-sight velocity approximations compared to the standard fractional shift $\Delta z$, offering a more rigorous spacing metric for evaluating galaxy evolution [Baldry et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181205135B/abstract). Furthermore, for upcoming multi-tracer galaxy surveys, semi-analytical multi-tracer Fisher matrices have been developed to forecast the constraining power of the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|angular power spectrum]] directly in harmonic space [Abramo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...08..073A/abstract). Modern frameworks have also introduced the "redshift fluctuation" as a gauge-invariant cosmological observable, utilizing redshift-weighted galaxy number counts to resolve small-scale radial modes in future analyses [Matthewson et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...09..065M/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Frontiers

Despite the success of the concordance cosmology in explaining the large-scale structure of the universe, several observational tensions and open questions continue to drive the future of redshift research. Key among these frontiers is the direct, real-time kinematic measurement of [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]] via the [[redshift-drift|Redshift drift]]. Because this drift occurs on the characteristic timescale of the Hubble time, detecting the signal requires extraordinary spectroscopic precision of $\Delta z \sim 10^{-9}$ over a multi-year baseline—for example, a 10-year span is expected to yield a signal-to-noise ratio of 1 [Kim et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015APh....62..195K/abstract). Achieving this level of precision will rely on the development of differential interferometric techniques, such as externally dispersed interferometers and spatial heterodyne spectroscopy, deployed on future 10-meter to 40-meter class telescopes [Kim et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015APh....62..195K/abstract).

A second major frontier involves resolving the active tensions between early-universe predictions and late-universe measurements of structure formation. As noted, metastudies of [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]] (RSD) from large surveys indicate that the observed [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] of cosmic structure is consistently lower than the values extrapolated from Planck cosmic microwave background data [Macaulay et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PhRvL.111p1301M/abstract). Overcoming the scale-dependent biases in non-linear modeling is critical for determining whether this discrepancy points toward new physics, such as a modified gravity framework [Simpson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvD..81d3513S/abstract), [Kwan et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748...78K/abstract).

High-precision redshift data are also being leveraged to test the constancy of fundamental physical parameters. Proposed methodologies combining galaxy ages, radial baryon acoustic oscillation modes ([[coherence-length|Coherence length]]), and gravitational wave standard sirens aim to measure the cosmological speed of light. Future observations have the potential to constrain the [[gravitational-constant|speed of light]] to a precision of $1.5\% - 2.0\%$, improving upon current $\sim 6\%$ constraints and testing its consistency with local standard model measurements [Santos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240905838S/abstract). Astrophysical redshift data from distant, highly energetic sources like Gamma-Ray Bursts (GRBs) and active galaxies are also used to test potential signatures of quantum gravity in vacuo, searching for anomalous energy-dependent velocities of light propagation [Amelino-Camelia et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph.10483A/abstract). Furthermore, measurements of the galaxy-galaxy $n$-point correlation functions can provide spectrum-independent limits on the energy density of a background of cosmological gravitational waves [Linder et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ApJ...328...77L/abstract).

### Alternative Models and Anomalies
While the expanding universe paradigm forms the undisputed foundation of the standard $\Lambda$CDM model, a number of alternative models and observational anomalies have been explored in the literature. Historically, observations of galaxy groups where physical companions exhibit significantly higher redshifts than the primary central galaxy led to proposals of an intrinsic or quantized "non-velocity" component to redshift, often framed as a [[discrete-redshift-model|Discrete-redshift model]] [Arp et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985ApJ...291...88A/abstract). However, rigorous statistical analyses of massive datasets, such as the 2dF QSO survey, have shown no evidence for these periodicities [Hawkins et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.336L..13H/abstract).

Other non-standard frameworks attempt to explain the redshift-distance relationship without an expanding spacetime. Examples include derivations of the [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] within a Euclidean static universe [Zaninetti et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhyEs..23..298Z/abstract), or modified [[geometric-redshifts|Geometric redshifts]] that attribute the appearance of cosmic acceleration to the spatial distribution of luminous matter rather than dark energy [Nyagisera et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Astro...3...43N/abstract). Additionally, Alcock-Paczynski tests utilizing BAO data from the SDSS-III/BOSS survey have occasionally been reported to disfavor the standard $\Lambda$CDM model at a $2.3\sigma$ confidence level, with some authors arguing that the data better align with a zero active mass equation of state ($Rh=ct$ universe) [Melia et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IJMPD..2650055M/abstract). Despite these isolated tensions and anomalous claims, the overwhelming consensus within the cosmological community maintains that the observed redshift is firmly governed by the FLRW metric and local peculiar kinematics.
