## Article

In observational cosmology, **redshift-space distortions** (RSD) are systematic, anisotropic biases in the observed three-dimensional clustering of galaxies. When constructing [[galaxy-mapping|maps of the universe]] from a [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]], astronomers rely on the cosmological [[redshift|Redshift]] to infer radial distances via the Hubble law. However, a galaxy's observed redshift is a combination of the cosmic expansion and its local [[cosmic-velocity-field|Cosmic velocity field]]—the peculiar velocity driven by gravitational interactions with surrounding large-scale structure. Because the line-of-sight component of this peculiar velocity introduces a kinematic Doppler shift, galaxies appear displaced from their true comoving coordinates. This mapping from real space to redshift space transforms an intrinsically isotropic matter distribution into an anisotropic pattern, providing a direct observational window into the dynamic assembly of the cosmos.

The physical manifestation of RSD is conventionally divided into two distinct, scale-dependent regimes. On large, quasi-linear scales, the coherent infall of galaxies toward overdense regions (such as superclusters) causes structures to appear artificially compressed along the line of sight. This phenomenon, known as the Kaiser effect [Kaiser et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987MNRAS.227....1K/abstract), is mathematically captured by the linear distortion parameter $\beta$, which relates the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] of structure to the linear [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy bias]]. Conversely, on small, highly non-linear scales, the rapid, randomized virial motions of galaxies within deep gravitational potential wells stretch the apparent clustering radially toward the observer, producing highly elongated features colloquially termed the "[[fingers-of-god|Fingers of God]]". By modeling these distortions using the two-dimensional [[redshift-space-correlation-function|Redshift-space correlation function]] or [[multipole-power-spectra|Multipole power spectra]] [Hamilton et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...385L...5H/abstract), cosmologists can isolate the dynamic kinematic signal from purely geometric expansion effects, such as those probed by the [[alcock-paczynski-test|Alcock-Paczynski test]].

As a direct probe of the rate at which matter clusters, RSD is a cornerstone technique for measuring the normalized growth rate of structure, [[f-8|fσ8]]. This makes it an indispensable tool for testing General Relativity, distinguishing between modified gravity theories, and constraining the properties of dark energy. While historical measurements have broadly supported the standard flat $\Lambda$CDM model, modern high-precision surveys have uncovered tantalizing tensions, such as a 2 to 4$\sigma$ discrepancy in the growth amplitude that may hint at new physics or unmodeled systematic errors. This article details the theoretical foundations and statistical frameworks of RSD, explores advanced non-linear modeling techniques like the [[gaussian-streaming-model|Gaussian streaming model]], and highlights emerging frontiers in the field. These future directions include the breakdown of the standard plane-parallel approximation via wide-angle and relativistic effects, as well as the use of [[higher-order-clustering-statistics|Higher-Order Clustering Statistics]]—such as the [[galaxy-bispectrum-multipoles|Galaxy bispectrum multipoles]] and the [[four-point-correlation-function|Four-point correlation function]]—to break parameter degeneracies and search for parity-violating physics beyond the standard model.

## Physical Origin and Fundamental Concepts

In observational cosmology, the three-dimensional distribution of galaxies is typically inferred from [[redshift-catalogs|Redshift catalogs]], where the observed [[redshift|Redshift]] is used as a proxy for distance. According to the Hubble law, the recessional velocity of a galaxy is proportional to its comoving distance $r$. However, the observed redshift is not purely cosmological in origin. Galaxies also exhibit a [[cosmic-velocity-field|Cosmic velocity field]] consisting of peculiar velocities induced by the gravitational pull of the surrounding large-scale structure [Dodelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020moco.book.....D/abstract).

Because of the Doppler effect, the line-of-sight component of a galaxy's peculiar velocity, $v_\parallel$, adds a kinematic contribution to the cosmological expansion. In the non-relativistic, distant-observer limit, the mapping between the true comoving position $\mathbf{r}$ and the apparent position in redshift space $\mathbf{s}$ is given by:
$$ \mathbf{s} = \mathbf{r} + \frac{v_\parallel}{aH} \hat{\mathbf{r}} $$
where $a$ is the cosmic scale factor, $H$ is the Hubble parameter, and $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ is the unit vector pointing along the line of sight. This mapping implies that a statistically isotropic galaxy distribution in real space will appear anisotropic in redshift space, a phenomenon known as redshift-space distortions (RSD).

The [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]] is driven by the gravitational instability of the underlying [[galaxy-density-fields|matter density field]]. As matter collapses into dark matter halos, it induces coherent bulk flows and random motions that deviate from the pure Hubble expansion. Because these velocities are sourced by the mass distribution, the resulting redshift-space distortions carry critical information about the growth of cosmic structure.

The physical manifestation of RSD is conventionally divided into two distinct scale-dependent regimes:
* **The Kaiser effect (large scales):** On linear to quasi-linear scales, galaxies undergo coherent infall toward overdense regions, such as superclusters. For an observer looking at such an overdensity, galaxies on the near side are falling away (increasing their observed redshift), while galaxies on the far side are falling toward the observer (decreasing their observed redshift). This causes the structure to appear artificially compressed or squashed along the line-of-sight [Kaiser et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987MNRAS.227....1K/abstract). This large-scale distortion is quantified by the redshift distortion parameter $\beta \approx \Omega_m^{0.6}/b$, which relates the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] of structure $f$ to the linear [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy bias]] $b$ [Kaiser et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987MNRAS.227....1K/abstract), [Fisher et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.279L...1F/abstract).
* **The [[fingers-of-god|Fingers of God]] (small scales):** On highly non-linear scales, galaxies reside within virialized structures such as galaxy clusters. The random, high-dispersion peculiar velocities of these galaxies within the deep cluster potential well translate to large Doppler shifts. As a result, the cluster appears artificially stretched along the line-of-sight, creating elongated structures that point radially toward the observer. 

By comparing the anisotropic redshift-space clustering—often analyzed via the [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] or the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]]—with isotropic real-space predictions, astronomers can isolate this kinematic signal. Because the large-scale amplitude of RSD depends directly on the rate at which structure forms, the parameter combination [[f-8|fσ8]] is universally used as a robust, bias-independent observable to test general relativity and probe the nature of dark energy [Song et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009JCAP...10..004S/abstract), [Beutler et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.3430B/abstract).

## Historical Development and Key Milestones

The recognition of redshift-space distortions (RSD) transformed peculiar velocities from a confounding observational nuisance into one of the most powerful probes of cosmological structure growth. Historically, the assumption that galactic redshifts served as direct proxies for comoving distance—a cornerstone of the Hubble expansion—was complicated by the realization that local gravitational interactions induce peculiar velocities. 

Early [[galaxy-mapping|three-dimensional mapping]] efforts, notably the [[cfa-redshift-survey|CfA Redshift Survey]], revealed a highly structured local universe characterized by filaments, voids, and clusters [Huchra et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ASPC....5...41H/abstract), [Davis et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982ApJ...253..423D/abstract). Within these maps, observers noted a striking radial elongation of dense galaxy clusters along the line of sight. This small-scale kinematic artifact, coined the "[[fingers-of-god|Fingers of God]]" effect, was correctly attributed to the virialized [[pairwise-velocity-dispersion|Pairwise velocity dispersion]] of galaxies within collapsed, non-linear structures [McGill et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990MNRAS.242..428M/abstract).

While the Fingers of God described non-linear, small-scale dynamics, the theoretical breakthrough for large, quasi-linear scales arrived with the seminal work of Nick Kaiser. Kaiser demonstrated that coherent large-scale infall of galaxies toward overdense regions systematically squashes the apparent clustering pattern along the line of sight [Kaiser et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987MNRAS.227....1K/abstract). This phenomenon, now universally known as the Kaiser effect, established the foundational theory of [[linear-growth-rate|structure growth]] via RSD. Kaiser showed that the amplitude of this anisotropy is governed by the distortion parameter $\beta$, defined as:
$$ \beta \approx \frac{\Omega_m^{0.6}}{b} $$
where $\Omega_m$ is the matter density parameter and $b$ is the linear [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy bias]]. This elegantly linked the observed clustering anisotropy directly to the growth rate of cosmic structure.

Following Kaiser's framework, Hamilton (1992) formalized the extraction of $\beta$ by expanding the redshift-space [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] into [[legendre-multipoles|Legendre multipoles]], demonstrating that linear RSD takes a characteristic quadrupole and hexadecapole form [Hamilton et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...385L...5H/abstract). To bridge the linear and non-linear regimes, early phenomenological approaches, such as the streaming model, were developed. These models typically convolved the real-space correlation function with a chosen velocity distribution function (often assumed to be a Gaussian or exponential) to account for both the Kaiser effect and the Fingers of God simultaneously [Fisher et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...448..494F/abstract).

During the 1990s, observational constraints on $\beta$ exhibited significant scatter—ranging from $\beta \sim 0.4$ to $\beta \sim 1.0$—depending on the galaxy tracer used (e.g., optical versus IRAS-selected samples) and the specific methods applied to separate non-linear dispersions from linear infall [Fisher et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994MNRAS.267..927F/abstract), [Ratcliffe et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.281L..47R/abstract). The field reached a critical milestone of precision with the advent of massive multiplexed spectroscopic campaigns, most notably the 2-degree Field [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] (2dFGRS). By mapping over 100,000 galaxies, the 2dFGRS provided a highly statistically significant detection of the Kaiser distortion, measuring $\beta = 0.43 \pm 0.07$ and decisively validating the linear infall paradigm on large scales [Peacock et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..283...19P/abstract), [Peacock et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001defi.conf..221P/abstract).

As survey volumes have expanded, the historical reliance on simple analytic dispersion models has proven insufficient for modern precision cosmology. Present analyses recognize that perturbative approaches and simple velocity distributions can introduce systematic errors of up to 10% in the recovery of the growth rate if applied too far into the quasi-linear regime. Consequently, contemporary research has largely shifted toward advanced non-perturbative frameworks, such as the [[gaussian-streaming-model|Gaussian streaming model]] and models employing generalized hyperbolic velocity distributions, to robustly extract unbiased cosmological constraints [Kuruvilla et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.2256K/abstract), [Eggemeier et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250118597E/abstract).

## Statistical Framework: Multipoles and Power Spectra

To isolate and quantify the anisotropic signatures of peculiar velocities, the clustering of galaxies is typically analyzed using the [[two-dimensional-power-spectrum|Two-dimensional power spectrum]] or the two-dimensional [[redshift-space-correlation-function|Redshift-space correlation function]]. In the distant-observer (or plane-parallel) approximation, the line-of-sight direction is treated as fixed across the survey volume. The separation vector between two galaxies, $\mathbf{s}$, is decomposed into a line-of-sight component, $\pi$ (or $s_\parallel$), and a transverse component, $r_p$ (or $s_\perp$). 

In Fourier space, this anisotropy is parametrized by the cosine of the angle between the wavevector $\mathbf{k}$ and the line of sight, defined as $\mu = \hat{k} \cdot \hat{z}$. In the linear regime, the redshift-space power spectrum $P^s(k, \mu)$ is related to the real-space [[cosmological-power-spectrum|matter power spectrum]] $P_m(k)$ by the Kaiser formula [Kaiser et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987MNRAS.227....1K/abstract):
$$ P^s(k, \mu) = (b + f \mu^2)^2 P_m(k) $$
where $b$ is the linear galaxy bias and $f$ is the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] of structure. The distortion is frequently expressed in terms of the parameter $\beta = f/b$.

To compress this two-dimensional information into robust 1D summary statistics, the power spectrum is expanded into [[legendre-multipoles|Legendre multipoles]]. The multipole moments $P_\ell(k)$ are obtained by integrating the 2D power spectrum over $\mu$, weighted by the Legendre polynomials $\mathcal{L}_\ell(\mu)$:
$$ P_\ell(k) = \frac{2\ell+1}{2} \int_{-1}^{1} P^s(k, \mu) \mathcal{L}_\ell(\mu) d\mu $$
Because the Newtonian plane-parallel approximation assumes symmetry along the line of sight ($\mu \rightarrow -\mu$), all odd multipoles identically vanish in linear theory. The clustering signal is completely described by just three non-zero even [[multipole-power-spectra|Multipole power spectra]]: the monopole ($\ell = 0$), the quadrupole ($\ell = 2$), and the hexadecapole ($\ell = 4$) [Hamilton et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...385L...5H/abstract). 

*   **Monopole ($P_0$):** Measures the angle-averaged overall clustering amplitude.
*   **Quadrupole ($P_2$):** Captures the leading-order anisotropic compression from coherent large-scale infall (the Kaiser effect).
*   **Hexadecapole ($P_4$):** Highly sensitive to the $\mu^4$ dependence in the Kaiser formula, but also heavily impacted by small-scale, non-linear thermal velocity dispersions known as the [[fingers-of-god|Fingers of God]] [Okumura et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JCAP...11..014O/abstract).

Modern surveys utilize fast Fourier-transform (FFT) based estimators to rapidly measure these multipoles over large volumes [Scoccimarro et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..92h3532S/abstract). Interestingly, RSD can effectively sharpen Baryon Acoustic Oscillation features along the line of sight, enhancing the detectability of the BAO bump in two-dimensional correlation maps [Tian et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...728...34T/abstract). However, extracting unbiased cosmological parameters from $P_2(k)$ and $P_4(k)$ requires careful modeling of the quasi-linear and non-linear regimes, often necessitating advanced frameworks like the [[gaussian-streaming-model|Gaussian streaming model]] to account for the complex probability distribution function of pairwise velocities. To mitigate these complexities, the galaxy clustering ratio (often denoted as $\eta$) has been introduced as a universal statistic that is independent of linear galaxy bias and redshift distortions, providing a more direct probe of the real-space power spectrum [Bel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...563A..36B/abstract). Alternative characterizations of the cosmic web also include novel information-theoretic statistics like graph entropy, which quantifies the connectivity and clustered nature of the underlying [[galaxy-density-fields|density field]] in redshift space [Valentina García-Alvarado et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200808164V/abstract).

### Wide-Angle and Relativistic Effects

While the plane-parallel approximation restricts the signal to even multipoles, modern large-volume surveys necessitate going beyond this limit. When the angular separation of galaxy pairs is large, the line-of-sight direction varies across the pair, generating "wide-angle" effects. Furthermore, relativistic effects—such as the transverse Doppler effect, [[gravitational-redshift|Gravitational redshift]], and Doppler magnification—introduce fundamental asymmetries into the observed clustering pattern.

These effects break the $\mu \rightarrow -\mu$ symmetry, generating non-zero odd multipoles in the cross-correlation of different galaxy populations [Beutler et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...03..040B/abstract). Most notably, relativistic distortions generate a measurable dipole ($\ell=1$) in the correlation function. This dipole has been successfully detected at the $\sim 2.7\sigma$ level in cross-correlations of BOSS CMASS galaxies [Alam et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.2822A/abstract). Although the modeling of these terms involves complex non-linear couplings between velocity and gravitational potential fields at quasi-linear scales (e.g., $< 20 \, h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$), odd multipoles offer a unique, model-independent test of the equivalence principle and General Relativity [Saga et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498..981S/abstract). Deviations from statistical isotropy can also be probed using observables derived from vector perturbations in peculiar velocities, which can be elegantly parameterized and measured via a bipolar spherical harmonics expansion [Tansella et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98j3515T/abstract).

## Modeling Nonlinearities and Small-Scale Dynamics

While the linear Kaiser formula successfully describes the anisotropy of clustering on large scales, it fails to match observations in the quasi-linear and non-linear regimes, typically diverging for wavenumbers $k \gtrsim 0.05 \ h \ \text{Mpc}^{-1}$ [Jennings et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410.2081J/abstract), [White et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447..234W/abstract). On these smaller scales, random virialized motions within dark matter halos smear out clustering along the line of sight, creating the prominent [[fingers-of-god|Fingers of God]] effect. Accurately extracting the linear growth rate from these scales requires advanced modeling of the underlying non-linear velocity field.

A foundational approach to mapping real-space clustering to redshift space is the streaming model, which convolves the real-space [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] with the probability density function (PDF) of line-of-sight peculiar velocities. In its simplest form, the [[gaussian-streaming-model|Gaussian streaming model]] assumes this velocity PDF is purely Gaussian [Fisher et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...448..494F/abstract). However, high-resolution $N$-body simulations have demonstrated that the true [[pairwise-velocity-dispersion|Pairwise velocity dispersion]] exhibits significant non-Gaussian wings. Upgrading the velocity PDF to an exponential or generalized hyperbolic distribution provides a vastly superior fit, significantly extending the model's validity to smaller scales [Kuruvilla et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.2256K/abstract). Moreover, halo clustering in redshift space exhibits a complex dependence on bias, requiring scale-dependent Gaussian streaming models to account for non-linear mappings between real and redshift space [Reid et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.417.1913R/abstract). Furthermore, the pairwise velocity distribution is heavily dependent on the local environment. By splitting [[galaxy-density-fields|Galaxy density fields]] into distinct local density bins, the velocity PDF within each bin becomes nearly Gaussian. This technique mitigates the severe cancellations present in the global average and yields substantially tighter, unbiased constraints on the growth rate [Paillas et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.5731P/abstract), [Gon et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250500608G/abstract).

Beyond phenomenological streaming distributions, analytical frameworks utilize Lagrangian perturbation theory (LPT) to track the non-linear displacement of mass elements. The Zeldovich approximation, representing first-order LPT, serves as a robust baseline for evaluating both non-linear clustering and wide-angle redshift-space distortions [Castorina et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479..741C/abstract), [Taruya et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.4162T/abstract). Advanced LPT models that incorporate the full resummation of long-wavelength infrared displacements can accurately predict the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] and correlation function at the percent level on quasi-linear scales [Chen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...03..100C/abstract).

There is currently an active debate in the literature regarding the optimal framework for modeling these quasi-linear scales. While the Effective Field Theory (EFT) of large-scale structure provides a rigorous perturbative expansion, recent research suggests that non-perturbative methods, such as the generalized hyperbolic streaming model, can outperform standard EFT by pushing the regime of validity down to smaller scales ($k \sim 0.35 \ h \ \text{Mpc}^{-1}$) [Eggemeier et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250118597E/abstract), [Eggemeier et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.112f3532E/abstract). 

Finally, any high-precision non-linear model must address velocity bias, as galaxies do not perfectly trace the underlying dark matter velocity field. Studies utilizing Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD) models reveal that luminous central galaxies are not entirely at rest at the potential minima of their host halos, possessing a velocity dispersion of approximately $30\%$ of the dark matter, while the velocity dispersion of satellite galaxies depends on luminosity, matching the dark matter for luminous samples but dropping to $\approx 85\%$ for faint samples [Guo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.4368G/abstract), [Tinker et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.368...85T/abstract). Explicitly parameterizing these kinematic differences is essential to avoid systematic biases of up to $10\%$ when extracting cosmological parameters from small-scale redshift-space distortions [Tinker et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.374..477T/abstract), [Okumura et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JCAP...11..014O/abstract). Furthermore, when extending models to encompass massive neutrino cosmologies, accurate theoretical predictions of halo clustering in redshift space must assume that halo velocities trace the cold-matter velocity field (CDM + baryons) rather than the total matter field, which includes the neutrino component [Verdiani et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.112d3545V/abstract).

## The Alcock-Paczynski Test and Geometrical Degeneracies

To analyze a [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]], observed angular positions and redshifts must be mapped to three-dimensional comoving coordinates. This transformation requires assuming a fiducial cosmological model. If the assumed model differs from the true cosmology of the Universe, it introduces an artificial, purely geometric distortion into the measured clustering signal. Specifically, the transverse and line-of-sight separations scale differently, governed by the angular diameter distance $D_A(z)$ and the Hubble parameter $H(z)$, respectively. The classic Alcock-Paczynski (AP) test exploits this effect: by assuming the underlying statistical isotropy of the Universe as a "standard sphere," any spurious anisotropy measured in the clustering can be used to constrain the expansion history.

In practice, applying the AP test is complicated by the fact that the true underlying clustering is not perfectly isotropic in redshift space. Peculiar velocities driven by the [[cosmic-velocity-field|Cosmic velocity field]] induce dynamical redshift-space distortions. Both the geometric AP effect and dynamical large-scale infall (the Kaiser effect) generate a quadrupole moment in the [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] and the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]]. A geometrical flattening factor $F$, arising from an incorrect background cosmology, can artificially mimic the signature of gravitational instability, introducing an effective distortion parameter $\beta_{\rm eff} \simeq 0.5(F-1)$ [Ballinger et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.282..877B/abstract). Disentangling this apparent geometric anisotropy from the dynamical anisotropy—which is parameterized by the growth rate of structure [[f-8|fσ8]]—is a primary challenge in extracting cosmological constraints from deep redshift surveys [Matsubara et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...615..573M/abstract), [Marulli et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.2566M/abstract).

Several analytical strategies exist to break this geometric-dynamic degeneracy. The shape of the geometric distortion is strictly dictated by the redshift-distance relation, whereas dynamical distortions exhibit a fundamentally different scale and redshift evolution [Li et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450..807L/abstract). Furthermore, the fully two-dimensional correlation function $\xi(r_\perp, r_\parallel)$ contains distinct features, such as the small-scale "Fingers of God" elongation and wide-angle corrections, that cannot be perfectly reproduced by a simple rescaling of $D_A$ and $H$ [Montanari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvD..86f3503M/abstract). Modern multipole analyses routinely fit $D_A(z)$, $H(z)$, and $f\sigma_8(z)$ simultaneously to isolate the expansion history from the growth rate [Chuang et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435..255C/abstract), [Linder et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvD..89f3525L/abstract). Incorporating higher-order statistics, such as the bispectrum, can further refine this separation by providing additional sensitivity to the true shape of the clustering [Song et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...08..007S/abstract). Beyond the traditional [[alcock-paczynski-test|Alcock-Paczynski test]], the zero-crossing of the two-point correlation function provides another robust geometric standard ruler related to the horizon size at matter-radiation equality, as this scale remains largely unaffected by non-linear evolution and redshift-space distortions [Prada et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1111.2889P/abstract).

While the joint measurement of AP and dynamical distortions has historically supported a flat $\Lambda$CDM concordance model, some recent analyses present intriguing tensions. For instance, tomographic AP tests utilizing the full shape of the redshift-space correlation function from SDSS and BOSS have reported a preference for a dark energy equation of state $w > -1$ at a $4.2\sigma$ level of significance, conflicting with the standard $w=-1$ prediction [Dong et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953...98D/abstract). The literature remains actively divided on whether such tensions indicate new physics beyond the standard model or if they stem from unmodeled systematic errors, such as baryonic feedback or the breakdown of perturbative models in the non-linear regime.

## Growth Rate of Structure and Constraints on Gravity

Redshift-space distortions offer a powerful observational tool for measuring the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] of structure formation, denoted by $f = d \ln \delta_m / d \ln a$, where $a$ is the cosmological scale factor and $\delta_m$ is the matter density contrast. Because galaxies act as biased tracers of the underlying dark matter distribution, linear measurements of redshift-space distortions primarily constrain the parameter combination $\beta = f/b$, leaving a fundamental degeneracy between the growth rate and the linear [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy bias]] $b$. To bypass this degeneracy, the standard observable used in cosmological analyses is the normalized growth rate [[f-8|fσ8]], where $\sigma_8$ is the root-mean-square amplitude of matter fluctuations in spheres of $8 h^{-1} {\rm Mpc}$. This parameter combination is largely independent of galaxy bias on linear scales, providing a robust metric for testing the assembly of large-scale structure [Song et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009JCAP...10..004S/abstract), [Percival et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.393..297P/abstract). However, moving beyond linear theory in RSD models allows for breaking the degeneracy between $f$ and $\sigma_8$, enabling separate estimation of the fluctuation amplitude in Stage-IV surveys [Song et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104d3528S/abstract).

By quantifying the anisotropy in the [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] and the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]], galaxy surveys have mapped the evolution of $f\sigma_8$ across cosmic time. In observing this evolution, the region around $z \approx 0.5$ offers an optimal balance for measuring the gravitational growth index, with $80\%$ of the signal derived from the local growth rate at the galaxy bin location . In the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]], the 6dF Galaxy Survey measured $f\sigma_8 = 0.423 \pm 0.055$ at an effective redshift of $z \approx 0.067$ [Beutler et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.3430B/abstract). At higher redshifts, surveys such as WiggleZ and VIPERS have constrained the growth rate up to $z \approx 0.9$ with precisions approaching 10% [Blake et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.2876B/abstract), [de la Torre et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...557A..54D/abstract). The precision of these growth measurements can be further augmented by incorporating direct measurements of galaxy motions. For instance, self-consistent maximum-likelihood modeling of the cross-covariance between RSD and direct peculiar velocity catalogs (such as in the 6dF Galaxy Survey) can reduce statistical uncertainties on structure growth by up to $50\%$ [Adams et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494.3275A/abstract). Furthermore, the normalized growth rate can be determined to $\sim 15\%$ precision using only peculiar motions (independent of galaxy bias) by combining 6dFGS data with Type Ia supernovae [Johnson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.3926J/abstract).

These measurements serve as a primary laboratory for distinguishing dark energy from modified gravity. In the standard $\Lambda$CDM framework governed by General Relativity, the linear growth rate is well-approximated by the parametrization $f \simeq \Omega_m(a)^\gamma$, where the gravitational growth index is $\gamma \approx 0.55$ [Stril et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.404..239S/abstract). Alternative models, such as $f(R)$ gravity or the Dvali-Gabadadze-Porrati (DGP) braneworld model, predict distinct values for $\gamma$ or introduce scale-dependent growth rates, even when their background expansion histories exactly mimic a cosmological constant [Guzzo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.451..541G/abstract), [Hernández-Aguayo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.2194H/abstract). Current analyses place constraints on $\gamma$ with uncertainties in the range of $\sim 0.04 - 0.10$, providing stringent consistency checks on General Relativity [Stril et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.404..239S/abstract). To formally distinguish between these theories, studies have introduced specific model-independent tests of the concordance GR+$\Lambda$CDM framework, such as the $\epsilon$ (scale-independent consistency check) and $\Upsilon$ (test of scale-dependence) parameters, which can definitively falsify standard models using galaxy redshift surveys [Acquaviva et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvD..82h2001A/abstract).

While the majority of large-scale structure measurements are consistent with a flat $\Lambda$CDM model, the literature currently exhibits notable tensions. Because redshift-space distortions are sensitive to the product of the expansion history and the growth of structure, they can test the consistency of the dark energy equation of state $w$. Analyses of certain datasets, such as the BOSS DR12 sample, have favored a lower amplitude of structure growth than predicted by the Planck $\Lambda$CDM cosmology, exhibiting discrepancies at the $3\sigma$ level [Ruiz et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..91f3009R/abstract). Furthermore, joint analyses utilizing extended [[alcock-paczynski-test|Alcock-Paczynski test]] frameworks alongside redshift-space distortions have reported tensions up to $4.2\sigma$ with the flat $\Lambda$CDM model, instead favoring an equation of state $w > -1$ [Dong et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953...98D/abstract).

It remains an active area of debate whether these tensions point to physics beyond the standard model—such as massive neutrinos, evolving dark energy, or modifications to General Relativity—or if they are artifacts of unmodeled systematic errors. In particular, the breakdown of linear theory on quasi-linear scales requires sophisticated non-linear modeling, and it is actively debated whether current tensions might be resolved by better accounting for baryonic feedback [Kwan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.3570K/abstract) or by utilizing more accurate mapping of non-Gaussian pairwise velocity distributions [Kuruvilla et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.2256K/abstract).

## Relativistic Effects and Wide-Angle Formalism

The standard Newtonian treatment of redshift-space distortions relies heavily on the plane-parallel (or "distant observer") approximation, which assumes a fixed line of sight for all galaxy pairs. However, as the [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] is measured over increasingly large comoving separations (often $>1$ Gpc) and wide angular extents in modern surveys, this approximation breaks down. Wide-angle effects arise because the line-of-sight direction varies significantly across the survey footprint, which alters the predicted angular dependence of the clustering signal. Theoretical treatments that drop this approximation—often utilizing exact spherical Fourier-Bessel expansions—demonstrate that wide-angle corrections can account for up to $\sim 10\%$ of the total signal on large scales at high redshifts [Bertacca et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JCAP...10..025B/abstract), [Castorina et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.4403C/abstract). 

Moving beyond Newtonian kinematics, the mapping from real space to redshift space along the past light cone is also sensitive to sub-dominant relativistic effects. While the primary distortion is driven by the line-of-sight component of the [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]], a complete relativistic treatment includes [[gravitational-redshift|Gravitational redshift]], Doppler magnification, transverse Doppler effects, and integrated Sachs-Wolfe terms [Bonvin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014CQGra..31w4002B/abstract), [Bertacca et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014JCAP...09..037B/abstract). Doppler magnification, for example, alters the observed apparent size and flux of galaxies, creating a dipolar pattern that can dominate over standard gravitational lensing magnification at low redshifts ($z \lesssim 0.5$) [Bonvin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.3936B/abstract). Furthermore, performing measurements in the observer's standard of rest generates a kinematic dipole overdensity, sometimes termed the "finger of the observer" effect [Elkhashab et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.1626E/abstract).

In the standard Kaiser framework, the correlation function possesses reflection symmetry, ensuring that odd [[legendre-multipoles|Legendre multipoles]] (such as the dipole and octupole) vanish. Relativistic effects inherently break this symmetry. Gravitational redshift and Doppler effects generate a predictable, non-zero dipole in the cross-correlation between different biased galaxy populations, such as bright and faint galaxies, or varying halo masses [Bonvin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014CQGra..31w4002B/abstract), [Saga et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498..981S/abstract). The first observational detection of this relativistic redshift asymmetry was achieved using the cross-correlation of BOSS CMASS galaxies at a significance of $\sim 2.7\sigma$ on scales of $\sim 10 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Alam et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.2822A/abstract). At quasi-linear and non-linear scales, however, modeling this asymmetry remains a complex theoretical challenge. The non-linear coupling between gravitational potential and velocity terms introduces ambiguities; current N-body simulations indicate a non-trivial sign flip in the gravitational redshift dipole at separations $\lesssim 20 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$, underscoring the necessity of further simulation-based verification [Breton et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.2671B/abstract), [Saga et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498..981S/abstract). Additionally, vector perturbations, particularly vorticity induced by non-linear clustering, contribute to the correlation function multipoles (especially the hexadecapole) and should be included in future high-precision analyses [Bonvin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JCAP...02..028B/abstract).

The breakdown of the plane-parallel approximation and the inclusion of relativistic terms also heavily impact [[higher-order-clustering-statistics|Higher-Order Clustering Statistics]]. Relativistic redshift-space distortions generate a guaranteed odd-parity signal in both the [[galaxy-bispectrum-multipoles|Galaxy bispectrum multipoles]] and the [[four-point-correlation-function|Four-point correlation function]] [Di Dio et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...04..053D/abstract), [Jeong et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.102b3530J/abstract). Specifically, leading relativistic corrections generate a significant dipole in the galaxy bispectrum, predominantly from relativistic redshift-space distortions, which can reach $10\%$ of the monopole amplitude [Clarkson et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486L.101C/abstract). Recent reports of apparent parity violation in the observed galaxy trispectrum highlight the need for careful theoretical modeling; sub-leading relativistic redshift-space distortions naturally produce parity-violating signatures that dominate on large scales and could mimic primordial new physics if not properly accounted for [Paul et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvL.133l1001P/abstract). 

For upcoming Stage-IV galaxy surveys, fully modeling these wide-angle and relativistic light-cone effects is no longer optional. Recent theoretical work has extended relativistic number count derivations to third order, including all post-Born lensing distortions and projection effects [Gervani et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.112h3541G/abstract). The failure to account for these subtle geometric and relativistic distortions will inevitably bias the extraction of the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] and the constraints placed on primordial non-Gaussianity [Bertacca et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JCAP...10..025B/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A..85E/abstract).

## Higher-Order Clustering and Alternative Probes

While analyses of redshift-space distortions (RSD) have historically focused on the [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] and the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]], these linear statistics suffer from a fundamental degeneracy between the linear growth rate of structure $f$ and the linear galaxy bias $b$. Because linear RSD primarily constrains the parameter combination $\beta = f/b$ (or equivalently [[f-8|fσ8]]), breaking this degeneracy requires the use of [[higher-order-clustering-statistics|Higher-Order Clustering Statistics]] or the integration of alternative cosmological tracers.

The [[three-point-correlation-function|Three-point correlation function]] and its Fourier-space counterpart, the bispectrum, are critical tools for disentangling galaxy bias from the matter density $\Omega_m$ and the growth rate [Verde et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.300..747V/abstract), [Zheng et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...614..527Z/abstract). On small scales, the monopole of the redshift-space bispectrum exhibits a strong configuration dependence driven by the velocity dispersions of galaxy clusters, explicitly breaking the hierarchical clustering ansatz in redshift space [Scoccimarro et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...517..531S/abstract). Recent advances in computing [[galaxy-bispectrum-multipoles|Galaxy bispectrum multipoles]] have enabled precise extractions of the anisotropic signal, culminating in the $14\sigma$ detection of the anisotropic $L=2$ mode in the bispectrum of the BOSS DR12 sample [Sugiyama et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484..364S/abstract). The practical application of higher-order statistics has also been advanced by the development of $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ scaling algorithms for computing the anisotropic [[three-point-correlation-function|Three-point correlation function]] by expanding the density field into spherical harmonics [Slepian et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.1468S/abstract). 

Furthermore, the inclusion of relativistic effects in higher-order statistics has resolved emerging theoretical anomalies. Relativistic RSD effects (such as the Doppler and [[gravitational-redshift|Gravitational redshift]] effects) generate a guaranteed parity-odd signal in the bispectrum and [[four-point-correlation-function|Four-point correlation function]] (trispectrum) that is strictly absent in standard Newtonian perturbation theory [Jeong et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190605198J/abstract), [Jeong et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.102b3530J/abstract). Recent reports of apparent parity violation in the observed galaxy trispectrum highlight the need for careful theoretical modeling; sub-leading relativistic redshift-space distortions naturally produce parity-violating signatures that dominate on large scales and could mimic primordial new physics if not properly accounted for [Paul et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvL.133l1001P/abstract).

Alternative tracers, most notably cosmic voids, offer highly competitive environments for testing RSD. Because voids are largely empty of virialized structures, the void-galaxy cross-correlation function is significantly less susceptible to non-linear [[fingers-of-god|Fingers of God]] effects. Consequently, simple linear theory can accurately describe RSD around voids down to scales of $\approx 2 \, h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Hamaus et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JCAP...07..014H/abstract). Measurements of the distortion parameter $\beta$ derived from void-galaxy cross-correlations yield high-precision constraints (e.g., $\beta = 0.36 \pm 0.05$ for the BOSS CMASS sample) that complement standard clustering [Achitouv et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvD.100l3513A/abstract). While high-redshift void constraints perfectly match $\Lambda$CDM predictions, a mild $2\sigma$ to $3\sigma$ deviation has been reported at lower redshifts ($z \approx 0.32$) [Hamaus et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JCAP...07..014H/abstract). To maintain this precision in future surveys, void RSD models must also incorporate the systematic expansion and line-of-sight off-centering induced by void dynamics, as well as the intrinsic ellipticity of the voids themselves [Correa et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.1871C/abstract).

Finally, the intrinsic alignment of galaxies—traditionally treated purely as a systematic contaminant in weak lensing—has emerged as a viable alternative probe of the [[cosmic-velocity-field|Cosmic velocity field]]. The anisotropic correlation of galaxy shapes (ellipticities) with the density field encodes both the [[alcock-paczynski-test|Alcock-Paczynski test]] signature and RSD effects. N-body simulations have demonstrated that linear alignment models successfully capture these anisotropies, allowing the extraction of the growth rate directly from intrinsic alignment statistics [Okumura et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494..694O/abstract). In addition to line-of-sight kinematics, there is growing interest in utilizing transverse peculiar velocities (proper motions) as a statistical cross-correlation probe of structure growth, providing a complementary signal to standard radial RSD [Duncan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E...9D/abstract). Another emerging approach involves the line correlation function, a tool designed to capture non-linear phase information in both the density and velocity fields, serving as a powerful complement to the standard two-point correlation function [Franco et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvD..99j3530F/abstract). Furthermore, researchers are exploring topological measures such as the genus, which can serve as a robust cosmic standard ruler that remains largely insensitive to non-linear evolution, galaxy bias, and redshift-space distortions [Park et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...715L.185P/abstract). Additionally, linear redshift distortions can enhance the integrated Sachs-Wolfe (ISW) galaxy-temperature cross-correlation signal, particularly at higher redshifts [Rassat et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0902.1759R/abstract).

## Systematic Uncertainties and Observational Challenges

Extracting robust cosmological constraints from redshift-space distortions (RSD) requires exquisite control over numerous [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]]. While linear theory provides a foundational framework, translating observed anisotropies into precise measurements of the growth rate of structure is complicated by astrophysical phenomena and observational limitations. 

Chief among the astrophysical challenges is the degeneracy between the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] $f$ and the linear [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy bias]] $b$. In the linear regime, the RSD anisotropy is primarily sensitive to the parameter $\beta$, defined as:
$$ \beta = \frac{f}{b} \approx \frac{\Omega_m^{0.6}}{b} $$
where $\Omega_m$ is the matter density parameter [Kaiser et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987MNRAS.227....1K/abstract). Consequently, measurements of the [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] in redshift space typically constrain the parameter combination [[f-8|fσ8]], where $\sigma_8$ is the amplitude of mass fluctuations. Isolating $f$ requires independent constraints on $b$, which can be achieved through higher-order statistics like the bispectrum or cross-correlations with weak lensing [Verde et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.300..747V/abstract), [Susperregi et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...546...85S/abstract). Furthermore, galaxy bias modeling is complicated by velocity bias; N-body simulations and Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD) modeling reveal that luminous central galaxies are not perfectly at rest within their host dark matter halos, exhibiting velocity dispersions roughly $30\%$ of the dark matter dispersion [Guo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.4368G/abstract).

On small, non-linear scales, the virialized motions of satellite galaxies produce the characteristic [[fingers-of-god|Fingers of God]] elongation. Modeling this regime is highly sensitive to the observational footprint, particularly due to fiber collisions. In multi-object spectroscopic surveys, the physical size of optical fibers prevents the simultaneous observation of target galaxies separated by small angular distances. This hardware limitation disproportionately removes close pairs—often satellite galaxies residing in dense environments—which artificially alters the empirical HOD [Hikage et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013JCAP...08..019H/abstract). If uncorrected, this depletion suppresses the 1-halo term and the higher-order [[legendre-multipoles|Legendre multipoles]] (such as the hexadecapole) of the power spectrum, biasing the inferred velocity dispersion [Hikage et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013JCAP...08..019H/abstract).

Inhomogeneous survey geometry and the radial [[redshift-distribution|selection function]] introduce additional layers of complexity. Variations in targeting efficiency must be meticulously modeled, as unmitigated angular systematics can spuriously enhance large-scale clustering. Techniques such as nulling angular modes in the correlation function have been proposed to remove unknown angular systematics, albeit at the cost of increasing statistical uncertainties on $f\sigma_8$ by up to $50\%$ [Paviot et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.1341P/abstract). Furthermore, for next-generation large-volume surveys, the selection function must account for relativistic projection effects, magnification bias, and evolution bias—the latter quantifying the physical change in the comoving galaxy number density relative to a conserved population [Maartens et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...12..009M/abstract), [Dam et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.108j3505D/abstract).

Finally, the accuracy of the assumed distances is strictly governed by [[source-redshift-uncertainty|redshift measurement uncertainties]]. In spectroscopic surveys, redshift errors are typically small (e.g., $\sigma_z \sim 0.002$ at $z=1$) and can be modeled with a Gaussian-convolved double exponential distribution [Guo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.4368G/abstract), producing a minimal systematic bias on $\beta$ compared to intrinsic non-linearities [Marulli et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.2566M/abstract). Conversely, photometric surveys suffer from much larger $\sigma_z$ errors, which heavily suppress line-of-sight clustering modes. However, RSD can still be extracted from photometric data using angular cross-correlations between narrow redshift bins [Ross et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.2193R/abstract), [Asorey et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.445.2825A/abstract). By heavily down-weighting the radial modes, the large photometric uncertainties mitigate the impact of non-linear small-scale RSD, allowing robust growth constraints with forecasted $1\sigma$ accuracies on $f\sigma_8$ of approximately $(17 \times b)\%$ (where $b$ is the linear bias) for Stage-III photometric surveys like DES [Ross et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.2193R/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

As observational astrophysics enters the era of Stage-IV surveys, such as the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), Euclid, and the Square Kilometre Array (SKA), the primary goal of redshift-space distortion (RSD) analyses is to achieve sub-percent precision in measurements of the dark energy equation of state $w$ and the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] of structure $f$ [Denissenya et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JCAP...06..030D/abstract), [Guzzo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180310814G/abstract). Reaching this precision requires addressing several open questions regarding theoretical modeling, systematic errors, and the breaking of cosmological parameter degeneracies.

A pressing issue in current RSD literature is a 2 to 4$\sigma$ tension observed in some datasets, which favor a lower growth amplitude ([[f-8|fσ8]] $\approx 0.8$) or an equation of state $w > -1$ compared to the flat $\Lambda$CDM predictions of the *Planck* concordance model [Ruiz et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..91f3009R/abstract), [Dong et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953...98D/abstract). It remains an open question whether these anomalies are early signatures of modified gravity or the result of unmodeled systematic errors. For instance, the impact of baryonic feedback on intermediate and small scales ($k > 0.1 \ h\text{Mpc}^{-1}$) can bias quadrupole measurements by up to $10\%$, necessitating careful mitigation in future dark-matter-only models [Kwan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.3570K/abstract). Similarly, distinguishing between perturbative Effective Field Theory (EFT) approaches and non-perturbative streaming models in the quasi-linear regime remains an area of active theoretical debate as models are pushed to smaller scales [Eggemeier et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.112f3532E/abstract).

To robustly isolate dark energy physics and break the standard degeneracy between $f$ and the linear galaxy bias $b$, the community is increasingly investing in joint probes and multi-tracer methodologies. A prominent roadmap involves the [[3x2pt-analysis|3x2pt analysis]], which combines RSD measurements from a [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] with [[cosmic-shear|Cosmic shear]] and galaxy-galaxy lensing. Such cross-correlations effectively untangle the $f\sigma_8$ combination, providing independent constraints on the amplitude of matter fluctuations $\sigma_8$ and the growth rate $f$ [de la Torre et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...608A..44D/abstract), [Perenon et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...642A.116P/abstract). Furthermore, cross-correlating traditional optical catalogs with 21-cm line intensity mapping from the SKA is expected to yield unprecedented precision, bypassing limitations inherent to single-tracer auto-correlations [Song et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251211353S/abstract).

The vast cosmological volumes mapped by next-generation surveys also require a paradigm shift from the traditional plane-parallel approximation to exact wide-angle and full-sky relativistic modeling. Wide-angle effects and relativistic corrections—such as gravitational redshift and Doppler magnification—introduce distinct asymmetries in the [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]], manifesting as non-zero dipoles in the cross-correlation of different galaxy populations [Bonvin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014CQGra..31w4002B/abstract), [Alam et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.2822A/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A..85E/abstract). Disentangling these relativistic signals from standard Doppler RSD provides a unique, model-independent test of the equivalence principle and General Relativity on horizon scales.

Finally, the exploration of [[higher-order-clustering-statistics|Higher-Order Clustering Statistics]] offers a frontier for discovering new physics. While the bispectrum is already utilized to break bias degeneracies, the [[four-point-correlation-function|Four-point correlation function]] (and the related [[matter-trispectrum|Matter trispectrum]]) has recently garnered attention due to apparent parity-violating signals. An ongoing open question is the extent to which these parity-odd signatures are driven by primordial physics versus sub-leading relativistic RSD effects [Paul et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvL.133l1001P/abstract). Leveraging these statistics, alongside novel observational channels such as the luminosity-distance space distortions of gravitational waves [Namikawa et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...01..036N/abstract), will define the next decade of RSD research.
