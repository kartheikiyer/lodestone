**Star-forming galaxies** are systems characterized by the active conversion of cold molecular gas into [[young-stellar-populations|Young Stellar Populations]]. Because massive [[ob-stars|OB stars]] have short main-sequence lifetimes, their presence dictates the observational signatures of these galaxies. Consequently, they are typically identified using [[star-formation-rate-tracers|Star formation rate tracers]] such as intense rest-frame ultraviolet continua, nebular recombination lines (e.g., H$\alpha$), and infrared emission radiating from dust-enshrouded regions [Calzetti et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001PASP..113.1449C/abstract). In the local universe, the vast majority of these systems populate the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]], exhibiting a tight, roughly linear correlation between their total [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] and their [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]]. This sequence implies that most galactic stellar mass is assembled via steady, continuous [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] rather than stochastic, violent episodes, distinguishing them fundamentally from the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] of [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]].

The physics governing this conversion of gas into stars is broadly parameterized by empirical scaling relations, most notably the Kennicutt-Schmidt law. While the total molecular gas content dictates a non-linear relationship with the star formation rate surface density, observations reveal that the availability of *dense* molecular gas scales linearly with star formation across many orders of magnitude [Wu et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...635L.173W/abstract), [Gao et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..237..331G/abstract). This leads to a recognized dichotomy in the modes of star formation. The majority of systems, such as mature [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]], undergo steady, "quiescent" star formation regulated by internal secular processes and inside-out disk growth. Conversely, extreme enhancements in [[average-star-formation-efficiency|Average star formation efficiency]] manifest as [[violent-starbursts|Violent starbursts]] and [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]]. These outliers are often triggered by dynamical instabilities or [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] that strip angular momentum from the gas, funneling massive quantities of material into dense nuclear cores.

From a cosmological perspective, the properties and abundance of star-forming galaxies trace the overarching [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]]. The global star formation rate density rose rapidly in the early universe, peaking approximately ten billion years ago during "Cosmic Noon" ($z \sim 1.5-3$) [Madau et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..148...88M/abstract). While rest-frame ultraviolet surveys initially constrained early stellar assembly, recent submillimeter observations have unveiled a massive population of [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|High-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies]] that are entirely optically invisible but contribute significantly to the early universe's stellar mass budget. The subsequent morphological evolution of these active galaxies into local early-type systems is mediated by central compaction and complex feedback mechanisms—including supernova-driven superwinds and [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]]—which regulate [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] and eventually strip or heat the gas reservoirs. 

This article provides a comprehensive overview of the physical properties, structural assembly, and cosmic evolution of star-forming galaxies. It explores the varied multi-wavelength indicators used to measure [[integrated-star-formation|Integrated star formation]], the molecular gas scaling laws that drive it, and the environmental and internal processes that precipitate the transition to quiescence (such as the intermediate phases seen in [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]]). Finally, it surveys active open questions in the field, including the precise mechanisms of morphological compaction prior to quenching, the universality of the [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] in extreme environments, the true census of optically dark early-universe galaxies now being uncovered by JWST, and the role of dense starbursts as sources of multi-messenger high-energy neutrino emission.

## Observational Signatures and Star Formation Indicators

The identification and characterization of star-forming galaxies rely on tracing [[young-stellar-populations|Young Stellar Populations]] across the electromagnetic spectrum. Because massive [[ob-stars|OB stars]] have short main-sequence lifetimes ($<10^8$ yr), their emission directly tracks recent star formation. Consequently, the primary [[star-formation-rate-tracers|Star formation rate tracers]] involve the direct ultraviolet (UV) continuum from these stars, nebular recombination lines from the surrounding ionized gas, and the infrared (IR) emission from dust that has reprocessed the stellar radiation. The physical properties of these ionized regions evolve significantly with cosmic time; for example, average electron densities in $z \sim 1.5$ galaxies are approximately 5 times higher than their local counterparts, an enhancement largely driven by their elevated [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] [Kaasinen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.3220K/abstract). At the shorter wavelength extremes, the global far-ultraviolet properties of star-forming galaxies—specifically in the relatively unexplored spectral region below $1200$ \AA—have been documented by the Hopkins Ultraviolet Telescope (HUT) Astro-2 atlas, which catalogs both continuum and line properties in the $912-1800$ \AA\ range [Leitherer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJS..140..303L/abstract).

In the optical regime, the H$\alpha$ recombination line at $6563$ \AA\ is a widely used star formation indicator. A foundational dataset for population synthesis modeling of this nebular emission is the integrated optical spectrophotometric survey of 417 nearby [[low-redshift-star-forming-galaxies|Low-redshift star-forming galaxies]], which provides a comprehensive baseline for local systems [Moustakas et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJS..164...81M/abstract). The H$\alpha$ equivalent width is particularly valuable as a tracer of the [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|Unusually high specific star formation rates]], providing a measure of current star formation relative to the underlying older stellar continuum [Lee et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...671L.113L/abstract). While radial H$\alpha$ profiles often show a sharp truncation in the [[outer-disk-star-formation|Outer disk star formation]], there is active debate over whether this H$\alpha$ cut-off represents a genuine physical suppression of star formation or an observational artifact driven by the stochastic, clustered nature of star formation at low gas surface densities [Pflamm-Altenburg et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455..641P/abstract).

A persistent challenge in quantifying integrated star formation is [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|Dust-Obscured Star Formation and Galaxies]], which removes approximately half of the stellar UV energy budget in the local universe and severely obscures young stellar populations [Calzetti et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001PASP..113.1449C/abstract). While the optical Balmer decrement (H$\alpha$/H$\beta$) is commonly used to correct for this, it frequently underestimates the true extinction in heavily enshrouded regions. Furthermore, the assumption of energy balance between dust attenuation and emission is frequently invalid for [[emission-line-galaxies|Emission-line galaxies]] at $z \sim 1.2-1.9$, complicating efforts to derive robust star formation rates solely from hybrid UV+IR indicators [Nagaraj et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913...34N/abstract). Consequently, near-infrared recombination lines such as Paschen-$\beta$ ($1.28$ $\mu$m) have proven superior for mapping spatially resolved obscured star formation, successfully probing regions with $>1$ mag more attenuation than can be traced by optical lines alone [Giménez-Arteaga et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..263...17G/abstract).

In the mid-infrared (MIR), the emission spectra of star-forming galaxies are typically dominated by broad features from Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs) spanning wavelengths of $3-20$ $\mu$m. The strong redshift dependence of these features passing through photometric bands results in significant color variations, which can now be utilized to identify star-forming galaxies out to high redshifts [Langeroodi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946L..40L/abstract). However, PAH emission is strongly dependent on the interstellar environment; for instance, [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|Star-forming dwarf galaxies]] exhibit a pronounced deficit in $8.0$ $\mu$m PAH emission compared to their more massive, metal-rich spiral counterparts [Hong et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...717..503H/abstract).

At the extremes of galaxy evolution, relying solely on rest-frame UV or optical indicators can lead to severe observational biases. Submillimeter and radio observations have revealed a dominant population of massive [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|High-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies]] at $z > 3$ that are entirely optically invisible. These systems, which can exhibit star formation rates exceeding $200\,M_{\odot}\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, are missed by traditional UV surveys and may account for up to $40\%$ of the total cosmic star formation rate density in the early universe [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.572..211W/abstract), [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract). Spectroscopic verification in the IR and submillimeter remains essential, as the highest-redshift candidate galaxies identified photometrically can sometimes be lower-redshift interlopers masquerading as early-universe objects due to unusual dust and nebular properties [Arrabal Haro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Natur.622..707A/abstract).

## The Star-Forming Main Sequence and Cosmic Evolution

Most star-forming galaxies exhibit a tight, roughly linear correlation between their [[stellar-component|Stellar component]] ($M_*$) and their [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] (SFR). This defining relationship, known as the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]], acts as a critical benchmark for characterizing galaxy growth. The sequence is frequently parameterized by the [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|Unusually high specific star formation rates]], defined as $\text{sSFR} \equiv \text{SFR}/M_*$. The relatively narrow scatter of the main sequence (typically $\sim 0.3$ dex) implies that the bulk of stellar mass assembly occurs via steady-state, secular [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] rather than violent, episodic events. Some studies suggest that SFR variations on the main sequence may originate from oscillations in accretion flows regulated by the stellar surface density [He et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250506868H/abstract). In this context, morphological asymmetry serves as a powerful predictor of star formation rate residuals for galaxies on the main sequence, as structural disturbances closely track temporary enhancements in stellar birth [Yesuf et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923..205Y/abstract). The complex interplay of multi-wavelength emissions along this sequence is further highlighted by the ultraviolet-infrared color-magnitude relation of star-forming galaxies, where flux ratios span over two orders of magnitude and show a clear dependence on absolute magnitude from $M_{W3} \sim -13$ to $-25$ [Brown et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018RNAAS...2..217B/abstract).

The normalization of the main sequence evolves fundamentally with cosmic time, tracking the overarching [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]]. At a fixed stellar mass, the sSFR increases significantly toward higher redshifts, reflecting the more abundant gas reservoirs of the early Universe. At epochs prior to the cosmic peak of star formation ($z > 3$), deep multi-wavelength surveys have uncovered a dominant population of [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|High-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies]] that are optically invisible but may account for up to 40% of the early star formation rate density [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.572..211W/abstract), [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract). Capable of converting gas into stars at rates exceeding $1000~M_\odot\,\text{yr}^{-1}$, these massive dust-obscured systems are likely the progenitors of the most massive present-day [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] [Riechers et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.496..329R/abstract).

To understand the distribution of galaxies in the SFR--$M_*$ plane, a "2-Star Formation Mode" framework is often adopted [Sargent et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793...19S/abstract). Under this paradigm, the main sequence represents steady, inside-out disk growth, while a secondary, rarer population of [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|Starburst Galaxy Evolution]] scatters significantly above the sequence. These starbursts—which include local [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] and extreme high-redshift systems—exhibit SFRs elevated by a factor of $\gtrsim 4$ relative to the main sequence [Silverman et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812L..23S/abstract). Rather than simply possessing larger gas fractions, starburst outliers exhibit a non-linear enhancement in [[average-star-formation-efficiency|Average star formation efficiency]] (SFE), plausibly triggered by [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] or global disk instabilities that funnel cold gas into dense nuclear cores [Sargent et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793...19S/abstract).

The trajectory of a galaxy along the main sequence is heavily dictated by its mass, a paradigm known as [[morphological-downsizing|Morphological downsizing]] or mass-accelerated evolution [Fang et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...858..100F/abstract). Massive galaxies formed the bulk of their stellar mass early in cosmic history and [[quiescent-galaxies|quenched]] rapidly, transitioning out of the star-forming population when the Universe was half its current age [Gonçalves et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...67G/abstract). Conversely, lower-mass galaxies exhibit more extended star formation histories, retain higher specific star formation rates in the local Universe, and progressively lose their cold gas reservoirs over much longer timescales [Calura et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...682..252C/abstract), [Villar et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...740...47V/abstract), [Pacifici et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...824...45P/abstract). Measurements of [[integrated-star-formation|Integrated star formation]] in 44 nearby [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]] in the local volume indicate that their specific star formation rates generally do not exceed $\log \text{SSFR} = -9.2\ \text{yr}^{-1}$ [Kaisin et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AstBu..68..381K/abstract). For the closest systems, such as Local Group dwarf galaxies, cool evolved stars pulsating in the fundamental mode are now being used to reliably reconstruct these extended star formation histories and dust production rates [Mahani et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IAUS..373..264M/abstract). Furthermore, halo mass tracking models suggest that typical $z \sim 2$ star-forming galaxies on the main sequence will ultimately evolve into Milky Way-like, $\sim L^*$ galaxies in the local universe [Conroy et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...679.1192C/abstract).

At high stellar masses, specific star formation rates drop rapidly, signaling a gradual suppression of star formation as galaxies transition to quiescence [Eales et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.3125E/abstract). Consequently, while local massive galaxy populations are dominated by quiescent systems, dusty star-forming galaxies completely dominate the massive galaxy population ($\log(M_*/M_\odot) > 10.3$) at early epochs ($z > 2$) [Martis et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..25M/abstract). While galaxy evolution is traditionally modeled as a strict bimodality separating the star-forming blue cloud from [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]], there remains active debate in the literature regarding this transition. Some authors argue against two distinct populations, instead proposing a unitary "Galaxy End Sequence" in which galaxies evolve gradually along a strongly curved, continuous track with no clear structural break between star-forming and passive states [Eales et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.3125E/abstract).

## Modes of Star Formation: Quiescent Disks and Violent Starbursts

Galactic star formation is broadly characterized by a "two-mode" framework: a steady, quiescent mode operating within rotationally supported [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], and a transient starburst mode characterized by significantly elevated efficiencies [Sargent et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793...19S/abstract). 

In the quiescent mode, which is typical of [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]], star formation proceeds gradually. The global gas consumption timescale—defined as the ratio of total gas mass to the star formation rate, $\tau_{\rm SF} \equiv M_{\rm gas} / \dot{M}_\star$—is typically on the order of $\tau_{\rm SF} \simeq (1 - 5) \times 10^9$ yr. This mode populates the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]], where [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|Unusually high specific star formation rates]] (SSFRs) are relatively modest (e.g., $\sim 0.1$ Gyr$^{-1}$ in the local Universe) and are primarily regulated by internal secular processes and the steady [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] from the cosmic web. At the lower mass end, [[giant-low-surface-brightness-galaxies|Giant low surface brightness galaxies]] represent an extreme late-type population that is heavily dark-matter dominated and supports very low, widespread star formation efficiencies [de Blok et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..117...39D/abstract).

In contrast, the starburst mode exhibits SSFRs that are 10 to 100 times higher, rapidly depleting molecular gas reservoirs on timescales of $\tau_{\rm SF} \sim 10^7 - 10^8$ yr. These [[violent-starbursts|Violent starbursts]] are traditionally understood to be driven by external dynamical perturbations, particularly [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] or strong tidal encounters, which strip gas of its angular momentum and funnel it into dense central concentrations. Because the formation of massive stars produces substantial quantities of dust, these events are heavily obscured at optical and ultraviolet wavelengths, re-radiating the bulk of their stellar luminosity in the far-infrared. At the extreme end, these events manifest as [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]], defined by $L_{\rm FIR} > 10^{11} L_\odot$, and Ultraluminous Infrared Galaxies (ULIRGs) with $L_{\rm FIR} > 10^{12} L_\odot$. During these violent interactions, gas fractions in LIRGs rise moderately toward advanced merger stages, robustly placing them above the star-forming main sequence [Shangguan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...870..104S/abstract). Despite the extreme conditions in ULIRGs, it has been highlighted that it is physically possible to scale the physics of smaller, more common star-forming events upward to encompass these massive starbursts [O'Connell et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph.12585O/abstract). These extreme mergers and ULIRGs are widely considered to be the direct progenitors of local [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] cores; furthermore, the tidal debris ejected presents a fertile environment for the subsequent formation of [[accreted-dwarf-galaxies|Accreted dwarf galaxies]]. Indeed, actively star-forming tidal dwarf candidates have been definitively identified in the tidal tails of [[compact-galaxies|Compact Galaxies]] [Iglesias-Páramo et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...550..204I/abstract), [Mirabel et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002luml.conf..108M/abstract).

The physical distinction between these modes is strongly reflected in the scaling relations of their interstellar media. While the total molecular gas mass (traced by CO emission) exhibits a non-linear Kennicutt-Schmidt relation with the star formation rate surface density ($\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$), the relationship between [[dense-gas-tracers|Dense gas tracers]] (such as HCN) and infrared luminosity is remarkably linear over eight to nine orders of magnitude. This linear scaling holds continuously from local galactic dense cores up to [[hyper-luminous-infrared-galaxies|Hyper-luminous infrared galaxies]] [Gao et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..237..331G/abstract), [Wu et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...635L.173W/abstract). During a starburst episode, the enhancement in star formation efficiency scales supra-linearly with the SFR increase, accompanied by a reduction in the overall gas fraction by a factor of two to three relative to their main-sequence progenitors [Sargent et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793...19S/abstract).

Although standard theoretical models posit that ULIRGs and massive starbursts are confined to compact nuclear volumes (typically $< 2$ kpc) due to merger-driven inflows, the literature reveals a more complex morphological landscape. Resolved H$\alpha$ imaging indicates that an appreciable fraction of luminous starbursts host [[spatially-extended-star-formation|Spatially extended star formation]], challenging the ubiquity of the purely nuclear, interaction-induced starburst paradigm [Hattori et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127..736H/abstract). Moreover, double-peak emission-line galaxies often show central star formation enhancements driven by recent minor mergers, adding to the diversity of central starburst triggers [Maschmann et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...664A.125M/abstract).

Finally, these extreme star-forming environments act as profound sources of non-thermal emission. Owing to their high supernova rates and strong interstellar magnetic fields, starburst galaxies are prominent reservoirs of cosmic rays. Consequently, they are expected to emit heavily in the high-energy regime via hadronic collisions and have been proposed as primary contributors to the diffuse high-energy gamma-ray and sub-PeV/PeV neutrino backgrounds [Tamborra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014JCAP...09..043T/abstract), [Ambrosone et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919L..32A/abstract), [Chang et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...805...95C/abstract). One such notable nearby example is the [[circinus-galaxy|Circinus Galaxy]].

## Molecular Gas Reservoirs and Scaling Laws

The macroscopic conversion of cold gas into stars within [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]] is empirically parameterized by the Kennicutt-Schmidt law. In an overarching evolutionary context, galaxy [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] controls the cold gas content and ultimately dictates the star formation rates of the main sequence population across cosmic time [Tacconi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ARA&A..58..157T/abstract). This scaling relation links the star formation rate surface density ($\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$) to the gas surface density ($\Sigma_{\rm gas}$) via a power law:
$$ \Sigma_{\rm SFR} = A \Sigma_{\rm gas}^N $$
where the index $N$ is typically found to be in the range of 1.0 to 2.0 when considering the total (atomic plus molecular) gas reservoir averaged over entire galactic disks. Interestingly, atomic hydrogen (traced in [[h-i-rich-galaxies|H I-rich galaxies]]) has dominated the cold gas mass budget of star-forming galaxies for at least the past three billion years, even as the global star formation rate steadily declined [Cortese et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848L...7C/abstract). The timescale on which a galaxy converts its available gas into stars is the gas depletion time, defined as $\tau_{\rm SF} \equiv M_{\rm gas} / \dot{M}_\star$. In normal spiral galaxies, $\tau_{\rm SF}$ is typically $(1-5) \times 10^9$ yr, whereas in extreme starbursts, the depletion timescale plummets to $10^7 - 10^8$ yr, which is comparable to the local dynamical time of the cold gas. Morphologically, within halo centers, star-forming gas is typically more flattened than the stars and dark matter, particularly for rotationally supported [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] [Hill et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505...65H/abstract). Furthermore, molecular gas extending into the outskirts of galaxies often traces spiral arms and serves to probe recent galaxy interactions or ram pressure stripping events [Watson et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161205275W/abstract).

While CO emission is the traditional tracer for the total molecular hydrogen (${\rm H}_2$) mass, observations of [[dense-gas-tracers|Dense gas tracers]], such as the $J=1\rightarrow0$ rotational transition of HCN, reveal a more fundamental scaling. There is a tight, linear correlation ($N \simeq 1$) between the dense molecular gas mass (traced by HCN luminosity) and the SFR (traced by infrared luminosity). This linear relationship extends over seven to nine orders of magnitude in luminosity, bridging the scales of local Galactic dense cores to high-redshift [[hyper-luminous-infrared-galaxies|Hyper-luminous infrared galaxies]] [Wu et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...635L.173W/abstract), [Gao et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..237..331G/abstract). In contrast, the correlation between the total molecular gas (CO) and the SFR is systematically non-linear. This suggests that the global star formation rate is governed primarily by a galaxy's capacity to form dense [[molecular-clouds-and-gas-clumping|Molecular Clouds and Gas Clumping]], rather than its total molecular gas content.

Recent spatially resolved surveys of nearby galaxies have challenged the assumption of a universal, density-threshold model for star formation. High-resolution mapping from the [[phangs-survey|PHANGS survey]] reveals that earlier-type massive galaxies frequently host significant CO-bright molecular reservoirs that entirely lack associated H$\alpha$ emission, implying that the presence of bulk molecular gas alone does not guarantee active star formation [Pan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927....9P/abstract). Furthermore, the [[dense-gas-fraction|Dense gas fraction]], observationally traced by the HCN-to-CO ratio, and the star formation efficiency of the dense gas, traced by the IR-to-HCN ratio, show systematic environmental variations across galactic disks. Specifically, the HCN-to-CO ratio (often $\sim 1/30$ globally) is found to correlate strongly with both the stellar surface density and the molecular-to-atomic gas ratio over a dynamic range of more than two orders of magnitude [Usero et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015AJ....150..115U/abstract). Paradoxically, the IR-to-HCN ratio systematically decreases in the high-surface-density, molecule-rich central regions of disks, dropping by factors of 6-8 compared to [[galaxy-outskirts|Galaxy outskirts]] [Usero et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015AJ....150..115U/abstract). In the Local Group, high-resolution mapping of the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC) similarly shows that the commonly observed [C II] deficit correlates primarily with local physical conditions—such as gas density and radiation field intensity—rather than global galaxy properties [Fischer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250909417F/abstract). These observations indicate that internal cloud kinematics—such as the Mach number and turbulence regulated by the galactic potential—may play a larger role in modulating the [[average-star-formation-efficiency|Average star formation efficiency]] than simple density thresholds.

To explain variations in star formation efficiency across different galaxy populations, a "2-Star Formation Mode" framework is often invoked. In this framework, galaxies are separated into a main sequence of quiescent star formation and a rarer starburst population. Starbursts exhibit star formation efficiency enhancements that scale supra-linearly with the boost in SFR, characteristic of merger-driven gas compression [Sargent et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793...19S/abstract). Under this paradigm, local [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] are not merely scaled-up main-sequence galaxies, but represent short-lived phases where internal gas reservoirs are depleted substantially faster than gas can be accreted from the cosmic web, resulting in gas fractions that are statistically reduced by factors of two to three compared to their main-sequence progenitors [Sargent et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793...19S/abstract).

## Chemical Abundances and the Fundamental Metallicity Relation

The gas-phase oxygen abundance and stellar metallicity of a star-forming galaxy serve as a fossil record of its assembly history, reflecting the complex interplay between continuous [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]], star formation, and metal-enriched outflows. A foundational scaling relation governing these [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] is the Mass-Metallicity Relation (MZR), wherein global galaxy metallicity correlates strongly with stellar mass ($M_*$). In the local universe, metallicity increases roughly as the square root of the stellar mass. However, establishing [[stellar-populations-by-metallicity|Stellar Populations by Metallicity]] from observational samples like the SDSS is complex; the assumed extinction law and burst length significantly influence the determination of these metallicities [Sextl et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...949...60S/abstract). Extended gaseous halos in [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]] may act as barriers that allow them to retain metals and enhance their overall abundance, mitigating the effects of outflow-driven metal loss [Legrand et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...560..630L/abstract). The MZR evolves significantly over cosmic time; at $z \simeq 3.3$, gas-phase metallicities are observed to be $\simeq 0.7$ dex lower than their local counterparts at fixed $M_*$ [Onodera et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...822...42O/abstract).

Chemical abundance patterns in high-redshift star-forming systems reveal the rapid timescales of their early assembly. Spectroscopic surveys of galaxies at $z \simeq 2-3$ show substantial alpha-enhancement relative to solar values, with $\text{O/Fe} \approx 2.2 \times (\text{O/Fe})_\odot$ extending across two decades in stellar mass [Strom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...925..116S/abstract). This indicates that the interstellar medium was rapidly enriched by core-collapse supernovae from [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] before Type Ia supernovae had sufficient time to contribute significant iron. Additionally, measurements of nitrogen abundances reveal that many $z \sim 2$ star-forming systems are observed near the evolutionary transition from primary to secondary nitrogen production, resulting in a steep $M_*$--N/H relation with large intrinsic scatter [Strom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...925..116S/abstract). Detailed analysis of the N/O versus O/H relationship in the CLASSY sample of local star-forming galaxies continues to resolve the specific nucleosynthetic pathways mapping nitrogen enrichment over time [Stephenson et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023RNAAS...7...31S/abstract). The physical conditions of the star-forming gas also evolve with time. Findings from the LEGA-C survey indicate that lower-mass galaxies at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 0.8$) exhibit higher nebular excitation (traced by the [O III]/H$\beta$ line ratio) than their local counterparts, directly mirroring the elevated excitation trends commonly observed in higher-redshift systems [Helton et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934...81H/abstract).

To account for the intrinsic scatter in the MZR, the framework is often extended into the Fundamental Metallicity Relation (FMR), a 3D surface correlating stellar mass, metallicity, and the [[integrated-star-formation|Integrated star formation]] rate (SFR) or molecular gas mass. While the standard FMR successfully describes a broad population of galaxies across multiple epochs, significant residual scatter remains. Recent principal component analyses have demonstrated that incorporating a fourth parameter—the [[stellar-and-galactic-core-structure|Stellar and Galactic Core Structure]] ($\Sigma_*$)—forms a tighter 4D hypersurface that reduces the metallicity dispersion by 50% relative to the standard molecular-gas FMR [Hashimoto et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.4424H/abstract). This surface density dependence is theoretically linked to variations in the local star formation efficiency and the dilution timescale of cosmic gas inflows [Hashimoto et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.4424H/abstract). Furthermore, the local environment modulates these abundances; galaxies in low-density regimes preferentially have lower gas metallicity than those in dense regimes at fixed stellar mass [Deng et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AJ....141..162D/abstract).

The precise universality of the FMR at early cosmic epochs remains a subject of active debate. While the FMR holds well locally, observations at $z \simeq 3.3$ indicate an offset of $\simeq 0.3$ dex from locally defined mass-metallicity-SFR relations. Furthermore, these high-redshift samples show no strong SFR-metallicity correlation within observational uncertainties, suggesting that the SFR may play a less dominant role in regulating metallicity at very high redshifts [Onodera et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...822...42O/abstract).

Internally, [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]] exhibit pronounced spatial gradients in their chemical properties, reflecting [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]]. Observational mapping shows that metallicity systematically decreases with galactocentric radius; as a general rule of thumb, the metal abundance drops by an order of magnitude for a hundred-fold decrease in gas surface density. Because these radial abundance gradients coincide with older stellar ages in the central regions and younger populations in the outskirts, they provide strong evidence that prolonged, inside-out star formation is the primary driver of chemical evolution in unperturbed galactic disks.

## Morphological Assembly and Resolved Structure

Spatially resolved observations of [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]] reveal that the morphological assembly of stellar mass predominantly proceeds from the inside out. In this inside-out growth scenario, older stellar populations are concentrated in [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]], while ongoing star formation is distributed at progressively larger galactocentric radii according to the [[galactic-star-formation-distribution|Galactic star formation distribution]]. Observationally, this manifests as negative age gradients and larger disk scale lengths in the rest-frame ultraviolet—which traces young stars—compared to the near-infrared, which traces the older stellar backbone [Wuyts et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753..114W/abstract), [Mondal et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909..203M/abstract). This structural evolution is observed continuously from the early universe, such as in the $z \approx 7$ proto-bulge system JADES-GS+53.18343−27.79097 where the specific star formation rate profile rises toward the outskirts [Baker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..141B/abstract), to local dwarf irregular galaxies [Čeponis et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019IAUS..344..267C/abstract). Furthermore, the size variation of galaxies across rest-frame wavelengths closely tracks their stellar mass, suggesting that size growth on short timescales is a self-regulated process governed by overall mass assembly [Jia et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..165J/abstract). Multi-wavelength comparisons reveal profound structural differences between components; for example, observations of $z \sim 2$ galaxies indicate that their molecular gas and dust continua can be 30-50% more compact than their rest-frame optical stellar light distributions [Kaasinen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...37K/abstract). 

At high redshift, the morphological structure of [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|High-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies]] and UV-selected populations is often highly irregular and characterized by [[clumpy-star-formation|Clumpy star formation]]. In this regime, star formation is concentrated in massive, kpc-scale clumps, likely driven by [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] in gas-rich systems [Hinojosa-Goñi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...592A.122H/abstract). While these off-center clumps are highly luminous in ultraviolet and emission-line tracers—contributing up to $\sim 20\%$ of the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] (SFR) in massive galaxies at $1 < z < 2$—they correspond to minor perturbations in the underlying stellar mass distribution, containing $\leq 7\%$ of the total stellar mass [Wuyts et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753..114W/abstract). Similar clump hierarchies are found in the local universe down to giant molecular cloud scales of $\sim 10$--$70$ pc, with masses ranging from $10^3$ to $10^6 M_\odot$ [Mondal et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909..203M/abstract). For example, UVIT imaging of the nearby dwarf irregular galaxy WLM reveals a complex demographic of [[active-star-forming-regions|Active star-forming regions]], showing distinct hierarchical structures and O-B associations with typical clump sizes of $4-50$ pc [Mondal et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018AJ....156..109M/abstract). Some models suggest these massive clumps migrate inward due to dynamical friction, eventually coalescing to form central bulges, though brief *in situ* episodic enhancements provide a competing explanation [Wuyts et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753..114W/abstract), [Hinojosa-Goñi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...592A.122H/abstract). Dynamical studies further support these structural models; recent resolved kinematic observations of a star-forming galaxy at $z \sim 2$ reveal a close agreement between the stellar and nebular velocity fields, validating the use of nebular emission as an accurate tracer of the underlying dynamical mass distribution [Rhoades et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250322039R/abstract).

The formation of dense central cores is a critical phase in morphological assembly that frequently precedes the cessation of star formation. This evolutionary pathway involves [[compaction-events|Compaction events]], wherein extended star-forming galaxies experience highly dissipative gas inflows that rapidly build up the central stellar surface density [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...865...49W/abstract), [Osborne et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902...77O/abstract). These compact star-forming systems—often referred to as "blue nuggets"—represent a transitional phase, contributing significantly to the cosmic star formation budget at $z \sim 1$--$2$ before moving toward the quiescent sequence [Osborne et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902...77O/abstract). When structural evolution is parameterized with a Sérsic profile, the effective radius ($r_e$) of star-forming galaxies scales as $r_e \propto (1+z)^{-0.72}$, while quiescent galaxies evolve more rapidly as $r_e \propto (1+z)^{-1.44}$ [Straatman et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...808L..29S/abstract). 

Recent high-resolution rest-frame near-infrared imaging by the James Webb Space Telescope has further refined this picture of [[morphological-downsizing|Morphological downsizing]]. While massive star-forming galaxies at cosmic noon ($z \sim 2$) appear as extended, [[clumpy-disks|Clumpy disks]] in the rest-frame optical, their rest-frame near-infrared emission shows highly concentrated, bulge-dominated structures [Benton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974L..28B/abstract). The radial surface brightness profiles steepen significantly from a slope of $\sim 0.3 \text{ dex}^{-1}$ at $1.5\, \mu\text{m}$ to $\sim 1.4 \text{ dex}^{-1}$ at $4.4\, \mu\text{m}$ within the inner $1 \text{ kpc}$ [Benton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974L..28B/abstract). This demonstrates that the high central mass densities traditionally associated with passive galaxies are already firmly in place during the active star-forming phase.

## Environmental Influence and Quenching Mechanisms

The evolution of star-forming galaxies is profoundly influenced by their local environment. Observations consistently demonstrate that galaxies in dense environments, such as [[nascent-galaxy-clusters|Nascent galaxy clusters]] and [[compact-galaxies|Compact Galaxies]], exhibit lower specific star formation rates and reduced gas fractions compared to their counterparts in the low-density field. For instance, star-forming galaxies in group environments at $z \sim 0.73$ exhibit higher gas-phase metallicities than field counterparts of similar mass [Zhou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A..97Z/abstract). This environmental dependence manifests as the morphology-density relation, wherein dense environments are predominantly populated by [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]], such as [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] and lenticulars (S0s), while the field is dominated by star-forming spirals. However, while early-type galaxies are traditionally viewed as definitively quenched, massive early-type galaxies appear to have similar ages regardless of whether they inhabit the field or cluster environments [van Dokkum et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASPC..379..249V/abstract). Nearly 47% of early-type (E/S0) galaxies in the local GAMA sample exhibit ongoing star formation activity that places them squarely on the spiral star-forming main sequence [Paspaliaris et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...669A..11P/abstract). Additionally, actively star-forming elliptical galaxies have been discovered at low redshifts in the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, which are likely the immediate progenitors of [[e-a-galaxies|E+A galaxies]] [Fukugita et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...601L.127F/abstract). These star-forming early-type galaxies often possess gas that is more concentrated and kinematically misaligned with their stars, suggesting an external origin for the gas such as recent mergers or accretion [Lee et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953...88L/abstract). Furthermore, globular cluster metallicities in early-type dwarf galaxies are higher than in late-type dwarf irregulars and spirals at a given age, pointing to distinct early enrichment histories depending on local morphology and environment [Sharina et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010vsgh.conf..181S/abstract). Despite these environmental influences, a comparative study of star-forming dwarf galaxies in the [[fornax-cluster-and-galaxy-studies|Fornax Cluster and Galaxy Studies]] and Hydra clusters shows that their chemical evolution often matches closed-box model predictions, indicating that internal processing can remain dominant even in dense environments [Vaduvescu et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...533A..65V/abstract).

**Mechanisms of Environmental Quenching**
As galaxies are accreted into massive dark matter halos, several external mechanisms act to remove or heat their gas reservoirs, eventually quenching star formation and transitioning the galaxy from the blue star-forming cloud to the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]]. 
* **Ram-pressure stripping**: When a galaxy falls into a cluster, the hydrodynamic pressure exerted by the dense intracluster medium (ICM) can rapidly strip away its cold interstellar gas. This process not only curtails star formation but also results in [[h-i-deficient-galaxies|H I-deficient galaxies]] with reduced dust-to-stellar mass ratios compared to field galaxies [Bianconi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.4599B/abstract).
* **Strangulation (Starvation)**: In this slower quenching scenario, the galaxy's extended hot gas halo is stripped or heated upon entering a denser environment. Without a replenishing supply of cooling gas to fuel the interstellar medium, the galaxy gradually consumes its remaining cold gas through star formation until the reservoir is exhausted.
* **Tidal interactions**: Gravitational interactions with other galaxies or the cluster potential can strip gas from the outer disk or drive it into the nucleus. While tidal forces can temporarily enhance [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] rates and trigger centralized starbursts, they ultimately accelerate the depletion of the gas reservoir [Mateus et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MsT...........M/abstract). Specifically, tidal perturbations from close companions can increase gas accretion rates and result in flatter slopes on the resolved star-formation main sequence [Morales-Vargas et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.4370M/abstract). Often, the true extent of these interactions is hidden; H I mapping reveals tidal tails and bridges in early-type spirals that appear completely undisturbed in conventional optical imaging [Hameed et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....125.3005H/abstract). 

The interplay of these mechanisms drives the structural transformation of galaxies. For instance, the morphological transformation of quiescent spiral galaxies into S0 galaxies is a primary driver for the build-up of the red sequence in clusters since $z \sim 1$ [Cerulo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472..254C/abstract).

**Cluster Dynamical State**
The quenching efficiency is also tied to the dynamical state of the host cluster. In relaxed, virialized clusters, star formation is typically quenched efficiently within the infall region at the cluster outskirts. Conversely, merging galaxy clusters can temporarily disrupt this paradigm; complex shock fronts and large-scale interactions can trigger widespread star formation in recently accreted, gas-rich galaxies, distinguishing their emission-line populations from those of relaxed clusters [Stroe et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912...55S/abstract). Tracking these populations across cosmic time reveals rapid evolutionary changes; for instance, the star-forming fraction among low-mass galaxies in rich X-ray clusters drops significantly between $z = 1.24$ and $z = 0.84$ [Nantais et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...556A.112N/abstract).

**The Transition to Quiescence**
[[transition-galaxies|Transition galaxies]] migrating between the actively star-forming blue cloud and the passive red sequence inhabit the "green valley." The transit through this intermediate state is characterized by declining star formation rates and is strongly influenced by the downsizing paradigm: massive galaxies underwent this transition rapidly at early cosmic epochs, while the mass flux density of galaxies migrating to the red sequence has shifted toward lower-mass systems at intermediate and low redshifts [Gonçalves et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...67G/abstract). 

Despite the clear role of the environment, the literature maintains an active debate regarding the relative importance of external environmental quenching versus internal quenching mechanisms. Processes such as central compaction, where galaxies grow dense stellar cores prior to quenching, may operate independently of local density [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...865...49W/abstract). In many cases, transitioning systems—such as [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]]—exhibit intermediate morphologies and weak molecular gas outflows, suggesting that internal gas depletion and low excitation may govern the final cessation of star formation regardless of large-scale environmental processes [French et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942...25F/abstract), .

## High-Redshift Populations and Early Galaxy Formation

The global [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]], quantified by the cosmic star-formation rate density $\dot{\rho}_*(z)$, rises sharply from the present day to its aforementioned peak at $z \sim 1.5-3$, before declining at earlier times [Madau et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..148...88M/abstract). Understanding the buildup of stellar mass during this decline requires a complete census of the earliest star-forming populations, as well as tracking their less luminous counterparts, such as [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|Star-forming dwarf galaxies]], which have been robustly identified at redshifts as high as $z \sim 0.5$ and beyond [Ohyama et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999AJ....117.2617O/abstract).

Studies of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] initially relied on rest-frame ultraviolet selection, specifically identifying "Lyman-break" galaxies (LBGs) via the strong absorption edge at $912$ \AA. LBGs, often identified as [[g-dropout-galaxies|g-dropout galaxies]], provide critical constraints on the Epoch of Reionization ($z \sim 6-10$), with current models suggesting their ionizing emissivity is sufficient to reionize the intergalactic medium [Bouwens et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..423..111B/abstract). However, ultraviolet spectroscopic analysis reveals that these high-redshift Lyman-break galaxies are typically highly reddened and extincted by dust, with properties matching local metal-rich starbursts [Heckman et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999mdrg.conf..227H/abstract). Spectroscopic confirmation of oxygen emission in lensed systems like MACS1149-JD1 at $z = 9.1096$ indicates that the onset of [[first-star-formation|First star formation]] occurred as early as $250$ Myr after the Big Bang ($z \sim 15$) [Hashimoto et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.557..392H/abstract). However, the literature presents a strong tension regarding the highest-redshift candidates: some photometrically selected $z \approx 16$ galaxies have been spectroscopically revealed as $z \approx 5$ impostors with unusual dust reddening and strong nebular emission. This highlights the absolute necessity of spectroscopic verification to prevent catastrophic redshift errors in the early universe [Arrabal Haro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Natur.622..707A/abstract). High-redshift structures can also be incredibly compact; intriguingly, standard morphology statistics show no dramatic difference between rest-frame UV and optical wavelengths for galaxies at $z > 7$, implying that their young stellar populations and recent star formation are co-spatial [Treu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942L..28T/abstract).

Furthermore, UV-based LBG surveys severely under-represent massive, dust-obscured populations. Submillimeter and radio observations have unveiled a dominant population of Dusty Star-Forming Galaxies (DSFGs) at $z > 3$ that are completely optically invisible [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.572..211W/abstract). These [[heavily-dust-obscured-starbursts|Heavily dust-obscured starbursts]] exhibit immense star formation rates, often exceeding $200 \, M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$, and may account for up to 40% of the total star-formation rate density in the early universe [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract). Average rest-frame sub-mm spectra of these DSFGs span $240-800\text{ GHz}$ and successfully detect transitions from CO, [C I], and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, detailing their complex interstellar chemistry [Reuter et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...948...44R/abstract). Extreme cases, such as a maximum starburst observed at $z=6.34$, demonstrate that environments mature enough to host chemically evolved, massive dust reservoirs existed less than $900$ Myr after the Big Bang [Riechers et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.496..329R/abstract). High-resolution observations continue to push the boundaries of known sub-galactic structures at these early epochs. For instance, recent JWST observations of the 'Cosmic Spear' at $z=6.2$ have led to the discovery of extremely compact ($\sim 5$ pc) star clusters with exceptionally high mass densities, offering a direct view into the earliest modes of concentrated stellar assembly [Abdurro'uf et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251208054A/abstract). Notably, the spectral energy distributions of these early DSFGs are systematically warmer and bluer than local templates, which can lead to significant miscalculations of infrared luminosities if local analogs are assumed [De Rossi et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869....4D/abstract).

Spatially resolved studies of early [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]] indicate an inside-out growth mechanism. Recent JWST observations show proto-bulge and disk structures emerging as early as $z \sim 7$ [Baker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..141B/abstract). Furthermore, observations of typical star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 3$ show well-ordered rotation, indicating that stable mass assembly via cold gas accretion is already well underway at these early epochs [Stark et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455..775S/abstract). By utilizing strong gravitational lensing, observers can resolve individual star-forming regions down to scales of $\sim 100$ pc at $z > 1$, detecting distinct variations in ultraviolet line profiles such as C IV and Mg II on sub-galaxy scales [Bayliss et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..309..251B/abstract). By cosmic noon, high-resolution rest-frame near-infrared imaging reveals that many massive star-forming galaxies already possess dense, bulge-dominated cores, with central surface brightness profiles steepening significantly at longer wavelengths [Benton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974L..28B/abstract). Observations of dense forming cores—such as GOODS-N-774 at $z=2.3$, which features a half-light radius of just $1.0$ kpc and a gas velocity dispersion of $317 \text{ km s}^{-1}$—support a scenario where intense central starbursts and compaction precede rapid quenching [Nelson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.513..394N/abstract). These highly concentrated, gas-rich structures are the direct star-forming progenitors of [[red-nuggets|Red nuggets]]: the extremely compact quiescent galaxies that dominate the massive early-type population at intermediate redshifts before later minor mergers build their [[extended-stellar-haloes|Extended stellar haloes]] [Beasley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.555..483B/abstract).

## Feedback, Outflows, and Multi-Messenger Emissions

Feedback from [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] (via stellar winds and supernovae) and [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] (AGN) plays a critical role in regulating the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] of galaxies. These processes inject immense thermal and mechanical energy into the interstellar medium, frequently launching [[galaxy-outflows|Galaxy outflows]] or "galactic superwinds." In doing so, starbursts serve as critical sites for polluting the inter-galactic medium with chemically-enriched gas via these massive outflows [Heckman et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..148..127H/abstract). Observations of interstellar Na I absorption lines in local [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]] indicate that the equivalent width and covering fraction of outflowing gas depend primarily on the star formation rate (SFR) per unit area ($\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$) rather than the global stellar mass, with typical outflow velocities ranging from $120$ to $160 \ {\rm km \ s^{-1}}$ [Chen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AJ....140..445C/abstract). The role of AGN feedback in this process remains a subject of active debate; while traditional models emphasize negative feedback that quenches star formation through massive gas ejection [Leslie et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455L..82L/abstract), idealized hydrodynamic simulations suggest that [[galaxy-and-gaseous-outflows|Galaxy and gaseous outflows]] can also trigger positive feedback, accelerating star formation in gas-rich disks at high redshift [Bieri et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812L..36B/abstract). Globally, the growth of the central supermassive black hole is intimately tied to the host's star formation; population studies reveal a strong linear correlation between average black hole accretion rates and galactic star formation rates across wide ranges in redshift and stellar mass [Chen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1401.2179C/abstract). Conversely, during the transition to quiescence in [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]], molecular gas observations reveal only weak outflows and low gas excitation, implying that violent mechanical feedback may subside rapidly or play a more limited role once the primary starburst concludes [French et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942...25F/abstract).

The shock-heating of the interstellar medium by superwinds produces extended diffuse X-ray emission. In the central regions of nearby star-forming galaxies, the $0.5\mathrm{-}2 \ {\rm keV}$ X-ray luminosity of this hot gas scales super-linearly with the local star formation rate:
$$ \log(L_{\rm 0.5-2\,keV}^{\rm gas} / {\rm erg\,s^{-1}}) = 1.34\,\log({\rm SFR}/M_\odot\,{\rm yr^{-1}}) + 40.15 $$
indicating that regions of intense stellar feedback convert a larger fraction of their supernova and wind energy into X-ray flux [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250617663Z/abstract). Globally, the relationship between the total X-ray and infrared luminosities is tightly correlated, suggesting that populations of massive X-ray binaries (often detected in [[x-ray-detected-galaxies|X-ray detected galaxies]]) also scale directly with the underlying star formation [Griffiths et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ApJ...360..483G/abstract). These linear scaling correlations between total X-ray, radio, and infrared luminosities are remarkably robust and have been shown to extend up to redshifts of $z \sim 1$ .

In addition to X-ray emission, star-forming galaxies act as vast reservoirs of cosmic rays, which are accelerated in supernova remnants and propagate through the magnetized interstellar medium. In dense, [[heavily-dust-obscured-starbursts|Heavily dust-obscured starbursts]], cosmic rays are largely confined, and their energy is lost "calorimetrically" via hadronic interactions with the ambient gas, generating copious gamma-ray emission through neutral pion decay [Pfrommer et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...847L..13P/abstract). In galaxies with lower star formation rates, adiabatic losses dominate, leading to a deviation from this calorimetric scaling [Pfrommer et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...847L..13P/abstract). 

These same hadronic interactions are predicted to be a primary source of high-energy astrophysical neutrinos via the decay of charged pions. Phenomenological models indicate that starburst galaxies could account for the diffuse sub-PeV and PeV neutrino flux observed by the IceCube observatory, provided that the cosmic-ray energy spectra in these environments are relatively hard, with spectral indices around $2.1\mathrm{-}2.2$ [Tamborra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014JCAP...09..043T/abstract), [Chang et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...805...95C/abstract). Spectra harder than $E^{-2.15}$ are in tension with the IceCube data, highlighting the value of multi-messenger constraints [Tamborra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014JCAP...09..043T/abstract). Furthermore, nearby star-forming centers such as the [[circinus-galaxy|Circinus Galaxy]] and the Small Magellanic Cloud are predicted to be sufficiently bright in neutrino production to be detectable as point-like sources by upcoming facilities like KM3NeT/ARCA, providing a direct observational test of hadronic emission models in galactic superwinds [Ambrosone et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919L..32A/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Ongoing research into star-forming galaxies focuses on resolving the sub-kiloparsec physics of star formation, understanding the cosmic assembly history, and identifying the precise mechanisms that eventually halt stellar birth. 

At $z > 3$, the true number density of massive, dust-obscured galaxies remains an open question. Because UV and optical surveys have historically missed these heavily obscured populations, recent radio and sub-millimeter data suggest that [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|High-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies]] could actually account for up to 40% of the early universe's cosmic star formation rate density [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.572..211W/abstract), [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract). Furthermore, morphological paradigms at cosmic noon are currently being upended by JWST observations. While [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] at $1.5 < z < 3$ frequently appear disk-dominated in rest-frame optical light, recent imaging at $4.4\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ reveals that many are significantly more bulge-dominated. This indicates that the high central stellar surface densities traditionally associated with quenching are already in place during the active star-forming phase [Benton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974L..28B/abstract).

The transition from the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] to quiescence is another area of active debate. The literature is currently conflicted between bimodal evolutionary models—which posit a distinct star-forming blue cloud and passive red sequence—and unitary models that propose a continuous, curved "Galaxy End Sequence" [Eales et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.3125E/abstract). Crucial to resolving this tension is the post-starburst phase (often observationally classified as [[e-a-galaxies|E+A galaxies]] or [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]]), which is widely recognized as representing one of the fastest and most dramatic transitions in galaxy evolution [French et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASP..133g2001F/abstract). Recent ALMA observations of molecular gas in post-starburst systems reveal low gas excitation and weak outflows, suggesting that the ultimate cessation of star formation may be driven primarily by low molecular gas density rather than solely by violent heat or AGN feedback [French et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942...25F/abstract). 

On sub-kiloparsec scales, the microphysics of star formation presents lingering questions regarding [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] variations. It is heavily debated whether extreme environments, such as those found in [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] and violent nuclear starbursts, produce stellar populations with a top-heavy initial mass function compared to the quiescent mode of star formation in normal disk galaxies. Conversely, extreme low-density environments present entirely different challenges; for instance, ultra-diffuse galaxies exhibit unusually low star formation efficiencies that challenge current theoretical models of feedback-driven stellar expansion [Kado-Fong et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...11K/abstract).

Finally, extreme star-forming environments are increasingly recognized for their role in high-energy astrophysics. Starbursts act as immense reservoirs of cosmic rays, and a compelling future direction is determining whether next-generation neutrino telescopes can definitively differentiate between hadronic emission originating from cosmic-ray confinement in starburst nuclei and emission driven by central [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] [Tamborra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014JCAP...09..043T/abstract), [Ambrosone et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919L..32A/abstract).
