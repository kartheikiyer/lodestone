In [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], **galaxy analogues** are local or low-redshift systems that replicate the extreme physical conditions characteristic of distant, early-universe [[galaxy-populations|Galaxy populations]]. Because the direct observation of primordial systems is fundamentally hindered by severe spatial resolution and surface brightness constraints, analogues serve as accessible, high-resolution laboratories. By matching key [[global-observables-of-galaxies|Global observables of galaxies]]—such as [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|Unusually high specific star formation rates]], [[low-metallicity-galaxies|low gas-phase metallicities]], and intense nebular emission—these systems allow astrophysicists to directly probe the interstellar medium physics, starburst dynamics, and ionizing photon escape mechanisms that governed the epoch of reionization and [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] [Blanton et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ARA&A..47..159B/abstract), [Shapley et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ARA&A..49..525S/abstract). 

The study of galaxy analogues spans a wide range of cosmic phenomena and structural scales. Prototypical high-redshift proxies, such as Lyman Break Analogues (LBAs) and compact "Green Pea" galaxies, successfully reproduce the kinematics and radiation fields of $z \sim 3$ [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], providing vital constraints on how ultraviolet photons escaped to reionize the intergalactic medium. In the near-field regime, researchers utilize high-resolution cosmological simulations to identify analogues of the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] and the Milky Way. These studies place the Local Group within a broader $\Lambda$CDM framework, revealing that while the Milky Way's overall star formation history is statistically normal, its detailed assembly history—shaped by sequential hierarchical mergers [Freeman et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ARA&A..40..487F/abstract)—and its residence in a cold, [[low-density-environments|low-density sheet environment]] are cosmologically rare [Aragon-Calvo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520L..28A/abstract). Furthermore, metal-poor [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] act as unevolved analogues for the first stages of cosmic chemical enrichment, allowing researchers to study pristine nucleosynthesis and the assembly of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] within intact protogalactic fragments.

Despite their immense utility, the mapping between local analogues and true high-redshift populations is not strictly one-to-one, leading to several critical open questions. For instance, while local ultraluminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs) are frequently compared to high-redshift [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|submillimetre galaxies]], unresolved tensions remain regarding their disparate gas density profiles and the relative dominance of central black holes versus extended starbursts. Additionally, the precise quenched fraction of [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] orbiting Milky Way analogues remains fiercely debated. Recent evaluations show that while modern surveys like SAGA and ELVES broadly agree with each other after matching magnitude limits, their combined low quenched fractions stand in stark tension with both Local Group observations and theoretical models of [[galaxy-environment|environmental quenching]] in [[low-density-environments|Low-density environments]] [Karunakaran et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5314K/abstract). Finally, in the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) era, understanding local analogues is increasingly crucial for breaking degeneracies in photometric data, as astronomers work to determine whether exceptionally [[bright-galaxies|luminous]] $z > 10$ candidates are genuinely primordial [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] or merely heavily obscured, low-redshift [[interloper-galaxies|Interloper galaxies]] masquerading at the cosmic dawn.

## Definition and Physical Basis of Galaxy Analogues

Galaxy analogues are defined as local or low-redshift systems that act as accessible laboratories for studying the extreme physical conditions characteristic of the early universe. By matching key global observables, these systems allow astronomers to bypass the severe spatial resolution and surface brightness limits that hinder the direct study of primordial galaxies.

The physical basis for selecting [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|high-redshift analogues]] relies on identifying galaxies that replicate the intense star-formation physics and interstellar medium (ISM) conditions of the distant universe. Primarily, this involves isolating [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] with an [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|unusually high specific star formation rate]] (sSFR, defined as the star formation rate per unit stellar mass, $\dot{M}_{\star}/M_{\star}$) and a characteristically low gas-phase metallicity. Prototypical examples include Lyman Break Analogues (LBAs) at $z \sim 0.2$ and compact "Green Pea" galaxies. These local systems successfully reproduce the ISM conditions of $z \sim 3$ Lyman Break Galaxies, exhibiting the strong nebular emission and asymmetric Ly$\alpha$ profiles necessary to study the escape fraction of ionizing radiation [Rauch et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AIPC.1480..271R/abstract), [Santana-Silva et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5183S/abstract). Because of these similarities, they are widely utilized as valid physical proxies for the sources of cosmic reionization at $z > 6$ [Rauch et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AIPC.1480..271R/abstract), [Boorman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250508885B/abstract).

The extreme conditions observed in these analogues are often dynamically driven. Spatial analysis of LBAs indicates that they frequently populate [[galaxy-overdensities|denser regions]] on small scales, suggesting that their intense starburst properties are triggered by nearby encounters and mergers [Santana-Silva et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5183S/abstract). This implies that local density and the immediate [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]] act as pre-processing mechanisms that drive high-redshift-like bursts of star formation before the galaxies assemble into larger [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]]. Additionally, local proxies like the DYNAMO analogues have been used to compare stellar specific angular momentum with actual $z \sim 1.5$ galaxies, revealing that star-formation-driven turbulence plays a crucial role in shaping the kinematics and angular momentum profiles of both populations [Sweet et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.5700S/abstract).

Furthermore, the exact powering mechanisms of the most extreme analogues remain an area of active debate. While high sSFRs generally point to starburst-dominated emission, the recent conclusive detection of low-luminosity X-ray [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|active galactic nuclei]] in Green Pea galaxies demonstrates that black hole accretion can coexist with these starburst signatures [Boorman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250508885B/abstract). This finding supports the hypothesis that low-luminosity active galactic nuclei might play a larger role in reionization than standard models predict, underscoring the necessity of multi-wavelength constraints when using local analogues to model the complex ionization fields of the early universe.

## Historical Development of Analogue Studies

Historically, the study of galaxy analogues evolved from attempts to map [[galaxy-structure|Hubble sequence]] formation across cosmic time. Early textbook models often assumed a monolithic collapse scenario, positing that galaxies like the Milky Way formed from a single protogalactic cloud [van den Bergh et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975A&A....44..231V/abstract). Consequently, early comparative studies focused on matching the spectral energy distributions and [[galaxy-properties|morphological properties]] of high-redshift objects to nearby morphological standards [Bruzual et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ASSL..141..161B/abstract). However, as evidence for [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|hierarchical assembly]] mounted, indicating that [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] assemble through sequential mergers [Freeman et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ARA&A..40..487F/abstract), the analogue paradigm shifted. Instead of treating entire local galaxies as frozen snapshots of the past, researchers began targeting specific physical states and transient phases in [[galaxy-populations|Galaxy populations]]. To disentangle these complex formation scenarios across cosmic epochs, extragalactic databases such as HyperLeda and the Migale project became critically important, providing researchers with [[global-observables-of-galaxies|homogeneous, multiparametric data]] for millions of galaxies [Theureau et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph..9831T/abstract).

A foundational shift in utilizing local populations as evolutionary analogues arose from the discovery of the [[butcher-oemler-effect|Butcher-Oemler effect]], which described a dramatic increase in the fraction of blue, star-forming galaxies in clusters at intermediate redshifts ($0.3 \lesssim z \lesssim 0.5$) compared to the local universe [Dickinson et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997hsth.conf..207D/abstract). Spectroscopic studies of these intermediate-redshift clusters revealed a substantial population of poststarburst galaxies (often referred to as 'E+A' or 'k+a' galaxies, famously detailed by early researchers like Dressler & Gunn). These systems display strong Balmer absorption lines indicative of recent, intense star formation, but lack emission lines, implying that their star formation was rapidly quenched within the last 1-2 Gyr. By tracking these poststarburst analogues, astronomers established an evolutionary link between actively star-forming field galaxies and the [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] that populate the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] in local [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]]. During this era, wide-field efforts like the CNOC2 Field [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] also tracked these evolutionary paths by characterizing moderate-redshift populations. Their results demonstrated that [[late-type-galaxies-and-stellar-populations|late-type galaxy analogues]] possess a significantly steeper faint-end slope of the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] compared to [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|early-type galaxies]] [Lin et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..146..190L/abstract).

As observations pushed to higher redshifts, mapping these evolutionary pathways encountered the so-called "redshift desert" ($1.4 < z < 3$). This observational gap presented inherent difficulties in mapping galaxy populations exactly where cosmic star formation and morphological differentiation reached their peak [Renzini et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Msngr.137...41R/abstract). The eventual discovery of luminous [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|submillimetre galaxies]] beyond this desert prompted comparisons with local ultraluminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs). Initially, local ULIRGs were proposed as direct analogues to these high-redshift sources based on similarities in their spectral energy distributions and extreme bolometric luminosities ($L_{\rm bol} \geq 10^{12} L_{\odot}$) [Trentham et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.305...61T/abstract). However, significant tensions emerged in this comparison. Theoretical arguments indicated that if high-redshift submillimetre galaxies possessed the same compact gas density profiles as local ULIRGs, their extreme luminosities would have to be driven primarily by active galactic nuclei. Conversely, if they were powered predominantly by star formation, their gas density profiles would need to be much more spatially extended than those of local ULIRGs, explicitly highlighting a fundamental structural difference between the two populations [Trentham et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.323..542T/abstract). 

In the modern era, the search for analogues has focused on mimicking the extreme interstellar medium conditions of the earliest galaxies responsible for cosmic reionization. Low-redshift ($z \sim 0.2$) Lyman Break Analogues (LBAs) have been identified as excellent proxies for $z \sim 3$ Lyman-break galaxies, sharing their high specific star formation rates and asymmetric Lyman-$\alpha$ emission profiles [Rauch et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AIPC.1480..271R/abstract). Observations indicate that LBAs populate denser regions at small scales, suggesting that the local [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]]—such as encounters and mergers—drives their extreme properties before they are accreted into larger clusters [Santana-Silva et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5183S/abstract). Furthermore, compact, [[low-metallicity-galaxies|low-metallicity]] Green Pea galaxies have emerged as vital laboratories for understanding high-redshift conditions. These systems replicate the starburst intensity necessary for ionizing photon escape, and multi-wavelength campaigns continue to refine our understanding of the interplay between starbursts and black hole accretion within them [Boorman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250508885B/abstract).

## Analogues of the Epoch of Reionization

Understanding the sources that drove the reionization of the intergalactic medium at $z > 6$ remains a primary objective of [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] and studies of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]]. While it is broadly accepted that [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] provided the bulk of the required ultraviolet photons, typical star-forming systems are overwhelmingly opaque to Lyman continuum emission. For reionization to proceed, either the number density of faint sources must have been exceptionally high, or early galaxies must have possessed a significantly higher escape fraction, $f_{\text{esc}}$, for ionizing radiation [Rauch et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AIPC.1480..271R/abstract). To bypass the observational limits of the ultra-high-redshift Universe, astronomers study low-redshift systems that replicate the intense conditions of the first galaxies. Direct observational searches for $z > 6$ systems rely heavily on characterizing the rest-frame UV [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and [[galaxy-mass-fractions|stellar mass function]]. These functions serve as key evolutionary metrics, allowing researchers to robustly compare the [[galaxy-properties|intrinsic properties]] of high-redshift candidates to those of local star-forming analogues [Finkelstein et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PASA...33...37F/abstract). Observations of local systems thereby complement direct high-redshift campaigns like the Brightest of Reionizing Galaxies (BoRG) survey, which utilizes the Hubble Space Telescope to measure the luminosity function and [[galaxy-clustering-and-redshift-estimation|spatial clustering]] of rare, [[bright-galaxies|luminous]] $z \sim 8$ sources to constrain early galaxy assembly [Trenti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AIPC.1480..238T/abstract).

Chief among local proxies are Lyman break analogues (LBAs) and "Green Pea" galaxies, typically found at $z \sim 0.2$. These systems are compact, [[low-metallicity-galaxies|Low-metallicity galaxies]] characterized by [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|exceptionally high specific star formation rates]] and kinematically complex interstellar media. Crucially, they serve as physical laboratories for studying the escape of ionizing radiation and often manifest as strong Lyman-alpha emitters with asymmetric, extended Ly$\alpha$ line profiles [Rauch et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AIPC.1480..271R/abstract). 

The physical mechanisms enabling the escape of ionizing photons in these analogues are heavily tied to their [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]] and dynamical histories. High $f_{\text{esc}}$ values require porous or damaged gaseous halos capable of leaking radiation into the intergalactic medium. Environmental studies indicate that LBAs preferentially reside in denser regions on small scales compared to the general field population, pointing to a high incidence of close galactic encounters [Santana-Silva et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5183S/abstract). These interactions and minor mergers trigger intense starbursts, drive gas inflows, and create tidal features that carve optically thin pathways through which ionizing radiation can escape [Rauch et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AIPC.1480..271R/abstract), [Santana-Silva et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5183S/abstract). 

Alongside young, hot stellar populations, the potential contribution of central black holes to the reionization photon budget remains an active area of investigation. This has been spurred by recent James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) discoveries of compact, early galaxies—often dubbed "Little Red Dots"—that exhibit ambiguous signs of [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] despite lacking robust X-ray detections. Investigations of local Green Pea galaxies provide critical baseline data for this paradigm, proving that low-luminosity AGN activity can indeed operate dynamically within the compact, low-metallicity environments typical of the epoch of reionization, suggesting they may have provided a non-negligible contribution to the global ionizing background [Boorman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250508885B/abstract).

## Milky Way and Andromeda (M31) Analogues

Identifying local galaxies that share the stellar mass ($M_* \approx 10^{10.5}$ to $10^{11.2} \rm M_{\odot}$), [[disk-galaxies|disc morphology]], and specific environment of the Milky Way (MW) and the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] (M31) is critical for placing the Local Group in a cosmological context. To systematically identify and study these systems, large-scale mapping efforts employ near-infrared flux-limited samples, creating comprehensive wide-field maps of stellar mass and star formation rates in the [[low-redshift-universe|local universe]] out to distances of 350 Mpc [Biteau et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..256...15B/abstract). 

As a benchmark $L^*$ barred spiral for near-field cosmology, the Milky Way uniquely resides in the 'green valley'—a transitional region in color-magnitude space between the active blue clouds of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] and the quiescent [[red-sequence|red sequences]] [Bland-Hawthorn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..529B/abstract). While the Milky Way has historically been considered a somewhat anaemic spiral, its [[global-star-formation-rate|star formation rate]] (SFR) is statistically typical, falling well within $2\sigma$ of observationally selected Milky Way analogues [Fraser-McKelvie et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.5030F/abstract), [Boardman et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.4943B/abstract). Structurally, however, the Milky Way exhibits a more compact disc scale length than many of its counterparts. This compactness accounts for perceived discrepancies in its relatively flat physical metallicity gradients; when normalized by disc scale length, MW [[steep-age-and-metallicity-gradients|chemical gradients]] closely match those of its analogues [Boardman et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.3672B/abstract). High-resolution cosmological simulations such as TNG50 successfully reproduce this structural diversity, demonstrating that MW/M31 analogues exhibit a wide variety of disc flaring profiles and bar fractions ($\sim 60\%$ at $z=0$) [Sotillo-Ramos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3915S/abstract), [Pillepich et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.535.1721P/abstract).

Analyses of our neighbor, Andromeda (M31), provide further local constraints. Spectroscopic studies of M31's [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] reveal alpha-element abundances that are higher than those of Milky Way field stars, but consistent with M31's inner halo, pointing to a complex early star formation history characterized by significant intergalactic gas inflow and rapid supernova bursts [Sharina M. et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220301011S/abstract).

Early models posited that the Galaxy formed from the monolithic collapse of a single protogalactic cloud [van den Bergh et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975A&A....44..231V/abstract). However, analyses of simulated analogues confirm a [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|hierarchical assembly history]]; large photometric and spectroscopic stellar surveys within our own Galaxy provide the crucial fossil evidence needed to observationally test these hierarchical merging theories [Wyse et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995PASP..107..785W/abstract). Modern mock analogues successfully reproduce the chemical and kinematic signatures of discrete accretion events matching the Gaia-Enceladus, Kraken, and Sequoia mergers [Freeman et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ARA&A..40..487F/abstract), [García-Bethencourt et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.1190G/abstract). Beyond merger history, the broad cosmological environment of the Local Group appears to be an outlier. MW analogues residing in cold, Local Sheet-like environments are exceptionally rare—occurring roughly once per $(180 \text{ Mpc})^3$ volume—which imparts atypically low spin parameters and distinct angular momentum alignments to the [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]].

The [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] of Milky Way analogues provide rigorous tests for $\Lambda$CDM predictions on sub-galactic scales. The presence of two massive satellites equivalent to the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds (LMC and SMC) is a rare $2.7\sigma$ configuration, found in only $\sim 0.4\%$ of MW-mass systems [Robotham et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.1448R/abstract). Theoretical models indicate that the accretion of a massive LMC-like satellite significantly boosts the total [[optical-richness|richness]] of the satellite population due to halo formation bias [Zhang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.2440Z/abstract). 

Observational constraints on these faint companions rely heavily on targeted programs like the SAGA survey and the ELVES program, which map the [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|Local Volume and Nearby Galaxy Studies]]. Currently, there is notable tension regarding the quenched fraction of these satellite populations. The SAGA survey initially reported a highly active satellite population. Conversely, early uncorrected comparisons suggested the ELVES program found higher quenched fractions. However, recent evaluations demonstrate that after matching absolute magnitude limits, the ELVES quenched fraction drops to $\sim 27\%$, bringing the SAGA and ELVES surveys into broad agreement [Karunakaran et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5314K/abstract). The true tension now lies between these combined survey results—which observe highly active satellite populations—and both localized observations of the Local Group and [[idealized-galaxy-models|hydrodynamical simulations]], which predict significantly higher quenched fractions [Karunakaran et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5314K/abstract).

Additionally, simulated Milky Way analogues inform indirect dark matter searches. Haloes matching MW kinematic constraints (e.g., in the APOSTLE and EAGLE suites) favor inner [[galaxy-cluster-mass-profiles|dark matter density profiles]] that are shallower than required to explain the Galactic Center GeV excess via dark matter annihilation [Calore et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...12..053C/abstract). Furthermore, their local velocity distributions are well-described by truncated Maxwellians, lacking the extragalactic high-speed tail once hypothesized to pervade the Solar neighborhood [Santos-Santos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...03..046S/abstract).

## Low-Metallicity and Dwarf Galaxy Analogues

Metal-poor, gas-rich [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] serve as accessible local laboratories for understanding the physical conditions of the early universe. Because galaxies obey a well-defined [[fundamental-scaling-relations|mass-metallicity relation]] where lower stellar masses correspond to progressively lower metallicities, these unevolved systems approach the nearly primordial gas conditions expected prior to widespread cosmic chemical enrichment. Consequently, they provide a critical window into the [[early-galaxy-formation|formation of the first stars]], early nucleosynthesis, and the assembly of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] within protogalactic fragments.

Furthermore, the study of spatially resolved gas-phase [[steep-age-and-metallicity-gradients|metallicity gradients]] provides insight into the evolutionary stage of these galaxies. Observations with MUSE have identified a novel trend across $0.1 \lesssim z \lesssim 0.8$: large, well-evolved galaxies typically exhibit negative metallicity gradients, while smaller, lower-mass systems—often analogues to [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf populations]]—exhibit a much higher spread in gradient values, reflecting their more chaotic, unevolved gas dynamics [Carton et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.4293C/abstract).

When examining the progenitors to luminous field galaxies, constraints on their [[h-i-rich-galaxies|molecular gas (CO)]] content are crucial. Surveys establishing upper limits on molecular gas in such progenitors have found that these limits are consistent with dynamical mass limits derived from simple cooling time arguments, emphasizing the pristine, unenriched nature of these early stellar nurseries [Lo et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999A&A...341..348L/abstract). To interpret early-universe phenomena such as cosmic reionization, high-redshift supernovae, and gamma-ray bursts, it is necessary to understand the evolution of massive stars in extreme [[low-metallicity-galaxies|Low-metallicity galaxies]]. The Sagittarius Dwarf Irregular Galaxy (SagDIG) is a premier local analogue for this regime, possessing an extremely low oxygen abundance of $12+\log(\text{O/H}) = 7.37$ [Garcia et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474L..66G/abstract). Spectroscopic surveys of SagDIG have revealed populations of OBA-type stars and red supergiant candidates, which represent some of the most metal-poor massive stars accessible to high-resolution follow-up in the Local Group [Garcia et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474L..66G/abstract). The study of these stars anchors theoretical models of massive star evolution in the nearly pristine environments characteristic of the first galaxies. 

At high redshifts, the unevolved gas reservoirs of such primordial systems are often observed in absorption as damped Lyman alpha (DLA) systems. Observations of the most metal-poor DLAs provide precise constraints on early nucleosynthesis, including the primordial abundance of deuterium [Pettini et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012nuco.confE..71P/abstract). Furthermore, these high-redshift gas clouds exhibit chemical abundance patterns that reveal the existence of DLA analogues to the Carbon-enhanced metal-poor stars found in the Milky Way halo, linking high-redshift gas to the oldest surviving stellar populations [Pettini et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012nuco.confE..71P/abstract).

Low-metallicity dwarf galaxies also offer critical insights into the formation of [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]]. The universally bimodal color distribution of Globular Clusters points to a blue, metal-poor population that formed early in high-redshift protogalactic fragments. The survival and distribution of these clusters in present-day [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] provide direct analogues for this early assembly phase. For example, the Fornax dwarf galaxy hosts 5 massive globular clusters, an exceptionally high specific frequency for its stellar mass [Shao et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.2339S/abstract). Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations tracking cluster formation in Fornax-mass analogues indicate that globular cluster systems in [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] are more centrally concentrated than those in isolated field dwarfs, with *in situ* clusters showing the highest degree of central concentration [Shao et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.2339S/abstract). Notably, the survival of multiple massive clusters within the central kiloparsec of such dwarfs can be consistently reproduced in $\Lambda$CDM models without necessitating a shallow dark matter core, provided that the orbital decay and [[galaxy-harassment|dynamical friction]] of the clusters are rigorously modeled [Shao et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.2339S/abstract).

## Environmental Drivers of Analogue Evolution

The [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]] exerts a primary influence on the star formation history and structural assembly of analogue systems, a relationship historically codified by the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]]. As galaxies transition from field environments into [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]], they generally shift from being late-type [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] to quiescent, [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]]. However, the exact mechanisms and timescales of this environmental quenching remain subjects of active study, particularly when comparing local analogues to their high-redshift counterparts. Even at fixed morphological types, local environmental effects play a critical role. Analyses leveraging the WINGS and OmegaWINGS local galaxy cluster surveys demonstrate that localized factors, such as environmental density and clustercentric distance, profoundly impact the star formation histories of these galaxies [Pérez-Millán et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1292P/abstract).

Studies of low-redshift Lyman Break Analogues (LBAs)—which serve as proxies for $z \sim 3$ starbursts—reveal that these compact systems are often subject to galaxy pre-processing. While LBAs occupy large-scale environments similar to the general field population, they are significantly [[galaxy-overdensities|overdense]] on small spatial scales [Santana-Silva et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5183S/abstract). This local enhancement suggests that nearby encounters and early-stage mergers drive their extreme, high-redshift-like star formation before they are eventually accreted and quenched within larger cluster potentials.

The broader cosmological context of Milky Way analogues highlights the peculiar nature of the Local Group's surroundings. As supported by cosmological simulations, Milky Way analogues located in comparable cold, sheet-like structures are exceedingly rare [Aragon-Calvo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520L..28A/abstract). Such environments effectively isolate their constituent galaxies, preserving strong alignments between the sheet and galaxy spins and resulting in atypically low spin parameters due to a relative deficit of recent mergers.

Within these Milky Way analogue halos, the environmental quenching of [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] presents a major observational tension. Efforts to measure the activity of these faint companions have yielded conflicting results: while surveys like SAGA and ELVES broadly agree on a relatively low quenched fraction, both stand in sharp contrast to the much higher quenched fractions observed in the Local Group and predicted by models [Karunakaran et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5314K/abstract). Resolving whether the Milky Way satellite system is a true statistical outlier or if observational selection effects drive this discrepancy remains a critical open question in near-field cosmology.

In the densest group and cluster environments, environmental drivers also govern the morphological and structural evolution of [[massive-galaxies|massive analogues]]. As the most massive collapsed objects in the universe, galaxy clusters serve as representative matter laboratories for studying the total baryon content of the cosmos [Kravtsov et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S.164K/abstract). By tracking the evolution of these systems from their high-redshift "childhood" as proto-clusters to their local "maturity", astronomers can strictly constrain cosmological models of structure formation [Ascaso et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013hsa7.conf..115A/abstract). [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Massive early-type galaxies]] ($10 < \log_{10}(M_{\star}/h^{-2} M_{\odot}) < 11$) at intermediate redshifts ($0.4 < z < 1.2$) are observed to be $\sim 25\%$ larger (by $\sim 0.5 h^{-1} \text{ kpc}$) in high-density environments compared to counterparts in the field, an effect likely driven by accelerated dry minor mergers [Cooper et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.419.3018C/abstract). Furthermore, [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|tidal interactions]] and [[galaxy-harassment|dynamical friction]] within these halos can dramatically alter the satellite mass distribution. Observations of nearby clusters, such as Abell 0426, show a steepening of the faint-end [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] ($\alpha = -1.44 \pm 0.04$ compared to the field value of $\alpha = -1.25 \pm 0.05$), which is consistent with the accumulation of a population of [[luminous-passive-dwarf-galaxies|low-mass red galaxies]] that are the dynamically stripped remnants of once-massive infallers [Conselice et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...573L...5C/abstract). Conversely, analyses of composite luminosity functions in other low-redshift clusters show flatter faint-end slopes in their dense inner regions, supporting the hypothesis that dwarf analogues are highly susceptible to total tidal disruption when orbiting too near to the central cluster potential [Barkhouse et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..268..289B/abstract). At the cores of these massive environments, [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] (BCGs) and the surrounding Intracluster Light (ICL) act as vital luminous tracers. Their structural profiles and stellar populations provide a direct record of the cluster's long-term assembly history and map the underlying dark matter distribution [Contini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240401560C/abstract).

Finally, the timescale of environmental quenching is strongly debated. While standard semi-analytic [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]] typically invoke rapid quenching mechanisms—such as ram-pressure stripping—upon cluster infall, observations suggest that star formation in cluster infallers may be terminated more gradually in some clusters, depending heavily on their specific merging histories and localized environments [Ellingson et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Ap&SS.285....9E/abstract), [Eales et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.3507E/abstract).

## Active Galactic Nuclei in Local Proxies

The advent of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has revealed a surprising abundance of candidate [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|active galactic nuclei (AGN)]] in the early universe, including the elusive "Little Red Dots." These high-redshift systems exhibit spectral features suggestive of AGN but largely lack detectable X-ray counterparts, complicating efforts to study their accretion physics, AGN feedback, and the early evolution of the [[fundamental-scaling-relations|black hole-stellar mass relation]] [Boorman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250508885B/abstract). To overcome these observational limits, researchers have turned to compact, low-mass, and low-metallicity [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] in the local universe—such as Green Pea galaxies—which serve as accessible proxies for the sources that drove cosmic reionization. 

Until recently, unambiguous X-ray signatures of AGN within Green Pea galaxies were missing from the literature. However, deep X-ray campaigns have now conclusively identified a low-luminosity AGN in the Green Pea galaxy SDSS J082247.66+224144.0 (J0822+2241) [Boorman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250508885B/abstract). This system exhibits a hard X-ray luminosity of $L_{2-10\text{ keV}} \sim 10^{42} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$. Observations spanning a 6.2-year rest-frame baseline demonstrate that while its 2-10 keV emission remained stable, the soft X-ray emission in the 0.5-2 keV band decreased by approximately $60\%$ [Boorman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250508885B/abstract). This distinct spectral variability is best explained by transient, low-column density obscuration surrounding the central engine, offering a direct view into the complex gas dynamics operating within these compact environments. 

Beyond X-ray observations, radio interferometry serves as a vital tool for probing these complex local environments. In analogue [[galaxy-groups|Galaxy groups]], [[megaparsec-scale-diffuse-radio-sources|radio continuum emission]] traces both hidden AGN activity and large-scale magnetic fields, providing a complementary window into the interplay between accreting black holes and their immediate interstellar environments [Nikiel-Wroczyński et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Galax...9...84N/abstract).

The identification of X-ray bright AGN in local proxies like J0822+2241 provides a critical multi-wavelength template for understanding Little Red Dots and other [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]]. By characterizing the X-ray emission and obscuration properties in low-redshift analogues, astronomers can better constrain the duty cycles and accretion rates of nascent supermassive black holes. This, in turn, sheds light on how low-luminosity AGN might have contributed ionizing photons during the epoch of reionization and how AGN feedback regulates [[global-star-formation-rate|star formation]] in low-mass halos [Boorman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250508885B/abstract).

However, it is important to note that tensions remain when extrapolating the properties of local AGN and starburst proxies to high-redshift populations. As initially highlighted by comparisons between local ULIRGs and [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|high-redshift submillimetre galaxies]] [Trentham et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.305...61T/abstract), [Trentham et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.323..542T/abstract), similar emission signatures can originate from fundamentally different structural profiles and powering mechanisms. Consequently, while Green Peas and similar compact analogues successfully replicate the kinematics and low-metallicity environments of early galaxies, determining the exact balance of starburst versus AGN power—and whether they share identical structural gas profiles—remains an area of active debate in the literature.

## Numerical and Hydrodynamic Simulations of Analogues

High-resolution [[idealized-galaxy-models|cosmological simulations]] and [[galaxy-models|semi-analytic models]] are critical tools for placing observed galaxy analogues into a broader $\Lambda$CDM context, allowing researchers to trace both their [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|assembly histories]] and future evolutionary trajectories. 

Within the [[low-redshift-universe|local Universe]], hydrodynamical simulations are heavily utilized to isolate and study analogues of the Milky Way (MW) and [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda (M31)]]. The TNG50 simulation, part of the IllustrisTNG suite, has generated extensive catalogs of MW/M31 analogues, typically selected by requiring a [[disk-galaxies|stellar disc morphology]], a stellar mass of $M_* = 10^{10.5 - 11.2} M_\odot$, and a local environmental density comparable to the Local Group [Pillepich et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.535.1721P/abstract). These simulated analogues exhibit a wide diversity in structural [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]], including complex vertical disc flaring that varies significantly among mono-age stellar populations [Sotillo-Ramos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3915S/abstract). Detailed kinematic analyses of these systems have successfully identified analogues to the Gaia-Enceladus, Kraken, and Sequoia merger events. The ability to reproduce these distinct chemical and kinematic tracks provides robust support for the hierarchical assembly of the Milky Way [Freeman et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ARA&A..40..487F/abstract), [García-Bethencourt et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.1190G/abstract), superseding older monolithic collapse paradigms [van den Bergh et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975A&A....44..231V/abstract). Within these high-resolution dark-matter simulations, researchers also observe pronounced mass segregation trends; for instance, the average mass of galaxy analogues systematically increases with decreasing radius within the virial radius of the host halo [Joshi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..761J/abstract).

Simulations also reinforce the atypical nature of the Milky Way's cosmic environment, noting that "Local Sheet" analogues impart unusually strong alignments between the sheet and galaxy spins [Aragon-Calvo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520L..28A/abstract). Furthermore, the EAGLE project and APOSTLE simulations have been used to model the dark matter velocity and density profiles of MW analogues. These models suggest that the dark matter profiles in the inner few kiloparsecs are shallower than those required to explain the Fermi GeV excess via dark matter annihilation [Calore et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...12..053C/abstract). Regarding [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]], there remains an active tension between theoretical predictions and observations: while $\Lambda$CDM simulations successfully capture the radial diversity of satellites [Font et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..783F/abstract), they often predict higher quenched fractions than those observed by the SAGA and ELVES surveys, revealing a persistent tension between theoretical [[galaxy-environment|environmental quenching]] efficiencies and wide-field local observations [Zhang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.2440Z/abstract), [Karunakaran et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5314K/abstract).

Beyond the local Universe, simulations are essential for studying analogues of the earliest [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] observed during the epoch of reionization. Theoretical models are increasingly used to interpret the extreme luminosities of $z > 10$ candidates discovered by JWST. For instance, semi-analytic models of the $z=11.1$ galaxy GN-z11 identify analogues residing in dark matter halos of $M_{\rm vir} \sim 10^{11} M_\odot$, suggesting that their extreme brightness is driven by rapid, smooth mass accretion rather than transient mergers [Mutch et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.3556M/abstract). Furthermore, mock JWST imaging derived from the BlueTides simulation has been used to validate the detection of stellar light in the host galaxies of high-$z$ quasars, accurately recovering host images and predicting star formation rates of $10^{2-3} M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$ [Berger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.4765B/abstract). Furthermore, simple quasi-equilibrium physical models of high-redshift disc analogues suggest that their scaling relations, such as the stellar mass-halo mass relation, are primarily driven by large-scale feedback physics rather than the small-scale details of their [[global-star-formation-rate|star formation]] recipes [Furlanetto et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.3394F/abstract).

Additionally, modeling massive analogues in [[clusters-of-galaxies|cluster environments]] highlights persistent theoretical challenges. Semi-analytic models often struggle to match the luminosity function and colors of high-redshift cluster galaxies simultaneously with local observations, indicating that current treatments of star formation and environmental quenching may require further refinement across cosmic time [Merson et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.1681M/abstract).

Despite these successes, identifying true analogues for the most extreme JWST candidates remains a challenge for $\Lambda$CDM-based [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]]. Reproducing the brightest candidates at $z \gtrsim 16$ often requires artificially boosting [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|star formation efficiencies]] through reduced feedback regulation and increased gas depletion rates [Qin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.1324Q/abstract). The literature currently reflects significant ambiguity over whether the most luminous $z > 12$ candidates (such as CEERS-1749) are genuinely primordial systems or merely "disguised" low-redshift contaminants, such as [[extreme-starbursts|dusty starbursts]] or quenched [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] at $z \sim 5$ [Naidu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220802794N/abstract), [Qin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.1324Q/abstract).

## Dark Matter Implications and Kinematics

High-resolution [[idealized-galaxy-models|cosmological hydrodynamical simulations]]—such as the EAGLE, APOSTLE, and IllustrisTNG projects—allow for the detailed study of dark matter halo properties by identifying Local Group analogues that match the Milky Way and [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda]] in total stellar mass, structural parameters, and rotation curves. Extracting the local [[galaxy-cluster-mass-profiles|dark matter density]] and [[redshift-distribution|velocity distribution]] from these simulated analogues has significant implications for both direct and indirect detection experiments, as well as for our understanding of local kinematic histories.

**Local Dark Matter Phase Space and Direct Detection**
The velocity distribution of dark matter in the Solar neighborhood is a critical input for direct detection experiments. In simulated Milky Way analogues, this distribution is well modeled by a generalized Maxwellian truncated at a specific escape speed, with peak speeds typically ranging from $223$ to $289\text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Bozorgnia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JCAP...05..024B/abstract). Crucially, simulated analogues show no evidence of an extragalactic high-speed dark matter component originating from larger structures like the [[virgo-cluster|Virgo Supercluster]]; instead, observed high-speed particles simply constitute the natural high-velocity tail of the local truncated Maxwellian [Santos-Santos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...03..046S/abstract). Applying these analogue-derived velocity distributions to direct detection data shifts exclusion limits in the dark matter mass and cross-section plane by a few GeV relative to the Standard Halo Model. However, these updated halo profiles are insufficient to resolve existing experimental tensions, such as the conflicting signals between the DAMA and LUX experiments [Bozorgnia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JCAP...05..024B/abstract).

**Density Profiles and Indirect Detection**
Analogues also provide essential baselines for indirect dark matter searches. When Milky Way analogues are selected to strictly match local observational constraints, their inner [[galaxy-cluster-mass-profiles|dark matter density profiles]] at radii of a few kiloparsecs are consistently shallower than the steep profiles required to explain the Fermi GeV excess via dark matter annihilation [Calore et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...12..053C/abstract). 

Investigations into [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] and [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf spheroidal analogues]] yield similar constraints. The dark matter pairwise relative velocity distributions in APOSTLE dwarf analogues have been used to derive empirical scaling relations between a dwarf's maximum circular velocity and the peak speed of its Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. This enables accurate calculations of velocity-dependent $J$-factors for indirect detection [Blanchette et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...03..021B/abstract). Furthermore, foreground contamination from the Milky Way's own halo can significantly alter the expected annihilation signals from satellites like the Sagittarius dwarf, depending on whether the annihilation cross-section is modeled as $s$-, $p$-, or $d$-wave [Vienneau et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240315544V/abstract).

Analogues have also been leveraged to address the [[galaxy-central-density|core-cusp problem]]. Simulations of [[fornax-cluster-and-galaxy-studies|Fornax dwarf analogues]] demonstrate that a cuspy Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) dark matter profile is physically consistent with the survival of its extended system of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]]. Although dynamical friction causes many massive clusters to sink and disrupt, approximately $3\%$ of Fornax analogues successfully retain five or more Globular Clusters, indicating that a shallow dark matter core is not strictly required to match the observed cluster populations [Shao et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.2339S/abstract).

**Kinematics and Asymmetric Drift**
Stellar kinematics in analogues offer an independent probe of the gravitational potential and recent merger history. In IllustrisTNG simulations of Andromeda (M31) analogues, the asymmetric drift—defined as the kinematic lag between the rotation curves of the collisional gas and the collisionless stellar populations—increases systematically with [[galaxy-stellar-age|stellar age]] [Quirk et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497.2870Q/abstract). Analogues that successfully reproduce the specific asymmetric drift observed in M31's disc are statistically more likely to have experienced a major $4:1$ merger within the last $4\text{ Gyr}$, linking present-day velocity distributions directly to recent accretion events [Quirk et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497.2870Q/abstract). In lower-mass regimes, analogues are used to forward-model dimensionless dark matter halo spin. By matching [[h-i-rich-galaxies|H I rotation curves]] and baryonic disc scale lengths to simulations, studies of dwarf analogues successfully reveal halo spin profiles that match simulated systems, though remaining uncertainties regarding the precise evolutionary history of the dark matter halos limit the precision of theoretical spin derivations [Ansar et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230709952A/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The unprecedented infrared sensitivity of JWST has pushed the study of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] to $z > 10$. However, identifying analogues for these primordial systems is complicated by severe redshift contamination. A prime example of this challenge is the highly luminous candidate CEERS-1749. Initial photometric data suggested it might be an extremely massive ($M_{\star} \approx 5 \times 10^9 M_{\odot}$) system at $z \approx 17$ [Naidu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220802794N/abstract). If confirmed, such a system would fundamentally challenge standard $\Lambda$CDM models of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]], which require artificially boosted star-formation efficiencies to reproduce such bright objects at that epoch [Qin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.1324Q/abstract). Conversely, strong environmental evidence suggests CEERS-1749 might instead reside in a $z \approx 5$ [[protoclusters|protocluster]]. In this scenario, intense line emission from a [[passive-galaxies|quiescent galaxy]] or from dusty starbursts conspires to perfectly mimic the spectral break of a $z > 10$ target [Naidu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220802794N/abstract), [Qin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.1324Q/abstract). Resolving this dichotomy—determining whether these candidates are uniquely luminous early galaxies or perfectly disguised $z \approx 5$ [[interloper-galaxies|Interloper galaxies]]—demands rigorous JWST spectroscopic follow-up.

At intermediate to high redshifts, the physical correspondence between high-$z$ [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|submillimetre (SCUBA) galaxies]] and local ultraluminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs) remains ambiguous. Theoretical considerations of their gas density profiles suggest fundamental differences. Specifically, it remains an open question whether the high-$z$ systems derive their immense bolometric luminosities ($> 10^{12} L_{\odot}$) primarily from spatially extended star formation or from [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|active galactic nuclei (AGN)]], and how these power sources and gas distributions contrast with those of their presumed local counterparts [Trentham et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.305...61T/abstract), [Trentham et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.323..542T/abstract).

In the near-field regime, significant tensions persist regarding the satellite populations of Milky Way analogues. The quenched fraction of [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] around Milky Way-mass hosts presents a stark observational conflict: survey data from SAGA and ELVES broadly agree on a relatively low quenched fraction, but this stands in sharp contrast to the highly quenched satellite populations observed within the Local Group and predicted by standard cosmological simulations [Karunakaran et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5314K/abstract). It remains actively debated whether the Milky Way and its satellites represent a rare statistical outlier—perhaps driven by the halo formation bias induced by the massive [[non-magellanic-classical-satellites|Large Magellanic Cloud]]—or if current cosmological simulations and wide-field observational selections mischaracterize quenching efficiencies in [[low-density-environments|Low-density environments]] [Zhang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.2440Z/abstract), [Font et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..783F/abstract). Future large-scale spectroscopic surveys and higher-resolution hydrodynamic models are required to reconcile these divergent quenched fractions and definitively place the Milky Way within its broader cosmological context.
