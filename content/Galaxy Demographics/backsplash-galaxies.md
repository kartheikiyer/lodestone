## Article

In extragalactic astronomy, **backsplash galaxies** are a distinct dynamical population of galaxies that have previously transited deep into the gravitational potential of a massive galaxy cluster but are currently observed residing outside of its virial radius ($R_{\rm vir}$). Unlike pristine field galaxies or first-time infallers, these objects have completed at least one pericentric passage through the dense cluster core before rebounding outward on highly eccentric orbits [Gill et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract). The existence of this population fundamentally challenges the traditional theoretical paradigm that environmental transformation strictly occurs and persists *within* dense environments. Instead, backsplash galaxies demonstrate that a single transit through the intracluster medium is sufficient to impart lasting evolutionary changes, making them critical laboratories for studying the timescales of environmental "nurture" on galaxy evolution.

During their passage through the inner cluster—often penetrating to within $0.5 R_{\rm vir}$—backsplash galaxies are subjected to extreme astrophysical forces. Hydrodynamic drag from the intracluster medium induces rapid ram pressure stripping, severely depleting the galaxy's atomic gas reservoir and triggering rapid environmental quenching [Smith et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518L..18S/abstract). Simultaneously, strong tidal forces and rapid encounters can strip an average of 40% of the galaxy's total mass, driving a morphological transformation from star-forming spiral galaxies to quiescent S0s or early-type systems, such as [[defunct-dwarf-spheroidals|Defunct dwarf spheroidals]] [Moore et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996Natur.379..613M/abstract), [Gill et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract). Cosmological simulations and Projected Phase Space (PPS) analyses reveal that these processed systems are highly abundant. They comprise roughly 50% to 60% of the galaxy population found in the $1-2 R_{\rm vir}$ infall regions of massive halos, with even higher fractions typically found around dynamically relaxed systems rather than [[unrelaxed-galaxy-clusters|Unrelaxed galaxy clusters]].

The backsplash phenomenon is also widely invoked to explain extreme and anomalous low-mass systems, such as isolated, quenched [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] found well beyond the boundaries of massive hosts like the Milky Way and Andromeda. Because low-mass galaxies in the isolated field are theoretically expected to remain gas-rich and actively star-forming, a backsplash origin is a leading explanation for their quiescence, fundamentally linking them to broader [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]]. However, the study of backsplash galaxies remains fraught with observational challenges. Distinguishing true backsplash systems from first-time infallers and [[interloper-galaxies|Interloper galaxies]] using 2D projected kinematics is highly susceptible to contamination. Consequently, major open questions in the field center on using statistical corrections and new 3D kinematic data (such as high-precision proper motions) to definitively separate these populations. Current research is actively investigating whether all isolated quenched dwarfs truly have a backsplash origin, their potential to exhibit post-starburst signatures or late-time rejuvenation, and their overall contribution to the transition of the cosmic web into the quiescent sequence.

## Definition and Orbital Kinematics

Backsplash galaxies are defined by a specific orbital trajectory: they are galaxies that currently reside in the outskirts of a host dark matter halo—typically between $1$ and $2 R_{\rm vir}$—but have previously passed through the inner regions of the system before rebounding outward past the virial radius [Gill et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract). Rather than being pristine "field" galaxies or first-time infallers, these systems carry the dynamical imprint of a prior pericentric passage through the dense cluster core.

Demographically, the backsplash population is substantial. Simulations and observations indicate that backsplash galaxies constitute roughly $50\%$ to $60\%$ of the galaxy population in the $1-2 R_{\rm vir}$ infall regions of massive clusters [Gill et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract), [Haggar et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.6074H/abstract). The exact fraction is a strong function of clustercentric distance, empirically modeled as $f \approx -0.052 (R/R_{\rm vir}) + 0.612$ [Pimbblet et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.411.2637P/abstract). Furthermore, this fraction is highly sensitive to the dynamical state of the host halo; dynamically relaxed clusters exhibit a significantly higher fraction of backsplash galaxies in their outskirts compared to [[unrelaxed-galaxy-clusters|Unrelaxed galaxy clusters]], as the latter often have chaotic, rapidly evolving potentials that disrupt clean orbital rebounds [Haggar et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.6074H/abstract), [Kuchner et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510..581K/abstract).

The orbital kinematics of backsplash galaxies dictate a severe physical transformation. Cosmological simulations demonstrate that approximately $90\%$ of these galaxies penetrate deep into the cluster potential, reaching within $0.5 R_{\rm vir}$ during their pericentric passage [Gill et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract). As a result of this deep transit, they suffer intense tidal disruption, losing on average $\sim 40\%$ of their total mass to the host cluster [Gill et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract). Following this passage, their exit trajectories often deflect widely from their original entry vectors along cosmic filaments [Kuchner et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510..581K/abstract).

Observationally separating backsplash galaxies from pristine first-infallers or interlopers is challenging. In 3D phase space, backsplash galaxies are kinematically distinct, generally exhibiting lower line-of-sight velocity offsets relative to the cluster mean than first-infall galaxies [Gill et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract), [Pimbblet et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.411.2637P/abstract). However, beyond the Local Group, observers must typically rely on the 2D Projected Phase Space (PPS) diagram, which introduces significant cross-population contamination and requires sophisticated statistical corrections to accurately classify galaxies (discussed further below).

The backsplash definition also extends to lower-mass systems, such as [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] in the Local Group. Isolated, quenched dwarf galaxies (e.g., Cetus) found well outside the virial radii of the Milky Way or Andromeda are frequently hypothesized to be backsplash objects, as isolation typically preserves star-forming gas [Simpson et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478..548S/abstract), [Shapiro et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251214808S/abstract). However, this assumption remains a subject of active debate. Recent precise 3D kinematic measurements using proper motions have revealed that some classic backsplash candidates, such as Tucana, have orbits inconsistent with a past pericentric passage around a massive host [Bennet et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..228B/abstract). Such tensions underscore the necessity of full 3D velocity vectors to definitively confirm a backsplash trajectory and separate true orbital rebounds from other quenching mechanisms.

## Identification via Projected Phase Space (PPS)

Distinguishing backsplash galaxies from pristine field galaxies, first-time infallers, and line-of-sight [[interloper-galaxies|Interloper galaxies]] is a complex observational challenge. Because the full 3D orbital trajectories of galaxies are rarely accessible, astronomers rely heavily on the Projected Phase Space (PPS) diagram. This diagnostic tool maps a galaxy's line-of-sight radial velocity offset (often normalized to the cluster's velocity dispersion, $\Delta v / \sigma$) against its projected cluster-centric distance (typically normalized to the virial radius, $R/R_{\rm vir}$ or $R/R_{200}$). 

In the PPS diagram, backsplash galaxies occupy a distinct kinematic locus. Having previously traversed the deep potential well of the cluster core, these galaxies have lost orbital energy to dynamical friction and experienced significant mass loss due to tidal disruption [Gill et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract). As they emerge and pile up near their orbital apocenters, they generally exhibit lower radial velocity offsets compared to galaxies falling in for the first time. Mixture modeling of this region indicates that the fraction of the backsplash population decreases radially, which can be parameterized as $f = -0.052(R/R_{\rm vir}) + 0.612 \pm 0.06$ [Pimbblet et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.411.2637P/abstract).

To systematically disentangle these overlapping populations, researchers frequently employ specialized probabilistic and machine-learning tools. A primary example is the ROGER (Reconstructing Orbits of Galaxies in Extreme Regions) code, which utilizes 2D projected phase-space coordinates to calculate the probability of a galaxy belonging to one of five distinct dynamical classes: cluster members, backsplash galaxies, recent infallers, infalling galaxies, and interlopers [Coenda et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.1934C/abstract), [Martínez et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4360M/abstract), [Muriel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A.258M/abstract). In addition to kinematic models, the identification of backsplash candidates is increasingly being aided by citizen science projects like Galaxy Zoo, which help isolate galaxies with visible ram pressure stripping features (jellyfish morphology) from standard cluster populations [Crossett et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241210060C/abstract).

Despite the sophistication of algorithms like ROGER, 2D PPS classifications are intrinsically plagued by contamination effects due to the loss of line-of-sight distance information. The literature highlights significant tension regarding the raw reliability of these classifications, noting that the backsplash category can suffer from important contamination rates from misclassified recent infallers and interlopers [Coenda et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.1934C/abstract), [Martínez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250204446M/abstract). 

To derive accurate physical properties for the true backsplash population—such as their specific star formation rates and stellar ages—these contamination effects must be rigorously accounted for. Recent methodologies achieve this by computing the classification scheme's confusion matrix from cosmological simulations and formally inverting it to correct the observed distributions statistically [Martínez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250204446M/abstract). When such corrections are applied, the blurring effect of 2D misclassification is removed, confirming that backsplash galaxies are systematically redder and have more suppressed star formation than first-time infallers at the same cluster-centric distance [Martínez et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4360M/abstract), [Martínez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250204446M/abstract).

## Environmental Quenching and Gas Stripping

As galaxies fall into a cluster, they penetrate deep into the gravitational potential, often reaching within $0.5 R_{\rm vir}$ of the core [Gill et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract). During this transit, they interact violently with the dense intracluster medium (ICM) and the host's global tidal field. Tidal disruption can strip approximately $40\%$ of a backsplash galaxy's total mass [Gill et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract), while hydrodynamic drag from the ICM induces extreme ram pressure stripping. These combined forces rapidly evacuate the galaxy's cold gas reservoir, resulting in a pronounced HI deficiency and fundamentally altering its evolutionary trajectory [Smith et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518L..18S/abstract), [Borrow et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520..649B/abstract). 

The removal of cold gas leads to immediate and severe environmental quenching. Detailed spectral fitting of satellite discs suggests that these galaxies evolve as "closed boxes" after their hot gas haloes are removed, confirming the rapid cessation of star formation upon cluster entry [Zhou et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.3723Z/abstract). Consequently, backsplash galaxies are physically and chemically distinct from first-time infallers residing at similar clustercentric distances. They exhibit older mean stellar ages, a significantly lower specific star formation rate (sSFR), and a higher overall fraction of quiescence [Coenda et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.1934C/abstract), [Martínez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4360M/abstract), [Ferreras et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4884F/abstract). A single passage through the cluster core is generally sufficient to halt star formation and reduce the galaxy's physical size [Martínez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4360M/abstract). Observational signatures of the active stripping phase can be seen in "jellyfish" galaxies, which display trailing tentacles of displaced gas; the prevalence of these features among recent infallers and backsplash candidates indicates that the stripping operates on timescales of $\lesssim 3 \text{ Gyr}$ [Roberts et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...650A.111R/abstract), [Muriel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A.258M/abstract). In many low-mass systems, star formation ceases entirely within $< 1 \text{ Gyr}$ of the initial infall [Simpson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478..548S/abstract).

A key area of investigation in environmental quenching is the timeline of a galaxy's transformation. The extreme ram pressure in the ICM strips gas so rapidly that star-forming spirals are quenched into passive, disc-dominated systems long before tidal harassment can fully transform them into bulge-dominated S0 or elliptical morphologies [Martínez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4360M/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251102964E/abstract). Spatially resolved spectroscopy of these passive spiral galaxies in cluster infall regions often reveals $\text{H}\delta$ absorption gradients, with younger stellar populations typically found in the outer regions. This indicates that their star formation ceased only a few gigayears ago [Ishigaki et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.382..270I/abstract). In contrast, transition populations, such as Green Valley galaxies, tend to avoid cluster cores entirely; this spatial distribution may explain their prolonged star-formation histories compared to the rapidly quenched cluster members that experience deep transits [Crawford et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...786...30C/abstract).

The severity of environmental quenching is also heavily influenced by the cluster's dynamical history and the galaxy's prior orbital environment. "Pre-processing" within cosmic filaments and smaller groups before a galaxy even enters the cluster virial radius can initiate gas depletion early [Kuchner et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510..581K/abstract), [Ferreras et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4884F/abstract). Transitional, [[compact-galaxies|Compact Galaxies]] exhibiting environmental quenching are also found in compact groups, suggesting these high-density systems act as efficient sites for the pre-processing of backsplash candidates [Montaguth et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5340M/abstract). Indeed, galaxies residing in high-density compact groups evolve significantly faster than their isolated counterparts, often exhibiting shorter star-formation timescales [Plauchu-Frayn et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...546A..48P/abstract). Because of this pre-processing, these galaxies are systematically smaller and more concentrated than those in the field, setting the stage for their later transition into the backsplash population [Coenda et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...543A.119C/abstract). Furthermore, the abundance of fully quenched backsplash galaxies is sensitive to the macroscopic state of the host halo; hydrodynamical simulations indicate that dynamically relaxed systems harbor a significantly higher fraction of backsplash galaxies in their outskirts compared to merging or [[unrelaxed-galaxy-clusters|Unrelaxed galaxy clusters]] [Haggar et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.6074H/abstract).

## Morphological Transformation and S0 Formation

Backsplash galaxies represent a critical transitional population that bridges the evolutionary gap between star-forming field spirals and the quiescent early-type galaxies characteristic of dense cluster environments. Having penetrated deep into the cluster potential—often to within $0.5 R_{\mathrm{vir}}$—these galaxies suffer significant environmental processing before exiting the cluster core on their outgoing trajectories [Gill et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract). 

As established, observational evidence indicates that in dense environments, the quenching of star formation generally precedes morphological transformation [Martínez et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4360M/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251102964E/abstract). Consequently, a single passage through a cluster is sufficient to drastically reduce specific star formation rates via ram-pressure stripping, leaving the exiting backsplash galaxy as a largely quiescent disk system [Martínez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4360M/abstract). 

Following the rapid cessation of star formation, secular and tidal processes gradually alter the galaxy's stellar structure. The fading of the star-forming disk, coupled with tidal heating from the cluster potential, transforms these quenched spirals into S0 galaxies. Thus, S0 galaxies are widely considered the "red and dead" descendants of cluster-processed spirals, with the backsplash population catching this morphological transformation in progress [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract), [Cerulo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472..254C/abstract). 

For lower-mass systems, the morphological transformation is even more severe. High-speed encounters, or "galaxy harassment," alongside tidal disruption in the cluster core, can strip an average of $\sim 40\%$ of the galaxy's total mass [Moore et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996Natur.379..613M/abstract), [Gill et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract). This combination of mass loss and tidal heating efficiently transforms small, gas-rich irregulars and disk galaxies into quiescent dwarf ellipticals and [[defunct-dwarf-spheroidals|Defunct dwarf spheroidals]] [Moore et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996Natur.379..613M/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract). Furthermore, observations indicate that some dwarf ellipticals appear in clusters after the bulk of the members have formed, suggesting that they are actually backsplash candidates created via tidal processes during their late infall [Conselice et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005nfcd.conf..277C/abstract). In extreme cases of tidal disruption during a cluster transit, the stellar disks of dwarf galaxies can be completely stripped, leaving behind only the nuclei which are observed as ultra-compact dwarf (UCD) galaxies [Drinkwater et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Natur.423..519D/abstract). The evolutionary connection between these dwarf classes highlights the importance of [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]] in the outskirts of massive halos.

Quantitatively, this structural evolution manifests as a systematic shift in photometric parameters. As the extended disk is stripped of its gas and subsequently fades, a galaxy's evolution branches depending on its initial structural properties. For many systems, the effective bulge-to-total ratio ($B/T$) increases, effectively migrating the system along the parallel-sequence Hubble classification from a late-type spiral to a bulge-dominated early-type S0 galaxy. Classical bulge-hosting spiral galaxies are particularly prone to transforming into S0 morphology in dense environments as their star formation is quenched [Mishra et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.5572M/abstract). Conversely, other systems evolve into spheroidal (Sph) galaxies, which are distinct, bulgeless S0s that form a sequence of decreasing $B/T$ ratios, culminating in structures with near-zero bulge components [Kormendy et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract). 

To bridge this evolutionary gap, intermediate populations are critical. For instance, 'blue spheroids' serve as a transition population; they appear structurally similar to fully passive red spheroids while still maintaining active star formation and gas reservoirs reminiscent of spirals [Mahajan et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475..788M/abstract). Furthermore, observations of cluster environments reveal that post-starburst galaxies frequently appear morphologically intermediate between active, gas-stripped jellyfish galaxies and fully passive red members [Bellhouse et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937...18B/abstract). By mapping these intermediate stages, backsplash galaxies provide a direct observational record of the mechanisms driving the morphology-density relation in the local Universe.

## Local Group and Low-Mass Backsplash Candidates

Observations and cosmological simulations of low-mass systems ($M_\star \sim 10^5 - 10^7 M_\odot$) near massive hosts provide a unique laboratory for studying the backsplash phenomenon. In low-density environments, such low-mass dwarf galaxies are generally expected to remain gas-rich and actively star-forming. For example, 'Little Blue Spheroids' (LBS) found in isolation are actively star-forming, rotating systems that are theorized to act as direct precursors to quiescent cluster dwarf ellipticals if subjected to environmental quenching during a cluster transit [Moffett et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.2830M/abstract). Therefore, the discovery of isolated, quiescent dwarfs strongly suggests a history of environmental pre-processing, such as ram-pressure stripping during a past transit through a massive host halo [Simpson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478..548S/abstract). Cosmological simulations of Milky Way-like halos indicate that "returning" backsplash systems—those that have passed through the host's virial radius, $R_{200}$, and exited—exhibit quenching properties virtually identical to satellite galaxies currently residing within the halo. These models demonstrate that dwarf systems located beyond $600$ kpc from their host are typically only quenched if their stellar masses fall below $10^7 M_\odot$ [Simpson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478..548S/abstract).

Recent surveys have identified several compelling backsplash candidates around nearby massive spirals. In the M81 group, the quenched, low-mass ($M_\star \sim 10^7 M_\odot$) dwarf dw0910+7326 (nicknamed "Blobby") has been identified as a prime backsplash candidate. Using surface brightness fluctuations (SBF), its distance was measured at $3.21^{+0.41}_{-0.36}$ Mpc, placing it at a projected clustercentric radius of $1.0 < R/R_{\rm vir} < 2.7$ outside the combined M81-M82 system [Casey et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.4715C/abstract). Similarly, near M101, observations have revealed a population of semi-resolved, quiescent dwarfs, notably including the newly discovered Sha DG-I and Sha DG-II. These galaxies feature absolute magnitudes between $M_V \approx -7.7$ and $-8.2$ and half-light radii $r_h \approx 110-204$ pc, with stellar populations and relative distances consistent with a backsplash origin after being tidally ejected from the M101 system [Shapiro et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251214808S/abstract). Furthermore, the backsplash mechanism has been proposed to explain isolated, quiescent ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs) like the candidate "Zangetsu" in the COSMOS field, which exhibits an extreme central surface brightness of $\mu_{0,g} = 26.60$ mag arcsec$^{-2}$ [Wei et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250514073W/abstract).

Despite the utility of the backsplash model in explaining isolated quenched systems, recent 3D kinematic data within the Local Group have introduced significant tension. Historically, classic isolated quenched dwarfs such as Cetus and Tucana were presumed to be backsplash galaxies that had previously interacted with the Milky Way or M31. However, high-precision proper motion measurements using the Hubble Space Telescope challenge this assumption [Bennet et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..228B/abstract). Orbital reconstructions indicate that Tucana cannot have interacted with any massive Local Group host. Cetus similarly shows a low probability ($\sim 4-6\%$) of having interacted with M31 or the Milky Way within the last $6$ Gyr, although an earlier encounter cannot be entirely ruled out due to the limitations of rigid orbital models at extended timescales [Bennet et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..228B/abstract). Other isolated dwarfs, such as Aquarius, Leo T, Pisces, and Pegasus, possess kinematics consistent with their first infall. These findings underscore the diversity of [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]], suggesting that environmental interaction with a massive host is not the sole quenching mechanism for isolated [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] in the $10^5 - 10^7 M_\odot$ regime [Bennet et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..228B/abstract).

## Post-starburst Signatures and Rejuvenation

The extreme environmental conditions encountered by backsplash galaxies during their transit through a cluster core can drastically alter their star formation histories. A fraction of these galaxies exhibit the classic "post-starburst" or "E+A" (also referred to as **K+A galaxies**) optical spectrum, denoting a system that has undergone a rapid truncation of star formation following a recent starburst phase [French et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASP..133g2001F/abstract). Spectroscopically, **Post-starburst (E+A)** galaxies are characterized by strong **Balmer absorption** lines (e.g., $\text{H}\delta$, $\text{H}\gamma$, $\text{H}\beta$) originating from a dominant population of A-type stars, coupled with a distinct lack of nebular emission lines such as $[\text{O II}] \ \lambda 3727$ or $\text{H}\alpha$ that would indicate ongoing star formation [Goto et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.357..937G/abstract).

To quantify this stellar population transformation, studies frequently utilize the spectral index $D_n(4000)$ alongside Balmer line equivalent widths (EWs). During their initial infall phase, galaxies exhibit an aging of their stellar populations and a decline in specific star formation rates compared to the infalling field population, as reflected by variations in the $D_n(4000)$ index, which indicate an older average stellar population closer to the cluster center [Qu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023RAA....23h5007Q/abstract). Despite their distinct spectral signatures, luminous post-starburst systems are a rare demographic, accounting for approximately $1\%$ of all quenching events at intermediate redshifts [Pattarakijwanich et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833...19P/abstract). Spatially resolved studies of green valley E+A galaxies confirm that they genuinely undergo widespread quenching, often completely lacking ongoing star formation throughout the entire system out to three effective radii [Greene et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910..162G/abstract). This transition is also evident at higher redshifts; evidence from $z \sim 1$ surveys demonstrates that post-starburst (k+a) galaxies are morphologically heterogeneous and typically reside in environments just as dense as those of fully quiescent galaxies [Vergani et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...509A..42V/abstract). Furthermore, the literature presents conflicting evidence regarding the dominant trigger for this rapid quenching phase; while some studies emphasize large-scale cluster mechanisms like ram-pressure stripping during core transit, others argue that local galaxy-galaxy interactions ($\lesssim 100 \text{ kpc}$) are the primary drivers of the E+A phase, even in cluster outskirts [Goto et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.357..937G/abstract).

While many backsplash galaxies evolve passively into the quiescent population, a subset shows evidence of **Rejuvenation**—a secondary phase of star formation following a period of complete quenching. This phenomenon can be triggered by late-time gas accretion or environmental perturbations that funnel surviving gas into the galactic center. For instance, barred galaxies that have been quenched during their cluster passage can undergo central rejuvenation; as they exit the cluster beyond the virial radius, the interplay between the galactic bar and gas perturbed by ram pressure can reignite nuclear star formation [Tawfeek et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...940....1T/abstract). 

Observationally, rejuvenating galaxies can blend in with standard star-forming populations in broadband photometry. However, they can be isolated spectroscopically by their highly depressed Balmer absorption features. In contrast to standard post-starburst galaxies, which are dominated by A-type stars, rejuvenating systems exhibit a relative deficit of these intermediate-age stars, instead resembling "O-A" spectra. Mildly rejuvenating galaxies can be effectively identified by $\text{H}\beta$, $\text{H}\gamma$, and $\text{H}\delta$ absorption equivalent widths of $\lesssim 3$ Å [Zhang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952....6Z/abstract). Identifying these rare evolutionary phases is crucial for understanding whether backsplash galaxies permanently join the quiescent sequence or experience cyclical episodes of star formation as they continue to orbit their host clusters.

## Insights from Cosmological Simulations

Because observing the true 3D orbital histories of galaxies is impossible for distant clusters, numerical simulations are critical for isolating the backsplash population from pristine field galaxies, first-time infallers, and [[interloper-galaxies|Interloper galaxies]] [Coenda et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.1934C/abstract), [Martínez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250204446M/abstract). By tracking the complete phase-space evolution of cluster satellites, simulators can disentangle the environmental effects of the cluster core from secular evolutionary processes.

Early high-resolution N-body simulations established the kinematic distinctness of this population, demonstrating that roughly $50\%$ of galaxies situated at clustercentric radii between $1$ and $2 R_{\rm vir}$ are on outbound or returning trajectories after having penetrated the cluster core. During this deep transit, they experience significant tidal disruption, suffering an average mass loss of $\sim 40\%$ compared to their pre-infall halo masses [Gill et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract). 

More recently, large-volume hydrodynamical simulations have allowed astrophysicists to trace the intertwined evolution of dark matter halos and baryonic properties. The Three Hundred Project, a suite of 324 cluster resimulations, demonstrates that the backsplash fraction is highly sensitive to the dynamical history of the host. Specifically, dynamically relaxed clusters accumulate a larger fraction of backsplash galaxies in their outskirts than [[unrelaxed-galaxy-clusters|Unrelaxed galaxy clusters]] [Haggar et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.6074H/abstract). Furthermore, these simulations show that backsplash trajectories can be highly deflected: $30\%$ to $60\%$ of galaxies located along cosmic filaments at $R_{\rm 200}$ are actually returning backsplash galaxies rather than pristine infalling systems [Kuchner et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510..581K/abstract). 

State-of-the-art cosmological magneto-hydrodynamical runs, such as IllustrisTNG, provide detailed insights into the structural and chemical transformations of these galaxies. In the TNG300-1 volume (which features a baryonic resolution of $M_{\rm b} \approx 1.1 \times 10^7 M_\odot$), machine learning classifiers applied to isolated clusters of mass $10^{13.0} < M_{\rm 200,mean}/M_\odot < 10^{15.5}$ reveal that backsplash galaxies systematically exhibit lower gas fractions, higher mass-to-light ratios, and larger stellar sizes compared to field galaxies of similar mass [Borrow et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520..649B/abstract). Their extended stellar sizes are interpreted as a direct consequence of tidal heating and stripping during the cluster core passage.

However, the extreme environmental history of backsplash galaxies also exposes the limitations of current subgrid physics. For instance, IllustrisTNG predicts anomalously low black hole occupation fractions for the backsplash population. Subsequent analysis has revealed this to be a numerical artifact: the black hole recentring schemes used in the simulation's subgrid physics frequently cause satellite galaxies to artificially shed their supermassive black holes when subjected to the strong tidal forces of a cluster core [Borrow et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520..649B/abstract). Consequently, while simulations successfully reproduce the macroscopic quenching, structural heating, and mass loss of the backsplash population, tensions remain in accurately modeling their central engines without tracking the microphysics of orbital dynamics at higher resolutions.

## Historical Context and Environmental Evolution

The realization that galaxy evolution is driven by both internal secular processes and external environmental 'nurture' is rooted in early observations of the [[butcher-oemler-effect|Butcher-Oemler effect]] and the morphology-density relation. Historically, it was established that denser environments preferentially host early-type, red, and gas-poor systems, whereas the isolated field is dominated by star-forming disks. Foundational spectroscopic surveys have demonstrated that the fraction of star-forming galaxies steadily decreases with local density across all environments, including the low-density field regions where backsplash galaxies typically reside [Mateus et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.349.1251M/abstract). In the framework of hierarchical assembly, massive clusters grow by continuously accreting these field galaxies and smaller groups over cosmic time. 

Standard textbook theory has long maintained that galaxies undergo these physical and morphological transformations strictly "once they enter" a dense environment. Within the cluster potential, infalling galaxies are subjected to aggressive environmental mechanisms. Notably, galaxy harassment—multiple high-speed encounters between cluster members—was shown to drive rapid morphological evolution, efficiently transforming small disk galaxies into dwarf spheroidal systems [Moore et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996Natur.379..613M/abstract). 

However, the traditional binary view of "in-cluster" versus "field" galaxies underwent a massive paradigm shift with the discovery of the backsplash population. High-resolution $N$-body simulations and detailed phase-space analyses revealed that a significant fraction of galaxies residing outside the cluster virial radius ($R_{\mathrm{vir}}$) have previously penetrated the cluster core and exited on highly eccentric orbits [Gill et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract). Studies consistently demonstrate that approximately $50\%$ to $60\%$ of galaxies located at cluster-centric distances between $1 R_{\mathrm{vir}}$ and $2 R_{\mathrm{vir}}$ are not pristine first-time infallers, but rather backsplash systems that carry a permanent record of tidal disruption and mass loss from their core passage [Gill et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1327G/abstract), [Haggar et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.6074H/abstract). The exact fraction of this population is intimately linked to the overarching hierarchical assembly of the host; dynamically relaxed clusters harbor a significantly higher proportion of backsplash galaxies in their outskirts than actively merging or [[unrelaxed-galaxy-clusters|Unrelaxed galaxy clusters]] [Haggar et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.6074H/abstract).

Furthermore, modern models of environmental evolution emphasize the concept of "pre-processing," wherein galaxies begin to quench and structurally evolve within cosmic filaments and infalling groups long before they ever cross the primary cluster's virial boundary [Kuchner et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510..581K/abstract), [Ferreras et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4884F/abstract). This demonstrates that environmental influence extends far beyond the cluster core. 

Despite consensus on the importance of these external mechanisms, the literature exhibits ongoing tension regarding the exact sequence of galaxy transformation. As previously noted, while quenching rapidly precedes morphological transformation in dense environments, other findings suggest that internal secular processes drive morphological changes first in the isolated field [Martínez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4360M/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251102964E/abstract). For example, this environmentally driven backsplash quenching contrasts with the evolution of the 'Red Misfit' population—red, star-forming galaxies that transition primarily due to internal secular processes rather than environmental interactions [Evans et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.5284E/abstract). Consequently, backsplash galaxies—having survived a single, extreme environmental passage—serve as a critical transitional population for disentangling the competing timescales of star formation cessation and structural evolution.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The robust identification and characterisation of backsplash galaxies remains a significant observational challenge due to projection effects in 2D phase-space analyses. While recent methodological advances—such as the inversion of a classification confusion matrix—can statistically correct for 2D misclassifications and recover underlying physical properties [Martínez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250204446M/abstract), definitive identification ultimately requires high-precision 3D kinematic data. Looking forward, next-generation large-scale spectroscopic campaigns, such as the *4MOST CHANCES* low-$z$ sub-survey, will systematically map cluster outskirts out to $5 R_{200}$. By combining deep photometry with dense spectroscopic sampling, such surveys will precisely map the orbital histories of galaxies in cluster peripheries and reduce phase-space degeneracy [Méndez-Hernández et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251019958M/abstract).

A major open question in the literature concerns the ultimate fate and true origin of isolated, quenched dwarf galaxies. As highlighted by recent proper motion studies of Local Group dwarfs like Cetus and Tucana, isolation and quiescence do not inherently guarantee a backsplash origin [Bennet et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..228B/abstract). This unresolved tension requires a reassessment of how dark matter subhaloes in the stellar mass range of $M_* \approx 10^5 - 10^7 M_\odot$ evolve, and highlights the need for alternative quenching mechanisms within the broader context of [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]]. 

Furthermore, the precise contribution of backsplash galaxies to the "Green Valley" transition population in the general field remains ambiguous. Disentangling the signatures of a single cluster passage from pre-processing in filamentary environments is inherently complex. It is currently unclear to what extent *cosmic web stripping* [Vulcani et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...914...27V/abstract) and group-level interactions mimic the star formation quenching and morphological transformations typically attributed to the extreme tidal and ram-pressure forces of the cluster core. 

Finally, linking local environmental physics to high-redshift galaxy assembly presents a burgeoning frontier. Researchers are actively investigating whether the dense environments found in early [[galaxy-overdensities|Galaxy overdensities]] drive the evolutionary cycles of extreme, newly discovered populations, such as *JWST Little Red Dots* (LRDs). Characterised by compact sizes, high star formation rates, and overmassive central black holes, LRDs are hypothesized to represent a low-spin galaxy population [Loeb et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RNAAS...8..182L/abstract). Recent observations of early interacting groups (e.g., the "Stingray" system) suggest that the rapid emergence or fading of these LRDs could be directly triggered by environmental effects [Mérida et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251006408M/abstract). To better understand these extreme objects, future research is also targeting local analogues of high-redshift compact star-formers, such as 'Blueberry' galaxies, to determine if their uniquely high star formation rates and anomalously low X-ray luminosities are influenced by recent environmental transits [Adamcová et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A..27A/abstract). Determining whether such high-$z$ systems eventually become the quenched, backsplash building blocks of local clusters remains a critical avenue for future research.
