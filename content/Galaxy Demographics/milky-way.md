The Milky Way is the galaxy that contains our Solar System, functioning as the foundational astrophysical laboratory for understanding the formation and evolution of $L^\star$ [[disk-galaxies|barred spiral galaxies]]. Structurally, the Galaxy is composed of several distinct components: a central [[supermassive-black-holes|supermassive black hole]], Sagittarius A*, residing within [[the-galactic-center|The Galactic Center]]; a secularly evolved [[alard-bar|stellar bar]] that projects as a boxy/peanut-shaped [[galactic-bulges|bulge]] [Minniti et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..245..323M/abstract); a bimodal disk system separated into chemically and kinematically distinct [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] and [[thick-disks|Thick disks]]; and an extended, metal-poor [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]] embedded within a massive dark matter halo. Historically conceptualized as a system in steady-state equilibrium [Mihalas et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981gask.book.....M/abstract), [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract), modern high-dimensional data have redefined the Milky Way as a highly dynamic, non-equilibrium structure undergoing active perturbations from infalling [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] and the reflex motion of the Milky Way induced by the Large Magellanic Cloud.

The advent of precision astrometry from the *Gaia* mission, coupled with massively multiplexed spectroscopic surveys, has revolutionized the field of [[galactic-archaeology|Galactic archaeology]]. By utilizing the invariant elemental abundances and 6D phase-space coordinates of millions of stars as fossil records, researchers have reconstructed the Galaxy's hierarchical assembly history. This chemo-dynamical evidence reveals a chaotic early epoch dominated by intense star formation and major accretion events, most notably an ancient collision with the [[kraken-merger|Kraken/LKH progenitor]] and the transformative Gaia-Enceladus merger approximately 10 billion years ago [Massari et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260118896M/abstract), [Venn et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.563...43V/abstract). Following these early catastrophic events, the Milky Way transitioned into a remarkably quiescent phase of secular evolution. This rare evolutionary pathway—occurring in less than 2% of similar-mass [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]—allowed for the prolonged, undisturbed [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|inside-out growth]] of its [[galactic-disk-structure-and-dynamics|stellar disk]] and the preservation of primordial structures like the recently identified PanGu proto-disk.

Despite these immense observational leaps, the Milky Way's exact position within the broader cosmological context remains a subject of active tension and revision. Recent spatial mapping suggests the Galactic disk exhibits a broken-exponential radial profile rather than a classical single exponential. This structural revision significantly lowers the Galaxy's estimated total [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass]] to roughly $2.6 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$, necessitating a higher dark matter-to-baryon ratio in the inner Galaxy than previously assumed. Furthermore, outstanding debates persist regarding the total mass of the dark matter halo, the exact pattern speed of the central bar, and whether the Milky Way harbors a classical, merger-driven bulge hidden within its central regions. Resolving these anomalies—alongside searches for an accreted "dark disk," the systematic near-infrared mapping of the Galaxy's heavily dust-obscured far side, and mapping large-scale magnetic fields—constitutes the primary frontier of contemporary Galactic astrophysics.

## Historical Development and the Gaia Revolution

Long before modern telescopic observations, the Milky Way held profound cultural significance; for example, the ancient Egyptian personification of the Galaxy as the sky goddess Nut utilized her torso and limbs as seasonal markers for the Galaxy's path across the heavens [Graur et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JAHH...27...28G/abstract). 

The historical understanding of the Milky Way’s physical structure evolved significantly over the 20th century. Early morphological mapping relied on the distribution of [[galactic-open-cluster-population|open clusters]] to trace the [[galactic-disk-structure-and-dynamics|Galactic disk]] [Moitinho et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..266..106M/abstract), culminating in the 1951 delineation of the local spiral arms using [[giant-h-ii-regions|H II regions]] as tracers [Gingerich et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985IAUS..106...59G/abstract). A foundational conceptual shift occurred during World War II when Walter Baade, utilizing blackout conditions at Mount Wilson Observatory, resolved distinct stellar components in nearby galaxies and the Milky Way. Baade introduced the concept of [[galactic-stellar-populations|stellar populations]], dividing the Galaxy into Population I—younger, metal-rich stars concentrated in the Galactic disk—and Population II—older, metal-poor stars residing in the [[galactic-bulges|central bulge]] and [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]]. 

For decades, Galactic models treated the Milky Way as a largely isolated system in dynamical equilibrium. However, anomalies such as the "G-dwarf problem"—a stark deficit of low-metallicity G-type stars compared to closed-box model predictions—highlighted the necessity of treating the Galaxy as an open system subject to continuous [[mass-accretion-and-assembly|gas accretion]] [Greener et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502L..95G/abstract). The late 20th and early 21st centuries saw the advent of massive ground-based spectroscopic surveys, such as RAVE, APOGEE, and GALAH. These surveys provided precise radial velocities and high-resolution chemical abundances for hundreds of thousands of stars, enabling "chemical tagging" to reconstruct the origins of distinct Galactic components based on their invariant elemental abundances [Martell et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150700079M/abstract), [Casey et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...840...59C/abstract).

The field was subsequently transformed by the European Space Agency's *Gaia* mission, which delivered unprecedented microarcsecond astrometry for over 1.3 billion stars [Eyer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019IAUS..339...50E/abstract). By combining *Gaia*'s proper motions and parallaxes with ground-based spectroscopy, astronomers gained access to full 6D phase-space coordinates. This wealth of data triggered a major paradigm shift: the Milky Way is no longer viewed as a steady-state system, but rather as a highly dynamic, non-equilibrium galaxy actively perturbed by satellite interactions, such as the reflex motion induced by the infalling [[satellite-galaxies|Large Magellanic Cloud]] [Petersen et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatAs...5..251P/abstract), [Kawata et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241212252K/abstract).

This era of precision astrometry cemented the field of [[galactic-archaeology|Galactic archaeology]], allowing researchers to unravel the Galaxy's hierarchical assembly. The most prominent early discovery was the identification of highly eccentric, radially anisotropic stellar debris in the halo, recognized as the remnants of a massive [[accreted-dwarf-galaxy-origin|dwarf galaxy merger]]—known as Gaia-Enceladus or the "Sausage"—that collided with the Milky Way approximately 10 billion years ago at $z \approx 2$ [Venn et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.563...43V/abstract), [Gallart et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NatAs...3..932G/abstract), [Fattahi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.4471F/abstract). Newer chemo-dynamical evidence points to distinct, even earlier massive mergers (such as [[kraken-merger|Kraken/LKH]]) that deposited mass into the innermost proto-Galaxy during the Universe's earliest epochs [Horta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016374H/abstract), [Massari et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260118896M/abstract).

## Structural Architecture: Disks, Bar, and Bulge

The Milky Way's structural architecture exhibits the complex morphology typical of an $L^\ast$ [[disk-galaxies|barred spiral galaxy]]. However, recent large-scale chemo-dynamical surveys have fundamentally revised the quantitative parameters defining its primary components: the disk system, the central bar, and the bulge.

**The Disk System**
The Galactic disk is classically separated into two chemically and kinematically distinct components: the [[thin-disks|thin disk]] and the [[thick-disks|thick disk]]. The thin disk is characterized by younger, metal-rich, and low-[$\alpha$/Fe] [[galactic-stellar-populations|stellar populations]], while the thick disk contains older, metal-poor, and high-[$\alpha$/Fe] stars [Bensby et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..298...17B/abstract), [Scott et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913L..11S/abstract). This thick disk was formed during an intense star formation phase between 9.0 and 12.5 Gyr ago, suggesting it is as massive as the thin disk and played a fundamental role in the [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy's genesis]] [Snaith et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...781L..31S/abstract). Though hierarchical formation paradigms long assumed the thick disk formed first, recent isochrone-fitting indicates an early co-formation of the thin and thick disks at $z > 2$, prior to the primary Gaia-Enceladus accretion event [Borbolato et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...994..126B/abstract). Furthermore, while the *radial scale length* of the high-$\alpha$ disk is nearly independent of [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar age]], the vertical scale height has evolved dramatically. The oldest known structural component—the "PanGu" population—maintained a disk-like geometry for over 13 Gyr, but its scale height-to-length ratio only dropped below 0.5 for populations younger than 12.5 Gyr [Xiang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..101X/abstract).

Beyond the main disk, simulations suggest that the infall of the [[galactic-satellite-galaxies|Sagittarius dwarf galaxy]] (Sgr) was a primary architect of the Galaxy's spirality, producing the flared outer disk and distant ring-like structures [Purcell et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Natur.477..301P/abstract). The overall surface brightness profile of the disk has also undergone a paradigm shift. Traditionally modeled as a single exponential decay, age-resolved spatial mapping reveals a *broken exponential profile*. The stellar density is nearly flat between Galactocentric radii of $R = 3.5$ and $7.5$ kpc before decaying exponentially in the outer disk [Lian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1302L/abstract). This broken profile yields a significantly larger half-light radius of $R_e \approx 5.75 \pm 0.38$ kpc [Lian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1302L/abstract). Concurrently, extrapolations utilizing the newly resolved flat inner disk profile suggest a lower total [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass]] of $M_\star \approx 2.6 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$ [Lian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990L..37L/abstract).

**The Bar and Bulge**
Inward of the disk, the Milky Way is dominated by a strong, secularly evolved bar. Due to our in-plane vantage point, this bar is viewed nearly end-on at an angle of $\sim 25^\circ-30^\circ$ relative to the Sun-Galactic center line. This projection creates the prominent [[galactic-bulges|boxy-peanut bulge]] morphology mapped by near-infrared surveys [Shen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020RAA....20..159S/abstract). Meanwhile, surveys of classical Cepheid variable stars have been used to map the young stellar population of the disk and its warped outer structure [Skowron et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Sci...365..478S/abstract). The dominance of this structure has led to the classification of the Milky Way as a "pure-disk" [[disk-galaxies|galaxy]] lacking a significant merger-driven classical bulge [Shen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020RAA....20..159S/abstract). However, this interpretation remains contested. Recent kinematics of the inner $1.5$ kpc reveal a chemically distinct spheroidal "knot" with nearly radial orbits. Rather than an ancient proto-galactic fragment, this knot has super-solar metallicities and shares a common genesis with the Galactic bar and inner disk, indicating it could constitute a [[galactic-bulges|classical bulge]] formed via secular evolution [Horta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240207986H/abstract). Further supporting a complex assembly, systems like Liller 1 have been identified as "fossil fragments" of hierarchical assembly in the bulge, hosting diverse stellar populations ranging from as young as 1-3 Gyr alongside 12 Gyr old stars [Ferraro et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatAs...5..311F/abstract).

The dynamical influence of the bar is governed by its *bar pattern speed* ($\Omega_{\mathrm{bar}}$), which dictates the location of resonant orbits within the disk. The exact value of this parameter is highly debated. While several models of stellar and gas kinematics advocate for a fast bar rotating at $\Omega_{\mathrm{bar}} \approx 35-40$ km s$^{-1}$ kpc$^{-1}$ [Shen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020RAA....20..159S/abstract), recent chemo-dynamical analyses of APOGEE-Gaia data strongly support a slower bar with $\Omega_{\mathrm{bar}} = 24 \pm 3$ km s$^{-1}$ kpc$^{-1}$. This slower rotation pushes the corotation radius out to $R_{\mathrm{CR}} \approx 9.4-9.8$ kpc, implying that the bar's dynamical influence extends well past the solar neighborhood [Horta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240207986H/abstract).

## The Galactic Center and Sagittarius A*

The innermost region of the Milky Way, situated approximately 8 kpc from the Sun, is a remarkably complex and extreme astrophysical environment. At the heart of [[the-galactic-center|The Galactic Center]] lies the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Central Molecular Zone]], a dense, ring-like accumulation of molecular gas spanning the inner few hundred parsecs [Henshaw et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ASPC..534...83H/abstract). Fueled by the inward transport of matter driven by the Galactic bar, the Central Molecular Zone exhibits extreme interstellar medium conditions. It is characterized by uniquely high gas densities, temperatures, pressures, and turbulent motions, making it the most vigorous [[star-forming-galaxies|star-forming environment]] in the Galaxy [Henshaw et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ASPC..534...83H/abstract).

Residing at the dynamical core of this region is Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*), the Galaxy's central [[supermassive-black-holes|supermassive black hole]]. Precision astrometric tracking of stars in the central milli-parsecs has firmly constrained its mass to $M \approx 4 \times 10^6 M_\odot$ [Schödel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..312..274S/abstract), [Ciurlo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320081C/abstract). Because of its unprecedented proximity compared to extragalactic nuclei, Sgr A* provides a unique laboratory for testing general relativity in strong gravitational fields. Observations of short-period stellar orbits passing extremely close to the black hole have successfully measured relativistic effects, including gravitational redshift and orbital precession [Ciurlo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320081C/abstract). Although Sgr A* is currently quiescent compared to [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|active galaxies]], it exhibits continuous multi-wavelength variability and flaring in the radio, infrared, and X-ray regimes [Ciurlo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320081C/abstract).

Sgr A* is embedded within a massive [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|nuclear star cluster]], which is the densest stellar system in the Milky Way and the only such extragalactic-analog cluster that can be resolved into individual stars [Schödel et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..439..222S/abstract), [Schödel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..312..274S/abstract). Historically assumed to have a quasi-continuous star formation history, recent observations of the broader nuclear disk (spanning a radius of $\sim 150$ pc) reveal a highly episodic assembly. The bulk of the nuclear disk formed more than 8 Gyr ago and was followed by a long period of quiescence [Nogueras-Lara et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..377N/abstract). This lull was punctuated by a violent [[extreme-starbursts|starburst event]] approximately 1 Gyr ago, which formed roughly $5\%$ of the nuclear disk's mass within $\sim 100$ Myr—arguably one of the most energetic star formation episodes in the Galaxy's history [Nogueras-Lara et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..377N/abstract). Star formation has since continued at a lower rate, with a notable increase up to $\sim 30$ Myr ago.

The interaction between the nuclear star cluster and Sgr A* also drives extreme physical processes, including intense [[galaxy-black-hole-assembly|black hole feedback]]. There is kinematic evidence that a massive gas cloud fell into the central parsec approximately 6 Myr ago, triggering the formation of a tightly bound disk of [[adolescent-massive-galactic-clusters|young stars]] and briefly activating Sgr A* [Nayakshin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478L.127N/abstract). It is hypothesized that highly super-Eddington outflows during this active phase injected immense kinetic energy ($>10^{55}$ erg) into the surrounding medium, driving a quasi-spherical gas shell outward. The fragmentation of this atomic hydrogen shell under extreme feedback pressure may have forged the anomalous population of young stars currently observed on randomly oriented orbits within the central parsec [Nayakshin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478L.127N/abstract).

## Chemodynamics and Galactic Archaeology

The field of [[galactic-archaeology|Galactic archaeology]] seeks to reconstruct the assembly history of the Milky Way by treating the kinematics and chemical compositions of individual stars as fossil records. In the modern era, this is achieved through "chemical tagging," utilizing astrometry from the *Gaia* mission alongside high-dimensional data from large-scale spectroscopic surveys such as APOGEE, GALAH, RAVE, and LAMOST [Martell et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150700079M/abstract), [Casey et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...840...59C/abstract). By mapping elemental abundances—often up to 29 individual elements per star—astronomers can group stars into chemically homogeneous, mono-abundance populations that trace distinct formation epochs and dynamical histories [Rix et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&ARv..21...61R/abstract), [Martell et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150700079M/abstract).

Chemodynamical surveys have definitively mapped a structural and chemical dichotomy in the Galactic disk. In the $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ versus $[\mathrm{Fe}/\mathrm{H}]$ plane, disk stars exhibit a pronounced bimodality, cleanly separating into an alpha-enhanced, metal-poor component and a low-alpha, metal-rich component [Bensby et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..298...17B/abstract), [Scott et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913L..11S/abstract), [Imig et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954..124I/abstract). These correspond broadly to the geometric [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] and [[thin-disks|Thin disks]], respectively. Globally, the integrated metallicity gradients of the Milky Way display an unusual $\Lambda$-like broken profile—mildly positive inside $R \approx 7 \text{ kpc}$ and steeply negative outside—which may be atypical compared to the general population of star-forming [[disk-galaxies|spiral galaxies]] [Lian et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7..951L/abstract). The exact substructure of the disk remains an area of active debate; for instance, some studies advocate for a distinct "metal-weak thick disk" (MWTD) exhibiting a rotational velocity of $\sim 150 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ compared to the canonical thick disk's $\sim 180 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Carollo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887...22C/abstract). 

Beyond the disk, stellar elemental abundances and kinematics reveal a violent early assembly history dominated by hierarchical accretion. Chemical dissection isolates primordial fragments and tracks the sequence of major dwarf galaxy mergers that populated the [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]]. Precision asteroseismology of indigenous halo stars, such as $\nu$ Indi, dates them to $11.0 \pm 0.7$ Gyr ago. Additionally, observations of [[chandrasekhar-mass-white-dwarf|white dwarf stars]] in the inner halo provide an independent age constraint of $11.4 \pm 0.7$ billion years, consistent with asteroseismic dating [Kalirai et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.486...90K/abstract), [Chaplin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..382C/abstract). The kinematic heating observed in these ancient stars provides a lower limit on the timing of major impact events like Gaia-Enceladus, indicating the Milky Way had already formed a substantial *in situ* population prior to these collisions [Chaplin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..382C/abstract), [Montalbán et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatAs...5..640M/abstract). Remnants of accreted building blocks are also preserved as anomalous [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]]. Systems such as [[omega-centauri|Omega Centauri]] and Terzan 5 display significant internal spreads in age and iron abundance, strongly suggesting they are the surviving nuclear cores of [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]] rather than simple stellar populations formed *in situ* [Lee et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Natur.402...55L/abstract), [Ferraro et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..483F/abstract).

However, chemical tagging also serves as a critical cautionary tool in archaeology. The Nyx stream, initially identified as a likely accreted dwarf remnant, was shown through GALAH and APOGEE abundances to lack an extragalactic origin, instead representing a high-velocity component of the indigenous thick disk [Zucker et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912L..30Z/abstract).

## Hierarchical Assembly and Major Merger History

In accordance with the standard cosmological paradigm, the Milky Way built up its mass through a process of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|hierarchical assembly]], characterized by the continuous accretion of gas and the merging of smaller [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] [Montalbán et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatAs...5..640M/abstract). The relics of this violent early history are preserved as kinematic and chemical fossils within the Galaxy's [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]] and [[thick-disks|Thick disks]]. Globally, the Milky Way's evolution can be synthesized into three physical phases: an initial disordered protogalaxy spin-up, an era of intense heating and torqueing driven by major gas-rich mergers like the Gaia-Enceladus event, and a subsequent smooth 'cooldown' into a kinematically cold young disk [Chandra et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...972..112C/abstract).

While the Gaia-Enceladus collision was long thought to be the primary early merger, recent astrometric and spectroscopic data have uncovered evidence for an even older, massive accretion event. Termed the Low-energy-Kraken-Heracles (LKH) merger (or simply the [[kraken-merger|Kraken merger]]), this event is estimated to have occurred just $\sim 1.5$ Gyr after the Big Bang [Massari et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260118896M/abstract). The LKH progenitor is thought to have had a [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass]] of $\sim 5 \times 10^8 M_\odot$. Owing to its early accretion, it deposited the majority of its debris deep within the inner $6$ kpc of the Galaxy [Horta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016374H/abstract), [Massari et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260118896M/abstract). Highly precise relative age determinations of inner-Galaxy star clusters strongly support its external origin and distinctness from the Galaxy's primary *in situ* progenitor [Massari et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260118896M/abstract).

Following LKH, the most defining accretion event in the Galaxy's history was the merger with the Gaia-Enceladus (also known as the *Gaia*-Sausage) [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxy]] [Venn et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.563...43V/abstract). Occurring approximately $10$ billion years ago ($z \approx 2$), the Gaia-Enceladus progenitor also had an estimated stellar mass of $\sim 5 \times 10^8 M_\odot$ [Massari et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260118896M/abstract). This major merger deposited a massive population of metal-rich stars on highly eccentric orbits, which now dominate the inner stellar halo [Fattahi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.4471F/abstract), [Gallart et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NatAs...3..932G/abstract). The impact dynamically heated the pre-existing *in situ* stellar populations, strongly influencing the structural evolution of the Milky Way's thick disk [Bignone et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...883L...5B/abstract). 

The hierarchical assembly process is actively traced through [[stellar-substructure|Stellar substructure]], such as stellar streams and accreted star clusters. Up to $25\%$ of the Milky Way's [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] (approximately $27$ to $47$ individual clusters) are estimated to be of extragalactic origin, having been accreted alongside their host dwarf galaxies [Forbes et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.404.1203F/abstract), [Lee et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0707.1705L/abstract). These accreted globular clusters exhibit age-metallicity relations that branch to younger ages compared to the *in situ* population [Forbes et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.404.1203F/abstract). Furthermore, groups of low-metallicity globular clusters in the outer halo show a planar alignment and a small time gap from indigenous clusters, supporting their origin from a captured satellite galaxy [Yoon et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002Sci...297..578Y/abstract). The most massive of these, [[omega-centauri|Omega Centauri]], exhibits multiple stellar populations with a significant spread in age and metallicity, identifying it as the likely stripped remnant core of a massive accreted dwarf galaxy [Lee et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Natur.402...55L/abstract).

Following the chaotic epoch of the LKH and Gaia-Enceladus mergers, the Milky Way has experienced an exceptionally quiescent dynamical history compared to similar mass [[disk-galaxies|spiral galaxies]] [Xiang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..101X/abstract), [Hammer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012EPJWC..1901004H/abstract). This long period of secular evolution allowed for the subsequent, largely undisturbed formation of the Galaxy's thin disk.

## The Proto-Galaxy and Earliest Disk Formation

The emergence of the Milky Way's earliest components represents a critical phase of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]]. Chemical and kinematic dissection of the Galaxy's oldest stellar populations indicates that star formation in the central regions began within $\approx 0.2$ Gyr of the Big Bang, as evidenced by ancient relic [[globular-clusters|globular clusters]] such as Tonantzintla 2 [Ortolani et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260118567O/abstract). Chemical tagging of unevolved, metal-poor stars ($-2 < [\text{Fe/H}] < -0.5$) reveals that the [[protogalaxies|proto-galaxy]] was composed of at least two primary fragments: an *in situ* progenitor and early accreted structures like Heracles. Integrating the density of these chemically defined populations yields a minimum stellar mass for the inner proto-galaxy of:

$$ M_{*} (r < 10\ \text{kpc}) = (9.1 \pm 0.2) \times 10^{8}\ M_{\odot} $$

[Horta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016374H/abstract). 

Recent chronochemodynamical mapping has uncovered the Galaxy's oldest surviving disk component, dubbed the PanGu population. Formed over 13 Gyr ago ($z > 2$), PanGu maintained a distinct disk geometry despite the chaotic merger environment of the early Universe [Xiang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..101X/abstract). The scale height-to-length ratio of this primordial disk dropped below 0.5 for populations younger than 12.5 Gyr. Today, the PanGu population constitutes a present-day stellar mass of $\approx 2 \times 10^{9}\ M_{\odot}$, making it a major early building block of the Milky Way [Xiang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..101X/abstract).

The conventional model of sequential disk formation—where [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] fully assemble before [[thin-disks|Thin disks]]—has been challenged by the discovery of early co-formation. Age estimates using Bayesian isochrone fitting demonstrate that an old thin disk population ($> 11$ Gyr) co-evolved with the thick disk at $z > 2$ [Borbolato et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...994..126B/abstract). This implies that the proto-Milky Way initiated the formation of a low-$\alpha$ disk *in situ*, without requiring a gas-rich merger to trigger it. Meanwhile, the high-$\alpha$ thick disk underwent a vigorous star formation phase, peaking at a rate of $\approx 11\ M_{\odot}\ \text{yr}^{-1}$ roughly 11 Gyr ago [Xiang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..101X/abstract).

This early assembly aligns with the paradigm of inside-out growth. Observations of morphologically mature high-redshift analogs (such as JADES-GS+53.18343-27.79097 within the first 700 million years of the Universe's history) exhibit highly compact cores ($R_{e} < 100$ pc) surrounded by actively [[star-forming-galaxies|star-forming disks]] ($R \approx 400$ pc) [Baker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..141B/abstract). Such [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] strongly supports the notion that the Milky Way first assembled a dense [[galactic-bulges|proto-bulge]] and an inner disk before progressively building its extended outer disk. Furthermore, observations of high-redshift spiral galaxies like BRI 1335-0417, which possessed central bulges and rotating disks within 1.4 billion years of the Big Bang, mirror the early structural maturity of the Milky Way's proto-disk [Tsukui et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...372.1201T/abstract). As described in prior sections, this *in situ* star formation was subsequently truncated and structurally heated by the Gaia-Enceladus merger approximately 10 billion years ago [Borbolato et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...994..126B/abstract). For the more recent assembly of the Milky Way, a "leaky accretion disk" model explains the interplay of gas inflows, outflows, and *in situ* star formation, successfully reproducing the observed radial metallicity profiles for stars formed within the last 4 Gyr [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...34W/abstract).

## Mass Distribution and the Dark Matter Halo

The mass of the Milky Way is overwhelmingly dominated by its extended dark matter halo, though the precise measurement of its total mass remains a subject of active debate. Current literature estimates for the dark matter halo mass broadly span between $1.0 \times 10^{12} M_\odot$ and $1.9 \times 10^{12} M_\odot$, reflecting tensions between different observational methodologies and dynamical models. 

Kinematics of halo tracer populations provide critical constraints on the Galactic gravitational potential. The asymmetry in the radial velocity distribution of background halo stars, caused by escaping hypervelocity stars, depends strongly on the halo's escape speed. Assuming a characteristic travel time of $\sim 330$ Myr, these constraints yield a favored halo mass of $1.2 - 1.9 \times 10^{12} M_\odot$ [Fragione et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017NewA...55...32F/abstract). Conversely, full 6D phase-space analyses of [[galactic-satellite-galaxies|Galactic satellite galaxies]] using Gaia astrometry, when compared against $\Lambda$CDM subhalo simulations, favor a lighter halo mass of approximately $1.0 - 1.2 \times 10^{12} M_\odot$ [Rodriguez Wimberly et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513.4968R/abstract). Furthermore, the clustering properties of luminous satellite galaxies in 4D phase space exhibit small-scale tensions with standard cold dark matter predictions, lacking the degree of substructure expected from simulations [Bozek et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...772..109B/abstract). At the extreme outskirts of the gravitational potential, the dark matter halo interacts with the broader Local Group environment; highly concentrated "Hermeian" halos have been identified that have passed through the potentials of both the Milky Way and the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] [Newton et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3612N/abstract).

In the inner Galaxy, the mass distribution is heavily influenced by the baryonic [[stellar-component|Stellar component]]. As a result of the recently identified broken-exponential structure of the [[galactic-disk-structure-and-dynamics|Galactic Disk Structure and Dynamics]], estimates of the Milky Way's total [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass]] have been driven down to roughly $2.6 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$ [Lian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990L..37L/abstract), significantly lower than traditional estimates of $4.6 - 6.0 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$. This downward revision implies a correspondingly larger dark-matter-to-baryon ratio in the inner Milky Way to maintain the observed rotation curve. Additionally, the dynamical modeling of the inner potential is complicated by the slower measured pattern speed of the Galactic bar, which extends its gravitational influence further into the disk [Horta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240207986H/abstract).

Traditionally modeled as a system in steady-state equilibrium, the Milky Way's dark matter halo is now understood to be undergoing active, non-equilibrium deformation. The infall of the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC)—a massive satellite with a mass exceeding $10\%$ of the Milky Way's—at a velocity of $\sim 327 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ is significantly perturbing the Galactic potential [Petersen et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatAs...5..251P/abstract). This interaction induces a pronounced LMC reflex motion, dislodging the Galactic disk from the halo's center of mass. Consequently, the disk is moving with respect to tracer stars in the outer halo, pointing toward an earlier location on the LMC's trajectory [Petersen et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatAs...5..251P/abstract). Time-dependent deformations in both the Milky Way and LMC dark matter halos exert strong gravitational influences on halo substructures, generating detectable kinematic signatures and actively reshaping structures like the Orphan-Chenab stellar stream [Lilleengen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518..774L/abstract).

Beyond the immediate stellar and dark matter components, the Milky Way is enveloped by a massive circumgalactic medium (CGM). However, analyzing this gas from our vantage point within the disk presents significant challenges. Studies utilizing the "Mocky Way" simulations have demonstrated that inside-out observations of the CGM are highly biased; in particular, H I 21 cm and absorption-line studies probe only a fraction of the high-latitude CGM mass [Zheng et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...896..143Z/abstract).

## The Milky Way in Cosmological Context

When evaluating the Milky Way in a cosmological context, astronomers compare its structural, chemical, and kinematic properties to extragalactic Milky Way analogues to determine whether it is a typical $L^\star$ [[disk-galaxies|spiral galaxy]]. Early scaling metrics, such as the Tully-Fisher relationship, suggested that the Milky Way is a relatively standard, average-sized spiral galaxy [Goodwin et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997astro.ph..4216G/abstract), [Reshetnikov et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000Ap.....43..145R/abstract). However, modern high-dimensional survey data reveal a more nuanced picture, indicating that while the Galaxy follows several broad extragalactic scaling relations, its specific assembly history makes it an outlier in several respects.

In terms of global photometric properties, the Milky Way is classified as a "red spiral" with a color of $g-r \approx 0.72$ mag. It currently resides in the sparsely populated Green Valley of the galaxy color-magnitude diagram—a transitional space between blue, actively [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] and red, quiescent [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|early-type galaxies]]—though it has only occupied this space for the last $\sim 3$ Gyr [Licquia et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AAS...22125411L/abstract), [Bland-Hawthorn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..529B/abstract), [Imig et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250717629I/abstract). Its star formation rate is estimated at $\log(\text{SFR}/M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}) \approx 0.22$, which is somewhat low but remains within $2\sigma$ of observationally selected Milky Way analogues [Fraser-McKelvie et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.5030F/abstract). Meanwhile, the downward revision of the Galaxy's total stellar mass to $M_\star \approx 2.6 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$ actually reduces its rarity in terms of [[supermassive-black-holes|supermassive black hole]] mass scaling relations [Lian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990L..37L/abstract).

Chemically, the Milky Way shares fundamental evolutionary traits with other massive spirals, most notably the G-dwarf problem. This phenomenon—an observed deficit of low-metallicity stars compared to the predictions of closed-box chemical evolution models—indicates that the Galaxy formed as an open system experiencing continuous [[mass-accretion-and-assembly|gas accretion]]. Data from the SDSS-IV MaNGA survey confirm that the G-dwarf problem is a generic feature of high-mass spiral galaxies, implying that the Milky Way's gas accretion history is relatively common [Greener et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502L..95G/abstract). The current gas-accretion rate onto the Milky Way is estimated at a few solar masses per year, which is sufficient to maintain its observed star formation rate [Richter et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430...15R/abstract).

Morphologically, the Milky Way is physically larger than inferred by canonical models due to its broken-exponential surface brightness profile, which yields a half-light radius of $R_e \approx 5.75 \pm 0.38$ kpc [Lian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1302L/abstract). While the Galaxy contains distinct [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] and [[thick-disks|Thick disks]], its thick disk is notably thinner and less extended than expected from cosmological zoom-in simulations of galaxies with significant satellite accretion [Guerrette et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24c5002G/abstract).

This compact thick disk is a symptom of the Milky Way's most unusual trait: its exceptionally quiescent recent merger history. In a direct Andromeda comparison, the two Local Group giants exhibit drastically different pasts. The [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] system possesses a more prominent bulge and a population of young globular clusters ($10^8$ to $5 \times 10^9$ years old), indicating major, recent dwarf galaxy accretion [Burstein et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...614..158B/abstract). In contrast, the Milky Way has not experienced a major merger since the Gaia-Enceladus event. This lack of recent violent accretion is exceedingly rare, occurring in fewer than 1.2% of local spiral galaxies, marking the Milky Way as an unusual survivor of pristine, secular disk evolution [Hammer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012EPJWC..1901004H/abstract), [Xiang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..101X/abstract).

This specific evolutionary track has important astrobiological implications. The Galaxy's relatively stable history allowed for the emergence of the [[galactic-habitable-zone|Galactic Habitable Zone]] (GHZ), an annular region located between 7 and 9 kpc from the center. This zone primarily contains stars formed 4 to 8 Gyr ago, providing the necessary metallicity and long-term environmental stability required for the development of complex life [Lineweaver et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Sci...303...59L/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite the transformative impact of the *Gaia* mission and large-scale spectroscopic surveys, several fundamental properties of the Milky Way remain actively debated. As the field moves beyond equilibrium models toward treating the Galaxy as a dynamic, perturbed system [Kawata et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241212252K/abstract), several critical questions have emerged that will drive the next decade of [[galactic-archaeology|Galactic archaeology]].

One major area of tension involves reconciling the newly proposed **broken-exponential disk profile** with traditional mass scaling relations. If the inner stellar density is indeed flat rather than steeply exponential, the resulting [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|lower stellar mass]] implies a much higher dark matter-to-baryon ratio in the inner Galaxy than historically assumed [Lian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1302L/abstract), [Lian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990L..37L/abstract). Validating this structural paradigm across multiple stellar tracer populations is crucial for accurately placing the Milky Way on fundamental scaling relations relative to other [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]].

Another unresolved issue is the existence of a **dark disk**. Cosmological simulations predict that massive satellite galaxies (with mass ratios $\gtrsim 1:10$) accreted at low inclinations are dragged into the Galactic midplane by dynamical friction, where they are tidally shredded. This process should deposit a thick accreted disk composed of both stars and dark matter. However, chemodynamical searches have yet to yield definitive evidence of an accreted stellar disk, suggesting the Milky Way has experienced an unusually quiescent recent merger history . Confirming the presence or absence of this "dark disk" has profound implications for laboratory-based dark matter direct detection experiments, which rely heavily on the assumed local dark matter phase-space distribution.

To answer these questions, observers must overcome the severe extinction and geometric biases inherent to our position within the midplane. **Far-side mapping** of the Galaxy—particularly the far side of the bar and the outer disk—is a paramount objective. Current optical surveys are heavily restricted by dust, necessitating deep, wide-field near-infrared campaigns. Proposals for extensive observing programs, such as a JWST survey of [[the-galactic-center|The Galactic Center]] [Schoedel et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231011912S/abstract) and concepts for next-generation massively multiplexed spectroscopic facilities [Bergemann et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215812B/abstract), aim to finally reconstruct the chemo-dynamical structure of the obscured Milky Way.

Another frontier of ongoing research involves the large-scale mapping of Galactic magnetic fields. A key objective is tracing magnetic field reversals in interarm regions, the detection of which strongly supports axisymmetric spiral (ASS) models of the Milky Way's magnetic structure [Vallee et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996A&A...308..433V/abstract).

The **Gaia-based future** of galactic astronomy relies on coupling precise astrometry with high-resolution, multi-epoch spectroscopy. The upcoming **4MOST survey** will be instrumental in this effort. By acquiring high-resolution spectra for millions of stars, 4MOST will systematically map the chemo-dynamics of the halo [Christlieb et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Msngr.175...26C/abstract), the disk and bulge [Bensby et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Msngr.175...35B/abstract), and the Milky Way's [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxies]] and stellar streams [Skúladóttir et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Msngr.190...19S/abstract). These comprehensive datasets will be essential to definitively measure the true pattern speed and extent of the Galactic bar, quantify the Large Magellanic Cloud's ongoing perturbation of the halo [Petersen et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatAs...5..251P/abstract), and chronologically reconstruct the Milky Way's complete assembly history.
