#
**I Zw 18** is a prototypical and heavily studied member of the [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCD) class, renowned for its extremely low metal content and vigorous ongoing star formation. Located approximately 18.2 megaparsecs from Earth, the galaxy exhibits a gas-phase oxygen abundance roughly 2% to 3% that of the Sun, making it one of the most extreme [[low-metallicity-galaxies|Low-metallicity galaxies]] known in the local universe [Vílchez et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...508..248V/abstract). Morphologically, the main body of I Zw 18 is dominated by two highly concentrated, intensely star-forming knots—designated the northwest (NW) and southeast (SE) components—embedded within an extensive, kinematically complex envelope of neutral hydrogen (H I) [van Zee et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998AJ....115.1000V/abstract). Its extreme chemical and morphological properties make it a premier local laboratory for studying [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]], as it closely mimics the near-primordial conditions expected in the early universe.

For decades, the evolutionary status of I Zw 18 was the subject of intense debate. Because early optical observations easily resolved massive young stars, it was widely hypothesized to be a truly primeval system—a local analog to [[protogalaxies|Protogalaxies]] undergoing its very first episode of star formation. However, early near-infrared studies already found evidence of older stellar populations and pushed back against this idea, showing that a significant fraction of its stars formed prior to the recent burst [Thuan et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...268..667T/abstract). Eventually, deep near-infrared and optical imaging with the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) resolved intermediate-age [[asymptotic-giant-branch|Asymptotic giant branch]] stars [Östlin et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...535L..99O/abstract) and successfully detected the [[tip-of-the-red-giant-branch|Tip of the red giant branch]] (TRGB) [Aloisi et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...667L.151A/abstract). These detections definitively confirmed the presence of an old stellar halo (>1 gigayear) and revised the galaxy's distance scale outward. The modern consensus describes I Zw 18 through a "slow cooking" [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]]: the galaxy experienced billions of years of ultra-low, quiescent star formation before a recent gravitational interaction with a nearby gas-rich companion, Component C, triggered its current starburst.

Today, research on I Zw 18 focuses on the extreme physics of its interstellar medium and the unresolved mechanisms driving its high-energy phenomena. The galaxy exhibits a profound deficit of both cold molecular gas and interstellar dust, characterized by an exceptionally low dust-to-gas ratio and a complete absence of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) emission. Furthermore, its gas is highly ionized, featuring strong emission lines from He II and [Ne V] that require ionization potentials up to 97 eV. The exact source of this high-ionization radiation remains an open question, with leading hypotheses pointing to ultra-luminous X-ray (ULX) sources, exotic metal-poor [[wolf-rayet-star-populations|Wolf-Rayet star populations]], or unusually hot X-ray binaries. By investigating these extreme radiation fields, metal mixing processes, and small-scale chemical inhomogeneities, astronomers use I Zw 18 as a crucial, spatially resolvable template for understanding compact, vigorously star-forming systems in the early universe, such as the newly discovered [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]].

## Classification and Morphological Structure

I Zw 18 is widely cited in the literature as a prototypical [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|blue compact dwarf]] (BCD) galaxy, characterized by an exceptionally low metal content of $12 + \log(\text{O/H}) \approx 7.17$ (roughly $2-3\%$ of the solar value) [Vílchez et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...508..248V/abstract), [Herrera-Camus et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...752..112H/abstract). Furthermore, SED fitting confirms that BCDs like I Zw 18 represent the extreme end of the star formation continuum for [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]], both in terms of relative mass involvement and the dramatic increase over previous [[star-formation-history|star formation rates]] [Janowiecki et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...836..128J/abstract). However, structurally, it is one of the most atypical BCDs known. In standard BCDs, the central starburst is embedded within an extended, low-surface-brightness (LSB) envelope composed of older stars. In I Zw 18, this extended LSB halo is instead overwhelmingly dominated by nebular emission—specifically ionized gas extending out to $\sim 16$ stellar scale lengths. This extended gas emission mimics an exponential stellar disk in optical surface brightness profiles but actually masks a highly compact underlying stellar host [Papaderos et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...393..461P/abstract), [Papaderos et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...537A.126P/abstract), [Janowiecki et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793..109J/abstract). 

The optical morphology of the main body is dominated by two prominent starburst knots, typically designated as the northwest (NW) and southeast (SE) components [Garnett et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...481..174G/abstract). The NW knot currently hosts the galaxy's most intense [[vigorous-star-formation|star formation]], reaching rates of $\sim 0.6 M_{\odot}\,\text{yr}^{-1}$, which is heavily responsible for the system's blue optical colors and extreme ionization state [Bortolini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.146B/abstract). High-resolution H-alpha imaging suggests that the ionized gas in this northwest knot may form an incomplete shell or ring structure approximately 200 pc across [Davidson et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989AJ.....97.1591D/abstract). Overall, the massive stars in these knots are distributed in a manner more closely resembling large extragalactic [[ob-stars|OB associations]] rather than a single, ultra-compact [[star-clusters|star cluster]] [Hunter et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...452..238H/abstract).

Located roughly $15^{\prime\prime}$ ($\sim 1.6$ kpc) to the northwest of the main body is an optically faint, detached companion known as Component C [Dufour et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...471L..87D/abstract), [Papaderos et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...393..461P/abstract). Deep spectroscopic observations have confirmed that Component C is physically associated with I Zw 18, sharing a smoothly varying velocity field and the systemic radial velocity of the main body's ionized gas [Dufour et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...471L..87D/abstract). Current consensus models suggest that the recent burst of star formation in the NW knot was likely triggered by a gravitational interaction or merger event with Component C [Bortolini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.146B/abstract), [Bortolini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240617429B/abstract).

Enveloping both the main body and Component C is a vast and kinematically complex H I envelope. High-resolution neutral hydrogen synthesis mapping reveals that the gas is highly fragmented, indicative of a cloud in the early stages of structural evolution [van Zee et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998AJ....115.1000V/abstract). Interestingly, ultraviolet imaging indicates that the neutral hydrogen column density is actually strongly peaked in the southeast half of the galaxy, rather than the more actively star-forming northwest [Brown et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...579L..75B/abstract). This H I structure is divided into multiple kinematic components: the main body gas (H I-A), which exhibits a steep velocity gradient consistent with solid-body rotation; gas associated with the companion (H I-C), which is kinematically separate at a velocity of $740\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}$; interbody gas connecting the two (H I-I); and a diffuse, kinematically distinct southern extension (H I-SX) that lacks an optical counterpart [van Zee et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998AJ....115.1000V/abstract). Furthermore, radio continuum imaging reveals a large superbubble and a bipolar outflow oriented along the rotation axis, indicative of a mostly non-thermal radio halo [Hunt et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...436..837H/abstract). This extensive gas reservoir and complex dynamical environment provide the necessary fuel for the galaxy's extreme starbursts.

## Distance Scales and the Evolutionary Debate

For decades, the evolutionary status of I Zw 18 was inextricably linked to uncertainties regarding its distance. Due to its extreme metal deficiency and vigorous ongoing starburst, early studies hypothesized that it was a genuinely [[protogalaxies|primeval galaxy]] undergoing its first burst of star formation in the local universe [Izotov et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...616..768I/abstract). This hypothesis was fueled by early observational limits; assuming a relatively close distance of $10$ to $12.6$ Mpc based on kinematics, optical campaigns easily resolved massive young stars but initially failed to detect a definitive population of older, redder stars [Izotov et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...616..768I/abstract). 

The debate peaked in the early 2000s as some indirect spectrophotometric models suggested a larger distance ($15$ to $20$ Mpc) was necessary to consistently explain the galaxy's spectral energy distribution and the missing older populations [Izotov et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph..7082I/abstract), [Izotov et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...560..222I/abstract). The ambiguity was definitively resolved by deep optical imaging with the Advanced Camera for Surveys (ACS) aboard HST. These observations successfully resolved faint, older stellar populations, allowing for a precise distance determination using the [[tip-of-the-red-giant-branch|Tip of the red giant branch]] (TRGB). The TRGB magnitude yielded a precise distance modulus and a corresponding physical distance of $D = 18.2 \pm 1.5 \text{ Mpc}$ [Aloisi et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...667L.151A/abstract). Furthermore, the time-series nature of these deep observations enabled the first detection of classical Cepheid variables in I Zw 18, which provided an independent distance modulus in excellent agreement with the TRGB results [Aloisi et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...667L.151A/abstract). 

While the $18$-$19$ Mpc distance has become the strong consensus, a probabilistic analysis of the resolved stellar population in Component C once yielded a modeled distance as high as $27$ Mpc [Jamet et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...509A..10J/abstract), though this outlier value has not been widely adopted. The revision of the distance scale to $\sim 18.2$ Mpc fundamentally altered calculations of I Zw 18's physical properties. At nearly twice the initially assumed distance, the intrinsic luminosity of the galaxy is approximately four times greater, necessitating corresponding upward revisions to its total stellar and gas masses. More profoundly, resolving these distant, faint populations definitively ruled out the hypothesis that I Zw 18 is a newly formed system, placing it firmly within the established evolutionary framework of other [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] rather than among truly [[nascent-galaxies|Nascent galaxies]].

## Stellar Populations and Star Formation History

With the distance firmly established, modern color-magnitude diagram fitting applied to deep near-infrared and optical data from both HST and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has mapped the galaxy's [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] (SFH) with unprecedented precision. These observations confirm a continuous distribution of stellar ages and reveal three primary structural components [Bortolini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240617429B/abstract):

1. **An old stellar halo:** A faint, red population dominated by [[tip-of-the-red-giant-branch|Red Giant Branch]] (RGB) stars older than 1 Gyr. For the majority of its lifetime (between 1 and 13.8 Gyr ago), the galaxy experienced a "slow cooking" phase characterized by a continuous, extremely low star formation rate (SFR) of $\sim 10^{-4}\ M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ [Legrand et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000A&A...354..504L/abstract), [Bortolini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.146B/abstract). This prolonged quiescent phase prevented rapid chemical enrichment, helping to maintain the galaxy's unusually low metal content.
2. **An intermediate-age population:** Spanning ages of $\sim 100$ to $800$ Myr, this population is characterized by evolved [[asymptotic-giant-branch|Asymptotic giant branch]] (AGB) stars and is primarily associated with the southeast star-forming region [Izotov et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...616..768I/abstract), [Bortolini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.146B/abstract). Deep near-infrared JHK imaging constrained the age of evolved populations to $\ge 200$ Myr and highlighted how ionized gas emission can mimic red colors in certain NIR filters [Hunt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...588..281H/abstract).
3. **A young starburst:** A population of massive main-sequence stars and supergiants younger than 30 Myr, heavily concentrated in the northwest knot [Hunter et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...452..238H/abstract), [Bortolini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.146B/abstract).

The spatial distribution indicates an inside-out formation process, as the younger massive main-sequence stars are distributed over a larger area than the older AGB stars [Izotov et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...616..768I/abstract). Over the past 1 Gyr, star formation in I Zw 18 has progressively increased, culminating in intense bursts approximately 100 Myr and 10 Myr ago [Bortolini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240617429B/abstract). The current starburst is exceptionally vigorous for a [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxy]], reaching an SFR of $0.6$ to $1.0\ M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ [Annibali et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AJ....146..144A/abstract), [Bortolini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.146B/abstract). Compared to a sample of 50 local [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|BCDs]] with a median star formation rate of $0.3\ M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, I Zw 18's current burst places it at the high-intensity end of the BCD population [Hopkins et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002AJ....124..862H/abstract). Analyses of starbursts in systems like I Zw 18 indicate that these intense star formation episodes are sustained over long baselines (>100 Myr), ruling out rapid global self-quenching [McQuinn et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...721..297M/abstract).

## Chemical Composition and Metallicity

I Zw 18 is recognized as one of the most metal-poor [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] in the local universe. Because of its pristine nature, I Zw 18 historically served as a primary target for determining the primordial helium mass fraction ($Y_p$), with early mass fraction estimates yielding anomalously low values of $0.122 \pm 0.015$ [Davidson et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985ApJS...58..321D/abstract), before subsequent studies derived values closer to $\sim 0.23$ [Vílchez et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...508..248V/abstract). Its gas-phase oxygen abundance is exceptionally low, with measurements consistently placing $12 + \log(\text{O/H})$ between $7.17$ and $7.24$ [Dufour et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ApJ...332..752D/abstract), [Vílchez et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...508..248V/abstract), [Herrera-Camus et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...752..112H/abstract). This corresponds to a metallicity approximately $1/50$ to $1/30$ of the solar value, serving as a defining benchmark for [[low-metallicity-galaxies|Low-metallicity galaxies]] [Thuan et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..255..348T/abstract). I Zw 18 held the record for the lowest known metallicity until the discovery of SBS 0335-052, which exhibits an oxygen abundance roughly 1.7 times lower [Izotov et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990Natur.343..238I/abstract). Furthermore, FUSE absorption spectra and mesoturbulence modeling have derived specific iron and silicon abundances ($[\text{Fe/H}] \approx -2.4$) that are consistent with Galactic metal-poor stars [Levshakov et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001A&A...373..836L/abstract).

Despite its low overall metallicity, the relative abundances of canbon, nitrogen, and oxygen provide crucial constraints on the galaxy's evolution. Ultraviolet spectroscopy has yielded $\log(\text{C/O})$ ratios of $-0.63 \pm 0.10$ and $-0.56 \pm 0.09$ for the NW and SE components, respectively [Garnett et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...481..174G/abstract). These values are unusually high compared to other comparably metal-poor irregulars and exceed predictions from pure massive-star nucleosynthesis. This elevated carbon abundance requires enrichment by a prior generation of intermediate- or lower-mass [[asymptotic-giant-branch|AGB stars]], further supporting the "slow cooking" evolutionary model [Garnett et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...481..174G/abstract), [Legrand et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000A&A...354..504L/abstract). 

Historically, long-slit spectroscopic mapping suggested that the chemical composition of I Zw 18 was substantially homogeneous across its main body and extended ionized envelope [Vílchez et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...508..248V/abstract). However, recent high-spatial-resolution observations using JWST/MIRI and Keck/KCWI have overturned this consensus, revealing significant chemical inhomogeneities on scales of $\sim 60\text{ pc}$. These variations include highly localized metal-poor pockets as well as metal-enriched gas that has likely been dispersed beyond the primary star-forming regions by intense stellar feedback [Rickards Vaught et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250712222R/abstract). 

## Interstellar Medium and High-Ionization Physics

I Zw 18 hosts an exceptionally complex and highly ionized interstellar medium (ISM) that challenges standard evolutionary models of [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|Star-forming dwarf galaxies]]. Recent high-resolution integral field spectroscopy from the Keck Observatory and JWST has revealed extreme states of high-ionization gas, probing the limits of stellar feedback and high-energy physics in near-primordial environments.

JWST Mid-Infrared Instrument (MIRI) observations have detected a suite of fine-structure emission lines indicative of a profoundly excited gas phase. Notably, the mid-infrared spectra exhibit [O IV] at $25.9~\mu\mathrm{m}$ and [Ne V] at $14.3~\mu\mathrm{m}$, transitions requiring ionization potentials of approximately $55~\mathrm{eV}$ and $97~\mathrm{eV}$, respectively [Hunt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...992...48H/abstract). The overall ionization state of I Zw 18 occupies the extreme upper envelope of known starburst systems. While such high ionization potentials are frequently associated with [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|active galactic nuclei]], the observed line ratios in I Zw 18 are not consistent with a central accreting supermassive black hole. Furthermore, comparisons of these line ratios with state-of-the-art photoionization and shock models strongly disfavor kinematic heating from supernova outflows as the primary ionizing mechanism [Hunt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...992...48H/abstract).

The optical regime is similarly dominated by high-energy transitions, most prominently strong, narrow-lined nebular He II emission ($\lambda4686$). Observations with the Keck Cosmic Web Imager (KCWI) have resolved multiple He III regions where the line flux ratio $\text{He II}/\text{H}\beta$ exceeds $2\%$ [Rickards Vaught et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...911L..17R/abstract). The precise ionizing source responsible for this emission remains an active debate. Morphological alignment between some He III regions and the galaxy's ultraluminous X-ray source (ULX) suggests the gas could be photoionized by beamed X-ray emission or unusually hot X-ray binaries [Rickards Vaught et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...911L..17R/abstract), [Hunt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...992...48H/abstract). Conversely, the high-ionization gas may instead be powered by a localized concentration of extremely metal-poor [[wolf-rayet-star-populations|Wolf-Rayet star populations]] or even pristine pockets of Population III-like primordial stars [Heap et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150402742H/abstract), [Rickards Vaught et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...911L..17R/abstract). 

Current theoretical frameworks struggle to fully reproduce these observed ISM conditions. For instance, empirically derived abundances of the $\text{O}^{3+}$ ion reveal that standard theoretical ionization correction factors underestimate the $\text{O}^{3+}/\text{H}^{+}$ abundance by a factor of 2 [Rickards Vaught et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250712222R/abstract). This implies that either standard stellar population models fail to predict the requisite extreme-ultraviolet continuum flux, or there are significant missing ionizing sources in the models, such as an intermediate-mass black hole or unusually hot binary products [Hunt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...992...48H/abstract), [Rickards Vaught et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250712222R/abstract).

## Dust Properties and Molecular Gas Deficiency

The extreme low-metallicity environment of I Zw 18 profoundly affects its cold gas and dust properties. It exhibits unusual ISM conditions characterized by an extreme deficiency in both dust and cold molecular gas compared to standard dwarf starbursts.

**Dust Content and PAH Deficit**
Observations utilizing the Spitzer and Herschel Space Telescopes have placed stringent upper limits on the total dust mass of I Zw 18, finding $M_{\text{dust}} \le 1.1 \times 10^4 M_{\odot}$ [Herrera-Camus et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...752..112H/abstract). This corresponds to an extremely low dust-to-gas ratio of $M_{\text{dust}}/M_{\text{gas}} \le 5.0 \times 10^{-5}$. If the dust-to-gas ratio scaled linearly with metallicity, the expected value would be nearly an order of magnitude higher, indicating that I Zw 18 is significantly dust-deficient even when accounting for its low metal content [Herrera-Camus et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...752..112H/abstract). Consistent with this lack of dust, the galaxy exhibits very low internal extinction ($A_V \lesssim 0.1$) [Wu et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...662..952W/abstract), [Hunt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...992...48H/abstract).

A defining mid-infrared characteristic of I Zw 18 is the complete absence of emission features from polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) [O'Halloran et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...641..795O/abstract), [Wu et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...662..952W/abstract). It is hypothesized that this lack of PAHs results not only from a dearth of heavy elements but also from the efficient destruction of existing fragile dust grains by supernova shocks. This destructive mechanism is supported by an observed enhancement in the $[\text{Fe II}]/[\text{Ne II}]$ mid-infrared line ratio [O'Halloran et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...641..795O/abstract). Despite the absence of PAHs and a far-infrared non-detection at $160~\mu\text{m}$, the thermal continuum emission exhibits a steep slope that closely resembles the spectral energy distributions of typical solar-metallicity starbursts [Wu et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...662..952W/abstract), [Herrera-Camus et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...752..112H/abstract).

**Molecular Gas Deficiency**
The formation of stars typically relies on a reservoir of cold molecular hydrogen ($\text{H}_2$), most commonly traced by carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$) emission. However, interferometric observations in I Zw 18 yield a surprisingly low upper limit for the CO luminosity of $L_{\text{CO}} < 1 \times 10^5~\text{K km s}^{-1}\text{ pc}^2$ [Leroy et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...663..990L/abstract). Relative to its intense [[vigorous-star-formation|star formation rate]], $B$-band luminosity, and massive neutral hydrogen envelope, I Zw 18 is markedly deficient in CO emission. The lack of detectable CO suggests that either star formation proceeds with extraordinary efficiency without a substantial molecular reservoir, or the galaxy possesses a highly atypical $\text{CO-to-H}_2$ conversion factor. Models estimate that the $\text{CO-to-H}_2$ ratio could be roughly $10^{-2}$ times the Galactic value, meaning a significant reservoir of "CO-dark" molecular gas could exist without producing detectable CO line emission [Leroy et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...663..990L/abstract). 

## Local Analog for High-Redshift Systems

Because of its extreme metal deficiency and intense star formation, I Zw 18 serves as a premier local laboratory for investigating [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] [Herrera-Camus et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...752..112H/abstract). The galaxy's highly ionized ISM—characterized by strong emission lines such as He II $\lambda4686$ and mid-infrared [Ne V]—mirrors the extreme radiation fields expected in primeval galaxies [Rickards Vaught et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...911L..17R/abstract), [Hunt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...992...48H/abstract). 

This extreme ionization and compact starburst morphology make I Zw 18 an accessible proxy for the recently discovered [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]]—a population of [[compact-galaxies|compact, high-redshift]] ($z \gtrsim 7$) galaxies experiencing early, intense episodes of star formation [Loeb et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RNAAS...8..182L/abstract). While Little Red Dots frequently exhibit broad emission lines indicative of overmassive central black holes, the highly ionized state of I Zw 18 provides a spatially resolvable local template for studying the complex interplay between massive star feedback and potential intermediate-mass black hole accretion in compact systems [Hunt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...992...48H/abstract).

Furthermore, I Zw 18 offers critical insights into early galaxy assembly. Crucially, the extended low-surface-brightness envelope of I Zw 18 is not an old stellar disk, but rather a vast halo of extended nebular emission that mimics an exponential stellar profile [Papaderos et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...393..461P/abstract). This morphological peculiarity serves as a powerful paradigm for high-redshift observations: in distant galaxies rapidly assembling their mass at [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|high specific star formation rates]], extended ionized gas might be observationally mistaken for a massive stellar spheroid, thereby introducing substantial biases in fundamental size-luminosity scaling relations [Papaderos et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...537A.126P/abstract).

Finally, the severe deficit of PAH emission and extremely low dust-to-gas ratio in I Zw 18 illustrates the dominant feedback mechanisms that likely governed the chemical evolution of [[nascent-galaxies|Nascent galaxies]] before significant dust reservoirs could accumulate [O'Halloran et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...641..795O/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] of I Zw 18 is becoming clearer, the exact dynamical triggers driving its current intense starburst remain an active area of research. Kinematic studies indicate that the main body of I Zw 18 and Component C are physically associated, suggesting that a recent gravitational interaction or merger ignited the peak SFR of $\approx 0.6\ M_{\odot}\ \text{yr}^{-1}$ [Dufour et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...471L..87D/abstract), [Bortolini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.146B/abstract). Future high-resolution mapping of the inter-body H I gas is required to fully understand how this interaction drives gas inflow and fuels the ongoing starburst in such an extremely metal-poor environment.

A major unresolved question concerns the specific origin of the highly ionized gas. The literature remains sharply divided on whether the strong high-ionization lines (e.g., [Ne V], He II) are powered by exotic, metal-poor stellar populations (such as low-wind [[wolf-rayet-star-populations|Wolf-Rayet star populations]] or Population III-like stars), or by accretion-driven high-energy phenomena like an ultraluminous X-ray source (ULX) or intermediate-mass black hole [Heap et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150402742H/abstract), [Hunt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...992...48H/abstract). Because standard stellar population models consistently underestimate the observed ionizing flux, determining the true power source will be critical for interpreting similar emission lines in high-redshift galaxies [Rickards Vaught et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250712222R/abstract).

Finally, the mechanisms of metal mixing and escape in I Zw 18 challenge current models of chemical evolution. The galaxy maintains an extreme oxygen deficiency despite forming stars for gigayears under a "slow cooking" regime [Legrand et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000A&A...354..504L/abstract). It remains uncertain to what extent the current low metallicity is a product of this long-term quiescent phase versus the efficient removal of newly synthesized elements by galactic winds. Recent discoveries of parsec-scale chemical inhomogeneities indicate that while stellar feedback disperses metals, the ejected material is not immediately or uniformly mixed into the surrounding neutral gas [Rickards Vaught et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250712222R/abstract). Tracking the precise pathways of metal diffusion and wind-driven escape remains a critical future direction for understanding local analogs of [[protogalaxies|primordial galaxies]].
