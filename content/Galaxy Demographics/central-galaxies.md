A **central galaxy** is the principal stellar system residing at or near the primary local minimum of a [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]]'s gravitational potential well. Occupying this privileged kinematic position fundamentally distinguishes central galaxies from orbiting [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]], which traverse the host halo and are subjected to severe environmental effects such as ram-pressure stripping and tidal truncation. Because intergalactic gas cooling and dynamical friction naturally funnel baryonic material toward the bottom of the potential well, central galaxies are typically the most massive and luminous objects within their respective systems. In high-mass environments such as massive groups and [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]], they frequently manifest as [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] (BCGs) or diffuse [[cd-galaxies|cD galaxies]], possessing extraordinarily extended stellar envelopes that blend seamlessly into the unbound intracluster light. 

The evolutionary trajectory of central galaxies is generally understood through the hierarchical $\Lambda$CDM framework of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. In the standard [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] paradigm, the initial dynamical assembly of massive central galaxies occurs at high redshift ($z > 3$) through [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] channeled along primordial cosmic filaments [Dubinski et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...502..141D/abstract). Concurrently, these galaxies undergo a rapid, early phase of *in situ* star formation that is primarily fueled by rapid gas cooling rather than merger-triggered starbursts [De Lucia et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375....2D/abstract). This is followed by a protracted secondary phase of late-stage growth dominated by "galactic cannibalism"—the steady accretion of gas-poor satellites whose orbits have decayed via dynamical friction [De Lucia et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375....2D/abstract). This complex assembly history leaves distinct structural and chemical imprints. For instance, massive central galaxies typically transition into quiescent [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] as feedback from their co-evolving supermassive black holes halts further gas cooling and star formation [Terrazas et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830L..12T/abstract). Furthermore, their structural axes strongly align with the surrounding [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]], and their systems of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] consistently exhibit a bimodal color distribution that explicitly traces the dual *in situ* and accreted origins of their stellar mass [Brodie et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ARA&A..44..193B/abstract).

Despite the broad successes of this hierarchical model, several critical tensions and open questions remain at the forefront of extragalactic research. Chief among these is a pronounced discrepancy regarding the exact timeline of mass assembly: while traditional semi-analytic models predict that massive central galaxies accumulate the bulk of their stellar mass very late in cosmic history, observational studies reveal that some BCGs were already nearly fully assembled by $z \sim 1.5$. Furthermore, the discovery of local [[massive-relic-galaxies|Massive relic galaxies]] that completely lack an accreted, metal-poor globular cluster population directly challenges the universality of late-stage hierarchical growth. The following sections detail the physical characteristics, structural scaling relations, and star formation histories of central galaxies, while exploring the ongoing debates surrounding their primary quenching mechanisms, the ambiguous physical boundaries between BCG envelopes and intracluster light, and the role of group-level pre-processing in shaping the satellite galaxies they eventually consume.

## Definition and Fundamental Characteristics

A central galaxy is defined as the principal stellar system residing at or near the primary local minimum of a [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]]'s gravitational potential well. Unlike orbiting [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]], which are subject to severe environmental effects such as ram-pressure stripping and tidal truncation within the host halo, central galaxies occupy a privileged kinematic position. This central location allows them to grow continuously through the accretion of cooling intergalactic gas and the cannibalization of satellites whose orbits have decayed via dynamical friction. Typically, the central galaxy is the most massive and luminous object in the halo, though exceptions frequently occur in unrelaxed or dynamically young [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]], where multiple dominant galaxies of comparable mass may temporarily coexist.

The structural and photometric properties of central galaxies are tightly coupled to the mass of their host halo. Observations and semi-analytic models demonstrate that the conditional luminosity function of central galaxies at a given halo mass is roughly log-normal with a narrow dispersion of approximately $0.15$ dex. Consequently, the stellar mass of a central galaxy is widely considered the most robust single indicator of the underlying dark matter halo mass, governing the system's broader clustering properties [Wang et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...819...58W/abstract). Dynamical modeling of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]—which frequently constitute these massive centrals—indicates that dark matter contributes approximately $30\%$ of the total mass within one effective radius, with even higher dark matter fractions found in slow-rotating, massive systems [Cappellari et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006EAS....20..127C/abstract). Furthermore, evidence from gravitational lensing and stellar kinematics in [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] has been used to test and constrain the $\Lambda$CDM predictions of cuspy inner density profiles [Sand et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.10703S/abstract). Relatedly, the evolution of the [[central-dark-matter-density|Central dark matter density]] and the total mass density slope serve as key indicators for testing these $\Lambda$CDM predictions in early-type central galaxies over the last $8 \text{ Gyr}$ [Tortora et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022FrASS...8..197N/abstract). The stellar-to-halo mass ratio, defined as $f_c \equiv M_{\star, c} / M_h$, serves as a crucial observable proxy for the assembly history of the halo. At a fixed stellar mass, central galaxies residing in halos with higher [[galaxy-mass-fractions|Galaxy mass fractions]] are inferred to have assembled their mass earlier, correlating with redder colors, more bulge-dominated morphologies, and smaller physical sizes [Lim et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455..499L/abstract). These properties reflect a broader continuum of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]; for instance, S0 and spheroidal galaxies can be understood as quenched versions of spiral and irregular galaxies, emphasizing the continuity of scaling relations that extend from giant centrals down to [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]].

The evolutionary trajectory of central galaxies diverges significantly from that of satellite populations. While low-mass centrals often remain active on the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]], high-mass central galaxies—typically those residing in halos above a critical threshold of $M_h \sim 10^{12}\text{--}10^{13} h^{-1} M_\odot$—are predominantly [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]]. The transition to quiescence in central galaxies is strongly correlated with internal structural properties rather than environmental stripping. Specifically, the [[galaxy-central-density|Galaxy central density]] within a $1\text{ kpc}$ radius and the mass of the central supermassive black hole are the primary predictors of star formation cessation [Terrazas et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830L..12T/abstract), [Whitaker et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838...19W/abstract). For massive centrals in the stellar mass range of $10^{9.5}\text{--}10^{11} M_\odot$, quenching occurs with rapid $e$-folding times spanning $0.5\text{--}1.5\text{ Gyr}$, with the most massive systems quenching the fastest [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...841....6H/abstract). While this rapid shutdown is generally attributed to active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback—which injects energy into the surrounding medium and prevents the further cooling of gas at the bottom of the potential well—Hahn (2017) explicitly notes that these specific $e$-folding timescales are highly consistent with gas depletion via strangulation, even if the exact physical mechanism remains an active topic of research [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...841....6H/abstract).

## Historical Development of the Formation Paradigm

Historically, the formation of massive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], which frequently reside as central galaxies in groups and clusters, was described by the monolithic collapse scenario. In this model, galaxies formed in a single, intense burst of star formation at high redshift, which was followed by the passive evolution of their [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]]. While this successfully explained the uniformly old stellar ages of massive ellipticals, it struggled to account for the full range of observed structural and kinematic properties.

The discovery of extended dark matter halos prompted a major paradigm shift. White & Rees (1978) proposed a foundational two-stage theory for [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]: dark matter halos first form and grow through gravitational instability and hierarchical clustering, after which baryonic gas cools and condenses within the central potential wells of these [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]] to form the luminous galaxy. By incorporating the Press-Schechter model to calculate the mass function of growing nonlinear structures, this framework laid the groundwork for the modern cold dark matter (CDM) paradigm.

Within this hierarchical framework, theories regarding the structural growth of central galaxies also evolved. Early models emphasized "galactic cannibalism," wherein a central galaxy slowly accumulates mass as satellite galaxies spiral into the center of the [[parent-halo|Parent halo]] due to dynamical friction. Although cannibalism does contribute to late-stage mass accretion, subsequent cosmological simulations revealed that this process alone is too slow to build the most massive central galaxies seen today. Historical constraints early on suggested that dynamical friction operating within virialized clusters was simply insufficient to account for observed [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] luminosities, pointing instead toward significant [[pre-virialized-clusters|Pre-virialized clusters]] assembly [Tremaine et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990dig..book..394T/abstract). Consequently, the paradigm shifted toward a broader model of hierarchical assembly. Central galaxies are now understood to undergo a rapid early phase of assembly through [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] of massive progenitors, often channeled along primordial cosmic filaments [Dubinski et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...502..141D/abstract). 

This understanding matured into the modern [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] model. In the first phase, the bulk of the central galaxy's stars form very early ($z > 3$) within multiple smaller progenitors, driven by rapid gas cooling. In the second phase, the central galaxy undergoes substantial late-stage growth dominated by the accretion of gas-poor satellites (dry mergers) [De Lucia et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375....2D/abstract). In this scenario, a central galaxy may assemble roughly half of its final mass after $z \sim 0.5$, accumulating an envelope of older stars that contributes to the extended surface brightness profiles characteristic of [[cd-galaxies|cD galaxies]].

Despite the successes of the hierarchical CDM framework, significant tensions remain in the literature regarding the exact timeline of mass assembly. Standard hierarchical models predict a protracted assembly over a Hubble time; for instance, semi-analytic models predict that central galaxies assemble roughly half of their mass after $z \sim 0.5$ [De Lucia et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375....2D/abstract), expecting only $\sim 22\%$ of the central galaxy's stellar mass to be assembled by $z \sim 1.5$ [Collins et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.458..603C/abstract). However, conflicting observational studies report that some [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] were already $> 90\%$ assembled by this epoch, suggesting a much earlier and more rapid period of growth than standard models predict [Collins et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.458..603C/abstract). Furthermore, the discovery of [[massive-relic-galaxies|Massive relic galaxies]] with unimodal, metal-rich [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] indicates that some central systems escaped the secondary late-stage accretion phase entirely, remaining virtually untouched since their initial collapse [Beasley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.555..483B/abstract). These ongoing discrepancies indicate that the precise balance and timing of early *in situ* starbursts versus late-time *ex situ* accretion remains an active and unresolved area of debate.

## Hierarchical Growth and Galactic Cannibalism

Within the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], the growth of central galaxies is generally understood through a two-phase process. Initially, the bulk of their constituent stars form at high redshifts ($z > 3$) in multiple smaller progenitor halos. These progenitors undergo rapid early assembly through gas-rich [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] along primordial cosmic filaments, naturally yielding the flattened, triaxial shapes that are characteristically aligned with the host dark matter halo [Dubinski et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...502..141D/abstract). Following this initial collapse, the central galaxy undergoes a protracted secondary phase of mass accretion, dominated by the continuous infall of [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]].

This late-stage mass accretion is driven primarily by dynamical friction. As satellite galaxies traverse the dense environments of [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]], they lose orbital energy and angular momentum to the extended dark matter halo, gradually sinking toward the cluster potential well. Upon reaching the center, they merge with the central galaxy—a process historically termed galactic cannibalism. Although early analytical models hypothesized very high cannibalism rates, subsequent modeling incorporating mass loss from tidal stripping demonstrated that dynamical friction timescales are significantly prolonged, reducing predicted growth rates to match observations. Notably, the orbital parameters of satellites cannibalized by central galaxies differ markedly from those of general cosmological halo-halo mergers; the orbits of these accreted satellites are typically less bound and less eccentric [Nipoti et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467..661N/abstract). The environment profoundly impacts these hierarchical timescales. For instance, central galaxies in [[compact-groups-of-galaxies|Compact groups of galaxies]] environments appear to evolve more rapidly than isolated galaxies, with their star formation activity suggesting that these compact configurations likely formed approximately $3 \text{ Gyr}$ in the past [Plauchu-Frayn et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...546A..48P/abstract). Observationally, this late-stage mass accretion leaves distinct dynamical imprints; for example, the discovery of complex phase-space substructures—such as stellar streams and shells—in the halo of M87 provides direct evidence of ongoing [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] accretion and active halo buildup [Romanowsky et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748...29R/abstract).

Because infalling satellites have typically been quenched by environmental processes prior to reaching the cluster core, this late-stage mass accretion is heavily dominated by dry mergers. The [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] of gas-poor, red satellite galaxies explains how central galaxies can significantly increase their mass at late times without triggering substantial new star formation, thereby preserving the apparent homogeneity and old age of their stellar populations [De Lucia et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375....2D/abstract). Observationally, the magnitude gap (often denoted $M_{14}$, the brightness difference between the central galaxy and the fourth brightest cluster member within $0.5 R_{200}$) serves as a robust tracer of this hierarchical assembly. Clusters with a large magnitude gap exhibit greater central mass accretion and a correspondingly larger fraction of diffuse intracluster light [Golden-Marx et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240902184G/abstract). Additionally, some dwarf ellipticals in clusters are likely tidal remnants created by these violent cluster interactions rather than being primordial building blocks, highlighting the transformative power of the cluster environment prior to final accretion [Conselice et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005nfcd.conf..277C/abstract).

While theoretical models confidently predict this protracted hierarchical growth, the exact epoch of mass assembly remains a subject of active debate. Semi-analytic models typically predict that central galaxies assemble surprisingly late, with half of their final stellar mass locked into a single object only after $z \sim 0.5$ [De Lucia et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375....2D/abstract). However, this heavily protracted assembly is in significant tension with certain observational studies. For instance, observations of [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] at high redshift indicate that some central systems had already assembled over $90\%$ of their local stellar mass by $z \sim 1.5$, directly conflicting with standard hierarchical models that predict only $\sim 22\%$ of the mass should be assembled by that epoch [Collins et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.458..603C/abstract). This discrepancy suggests that at least some central galaxies may experience an exceptionally rapid early growth phase rather than prolonged hierarchical cannibalism over a Hubble time.

## Structural Morphology and Brightest Cluster Galaxies (BCGs)

[[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] (BCGs) are among the most massive and luminous galaxies in the Universe, typically residing at the gravitational centers of massive dark matter halos. Structurally, many BCGs are classified as [[cd-galaxies|cD galaxies]] due to the presence of an extraordinarily extended, diffuse stellar envelope. These galaxies exhibit high central surface brightnesses that transition into extended wings, often best characterized by a Sérsic index of $n > 4$. They can attain stellar masses exceeding $10^{12} M_\odot$ and often contribute up to $\sim 30\%$ of the total optical luminosity in rich [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]].

The prominent diffuse envelopes of cD galaxies naturally blend into the intracluster light (ICL)—a vast, unbound stellar component that permeates the cluster core. The physical distinction between the BCG's outer stellar halo and the ICL is highly ambiguous, as both components grow concurrently through the same hierarchical mechanisms [Contini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240401560C/abstract). Tidal stripping, galaxy harassment—high-speed encounters that transform small, infalling disk galaxies into [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] [Moore et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996Natur.379..613M/abstract)—and the disruption of accreted satellites continuously feed the [[extended-stellar-haloes|Extended stellar haloes]] and the extended envelope [Mihos et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..317...27M/abstract). In extreme cases, such as the cD galaxy in Abell 2029, the smooth extended envelope can be traced to distances exceeding $1 \text{ Mpc}$ [Uson et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990Sci...250..539U/abstract). X-ray observations of massive central galaxies like M87 also indicate the presence of a massive dark halo, with a total mass of roughly $4 \times 10^{12} M_\odot$ within $300 \text{ kpc}$, yielding an exceptionally high mass-to-light ratio in the outer envelopes [Sarazin et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987IAUS..127..179S/abstract). Photometric studies suggest that the stellar light within the BCG-to-ICL transition region (typically spanning radii of 30-80 kpc) correlates strongly with the total mass of the host dark matter halo [Golden-Marx et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240902184G/abstract).

The unique [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] and assembly of BCGs are also reflected in their [[galaxy-shapes|Galaxy shapes]] and kinematics. Cosmological simulations indicate that central galaxies form early through [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] along primordial cosmic filaments, resulting in flattened, triaxial [[stellar-distributions|Stellar distributions]] [Dubinski et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...502..141D/abstract). Consequently, the major shape axes of central galaxies exhibit a strong alignment with both their host dark matter halos and the surrounding large-scale structure [Rodriguez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A.215R/abstract). Kinematically, the velocity dispersion profiles of some cD galaxies are observed to rise significantly in their extended envelopes, approaching the velocity dispersion of the cluster itself. This highlights the dynamical transition from the local galactic potential to the broader cluster-dominated potential. Furthermore, the orbital parameters of satellites merging with central galaxies are characteristically less bound and less eccentric than standard halo-halo encounters, which influences the structural thickening of the BCG over time [Nipoti et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467..661N/abstract).

The structural dominance of the BCG within its environment is frequently parameterized by the magnitude gap, $M_{14}$ (defined as the difference in luminosity between the BCG and the fourth-brightest cluster member). The magnitude gap serves as a robust observational proxy for the hierarchical assembly history of the BCG+ICL system and the dynamical relaxation of the host cluster [Golden-Marx et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240902184G/abstract). Clusters with larger $M_{14}$ values generally exhibit a more massive ICL fraction, reinforcing the consensus that the late-stage dry mergers responsible for augmenting BCG stellar mass simultaneously build the diffuse intracluster environment.

## Star Formation Histories and Quenching Mechanisms

Central galaxies, particularly those residing in massive dark matter halos, typically exhibit star formation histories characterized by early, rapid assembly followed by a definitive transition to the red sequence. Theoretical models and observational constraints indicate that massive central galaxies quench with $e$-folding times ranging from $0.5$ to $1.5 \text{ Gyr}$ [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...841....6H/abstract). For a central galaxy with a stellar mass of $M_* \approx 10^{10.5} M_\odot$, the total migration time from the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] to quiescence takes approximately $4 \text{ Gyr}$. This process is generally $\sim 2 \text{ Gyr}$ longer than the quenching timescales for [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]], suggesting that centrals and satellites are governed by different primary quenching mechanisms [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...841....6H/abstract). While the literature traditionally enforces a strict bimodality between star-forming and [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]], some models argue this distribution may instead represent a continuous sequence, where the "red sequence" simply arises because optical colors evolve very slowly below a critical specific star formation rate [Eales et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.3125E/abstract). At earlier cosmic times, E+A (post-starburst) galaxies also serve as a crucial intermediate state between star-forming field spirals and quiescent cluster early-types, a transition driven heavily by the gas-rich environments of the early universe [Falkenberg et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.397.1940F/abstract).

The cessation of star formation is intimately linked to structural thresholds. Rather than global size or Sérsic index alone, the [[galaxy-central-density|Galaxy central density]]—typically measured within a $1 \text{ kpc}$ physical radius—serves as the most robust predictor of quiescence at a fixed stellar mass [Whitaker et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838...19W/abstract). Central galaxies with high central densities exhibit systematically lower specific star formation rates, while their lower-density counterparts remain blue and actively star-forming. This quenching threshold is not static; it evolves strongly over cosmic time, increasing by $>0.5 \text{ dex}$ between $z \sim 2.0$ and $z \sim 0.7$ [Whitaker et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838...19W/abstract). Additionally, transition states such as the 'BreakBRD' population—galaxies that exhibit truncated disk star formation but maintain centrally concentrated star formation—represent a potential intermediate step in this quenching and structural growth sequence [Tuttle et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889..188T/abstract).

During the infall of satellite galaxies, star formation is progressively inhibited from the cluster outskirts to the center. However, [[cluster-substructure|Cluster substructure]] can act to temporarily slow this transition from a star-forming field galaxy to a quiescent cluster member [Qu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023RAA....23h5007Q/abstract). Furthermore, observations of infalling populations—specifically Luminous Compact Blue Galaxies (LCBGs) and Blue Cloud (BC) galaxies—reveal a high degree of subclustering. This indicates that star formation in these systems is often triggered or altered by galaxy-galaxy interactions during cluster infall, rather than a simple global cluster effect, while green valley galaxies merely avoid cluster cores, potentially explaining their prolonged star-formation histories [Crawford et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...786...30C/abstract). In the central regions themselves, galactic interactions and ongoing mergers can lead to an increased fraction of molecular gas, which complexly drives both the initial enhancement and the subsequent rapid suppression of central star formation efficiency [Garay-Solis et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952..122G/abstract).

To explain the abrupt suppression of star formation in the deep potential wells of massive systems, models rely heavily on feedback from active galactic nuclei (AGN). Without continuous energy injection, cooling gas in halos above a critical mass threshold,
$$M_{\rm halo} \gtrsim 10^{13} h^{-1} M_\odot$$
would predictably fuel ongoing star formation. Observational evidence strongly supports the [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] feedback paradigm: at fixed stellar mass, quiescent central galaxies harbor directly measured supermassive black holes that are systematically more massive than those in star-forming galaxies [Terrazas et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830L..12T/abstract). This indicates that a critical black hole mass must be reached to deposit sufficient thermal or kinetic energy into the surrounding gas to maintain quiescence.

The broader transition epoch—defined as the lookback time when a cluster's galaxy population becomes less star-forming than the field—scales with the halo-to-stellar mass ratio. Low-mass galaxies in massive clusters quenched roughly $7.6 \text{ Gyr}$ ago, whereas massive galaxies in lower-mass systems transitioned around $5.2 \text{ Gyr}$ ago [Jeon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941....5J/abstract). 

Despite the overarching picture of massive central galaxies as entirely quiescent, the literature presents notable exceptions. Spectroscopic observations of some nearby [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] reveal negative age gradients, featuring young stellar populations and ongoing star formation concentrated within the inner $2 \text{ kpc}$ [Salvador-Rusiñol et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.3368S/abstract). This tension highlights that while global mass quenching via AGN feedback dominates, localized rejuvenation—potentially fueled by *in situ* stellar mass return or residual cooling flows—can sustain or re-trigger star formation in the cores of otherwise quiescent systems.

## Globular Cluster Systems as Assembly Tracers

[[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] (GCs) serve as long-lived fossil records that trace the complex assembly histories of central galaxies. Recent theoretical advancements view these clusters not as anomalous objects, but rather as the natural outcome of high-pressure star formation operating in the dense, gas-rich environments of high-redshift galaxies. This framework explicitly helps astronomers leverage GC demographics as a robust tracer of galaxy assembly across cosmic time [Kruijssen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250116438K/abstract). The global properties of GC systems are intimately tied to their host dark matter halos; for instance, the total mass of a GC system scales linearly with the mass of its [[parent-halo|Parent halo]] [Forbes et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472L.104F/abstract), [Beasley et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020rfma.book..245B/abstract). Furthermore, the spatial extent of a central galaxy's GC system typically reaches 3 to 5 times the host galaxy's effective radius, scaling linearly with the cluster virial radius $R_{200}$ and proportionally to $M_{200}^{1/3}$ [Forbes et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472L.104F/abstract).

The GC systems of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] almost universally exhibit a bimodal optical color distribution, which divides the system into two distinct sub-populations [Brodie et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ARA&A..44..193B/abstract), [Kissler-Patig et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..211...55K/abstract). The red, metal-rich clusters are generally understood to have formed *in situ* during the rapid, dissipational collapse and early starburst phases of the central galaxy. Conversely, metal-poor globular clusters—which comprise the blue sub-population—are predominantly accreted at later times from lower-mass [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] as they fall into the central potential and undergo tidal stripping [Brodie et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ARA&A..44..193B/abstract), [Beasley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.555..483B/abstract). Indeed, the spectacular richness of globular cluster systems in massive central galaxies likely results from the cumulative accretion of numerous smaller [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] over cosmic time [Richtler et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASPC..470..327R/abstract). During this ongoing dynamical evolution, massive central galaxies engage in 'globular cluster swapping,' where the tidal accretion of clusters from disrupted satellites helps to balance the losses of their own clusters due to tidal stripping by the broader cluster potential [Muzzio et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...301...23M/abstract).

By analyzing the spatial and kinematic distributions of these sub-populations, astronomers can reconstruct the hierarchical buildup of [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]]. An observable consequence of this inside-out assembly is mass stratification within the GC system. For example, in the S0 galaxy NGC 3115, the GC system exhibits pronounced mass stratification where the effective radius of the GC system increases monotonically from the bright to the faint end of the luminosity function. Concurrently, the turnover magnitude of the GC luminosity function becomes fainter at larger galactocentric radii [Dou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250921975D/abstract). This stratification is a direct signature of later-accreted satellites depositing their less massive clusters at broader galactocentric distances. Furthermore, individual field stars scattered throughout the stellar halo can be chemically tagged using light-element abundance anti-correlations. This allows astronomers to identify specific "migrants" that were tidally stripped from globular clusters, providing a further tool to trace the assembly of the halo [Martell et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..334...38M/abstract). This chemical evidence also supports the conclusion that a significant fraction of the broader galactic halo field stellar population likely formed through the dissolution of early globular clusters or the accretion of ancient dwarf spheroidal (dSph) galaxies [Cohen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..266..149C/abstract).

During the tidal stripping of satellite galaxies, the densest nuclear regions of the disrupted dwarfs may survive and remain in the central galaxy's halo. These remnants are often observed as ultra-compact dwarf galaxies (UCDs), which have masses of $10^6$ to $10^8 M_{\odot}$ and half-light radii of $10$ to $100\text{ pc}$ [Hilker et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009RvMA...21..199H/abstract). The literature currently contains an active debate regarding the exact nature of UCDs: while some evidence suggests they are simply the extreme high-mass end of the GC population, kinematic and structural distinctions strongly lean toward the theory that they are the stripped nuclei of [[nucleated-galaxies|Nucleated galaxies]] [Hilker et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUGA..2215712H/abstract), [Hilker et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009RvMA...21..199H/abstract), [Forbes et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PASA...28...77F/abstract). 

While the bimodal GC paradigm strongly supports the standard hierarchical assembly model, notable exceptions exist that challenge its universality. Certain massive galaxies, such as NGC 1277, possess a unimodal, exclusively red GC system [Beasley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.555..483B/abstract). The complete absence of an accreted blue population indicates that such [[massive-relic-galaxies|Massive relic galaxies]] formed their entire stellar mass in a massive early burst and subsequently escaped any late-stage accretion. This demonstrates that continuous late-time hierarchical growth is not strictly universal for every central galaxy, while simultaneously confirming that blue clusters are indeed the definitive tracer of an accreted population in typical massive galaxies [Beasley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.555..483B/abstract).

## Central Black Hole Co-evolution

Almost all massive central galaxies harbor Central Massive Objects (CMOs) at their cores, taking the form of either supermassive black holes (SMBHs) or [[compact-galactic-nuclei|Compact galactic nuclei]] [Böker et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..266...58B/abstract). The mass of the central SMBH, $M_{\rm BH}$, exhibits tight empirical correlations with the large-scale structural and kinematic properties of the host galaxy's spheroid, indicating that the black hole and the galaxy assemble in concert. Most notably, $M_{\rm BH}$ scales tightly with the central stellar velocity dispersion, $\sigma$, in what is known as the M-sigma relation, as well as with the total [[galaxy-spheroid-mass|Galaxy spheroid mass]] and luminosity. In the $V$-band, the black hole mass scales approximately linearly with the spheroid luminosity $L$, following $\log (M_{\rm BH}/M_\odot) \simeq -1.83 + \log (L/L_\odot)$ [van der Marel et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..182...65V/abstract). Recent studies also demonstrate a strong anti-correlation between $M_{\rm BH}$ and the Central Intensity Ratio (CIR), providing an additional photometric parameter that is less influenced by central engine excess flux for estimating central black hole masses [Vinod et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023RAA....23d5008V/abstract).

The co-evolution of the SMBH and its host galaxy is deeply tied to the cessation of star formation. Among central galaxies of similar stellar mass, quiescent systems consistently host more massive black holes than their star-forming counterparts [Terrazas et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830L..12T/abstract). This strongly supports models wherein AGN feedback, driven by gas accretion onto the SMBH, provides the energy necessary to prevent gas cooling and drive galaxies off the star-forming main sequence. Consequently, the central stellar density within the inner $1 \text{ kpc}$ serves as a highly reliable structural predictor for the onset of quiescence, surpassing global parameters like the Sérsic index or total galaxy size [Whitaker et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838...19W/abstract).

The hierarchical assembly of central galaxies through [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] leaves distinct dynamical imprints on the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]]. When two massive galaxies with pre-existing SMBHs merge, the resulting binary black hole system scours stars from the center via dynamical friction. This process transforms steep central density profiles into characteristic "weak cusps" where the central volume density profile scales as $\rho \propto r^{-1/2}$ [Nakano et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...525L..77N/abstract). Furthermore, as the central black hole grows, it induces chaotic mixing that rounds out the inner stellar orbits. However, simulations show that this chaos-induced rounding is highly localized; the global triaxiality of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] resists stochasticity and can survive the growth of the central black hole over many dynamical times [Holley-Bockelmann et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...567..817H/abstract). The structural complexity of these early-type central regions extends beyond simple spherical profiles; for example, surveys indicate that roughly $20\%$ of barred S0-Sa [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] possess intricate secondary central bars, adding further non-axisymmetric features to the inner potential well [Erwin et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..182..243E/abstract). These [[double-barred-galaxies|Double-barred galaxies]] play a crucial role in regulating the gas inflow necessary to feed central black holes and build nuclear star clusters [van der Marel et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001IAUS..205..154V/abstract). Furthermore, there is a potential feedback loop in the early universe where the dynamical evolution and sinking of primordial globular clusters to the galactic center can provide the fuel required to trigger initial AGN activity [Capuzzo-Dolcetta et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..249..237C/abstract).

In many galaxies, particularly at lower masses or later Hubble types, the Central Massive Object manifests as a nuclear star cluster rather than a dominant SMBH. NCs are the densest known stellar systems, typically possessing effective radii of $r \sim 5 \text{ pc}$ and stellar masses of $M \sim 10^6 - 10^8 M_\odot$. It remains debated in the literature whether NCs and SMBHs represent distinct evolutionary products or belong to a single, continuous family of Compact Massive Objects governed by similar host-galaxy scaling relations [Melo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JPhCS.689a2008M/abstract). While they share similar fundamental plane scalings, their origins may fundamentally differ: evidence suggests that many nuclear star clusters could be the remnant nuclei of [[accreted-dwarf-galaxies|Accreted dwarf galaxies]] that migrated to the center via dynamical friction, rather than having formed purely *in situ* [Böker et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..266...58B/abstract).

## Galaxy-Halo Alignment and Large-Scale Structure

Central galaxies, residing at the bottom of their host dark matter potential wells, frequently exhibit strong spatial and kinematic alignments with both their [[parent-halo|Parent halo]] and the broader cosmic web. This preferential alignment arises naturally in hierarchical structure formation, where anisotropic accretion of gas and satellite galaxies occurs predominantly along [[primeval-large-scale-structures|Primeval large-scale structures]]. As a result, massive central galaxies—particularly [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] (BCGs)—often develop as flattened, triaxial systems whose major axes align with the primordial filaments from which they assembled and the long axis of the cluster's general galaxy distribution [Dubinski et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...502..141D/abstract).

Within the virial radius, the shape of the central stellar component correlates strongly with the shape of the host [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]]. Hydrodynamical simulations and observational surveys indicate that this stellar-halo alignment increases in strength as a function of halo mass [Rodriguez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250313602R/abstract), [Rodriguez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A.215R/abstract). In massive clusters, the alignment is also closely linked to the dynamical state of the intra-cluster medium; for instance, BCGs in dynamically relaxed, low central entropy clusters show a strong alignment with the cluster's X-ray emission center [Kim et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...836..105K/abstract). However, the shapes of the central stellar component and the extended dark matter halo can sometimes decouple or evolve differently depending on the specific recent assembly history of the system [Rodriguez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250313602R/abstract).

The alignment of the central galaxy extends to the spatial distribution of its orbiting [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]]. Satellites are generally not distributed isotropically; instead, they trace the flattened or triaxial geometry of the host halo and often exhibit pronounced lopsidedness or planar structures [Wang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...914...78W/abstract). This axis asymmetry is statistically significant even around isolated central galaxies, where lopsidedness correlates with the host's stellar mass and color, as well as its proximity to massive neighboring structures. Satellites located further from the central galaxy tend to preserve a stronger asymmetric signal, reflecting their recent infall along specific cosmological filaments before virialization and dynamical friction completely erase their initial orbital trajectories [Wang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...914...78W/abstract).

On larger scales, the orientation of central galaxies remains tied to the cosmic web, including large-scale filaments and cluster outskirts. While raw measurements suggest that central galaxy alignment varies strongly across different large-scale environments, recent large-volume simulations (such as TNG300) combined with spectroscopic data (e.g., SDSS DR18) indicate that much of this variation is primarily driven by the local density rather than the global topology [Rodriguez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A.215R/abstract). Once local overdensities are controlled for, most environmental trends diminish, though a marginal but persistent alignment with cosmic filaments remains [Rodriguez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A.215R/abstract). This residual alignment preserves the fossil record of the anisotropic [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] and accretion flows that drove the early, rapid growth phases of the central galaxy.

## High-Redshift Ancestors and Massive Relic Galaxies

The progenitors of modern central galaxies can be traced back to overdense structures in the early Universe. Observations of [[protoclusters|Protoclusters]] at $z \sim 4$ have identified ultraviolet-bright proto-brightest cluster galaxies (proto-BCGs) that already exhibit distinct structural and photometric properties compared to field galaxies [Ito et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...878...68I/abstract). These proto-BCGs display effective radii approximately $28\%$ larger than field galaxies at the same epoch and possess redder rest-frame $i-z$ colors, indicative of elevated dust content and accelerated early mass assembly. Furthermore, studies of protoclusters at $z \sim 2.23$ detect early morphological transformations and clumpy, potentially merging galaxies, which serve as direct signatures of early cluster core formation [Golden-Marx et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251114843G/abstract).

During the epoch $z \sim 2-3$, the progenitors of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] frequently appear as compact, actively star-forming or recently quenched systems, often colloquially termed "red nuggets." Unlike local massive ellipticals, these high-redshift ancestors possess prominent stellar disks and smaller effective radii [Davari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...836...75D/abstract). Observations at $z \sim 3$ reveal an extremely efficient gas-to-star conversion within these rotating compact sources, which likely represents the initial assembly phase of massive spheroidal bulges before they are transformed by later mergers [Stark et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455..775S/abstract). According to the paradigm of [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]], an initial rapid dissipational collapse and intense central starburst builds this dense core. This is subsequently followed by an extended phase of dry minor mergers that puff up the galaxy, destroying the primordial disk and building an extended stellar envelope. Over cosmic time, this red sequence undergoes significant morphological transformation: while massive ellipticals dominate the high-mass end, S0 galaxies become increasingly more frequent at fainter luminosities at lower redshifts [Cerulo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472..254C/abstract).

However, the exact timeline of this mass assembly remains a subject of active debate, and the literature is explicitly conflicting regarding the late-stage growth of BCGs. While textbook hierarchical models and some cosmological simulations predict that central galaxies undergo a protracted formation—expecting only $\sim 22\%$ of their final stellar mass to be assembled by $z \sim 1.5$ [Collins et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.458..603C/abstract)—observational studies have challenged this timeline. Observations of [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] at $z \sim 1.5$ suggest they have already assembled up to $90\%$ of their $z=0$ stellar mass, implying a much earlier and more rapid period of growth rather than a slow, continuous hierarchical assembly over a Hubble time [Collins et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.458..603C/abstract). 

The existence of [[massive-relic-galaxies|Massive relic galaxies]] in the local Universe provides a unique observational window into this early formation phase. Relic galaxies, such as NGC 1277 in the Perseus Cluster, are nearby systems that formed the entirety of their stellar mass in an intense, early burst and subsequently bypassed the second phase of hierarchical accretion entirely. The pristine, un-evolved nature of NGC 1277 is unequivocally demonstrated by its system of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]]. In typical massive central galaxies, the globular cluster color distribution is bimodal: a red (metal-rich) population formed *in situ* during the initial collapse, and a blue (metal-poor) population accreted later from disrupted satellites. In contrast, the globular cluster system of NGC 1277 is unimodal and entirely red [Beasley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.555..483B/abstract). Dynamical models and merger simulations suggest that the accreted fraction in NGC 1277 accounts for at most $10\%$ of its total stellar mass. These rare objects confirm that while the vast majority of central galaxies undergo extensive late-stage growth, a subset survives as intact structural and chemical fossils of the high-redshift red nugget population. Beyond massive cluster relics, this evolutionary contrast is also evident in highly isolated galaxies. Many isolated disk galaxies appear to have ceased their merging processes entirely around $z \sim 1.4$ and have evolved purely passively since that epoch, serving as undisturbed residuals of the universe's primordial building blocks [Galletta et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...456...91G/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Although the broad strokes of central galaxy evolution are established within the hierarchical structure formation paradigm, several critical questions remain unresolved, particularly concerning their late-stage assembly, quenching mechanisms, and interactions with their immediate environments.

One of the most active areas of debate involves the spatial and evolutionary boundaries of [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] (BCGs). While numerical simulations and observations demonstrate that BCG stellar mass and intracluster light (ICL) grow concurrently through hierarchical mergers, the exact physical distinction between the two remains ambiguous. Examining the stellar light properties within a fixed ICL transition region—typically spanning galactocentric radii of $30\mathrm{-}80 \text{ kpc}$—serves as a powerful diagnostic. In particular, combining the overall ICL fraction with the magnitude gap (often denoted as $M_{14}$, the brightness difference between the central galaxy and the fourth brightest cluster member) provides a promising observable metric to determine whether the host cluster is dynamically relaxed or unrelaxed [Golden-Marx et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240902184G/abstract).

Equally contentious is the primary driver of star formation cessation in central galaxies. While massive centrals transition off the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] more rapidly than satellites—exhibiting $e$-folding decay timescales of $\sim 0.5\mathrm{-}1.5 \text{ Gyr}$—the literature remains divided on the exact physical mechanisms governing this mass quenching [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...841....6H/abstract). Cosmological simulations suggest that mass quenching in high-mass galaxies is largely independent of the global cluster environment, contrasting sharply with the environmental quenching pathways that dominate low-mass satellites [Jeon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941....5J/abstract). The efficiency of feedback from supermassive black holes is frequently cited as the primary quenching mechanism, supported by observations that quiescent central galaxies host systematically more massive black holes than star-forming centrals at a fixed stellar mass [Terrazas et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830L..12T/abstract). However, high central stellar densities within $1 \text{ kpc}$ are also remarkably robust predictors of quiescence. It is currently ambiguous whether structural compaction or active galactic nucleus feedback acts as the fundamental quenching trigger, or if the two are inextricably linked [Whitaker et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838...19W/abstract).

Finally, understanding the late-time mass accretion of central galaxies requires disentangling *in situ* cluster processes from pre-processing. Many satellites accreted by the central galaxy have already undergone structural and chemical transformations in lower-mass environments before falling into the main cluster potential. Observations of transitional galaxies, such as star-forming early-types, indicate that significant morphological evolution and initial quenching often occur via pre-processing in group environments or along cosmic filaments [Brambila et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523..785B/abstract). The exact redshift at which the morphology-density relation and pre-processing signatures first emerge in non-virialized [[protoclusters|Protoclusters]] is still actively investigated. Resolving the relative contributions of pre-processing versus direct cluster-driven transformation remains a critical hurdle in constructing a unified timeline for the final mass assembly of central galaxies.
