Compact galaxies constitute a diverse morphological class defined by exceptionally small effective radii and unusually high surface brightnesses, placing them well below the standard mass-size scaling relations of normal [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]] and [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. First systematically identified in the 1960s as star-like sources with broad emission lines—subsequently known as [[zwicky-galaxies|Zwicky galaxies]]—compact galaxies are now understood to span a vast range of masses, evolutionary states, and cosmic epochs [Zwicky et al. (1964)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1964ApJ...140.1467Z/abstract). Rather than forming a single homologous group, the "compact" designation encompasses objects ranging from transitionary sub-galactic stellar systems to the hyper-dense cores of the universe's most massive galaxies. Because their deep potential wells and high stellar mass densities isolate the physical mechanisms of star formation and feedback in extreme limits, these objects serve as vital probes for models of [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] and quenching.

At the high-mass end, compact galaxies dominate the quiescent population at cosmic noon ($z \sim 2$). Commonly referred to as [[red-nuggets|Red nuggets]], these massive [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] exhibit effective radii roughly four to five times smaller than local ellipticals of comparable mass, yielding stellar mass densities similar to present-day [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] [Bezanson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697.1290B/abstract), [Buitrago et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0811.2931B/abstract). Cosmological models posit that these dense remnants grew into modern giant ellipticals through inside-out assembly, maintaining their compact cores while accreting extended [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]] via minor mergers. More recently, James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observations have pushed the frontier of compact galaxy evolution to $z > 7$ with the discovery of [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]]. The extreme inferred densities and broad emission lines of these nascent objects have ignited intense debate over whether they host overmassive black holes or if their spectral signatures are purely the result of hyper-dense, virialized stellar kinematics.

In the local and low-mass regimes, compact galaxies provide accessible laboratories for studying vigorous starbursts and tidal galaxy disruption. [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCDs) and luminous compact blue galaxies are characterized by steep rotation curves, low specific angular momentum, and immense central gas reservoirs that fuel intense, metal-poor starbursts [van Zee et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....122..121V/abstract). At the absolute faintest extremes, ultra-compact dwarfs (UCDs) and compact ellipticals (cEs) blur the boundary between classical [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]] and the largest [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] [Drinkwater et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000PASA...17..227D/abstract), [Norris et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1151N/abstract). Many of these metal-rich compact stellar systems are now widely recognized as the surviving [[compact-galactic-nuclei|Compact galactic nuclei]] of once-larger progenitor galaxies that were subjected to severe tidal stripping by massive hosts.

The accelerated evolution and rapid quenching of compact galaxies are frequently tied to their surrounding environments. For instance, the dense, low-velocity-dispersion conditions found in [[compact-groups-of-galaxies|Compact groups of galaxies]] act as highly efficient preprocessing engines, driving rapid morphological transformations and leaving a distinct mid-infrared "canyon" of transitioning systems [Walker et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AJ....140.1254W/abstract). Isolating the precise feedback mechanisms required to truncate star formation so abruptly—whether driven by [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] or environmental gas stripping—remains an active frontier. Furthermore, on the scale of individual systems, despite extensive study, fundamental questions remain regarding the universality of the stellar initial mass function (IMF) within the internally extreme stellar-density environments of compact galaxies.

## Definition and Physical Taxonomy of Compact Galaxies

Compact galaxies are broadly defined by their extreme structural parameters: they exhibit unusually high surface brightness and stellar mass density relative to typical systems of equivalent mass. In the parameter space of the galaxy size-mass relation, compact galaxies fall significantly below the sequences defined by normal [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] and [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. Rather than forming a single homologous class, the "compact" designation encompasses a diverse physical taxonomy spanning several orders of magnitude in mass, luminosity, and evolutionary state. In large optical catalogs, extreme compactness often complicates accurate identification; for example, approximately 1% of galaxies in the Millennium Galaxy Catalogue are misclassified as stars due to extreme compactness, predominantly intrinsically small, blue, star-forming objects [Liske et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.369.1547L/abstract). In modern astrophysics, compact systems tracked in blue luminosity catalogs of nearby galaxies also serve as vital structural tracers for localizing potential compact binary coalescence hosts in gravitational-wave searches [Kopparapu et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...675.1459K/abstract).

At the high-mass end of the taxonomy, compact galaxies are predominantly observed as high-redshift [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]], often referred to as [[red-nuggets|Red nuggets]]. At $z \sim 2$, these massive systems possess stellar masses $M_* \gtrsim 10^{11} M_\odot$ but exhibit an effective radius $r_e \lesssim 1-2$ kpc, making them a factor of 4 to 5 times smaller than local ellipticals of the same mass [Bezanson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697.1290B/abstract), [Buitrago et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0811.2931B/abstract). Consequently, their [[galaxy-central-density|stellar mass densities]] are orders of magnitude higher than their local counterparts, occasionally reaching $\sim 2 \times 10^{10} M_\odot \text{ kpc}^{-3}$, which is comparable to the densities of present-day [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] [Buitrago et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0811.2931B/abstract). The morphologies of these objects present a complex structural picture. While many exhibit steep, spheroid-like surface brightness profiles with a Sérsic index $n > 2$ [Buitrago et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0811.2931B/abstract), there is also evidence of a unique structural hybrid. A significant fraction possess [[structural-concentration-and-profiles|Sérsic indices]] resembling local disk-dominated populations, but their ellipticities remain consistent with massive early-type galaxies [Chevance et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...754L..24C/abstract). Despite this mismatch, local analogs do suggest some may host prominent rotating disks . More recently, JWST observations have identified an even more extreme population of [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]] at $z > 7$. These objects feature half-light radii of $r_e \sim 100$ pc and implied velocity dispersions up to $\sim 1500 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, which may represent either extreme core-formation phases or the low-spin tail of galaxy formation [Baggen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240807745B/abstract).

In the low-mass regime, compact galaxies represent the transitionary population between classical [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] and massive [[star-clusters|Star clusters]]. This category includes compact ellipticals (cEs) and ultra-compact dwarfs (UCDs). UCDs typically display absolute magnitudes around $M_V \sim -11$, though recent deep surveys have extended their faint tail down to unprecedentedly low luminosities of $M_V = -9$, with extreme compactness measuring $r_e \sim 10-20$ pc [Brodie et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AJ....142..199B/abstract). Their average surface brightness is comparable to the rare cE galaxies (such as the prototypical M32), which lie smoothly on the extended size-luminosity relation of regular ellipticals but have unusually high surface brightness for their luminosity. Many of these low-mass compact systems feature metal-rich stellar populations and are hypothesized to be the tidally stripped remnants of once-larger [[nucleated-galaxies|Nucleated galaxies]] [Norris et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1151N/abstract), [Chilingarian et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Sci...326.1379C/abstract).

The taxonomy of compact galaxies also encompasses active, gas-rich systems, most notably [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCDs) and luminous compact blue galaxies (LCBGs). These objects are structurally distinct from typical dwarf irregulars; they are characterized by centrally concentrated mass distributions, steep rotation curves, and low specific angular momentum [van Zee et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....122..121V/abstract). The compact nature of the underlying host galaxy enables short, intense episodes of [[vigorous-star-formation|Vigorous star formation]] [Janowiecki et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793..109J/abstract). Local LCBGs often feature scale lengths $\lesssim 2$ kpc and possess baryonic gas fractions and [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|specific star formation rates]] comparable to high-redshift [[clumpy-galaxies|Clumpy galaxies]] [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] [Noeske et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...640L.143N/abstract), [Garland et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...807..134G/abstract).

## Historical Development: From Zwicky's Catalogs to 'Red Nuggets'

The systematic study of extremely dense stellar systems began in the 1960s with the observational surveys of Fritz Zwicky. Utilizing the 48-inch Schmidt telescope, Zwicky identified a novel class of objects that appeared almost stellar on photographic plates but exhibited distinct galactic spectra. He found approximately one such system brighter than the 17th apparent magnitude per 5 square degrees, with apparent diameters ranging from $2.4''$ to $12''$ [Zwicky et al. (1964)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1964ApJ...140.1467Z/abstract). These objects, later recognized as [[zwicky-galaxies|Zwicky galaxies]], were characterized by unusually broad emission lines. Follow-up spectroscopy of Zwicky's 'extremely compact' galaxies revealed a variety of spectra, including [[seyfert-galaxies|Seyfert nuclei]] with broad emission lines [Ulrich et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975A&A....40..337U/abstract). Early dissertation studies of red compact galaxies demonstrated they follow the same spheroid scaling laws and $r^{1/4}$ profiles as normal elliptical galaxies [Kormendy et al. (1976)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1976PhDT.........2K/abstract). Zwicky measured internal velocity dispersions reaching up to $1640 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ in the most extreme cases, yielding high indicative masses and establishing the first observational proof that galaxies could exist in highly compact configurations. 

For decades, these systems were largely treated as peculiar local anomalies, though local discoveries continued to hint at their prevalence. Spectroscopic surveys in Fornax, for example, have identified that nearly 3% of local galaxies ($z < 0.2$) are compact and unresolved on photographic survey plates [Drinkwater et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...511L..97D/abstract). However, a major paradigm shift occurred in the late 2000s when deep extragalactic surveys revealed that compact, [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] were actually ubiquitous in the early Universe. Observations of massive systems ($M \gtrsim 10^{11} M_\odot$) at redshifts $1.7 < z < 3$ uncovered a population of [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] that were structurally distinct from present-day [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. Colloquially termed "[[red-nuggets|Red nuggets]]", these high-redshift systems were found to have effective radii $4$ to $5$ times smaller than local ellipticals of comparable mass [Buitrago et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0811.2931B/abstract). Consequently, their average stellar mass densities within the effective radius were found to be two orders of magnitude higher than those of their local counterparts [Bezanson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697.1290B/abstract).

The discovery of [[red-nuggets|Red nuggets]] forced a reevaluation of massive galaxy formation, shifting models away from monolithic collapse toward an "inside-out" hierarchical growth scenario. In this framework, compact galaxies at $z \sim 2$ act as the dense, original cores of modern giant ellipticals. Over 10 Gyr, they grow primarily in size—by a factor of $\sim 5$—through the accretion of smaller [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] via minor mergers deposited in their [[galaxy-outskirts|Galaxy outskirts]]. The interpretation of this structural evolution is heavily influenced by progenitor bias. Studies of quenched galaxies at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 1.2$) demonstrate that compact systems are systematically $0.5$ to $2 \text{ Gyr}$ older than their more extended counterparts [Williams et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838...94W/abstract). This implies that the extreme densities of [[red-nuggets|Red nuggets]] may simply reflect the smaller average sizes of galaxies at their earlier epoch of star-formation quenching, rather than high stellar density being the direct physical trigger for quiescence.

Most recently, the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has extended the study of compact galaxies to the cosmic dawn, identifying a population of extremely distant ($z > 7$) objects known as [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]]. These sources exhibit half-light radii on the order of $100 \text{ pc}$ and inferred masses of $10^{10} - 10^{11} M_\odot$, yielding central densities an order of magnitude higher than any other known galaxy population [Baggen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240807745B/abstract). An active debate currently surrounds the physical nature of their broad H$\beta$ emission lines ($\sigma > 1000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$). While canonically interpreted as the signature of an accreting supermassive black hole in a low-spin, compact gaseous disk [Loeb et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RNAAS...8..182L/abstract), recent analyses suggest that these broad lines could alternatively be produced by the extreme stellar kinematics and velocity dispersions ($\sim 1500 \text{ km s}^{-1}$) inherent to these ultra-dense stellar cores without strictly requiring an active galactic nucleus [Baggen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977L..13B/abstract), [Baggen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240807745B/abstract).

## Massive Quiescent Compacts at High Redshift

Observations of the high-redshift universe ($z \sim 2$) have revealed a population of massive [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] with remarkably small physical sizes, often referred to as [[red-nuggets|Red nuggets]]. These systems exhibit effective radii that are roughly a factor of 4 to 5 smaller than local [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] of comparable stellar mass, yielding stellar mass densities several orders of magnitude higher [Buitrago et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0811.2931B/abstract). The extreme compactness of these galaxies is corroborated by their kinematics; spectroscopic measurements reveal stellar velocity dispersions reaching up to $\sigma \approx 510$ km s$^{-1}$, confirming that their high stellar densities are matched by correspondingly high dynamical mass [van Dokkum et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.460..717V/abstract). Gravitationally lensed $z \sim 2$ compact quiescent galaxies further reveal high mass-to-light ratios and provide the first confirmation of passive lenticular morphologies at these redshifts [Geier et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777...87G/abstract). 

The formation of these dense cores is thought to involve highly dissipative, gas-rich events in the early universe. At early epochs, compact clusters of massive red galaxies at $z=1.5$ indicate that the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] was already established in dense cores [McCarthy et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...664L..17M/abstract). [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|High-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies]] (such as submillimeter galaxies at $z=3-6$) are prime progenitor candidates, likely forming the bulk of their stellar mass in an early surge with a duty cycle of $\sim 40$ Myr before undergoing rapid quenching [Toft et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782...68T/abstract). Observations of massive compact cores in the process of formation at $z=2.3$ exhibit gas velocity dispersions of $\sim 317$ km/s, structurally matching their likely quiescent descendants [Nelson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.513..394N/abstract). Analytically bridging the local and high-redshift regimes, compact submillimeter dust continuum sources observed in the Milky Way's [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Central Molecular Zone (CMZ)]] may represent scaled-down local progenitors for massive star cluster formation, with individual compact objects reaching spatial scales up to 0.4 pc [Hatchfield et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..251...14H/abstract). Observations of compact [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] at $z \sim 2.3$ reveal severely depleted baryonic gas fractions and gas depletion timescales of $\lesssim 100$ Myr, capturing the rapid transition toward quiescence [Spilker et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...19S/abstract). Analyses of compact star-forming galaxies at $2 < z < 3$ reveal that systems with and without active galactic nuclei share strikingly similar stellar mass and specific star formation rate distributions, suggesting that AGN activity in these forming cores may be driven primarily by internal secular processes rather than distinct merger triggers [Lu et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PASP..132h4101L/abstract). Additionally, compact [[e-a-galaxies|E+A galaxies]] observed at intermediate redshifts ($0.2 < z < 0.8$) likely represent a later epoch of this same rapid quenching pathway [Zahid et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..146Z/abstract). In intermediate-redshift clusters ($z < 1$), compact star-forming galaxies are preferentially quenched to become [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] through feedback and environmental stripping [Socolovsky et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.1640S/abstract).

Following their initial quenching and transition to passive evolution, these compact cores undergo significant size enlargement to become the giant ellipticals seen in the local universe. This structural evolution is largely driven by inside-out growth, wherein the dense $z \sim 2$ compacts act as the unperturbed central cores of modern ellipticals, while their extended halos are assembled through a series of gas-poor minor mergers [Bezanson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697.1290B/abstract). Theoretical models and empirical constraints indicate that this size growth is highly efficient, scaling approximately as:
$$ \frac{r_f}{r_i} \sim \left( \frac{M_f}{M_i} \right)^2 $$
where $r$ and $M$ denote the effective radius and stellar mass, respectively [Bezanson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697.1290B/abstract). Despite individual galaxies growing in size, the overall comoving number density of massive compact quiescent galaxies remains remarkably constant from $z \sim 1$ down to $z \sim 0.2$. This indicates that the size evolution of older systems is balanced by the continual quenching and addition of new compact systems at lower redshifts [Damjanov et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793...39D/abstract), [Damjanov et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806..158D/abstract). However, it is important to clarify that while the general population of compact quiescent galaxies shows limited evolution from $z \sim 1$, the most extreme "ultracompact" galaxies undergo a significant number density decrease by up to a factor of five over the same period [Poggianti et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777..125P/abstract).

The structural and kinematic properties of the $z \sim 2$ compact population remain a subject of active debate. While traditionally assumed to be dispersion-dominated spheroids, structural analyses reveal a mismatch: their Sérsic indices often suggest a disk-like profile, yet their ellipticities are consistent with early-type spheroids [Chevance et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...754L..24C/abstract). Furthermore, [[massive-relic-galaxies|Massive relic galaxies]] (e.g., NGC 5845) display prominent rotating disks , although other studies of $z \sim 2$ compacts find no evidence of spatially extended rotating gas [Spilker et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...19S/abstract). 

Complicating the high-redshift evolutionary picture are recent JWST discoveries of [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]] at $z > 7$. These objects feature hyper-dense cores with half-light radii $r_e \sim 100$ pc. The literature is currently conflicting on whether the broad emission lines (e.g., H$\beta$ widths $> 1000$ km s$^{-1}$) observed in these nascent systems are driven by central active galactic nuclei [Loeb et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RNAAS...8..182L/abstract) or if they simply reflect the extreme stellar velocity dispersions of forming hyper-compact cores without requiring an AGN [Baggen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977L..13B/abstract), [Baggen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240807745B/abstract).

## Blue Compact Dwarfs (BCDs) and Luminous Compact Blue Galaxies (LCBGs)

[[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCDs) and Luminous Compact Blue Galaxies (LCBGs) represent a unique class of star-forming systems characterized by their compact optical sizes, high surface brightnesses, and intense, highly localized starbursts. With [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|extreme specific star formation rates]] and remarkably low metallicities (ranging from $\sim 1/40$ to $1/3$ $Z_\odot$ [Thuan et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..255..348T/abstract)), these objects act as critical local laboratories for studying the conditions of the primordial universe. In optical imaging, BCDs are so extreme in their compactness that they often lack an obvious underlying galaxy and appear almost stellar [Thuan et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987nngp.proc...67T/abstract). Reviews of dwarf compact galaxies underscore their relevance as analogs for high-redshift Lyman-alpha systems and forming galaxies at early epochs [Kunth et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989epg..conf...22K/abstract). Large spectroscopic atlases of BCDs provide rest-frame spectral benchmarks for comparing local starbursts to high-redshift [[emission-line-galaxies|Emission-line galaxies]] [Kong et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...389..845K/abstract). Local LCBGs and BCDs, driven by exceptionally high H I gas fractions that routinely reach $\sim 50\%$, serve as low-redshift analogues to the turbulent, high-redshift [[clumpy-galaxies|Clumpy galaxies]] and ultraviolet-luminous systems of the early cosmos [Garland et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...807..134G/abstract). Recently, smaller and more extreme local analogues known as [[blueberry-galaxies|Blueberry galaxies]] have also been identified as local counterparts to high-redshift compact star-forming systems [Adamcová et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A..27A/abstract).

Historically, BCDs were sometimes modeled as merely a transient evolutionary phase of typical gas-rich dwarf irregular galaxies. However, kinematic and structural analyses reveal that BCD hosts are a distinct, long-lived class. Compared to typical dwarf irregulars, the underlying host galaxies of BCDs exhibit significantly more concentrated mass distributions, steeper rotation curves, smaller exponential scale lengths, and low specific angular momentum [van Zee et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....122..121V/abstract), [Janowiecki et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793..109J/abstract). Near-infrared (NIR) imaging has been critical in identifying 'Type V' surface brightness profiles in BCD hosts, characterized by an inwards flattening that helps separate the underlying host galaxy structure from central starburst contamination [Noeske et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Ap&SS.284..615N/abstract). BCDs show a systematic excess of $K$-band light, indicating that an underlying population of old red giants often dominates the global flux and mass despite recent starburst activity . Furthermore, the colors of faint host halos surrounding local BCDs provide clues about the properties of the starburst progenitor galaxies [Bergvall et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005mmgf.conf..355B/abstract). Mass modeling of dark-matter-dominated systems, such as the dark BCD NGC 2915, reveals a dark matter halo with an exceptionally dense core that dominates the mass distribution at almost all radii [Meurer et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PASA...14...77M/abstract). In cluster environments like Virgo, BCDs have larger H I profile widths than typical dwarf irregulars of comparable luminosity, with luminosities redistributed into compact central regions [Hoffman et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ApJ...339..812H/abstract).

The deep central potential well and low angular momentum naturally predispose the galaxy to powerful, centrally concentrated bursts [van Zee et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....122..121V/abstract). Star formation rates in local BCDs derived from 1.4 GHz and 60 $\mu$m luminosities show excellent agreement and span nearly five orders of magnitude [Hopkins et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002AJ....124..862H/abstract). Additionally, compact and populous [[super-star-clusters|Super star clusters]] are rare in faint or low-star-formation dwarf irregulars, typically requiring the high column density and pressure environments characteristic of BCDs to form [Billett et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002AJ....123.1454B/abstract). The intense starburst activity in these galaxies leads to predictions that LBCGs have high Type II supernova rates (0.05 to 0.2 SN/year), making them primary targets for [[extragalactic-supernovae|supernova surveys]] [Smith et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AAS...21114116S/abstract). Advanced three-dimensional spectroscopy of BCDs identifies supersonic narrow bands in velocity dispersion-intensity diagrams, capable of unveiling massive stellar clusters even when observing unresolved, distant compact galaxies [Martínez-Delgado et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AJ....133.2892M/abstract). Starburst-driven shock waves in BCDs can also trigger the formation of self-gravitating interstellar clouds in the extended envelopes, fueling subsequent episodic star formation .

The starbursts in BCDs and LCBGs consume gas rapidly; molecular gas depletion timescales range from $30$ to $200$ Myr [Garland et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004dimg.conf..147G/abstract). While BCDs are traditionally defined by their short-lived, violent bursts, recent detailed studies of their [[stellar-populations|stellar populations]] suggest that some possess steady, continuous star formation interspersed with brief burst phases [Recchi et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...384..799R/abstract), [Chandar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...965...95C/abstract). Indeed, populations of quiescent BCD candidates (QBCDs) are estimated to be approximately 30 times more abundant than active BCDs, indicating that the extreme starburst phase represents only a very small fraction of their total lifetime [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...685..194S/abstract). Detailed chemical evolution models of BCDs like NGC 1569 indicate that their elemental enrichment is best reproduced by long-lasting episodes of star formation, where the continual infall of massive gas clouds can trigger successive starbursts while simultaneously hampering the outward expansion of galactic winds [Recchi et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...445..875R/abstract). Chemical evolution models successfully account for G-dwarf distributions and elemental ratios by incorporating these specific star formation histories [Matteucci et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989epg..conf..297M/abstract). [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] serve as fossil tracers in BCDs, providing powerful tools to model the galaxy's starburst and assembly history [Östlin et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.265..207O/abstract). For instance, NGC 6789, an isolated BCD in the Local Void, contains a red giant population at large radii, indicating a minimum age of 1 Gyr despite its active current star formation [Drozdovsky et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...551L.135D/abstract).

The ignition of these extreme, metal-poor starbursts is frequently tied to environmental triggers. Dwarf-dwarf mergers and tidal interactions funnel massive amounts of H I gas into the central regions, a scenario supported by the detection of extended stellar shells and disturbed kinematics in many BCDs [Nidhi Chhatkuli et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221207022N/abstract), [Garland et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...807..134G/abstract). [[low-metallicity-galaxies|Extremely metal-poor BCDs]] often appear in synchronized pairs, suggesting they co-evolve through recurrent mild tidal interactions throughout their history [Papaderos et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASSP...28..321P/abstract). However, while most BCDs follow large-scale structures, a minority are found in voids, suggesting they do not all result from tidal interactions between massive galaxies [Pustil'nik et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...443..499P/abstract). Internal secular processes—such as the inward migration of massive star-forming clumps—have also been proposed to build up the dense nuclear regions characteristic of BCDs [Elmegreen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...747..105E/abstract). In a cosmological context, LCBGs were vastly more common at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 0.5 - 1.0$) and contributed significantly to the cosmic star formation rate [Crawford et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...636L..13C/abstract). Furthermore, the ultimate fate of these intermediate-redshift LCBGs exhibits a strong environmental dependence; they are projected to evolve into entirely different morphological classes depending on whether they reside within dense [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] or in the isolated field [Crawford et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph..1448C/abstract). Both kinematic and structural evidence strongly indicate that these intermediate-redshift cluster LCBGs are the direct progenitors of the dense, passive [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]] that populate local galaxy clusters today [Crawford et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...817...87C/abstract).

## The Star Cluster-Galaxy Divide: Compact Ellipticals and UCDs

The structural parameter space between the largest [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] and the smallest galaxies is bridged by a heterogeneous population of compact stellar systems. These objects, primarily comprising compact elliptical galaxies (cEs) and ultra-compact dwarfs (UCDs), exhibit effective radii of $6 \lesssim R_e \lesssim 500 \text{ pc}$ and stellar masses spanning $2 \times 10^6 \lesssim M_* \lesssim 6 \times 10^9 M_\odot$ [Norris et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1151N/abstract). Galaxy clusters host a diverse range of these dwarf systems, from 'Ultra Compact Objects' (UCOs) to low-surface brightness ellipticals [Infante et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Ap&SS.285...87I/abstract). Their extreme densities and structural properties have historically obscured the divide between massive star clusters and true galaxies. These compact massive objects, specifically nuclear star clusters (NCs) found in the centers of spiral and other galaxies, obey tight scaling relationships with their host galaxy properties (such as absolute magnitude and velocity dispersion) that are remarkably similar to those obeyed by [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] [van der Marel et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..241..475V/abstract). This parallel scaling behavior strongly suggests that both SMBHs and dense nuclear clusters belong to a unified family of "[[massive-compact-objects|Massive compact objects]]" that co-evolve directly with their host systems [Böker et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..266...58B/abstract), [Melo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JPhCS.689a2008M/abstract). Furthermore, compact objects such as massive galaxies and dense clusters act as efficient gravitational lenses, with microlensing and multiple images providing critical mass distribution measurements [Wu et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996FCPh...17....1W/abstract).

**Compact Elliptical Galaxies (cEs)**
The prototypical compact elliptical is M32, a dense companion to the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]]. cEs are characterized by unusually high surface brightnesses and old, metal-rich stellar populations that depart significantly from the mass-metallicity relation of typical [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]] [Chilingarian et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Sci...326.1379C/abstract), [Janz et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456..617J/abstract). Their elevated metallicities strongly support a standard model wherein cEs are the surviving bulges or [[compact-galactic-nuclei|Compact galactic nuclei]] of massive progenitors that have been tidally stripped of their extended envelopes by a larger host [Chilingarian et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Sci...326.1379C/abstract). Comparing structural parameters for dEs and dSphs in distant clusters like Hydra I and Centaurus helps map the continuity between various dynamically hot stellar systems under this paradigm [Misgeld et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....48..207M/abstract). 

However, the exact origins of cEs remain a subject of active debate. The recent discovery of a bimodal environmental distribution suggests the existence of an alternative formation channel for at least a subset of the population [Chen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934L..35C/abstract). Additionally, hydrodynamical simulations indicate that high ram pressure during pericentric passages can confine metal-rich gas and trigger intense starbursts, creating metal-rich cEs without relying exclusively on gradual tidal stripping [Bian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...979L..33B/abstract). Ongoing mergers, such as SDSS J1343+3644, provide direct local laboratories for the formation of compact elliptical galaxies through intense starbursts following satellite interaction [Nidhi Chhatkuli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250409801N/abstract).

**Ultra-Compact Dwarfs (UCDs) and Massive Clusters**
Observations in dense environments like the [[coma-cluster|Coma Cluster]] have discovered compact stellar systems whose internal velocity dispersions significantly exceed those expected for typical early-type galaxies of their luminosity, highlighting the extreme dynamical state of these objects [Price et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.397.1816P/abstract). Originally discovered in the Fornax cluster with absolute magnitudes $M_B \sim -11$ and sizes of $10\text{--}20\text{ pc}$ [Drinkwater et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000PASA...17..227D/abstract), UCDs occupy the faint, compact end of this transitional regime. The literature presents competing paradigms for their origin: UCDs are either the extreme, high-mass tail of the [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] population, or they are the remnant nuclear star clusters of disrupted dwarf galaxies. Another model is "cluster cannibalism," wherein UCDs and nuclear clusters form through the multiple merging of standard star clusters driven into the central regions by dynamical friction [Bekki et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...610L..13B/abstract). Globular cluster systems in massive [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] (BCGs) exhibit a near-universal log-normal luminosity function shape, reflecting the fundamental processes of cluster formation, though with slight spatial dependencies in the outer halos [Harris et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...797..128H/abstract). Recent wide-field mapping of UCDs in the Coma cluster reveals that they are substantially more centrally concentrated around giant elliptical hosts (such as NGC 4874 and NGC 4889) compared to standard globular cluster populations, further linking their origins to the deep central potentials of massive galaxies [Pomeroy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250602296P/abstract).

Modern consensus points to a "dual channel" origin, confirming that UCDs are a mixed population [Brodie et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AJ....142..199B/abstract), [Norris et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1151N/abstract). Field and group UCDs provide direct evidence for both formation channels, including cases resulting from recent tidal stripping and those formed as giant clusters during major mergers [Norris et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....48..259N/abstract). Surveys in the Fornax cluster identify peculiar compact stellar systems with distorted outer structures, reinforcing the tidal stripping origin for objects at larger cluster-centric distances [Wittmann et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.4450W/abstract). Below a mass threshold of a few times $10^7 M_\odot$, compact systems align with the properties of massive star clusters. The discovery of young, luminous clusters in the NGC 253 central starburst suggests intense star formation is essential for creating [[super-star-clusters|Super star clusters]] with masses $>1.5 \times 10^6 M_\odot$ [Watson et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996AJ....112..534W/abstract). The M31 disk hosts a surprisingly rich population of intermediate-mass compact clusters with ages spanning 30 Myr to 3 Gyr, a population relatively scarce in the Milky Way [Vansevičius et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...703.1872V/abstract). High-resolution HST surveys in early-type spirals like M81 have cataloged over 100 compact clusters, demonstrating the presence of compact systems across both spiral and interarm regions; these populations include both typical [[red-globular-clusters|Red globular clusters]] and a young population likely sparked by tidal encounters with M82 [Chandar et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....122.1330C/abstract), [Chandar et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....122.1342C/abstract). Surveying H$\alpha$-bright point and compact sources in the spiral M33 helps distinguish between Wolf-Rayet stars, planetary nebulae, and compact OB associations [Moody et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AJ....154..268M/abstract). Broader modern surveys like the PHANGS-HST catalogs provide a high-resolution census of $\sim 100,000$ compact systems across 38 spiral galaxies, defining the young cluster locus and old globular clumps [Maschmann et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..273...14M/abstract). Advanced SED-fitting for these large star cluster samples utilizes categorical priors from morphology and localized emission to mitigate age-reddening degeneracies [Thilker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJS..280....1T/abstract). Conversely, above the mass threshold, UCDs show overly enriched metallicities identifying them as stripped galaxy nuclei [Janz et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456..617J/abstract).

## Galaxy Evolution in Compact Group Environments

[[compact-groups-of-galaxies|Compact groups of galaxies]], such as the well-studied Hickson Compact Groups (HCGs), represent some of the most extreme environments in the local Universe. Objectively defined catalogs from SDSS data have identified hundreds to over 1,200 compact groups at a median redshift of $z \sim 0.13$, enabling large statistical environment studies and providing robust estimates of group crossing times and virial masses [Lee et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127.1811L/abstract), [Deng et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008CEJPh...6..185D/abstract). Characterized by high galaxy number densities and low velocity dispersions that are comparable to the internal velocities of the galaxies themselves, these systems are highly conducive to frequent and prolonged gravitational encounters [Hickson et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ARA&A..35..357H/abstract). The combination of tidal stripping, ram-pressure effects, and galaxy-galaxy mergers in these dense environments accelerates the structural and photometric evolution of member galaxies, driving rapid morphological transformation [Montaguth et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5340M/abstract). Within these environments, hierarchical assembly is highly regimented. For instance, the [[locally-brightest-galaxies|first-ranked (most massive) galaxy]] in an early-formed compact group typically undergoes rapid quenching and morphological transformation into a bulge-dominated system significantly earlier than those in recently-formed groups, and they are substantially more likely to have their evolution driven by major mergers compared to second-ranked galaxies [Zandivarez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250313761Z/abstract). The role of these groups in large-scale cosmic webs is significant; associations between compact groups and larger-scale structures (such as loose groups and clusters) become increasingly frequent for groups identified at higher redshifts, tracking the hierarchical buildup of structure [Andernach et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..329...67A/abstract).

A defining empirical signature of this accelerated evolution is the "mid-infrared canyon"—a statistically significant deficit of galaxies exhibiting intermediate $3.6$--$8.0\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ (Spitzer/IRAC) and $4.6$--$12\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ (WISE) colors [Walker et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AJ....140.1254W/abstract), [Zucker et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...821..113Z/abstract). Unlike in the field, galaxies in compact groups are distinctly bimodal, found either as actively star-forming, dust-rich systems (MIR-red) or as fully [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] (MIR-blue) [Gallagher et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...673..730G/abstract), [Walker et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....143...69W/abstract). The sparsely populated MIR transition zone indicates that once star formation begins to quench in a compact group member, the transition occurs over a very short timescale. This prominent 'gap' in the infrared color space of Hickson Compact Groups specifically separates gas-rich groups harboring active star formation from gas-poor groups dominated by normal, older stellar populations [Johnson et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AJ....134.1522J/abstract). Interestingly, galaxies residing within the MIR canyon are almost exclusively found on the optical [[red-sequence|Red sequence]], harboring early-type (E/S0) or early-spiral (Sa-Sbc) morphologies [Walker et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...775..129W/abstract), [Zucker et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...821..113Z/abstract). Elliptical galaxies in [[shakhbazian-compact-groups|Shakhbazian compact groups]] exhibit more irregular and less boxy isophotes than field counterparts, indicating persistent dynamical disturbances [Bettoni et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995AJ....109...32B/abstract). Furthermore, Shakhbazian groups have significantly lower IRAS detection rates than Hickson groups, likely due to a higher fraction of early-type galaxies and larger distances [Tovmassian et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&AS..130..207T/abstract). Ellipticals in HCGs show enhanced [Mg/Fe] ratios and depleted metallicities compared to field counterparts, suggesting a rapid truncation of star formation following merger events [de la Rosa et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AJ....133..330D/abstract).

The bimodal MIR distribution is fundamentally driven by a bimodality in [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|specific star formation rates]] (sSFR) [Walker et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AJ....140.1254W/abstract), [Lenkić et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.2948L/abstract). H$\alpha$ luminosity in HCGs correlates with velocity dispersion and compactness, suggesting that interactions in lower-dispersion groups drive the highest star formation levels [Severgnini et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001Ap&SS.276..749S/abstract). Yet, direct comparisons of disk galaxies in compact groups vs. the field show similar median [[normalized-star-formation-rate|Normalized star formation rate]], though the group distribution is significantly broader [Iglesias-Páramo et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518...94I/abstract). Generally, the sSFR of individual group members strongly correlates with the total neutral hydrogen (H I) content of the group [Gallagher et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...673..730G/abstract), [Walker et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....151...30W/abstract). Analysis of group-wide gas reservoirs reveals that the total H I mass fraction in compact groups shows a weak correlation with the group's dynamical crossing time, which supports the hypothesis that the sequential, ongoing acquisition of neighboring field galaxies continually influences the group's overall gas content [Liu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RAA....22j5018L/abstract). Evolved compact groups frequently exhibit intergalactic H II regions and tidal dwarf galaxy candidates within their intragroup H I tails [Torres-Flores et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...507..723T/abstract). Deep photometry of HCGs reveals shell systems and extended envelopes, though only the most X-ray luminous groups exhibit significant diffuse light in the group potential [Pildis et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995AJ....110.1498P/abstract). CDM simulations show that while X-ray emission is common, the systems may only be physically compact for a fraction of their X-ray-luminous lifetimes [Pildis et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995PASP..107.1259P/abstract). The X-ray evolution of HCGs is characterized by a correlation between diffuse hot gas and point-source luminosities, tracing the rapid buildup of stellar populations [Fuse et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764..175F/abstract). X-ray observations of spiral-only groups like SCG0018-4854 detect hot diffuse intergalactic medium emission, revealing the potential well properties of dynamically young systems [Trinchieri et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...484..195T/abstract). 

The accelerated evolution within compact groups also leaves a distinct imprint on the group's overall luminosity function. Approximately 70% of Hickson compact groups are spatially coincident with larger loose groups or clusters, reflecting their hierarchical assembly context [Rood et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994PASP..106..413R/abstract). However, chance projections also occur; statistical analysis predicts that $\sim 35\%$ of compact quintets contain a single discordant redshift due to projection of field galaxies [Hickson et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ApJ...329L..65H/abstract). Large SDSS samples indicate that galaxies in compact environments have brighter characteristic magnitudes than field galaxies, regardless of group mass [Zandivarez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.1231Z/abstract). HCG luminosity functions show a bimodal population of bright and dwarf galaxies, suggesting they are bound building blocks of hierarchical structure like galaxy clusters [Krusch et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...459..759K/abstract). Deep imaging of HCGs reveals a rich, spatially extended population of mainly passively evolving dwarf galaxies, whose composite luminosity function shows a steep rise at the faint end [Bomans et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ggnu.conf..157B/abstract). Detailed multi-wavelength analysis of HCG 59 reveals low-luminosity AGN activity and previously unassociated dwarf galaxy populations that increase the group's inferred dynamical mass [Konstantopoulos et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...745...30K/abstract). Star cluster age distributions in compact groups serve as valuable proxies for reconstructing the groups' overall star formation history and dynamical evolutionary state [Konstantopoulos et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011sca..conf..133K/abstract). For example, age-dating star clusters in Stephan's Quintet allows for the reconstruction of tidal interaction intervals between group members over the past 400 Myr [Fedotov et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AJ....142...42F/abstract). Bright group members frequently merge to form massive, central elliptical galaxies, while tidal debris from major interactions replenishes the faint-end dwarf population [Pildis et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...455..492P/abstract), [Hunsberger et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...505..536H/abstract).

## Structural Scaling Relations and the Fundamental Plane

The position of a galaxy in the three-dimensional parameter space of effective radius ($R_e$), central velocity dispersion ($\sigma$), and mean surface brightness ($\langle I \rangle_e$) provides fundamental insights into its assembly history and dynamical state. For systems in strict virial equilibrium, these parameters are linked by the relation:
$$ R_e \propto \sigma^2 \langle I \rangle_e^{-1} \Upsilon^{-1} $$
where $\Upsilon$ represents the mass-to-light ratio. Compact galaxies, characterized by their extreme structural concentration and high surface brightness, occupy unique regions on and off this standard Fundamental Plane, challenging models of galaxy scaling relations.

At the high-mass end, compact [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] at $z \sim 2$ (often termed [[red-nuggets|Red nuggets]]) are significantly offset from the local Fundamental Plane of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. With effective radii 2 to 5 times smaller than local counterparts of the same mass, these systems exhibit exceptional stellar densities and correspondingly high velocity dispersions, which can exceed $510 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [van Dokkum et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.460..717V/abstract). To reconcile their compact state with the diffuse envelopes of modern massive ellipticals, these galaxies must undergo efficient size growth over cosmic time. Scaling relations indicate that their size evolution is exceptionally steep, scaling approximately as $R_{\rm final}/R_{\rm initial} \propto (M_{\rm final}/M_{\rm initial})^2$ [Bezanson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697.1290B/abstract). However, at $z < 0.6$, quiescent compact galaxies are found to lie on the same stellar mass fundamental plane as extended systems, acting as the high-density tail of the normal population [Zahid et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...821..101Z/abstract). At even higher redshifts ($z > 7$), newly discovered "Little Red Dots" exhibit implied central densities so extreme that their virial kinematics may produce broad emission lines ($\sigma > 1000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$) even in the absence of an active galactic nucleus, though this interpretation remains a subject of active debate [Baggen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977L..13B/abstract), [Baggen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240807745B/abstract).

In the low-mass regime, compact stellar systems such as ultra-compact dwarfs (UCDs) and compact ellipticals (cEs) bridge the parameter space between massive [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] and [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]] [Norris et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1151N/abstract). Unlike typical [[faint-stellar-systems|Faint stellar systems]], which follow a continuous mass-metallicity relation, UCDs and cEs are frequently outliers, exhibiting anomalously high metallicities for their mass [Janz et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456..617J/abstract). Above a stellar mass threshold of $\sim 2.5 \times 10^6 \, M_\odot$, UCDs depart from the constant-size scaling of [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] and follow a distinct mass-size relation. Furthermore, their dynamical mass-to-light ratios are roughly twice as large as those of globular clusters of comparable metallicity [Hilker et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009RvMA...21..199H/abstract). These structural and chemical scaling deviations strongly support the consensus that many UCDs and cEs are the surviving, tidally stripped nuclei of more massive progenitor galaxies [Chilingarian et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Sci...326.1379C/abstract), [Brodie et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AJ....142..199B/abstract).

Finally, star-forming compact systems such as [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCDs) also display distinct structural scaling. While they share luminosities with typical dwarf irregulars, BCDs possess significantly more concentrated mass distributions, steeper rotation curves, and lower specific angular momentum [van Zee et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....122..121V/abstract). The compact nature of their underlying older stellar hosts is not a transient feature of the starburst but a long-lived structural parameter; this deep central potential and high structural concentration enable the high gas surface densities required to trigger and sustain their intense, localized starbursts in virial equilibrium [Janowiecki et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793..109J/abstract). Recent structural scaling studies show that compact star-forming galaxies exhibit much steeper [[galaxy-central-density|stellar mass surface density gradients]] and substantially elevated sSFR in their inner regions compared to extended galaxies of the exact same stellar mass [Lin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..175L/abstract).

## JWST Frontiers: 'Little Red Dots' and High-Redshift Assembly

Recent observations with the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have unveiled a distinct population of extremely compact, heavily reddened systems at $z \gtrsim 7$, colloquially referred to as [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]]. These objects provide a novel window into [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] and represent some of the most extreme environments of mass assembly yet discovered in the early Universe.

Photometric and spectroscopic analyses indicate that Little Red Dots are characterized by exceptionally small effective radii—often on the order of $r_e \sim 100 \mathrm{\,pc}$—while hosting substantial stellar masses estimated between $M_* \sim 10^{10}$ and $10^{11} \mathrm{\,M_\odot}$ [Baggen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977L..13B/abstract). This extreme concentration places their [[galaxy-central-density|central mass densities]] an order of magnitude higher than those of any previously known galaxy population, structurally mimicking local ultra-compact dwarfs but operating at vastly higher mass scales. Furthermore, the detection of prominent Balmer breaks at $\lambda_{\rm rest} \sim 3500 \mathrm{\,\mathring{A}}$ indicates that their rest-frame optical light is dominated by relatively [[mature-stellar-populations|evolved stellar populations]] with ages of approximately $200 \mathrm{\,Myr}$ [Baggen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977L..13B/abstract).

A defining and highly debated feature of these galaxies is the presence of broad emission lines, particularly H$\beta$, exhibiting velocity dispersions of $\sigma > 1000 \mathrm{\,km\,s^{-1}}$ [Baggen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977L..13B/abstract). The literature is currently divided and actively conflicting regarding the physical mechanism driving these extreme kinematics:

*   **The AGN Hypothesis:** The canonical interpretation posits that the broad emission lines are powered by central [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] accreting at high rates. Under this framework, Little Red Dots may represent the low-spin tail of the high-redshift galaxy population. In such low-spin systems, gas settles into a highly compact disk, accelerating central star formation and efficiently feeding an overmassive black hole [Loeb et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RNAAS...8..182L/abstract).
*   **The Stellar Kinematics Hypothesis:** Alternatively, if the continuum contribution from an active nucleus is negligible beyond the Balmer break, the broad lines could be a direct consequence of the hyper-dense stellar environment. The gravitational velocity dispersions implied by the extreme compactness and high stellar masses ($\sim 1500 \mathrm{\,km\,s^{-1}}$) are remarkably consistent with the observed H$\beta$ line widths [Baggen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977L..13B/abstract). This suggests that the emission lines might not require an active nucleus at all, but instead reflect the virialized kinematics of a purely stellar system undergoing severe [[compaction-events|Compaction events]].

These competing models highlight a critical unresolved tension in [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]]: whether the unique spectral signatures of Little Red Dots are artifacts of overmassive black holes or the natural consequence of forming hyper-[[dense-stellar-systems|Dense stellar systems]] in the early Universe. Resolving this ambiguity is essential for mapping the full evolutionary timeline of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] and connecting these $z \gtrsim 7$ objects to their likely descendants, the massive, compact [[red-nuggets|Red nuggets]] observed at $z \sim 2$ [Bezanson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697.1290B/abstract).

## Open Questions: IMF Variability and Quenching Mechanisms

The extreme stellar densities and short dynamical timescales of compact galaxies pose significant challenges to standard models of galaxy evolution, particularly regarding the universality of the stellar initial mass function (IMF) and the mechanisms responsible for quenching star formation.

**IMF Variability in High-Density Environments**
A major ongoing debate concerns whether the IMF varies systematically in compact, low-velocity dispersion environments. While strong gravitational lensing and spatially resolved kinematics suggest a "bottom-heavy" IMF in typical massive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], the situation in compact galaxies is less clear. Spectroscopic analyses of ultra-compact dwarfs (UCDs) and compact ellipticals (such as M32) reveal only a $\sim 50\%$ variation in normalized stellar mass-to-light ($M/L$) ratios, in stark contrast to the roughly factor-of-four variation observed across broader early-type samples [Villaume et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...850L..14V/abstract). This relatively modest variation, despite the extreme stellar densities, indicates that metallicity alone does not drive IMF variability, leaving the exact dependence of the IMF on local star formation efficiency and gas pressure as an open question.

**Star Formation Efficiency and Submillimeter Progenitors**
The rapid assembly of compact galaxies implies an extraordinarily high star formation efficiency prior to quenching. Current evolutionary models point to gas-rich, [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|High-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies]]—often observed as [[compact-dusty-starbursts|submillimeter galaxies (SMGs)]] at $z \sim 3-6$—as the direct progenitors of compact [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] at $z \sim 2$. Kinematic and structural comparisons show that these highly obscured starbursts match the formation redshifts, sizes, and internal velocities of their quiescent descendants, with an inferred starburst duty cycle of merely $\sim 42$ Myr [Toft et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782...68T/abstract).

Observations of intermediate compact [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] at $z \sim 2$ capture this rapid transition in action. These transitional objects possess baryonic gas fractions $\gtrsim 5$ times lower and gas depletion timescales $\gtrsim 10$ times shorter than extended star-forming galaxies of similar mass. With current star formation rates, these compact systems are projected to exhaust their gas reservoirs in $\lesssim 100$ Myr [Spilker et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...19S/abstract), necessitating exceptionally abrupt quenching mechanisms. 

**Feedback Mechanisms and the AGN Debate**
Identifying the specific feedback mechanisms responsible for such rapid quenching remains contentious. In local environments, such as [[compact-groups-of-galaxies|Compact groups of galaxies]], dense conditions foster accelerated evolution. Galaxies in these environments exhibit a bimodal mid-infrared color distribution with a prominent "gap," suggesting that gas stripping and tidal interactions truncate star formation so rapidly that galaxies rarely occupy intermediate specific star formation rate phases [Walker et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AJ....140.1254W/abstract), [Lenkić et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.2948L/abstract). 

At higher redshifts, the debate centers on the necessity of feedback from [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] versus extreme stellar kinematics. This tension is epitomized by the recent JWST discovery of [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]] at $z > 7$. These ultra-dense objects exhibit broad Balmer emission lines (e.g., H$\beta$ with velocity dispersions $\sigma > 1000$ km s$^{-1}$). While traditionally interpreted as evidence of an accreting active galactic nucleus (AGN) residing in a compact gaseous disk [Loeb et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RNAAS...8..182L/abstract), recent studies argue that the extreme central densities of these galaxies ($M_* \sim 10^{10} - 10^{11} M_\odot$ within $r_e \sim 100$ pc) yield implied velocity dispersions that perfectly match the observed line widths [Baggen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240807745B/abstract), [Baggen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977L..13B/abstract). If the emission lines are entirely driven by hyper-dense stellar kinematics rather than AGN activity, it forces a re-evaluation of whether AGN feedback is strictly required to quench the most compact galaxies in the early Universe.
