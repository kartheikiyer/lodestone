**Low-metallicity galaxies** are stellar systems characterized by severely sub-solar abundances of [[chemical-elements|Chemical elements]] heavier than helium ($Z \ll Z_\odot$). In extragalactic astrophysics, this chemical deficiency is typically quantified using the [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]], expressed as the logarithmic [[oxygen-in-astrophysics|Oxygen in Astrophysics]] abundance relative to hydrogen, $12 + \log(\text{O/H})$ [Pagel et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981ARA&A..19...77P/abstract). Spanning classifications from vigorously star-forming [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCDs) to the rarest [[ultra-low-metallicity-empgs|Ultra-low metallicity EMPGs]] (XMPGs) which possess less than 5% of the solar oxygen abundance, these objects serve as accessible, local laboratories for the extreme conditions prevalent during [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]]. By offering a window into environments with low [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]]s and hard [[star-forming-regions-and-cores|Star-Forming Regions and Cores]], low-metallicity systems are critical for understanding the primitive interstellar medium.

The internal [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] of low-metallicity galaxies diverge significantly from those of [[mature-stellar-populations|Mature stellar populations]]. The evolution of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] in these environments is governed by reduced atmospheric opacities, which weaken radiation-driven stellar winds and allow stars to maintain rapid rotation throughout their main-sequence lifetimes. This pathway can lead to quasi-chemically-homogeneous evolution, producing [[luminous-blue-stars-and-galaxies|Luminous Blue Stars and Galaxies]], capable of driving the high ionizing photon fluxes observed in primeval [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]]. Furthermore, the lack of dust shielding in the metal-poor interstellar medium causes standard molecular tracers like carbon monoxide (CO) to be easily photodissociated by ultraviolet radiation. This localized destruction results in vast reservoirs of "CO-dark" molecular gas, necessitating the use of alternative [[dense-gas-tracers|Dense gas tracers]] (such as [C II]) to accurately map the fuel for star formation [Ramambason et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...681A..14R/abstract), [Archer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....169..301A/abstract). Additionally, these metal-poor dwarfs frequently host accreting intermediate-mass black holes, making them critical testbeds for understanding the earliest active galactic nuclei in [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]].

On a macroscopic scale, low-metallicity galaxies provide vital constraints on the fundamental [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|Galaxy chemical enrichment mechanisms]], such as the [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]]. In both local extreme outliers and high-redshift systems, the continuous accretion of pristine gas from the cosmic web can rapidly dilute galactic metal content, temporarily suppressing [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] below the predictions of standard evolutionary models. The advent of JWST has revolutionized this field, uncovering populations of extremely metal-depleted [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|Star-forming dwarf galaxies]] at $z > 6$—while concurrently revealing that [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] at these epochs often appear unexpectedly metal-rich—challenging established timelines of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. Consequently, current research is driven by several pressing open questions: resolving the severe tensions between observed strong stellar wind profiles and failed [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] synthesis models at extremely low metallicities, calibrating high-redshift chemical abundance diagnostics, and the ongoing search for true, metal-free Population III analogs.

## Definitions and Classification of Low-Metallicity Systems

In extragalactic astrophysics, the chemical composition of star-forming systems is predominantly quantified using the [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]], expressed logarithmically as the [[oxygen-in-astrophysics|Oxygen in Astrophysics]], $12 + \log(\text{O/H})$. Oxygen is the most abundant heavy element by mass and is readily measured via strong nebular emission lines in [[h-ii-galaxies|H II galaxies]]. To contextualize these measurements, abundances are frequently scaled to the solar value on the [[metallicity|Metallicity]] scale, denoted as $Z/Z_\odot$, where the reference solar oxygen abundance is typically adopted as $12 + \log(\text{O/H}) \approx 8.69$. Consequently, low-metallicity systems are broadly defined as those with sub-solar chemical abundances, though the thresholds for specific galaxy classifications are considerably more restrictive.

One of the most extensively studied classes of metal-poor systems is the [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCDs). These are physically small, gas-rich [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|Star-forming dwarf galaxies]] undergoing [[vigorous-star-formation|Vigorous star formation]]. Spectroscopically, they are characterized by strong, high-excitation emission lines with large equivalent widths superimposed on a faint, blue stellar continuum [Griffith et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...736L..22G/abstract). BCDs typically exhibit oxygen abundances in the range of $12 + \log(\text{O/H}) \approx 7.6$ to $8.0$, corresponding to roughly $1/10$ to $1/5$ $Z_\odot$. Because of their unevolved chemical states and high [[star-formation-rate-indicators-and-metrics|Star Formation Rate Indicators and Metrics]], BCDs are frequently treated as local analogs to primeval high-redshift [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] [Kunth et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000A&ARv..10....1K/abstract). A classic anchor for this population is [[i-zw-18|I Zw 18]], which has an iron abundance near $1/50$ $Z_\odot$ ($[\mathrm{Fe}/\mathrm{H}] \approx -1.7$) [Szécsi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...581A..15S/abstract). 

Another distinct population of metal-poor systems comprises Low Surface Brightness (LSB) galaxies. These are significantly dark-matter dominated and chemically unevolved, representing a class of slowly evolving systems that have historically experienced much lower [[average-star-formation-efficiency|Average star formation efficiency]] [de Blok et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..117...39D/abstract).

At the extreme lower end of the metallicity distribution are the [[ultra-low-metallicity-empgs|Ultra-low metallicity EMPGs]] (XMPGs), sometimes referred to in the literature as extremely metal-deficient (XMD) galaxies. The formal classification for an XMPG typically requires a gas-phase oxygen abundance of $12 + \log(\text{O/H}) \le 7.35$, which translates to $\lesssim 1/20$ $Z_\odot$ [Guseva et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...599A..65G/abstract), [McQuinn et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...891..181M/abstract). These rare systems serve as critical laboratories for testing [[massive-star-formation|Massive Star Formation]], stellar wind physics, and interstellar medium properties at near-primordial abundances. Recent discoveries in the [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|Local Volume and Nearby Galaxy Studies]], such as the Leoncino Dwarf (AGC 198691)—identified robustly via the [[luminous-red-giant-stars|Luminous red giant stars]] branch method on HST imaging in the Arecibo survey [Tikhonov et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AstL...45..750T/abstract)—have pushed the empirical boundary even lower, revealing oxygen abundances below $3\%$ of the solar value [McQuinn et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...891..181M/abstract). 

While local XMPGs and BCDs offer high-resolution views of metal-poor environments, the absolute extremes of the metallicity scale are increasingly being probed in the early universe. Recent deep spectroscopic surveys have identified nascent galaxies during the epoch of [[first-star-formation|First star formation]] exhibiting extreme metal deficiency. For instance, the gravitationally lensed galaxy AMORE6 at $z=5.725$ currently represents the most [[extremely-metal-poor-stars|Extremely metal-poor stars]] detected to date, with a firm upper limit on its oxygen abundance of $< 0.12\%$ $Z_\odot$ [Morishita et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250710521M/abstract). Such objects approach the theoretical [[missing-metal-free-halo-stars|Missing metal-free halo stars]] limit, highlighting the continuous spectrum of low-metallicity environments from [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] to the first galaxies in the universe. Furthermore, accurately classifying these systems is complicated by significant observational uncertainties that affect the derivation of [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and chemical abundances.

## Stellar Populations and Massive Star Evolution

The evolution of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] in metal-deficient environments diverges significantly from their metal-rich counterparts, primarily due to reduced atmospheric opacities. This fundamental difference alters their radiation-driven mass-loss rates, stellar rotation profiles, and ionizing photon budgets.

Because the driving force of massive star winds depends on metal absorption lines, low-metallicity [[ob-stars|OB stars]] are expected to exhibit weaker winds. Observational campaigns in [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]], such as IC 1613 (where oxygen abundances are $\sim 10\%$ solar) and [[sextans-a|Sextans A]] ($\sim 6\%$ solar), are critical for establishing empirical bounds on these mass-loss rates [Telford et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021hst..prop16767T/abstract). Ultraviolet spectroscopy in IC 1613 confirms that the stellar wind terminal velocity is systematically lower than in Milky Way stars, though it remains comparable to Magellanic Cloud counterparts [Garcia et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...788...64G/abstract). However, at extreme metal deficiencies, the literature reveals significant conflicts between theory and observation. Deep spectroscopy of local starbursts demonstrates a marked tension: the observed strong stellar wind profiles are not successfully reproduced by current [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] synthesis models [Senchyna et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930..105S/abstract). 

Stellar rotation assumes a dominant role in the evolutionary tracks of metal-poor stars. Because these stars lose less mass and angular momentum through stellar winds, they can maintain rapid rotation throughout their main-sequence lifetimes. Evolutionary models calibrated to extremely metal-poor conditions, such as those found in [[i-zw-18|I Zw 18]], indicate that even moderately fast rotators undergo efficient internal mixing, leading to quasi-chemically-homogeneous evolution [Szécsi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...581A..15S/abstract). Instead of expanding into [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]], these stars remain compact and heat up, reaching effective temperatures up to $T_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 90,000$ K during core hydrogen burning.

This evolutionary pathway produces TWUIN stars (Transparent Wind Ultraviolet INtense stars). Characterized by exceptionally hot photospheres and weak, low-density winds, TWUIN stars emit copious amounts of hard ionizing radiation. The abundance of these stars in low-metallicity environments provides a natural mechanism to explain the anomalously high He II ionizing photon fluxes observed in metal-poor [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] [Szécsi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...581A..15S/abstract).

The detailed [[stellar-chemical-abundances|Stellar Chemical Abundances]] patterns and end states of these stars offer further insight into their local enrichment histories. While massive stars in some dwarf galaxies like IC 1613 exhibit sub-solar ratios, integrated light from other extremely metal-poor starbursts shows significant measuring 2 to 4 times the solar value [Senchyna et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930..105S/abstract). The Fe II absorption feature in Type IIP supernovae serves as a direct indicator of the progenitor star's metallicity, exhibiting notably weaker profiles in low-luminosity hosts [Scott et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...870L..16S/abstract). Additionally, lower-mass stars completing their evolution play a critical role. Thermally pulsing asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stars in low-metallicity environments possess high luminosities and significantly contribute both to the [[rest-frame-galaxy-properties|Rest-frame galaxy properties]] and to chemical enrichment [Rosenfield et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...822...73R/abstract). Understanding these unique evolutionary channels across stellar mass scales is crucial for bridging the gap between local metal-poor dwarfs and the first generations of stars that initiated [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]].

## The Interstellar Medium and 'CO-dark' Gas

The interstellar medium (ISM) of low-[[metallicity|Metallicity]] galaxies is characterized by a low [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]] and a correspondingly diminished capacity to shield cold gas from the ambient [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]]. In these environments, the structural properties of [[star-forming-regions-and-cores|Star-Forming Regions and Cores]] differ fundamentally from those in the Milky Way, complicating efforts to measure their gas mass and star-formation potential. Because molecular hydrogen lacks a permanent dipole moment, it cannot be easily observed in emission at cold temperatures. Instead, carbon monoxide (CO) is utilized as the standard tracer of molecular gas. However, the paucity of dust in metal-poor galaxies allows destructive ultraviolet photons to penetrate deeply into the ISM. While molecular hydrogen can effectively self-shield, the less abundant CO molecules are readily photodissociated. This differential destruction creates extensive regions wherein carbon exists primarily as ionized or neutral atomic carbon, leaving a vast reservoir of "CO-dark molecular gas" that is invisible to traditional CO surveys [Ramambason et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...681A..14R/abstract), [Archer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....169..301A/abstract).

Detailed observations of nearby [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|Star-forming dwarf galaxies]] vividly illustrate this phenomenon. In the Local Group dwarf WLM, estimations of the molecular gas content indicate that CO-dark gas constitutes a dominant fraction of the total star-forming reservoir [Archer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....169..301A/abstract). High-resolution interferometry reveals that the surviving CO emission is not smoothly distributed but is heavily fragmented. For example, ALMA observations of the dwarf galaxy NGC 6822 show that CO is confined to small, dense clumps representing a very low volume filling factor. Further complicating the ISM structure, intense radiation fields severely alter gas conditions, a process also observed in [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] where radiation fields sharply intensify in advanced merger stages [Shangguan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...870..104S/abstract). In metal-poor systems, some host [[extreme-starbursts|Extreme starbursts]]. A prototypical example is the dwarf starburst II Zw 40, where high-resolution radio and optical observations reveal young massive clusters driving extremely high star formation rate surface densities [Kepley et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....147...43K/abstract).

The profound structural differences in the metal-poor ISM have major implications for the CO-to-molecular hydrogen conversion factor. While it is established that the globally integrated conversion factor is significantly elevated at low metallicities, recent radiative transfer modeling and observations demonstrate high dispersion in the relation [Ramambason et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...681A..14R/abstract). This variance is strongly anti-correlated with the [C II]/CO emission ratio and is driven by the internal [[gas-clumping|Gas clumping]] of the clouds. 

Because CO emission traces only the most deeply embedded peaks of the molecular cloud distribution, it is an unreliable indicator of the total mass in low-metallicity environments. To accurately map the fuel for star formation in these conditions, alternative [[dense-gas-tracers|Dense gas tracers]] are required. Theoretical models increasingly point to fine-structure cooling lines—most notably [C II] 158 $\mu$m and [C I] 609 $\mu$m—as the most robust empirical tracers of the total CO-dark molecular reservoir [Ramambason et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...681A..14R/abstract).

## Scaling Relations and Chemical Enrichment Histories

The chemical properties of galaxies scale fundamentally with their total [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] and luminosity, establishing the [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]] and the [[z-l-relation|Z-L relation]]. These correlations serve as a fossil record of [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|Galaxy chemical enrichment mechanisms]] and gas exchange over a galaxy's lifetime. In star-forming systems, gas-phase oxygen is the primary tracer of enrichment, typically quantified as the [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] $12 + \log(\text{O/H})$. For low-metallicity environments, robust abundances are preferentially derived using the direct method, which relies on measuring the electron temperature-sensitive [O III] $\lambda 4363$ emission line [Guseva et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...599A..65G/abstract).

While most local galaxies follow a tightly defined mass-metallicity relation, [[ultra-low-metallicity-empgs|Ultra-low metallicity EMPGs]] (XMP) galaxies frequently deviate from standard scaling relations. Many XMPs are significant outliers below the luminosity-metallicity relation, a phenomenon generally attributed to the recent [[galaxy-enrichment-processes|Galaxy enrichment processes]] of pristine gas from the cosmic web. This rapid gas infall triggers localized starbursts while simultaneously diluting the global oxygen abundance relative to the galaxy's luminosity [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...810L..15S/abstract). Conversely, [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] formed from pre-enriched debris sit systematically above these relations at all epochs [Dumont et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.2866D/abstract).

The legacy of these low-mass building blocks is preserved in the [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]] and [[galactic-globular-cluster-system|Galactic globular cluster system]] of larger galaxies. For instance, the outermost halo of the Milky Way exhibits a [[negative-metallicity-gradients|Negative metallicity gradients]], providing direct evidence for the hierarchical assembly of our galaxy through the accretion of metal-poor dwarf systems [Dietz et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...894...34D/abstract). Similarly, evidence for a metal-weak [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] points to its formation from the early accretion of a metal-poor dwarf galaxy [Carollo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887...22C/abstract). Globular clusters provide a fossil record of these early conditions; in [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]], they typically show higher metallicities at a given age than those in late-type dwarf irregulars [Sharina et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010vsgh.conf..181S/abstract).

Stellar archaeology provides further detail; the study of [[extremely-metal-poor-stars|Extremely metal-poor stars]] halo stars yields critical insight into the nature of Population III progenitors. Rare carbon and oxygen isotopes in nearby [[stellar-populations-by-metallicity|Stellar Populations by Metallicity]] serve as long-lived tracers of chemical enrichment [González Picos et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1692G/abstract). Furthermore, not all extremely metal-poor stars are ancient; simulation results reveal that up to 20% of stars with less than 10% solar metallicity form as recently as $z < 2$ [Pakmor et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.3602P/abstract). Additionally, a galaxy's larger-scale environment heavily dictates its enrichment history. Distinguishing between satellite and [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]] reveals that satellites often experience shorter periods of star formation due to the removal of their hot gas haloes [Zhou et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.3723Z/abstract). Star formation tracking also requires caution; the use of metal ratios in [[ghostly-dla-systems|Ghostly DLA systems]] compared to local dwarfs reveals that sulfur and zinc do not perfectly trace alpha-element patterns. Interpreting the cessation of star formation requires unified models that transition galaxies toward a [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] state, often following a sequence into [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]]. Even within a single system, the chemical distribution can be inhomogeneous due to incomplete mixing of [[chemical-elements|Chemical elements]] [McWilliam et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ARA&A..35..503M/abstract).

To account for the intrinsic scatter, the Fundamental Metallicity Relation introduces the [[star-formation-rate-indicators-and-metrics|Star Formation Rate Indicators and Metrics]] as a third parameter. It is also notable that across seven orders of magnitude in stellar mass, there appears to be a continuous upper limit for [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|Unusually high specific star formation rates]], bounding the peak efficiency of galaxy growth [Feulner et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...451L..13F/abstract). However, recent JWST observations probing [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] have challenged the universality of these relations in the early universe. At redshifts $z = 7-10$, low-mass systems show oxygen abundances roughly a factor of four lower than expected from the local relations [Heintz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7.1517H/abstract). 

Observations of high-redshift chemical scaling relations currently present a complex picture. While early dwarf galaxies show severe offsets, observations of more [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] at $z \sim 6-8$ reveal near-solar metallicities that align perfectly with the local relations [Rowland et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250110559R/abstract). This mass-dependent evolution is corroborated at intermediate redshifts, where massive galaxies have nebular excitations similar to $z=0$ systems.

Furthermore, the primary physical driver of the mass-metallicity relation itself remains an active debate. The conventional paradigm posits that galactic outflows preferentially eject metal-enriched gas, a process described in [[dwarf-galaxy-physical-processes|Dwarf Galaxy Physical Processes]]. However, an alternative framework suggests the relation arises from a variable stellar initial mass function, wherein low-star-formation environments form fewer massive stars [Köppen et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375..673K/abstract).

## Drivers of Chemical Dilution: Accretion and Outflows

The [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]] of a galaxy reflects a competitive balance between internal stellar nucleosynthesis and the broader cosmic baryon cycle. In low-metallicity environments, external mechanisms dominate the [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|Galaxy chemical enrichment mechanisms]], systematically suppressing chemical abundances. 

[[galaxy-enrichment-processes|Galaxy enrichment processes]] from the cosmic web acts as the primary driver of chemical dilution. At high redshifts, JWST observations reveal that early [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] exhibit oxygen abundances approximately one-fourth of those predicted by standard relations [Heintz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7.1517H/abstract). This severe offset is attributed to the continuous infall of pristine intergalactic gas, which effectively dilutes the metal reservoirs. This cycle is reflected in absorption line studies; evidence suggests a dual population of low-metallicity gas associated with the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] (CGM) of galaxies and the broader cosmic web [Berg et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944..101B/abstract).

In the local Universe, [[ultra-low-metallicity-empgs|Ultra-low metallicity EMPGs]] (XMP) systems frequently appear as outliers in standard [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]]. High-resolution spectroscopy of localized starburst regions within some XMPs reveals extreme inhomogeneous [[recycling-astrophysics|Recycling (astrophysics)]]. For example, massive star-forming clumps can exhibit metallicities much lower than the broader host galaxy disk averages [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...810L..15S/abstract). Because interstellar gas azimuthally mixes on rotational timescales, these severe, localized abundance drops provide direct evidence for the recent impact of cold streams of metal-poor gas. Further evidence is found in extraplanar H II regions, where the [[galactic-metal-abundance-gradient|Galactic metal abundance gradient]] underscores the role of unmixed, infalling gas in driving dilution [Howk et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...856..166H/abstract).

Conversely, the ejection of newly synthesized metals via galactic winds prevents [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] from retaining their nucleosynthetic yield. Standard models posit that feedback, as explored in [[dwarf-galaxy-physical-processes|Dwarf Galaxy Physical Processes]], efficiently ejects metal-enriched gas into the circumgalactic medium before it can be recycled. During [[extreme-starbursts|Extreme starbursts]] episodes, these vigorous outflows can completely disrupt the gas disk and flatten pre-existing [[negative-metallicity-gradients|Negative metallicity gradients]] [Ma et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.4780M/abstract). 

While the outflow paradigm is widely favored, an alternative explanation suggests that the relations could be partially driven by a variable initial mass function, where environments with low star formation rates naturally produce fewer massive, metal-yielding stars, thus mimicking the chemical signature of massive outflows [Köppen et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375..673K/abstract). This contributes to the complexity of [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] in the early universe.

## Massive Black Holes and AGN in Dwarf Galaxies

Local low-metallicity [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] serve as pristine analogs to the early Universe, providing a critical astrophysical laboratory for studying supermassive black hole seeds. Within the broader context of galaxy evolution, galaxies evolve along a sequence from the star-forming [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] through intermediate phases (such as Seyfert/LINER) to the quiescent [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] [Leslie et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455L..82L/abstract). In dwarf galaxies, however, identifying this [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] (AGN) phase is notoriously difficult; their optical narrow-line ratios frequently mimic those of [[seyfert-galaxies|Seyfert galaxies]] or [[active-star-forming-regions|Active star-forming regions]], causing traditional diagnostics to fail [Cann et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...895..147C/abstract). 

To circumvent optical biases, recent studies have leveraged high-ionization coronal lines and multiwavelength observations. The detection of [Si VI] and broad Pa alpha emission in SDSS J1056+3138 confirmed a highly accreting black hole, despite the source being heavily obscured in X-rays. Similarly, observations of an extremely metal-poor [[nascent-galaxies|Nascent galaxies]]—a system with a stellar mass nearly 500 times lower than the Large Magellanic Cloud—revealed compact coronal line emission alongside a broad H alpha component [Reefe et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946L..38R/abstract).

Deep X-ray and radio continuum observations have also proven uniquely suited for finding accreting massive black holes hidden at optical wavelengths. For example, spatially coincident hard X-ray and radio point sources in an interacting dwarf system indicate an accreting black hole. Systematic archival X-ray studies of low-metallicity dwarfs have successfully isolated new candidates by searching for sources that significantly exceed the expected X-ray output of standard [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]].

The growing census of accreting black holes in metal-poor environments has introduced significant tension regarding black hole scaling relations. While some low-metallicity black holes appear to fall on extrapolations of the relations established for more massive systems, others strongly deviate. Furthermore, scaling relations are complicated by the fact that the underlying [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] produce an elevated X-ray luminosity per unit [[star-formation-rate-indicators-and-metrics|Star Formation Rate Indicators and Metrics]] due to the enhanced formation of massive X-ray binaries [Garofali et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903...79G/abstract).

## Historical Development: From I Zw 18 to Large Sky Surveys

The study of low-metallicity galaxies began with the characterization of individual archetypes. Among the most fundamental of these is [[i-zw-18|I Zw 18]], a prototypical metal-poor system historically recognized for its extreme oxygen deficiency [Kunth et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000A&ARv..10....1K/abstract). As one of the most thoroughly studied local objects of its kind, [[i-zw-18|I Zw 18]] became a premier laboratory for testing [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] synthesis models. Theoretical models calibrated against such systems demonstrated that [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] at low metallicities can evolve quasi-homogeneously due to fast rotation [Szécsi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...581A..15S/abstract). 

For decades, historical abundance studies relied heavily on the serendipitous discovery of isolated, extreme objects. The advent of large-scale spectroscopic surveys transformed the field by enabling systematic searches for metal-deficient [[emission-line-galaxies|Emission-line galaxies]]. Using data from SDSS DR12, researchers systematically identified a massive sample by targeting the electron temperature-sensitive emission lines, a direct method for determining [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]] [Guseva et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...599A..65G/abstract). This survey identified many [[ultra-low-metallicity-empgs|Ultra-low metallicity EMPGs]] (XMD) galaxies. Understanding these populations requires tracing the broader [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]]. Results from deep surveys confirmed the phenomenon of 'downsizing,' where [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] shifted from early massive systems to lower-mass systems by $z \sim 1$ [Crampton et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..199C/abstract). Further characterizing the transition to [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]], multiwavelength studies have identified the intermediate [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] stages of quenching. These expansive samples revealed that XMD galaxies strongly deviate from the scaling relations defined by more evolved [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] [Guseva et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...599A..65G/abstract).

While optical surveys relied on emission line selections, the WISE survey opened a complementary window for identifying low-metallicity [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]]. ## Local Analogs of the High-Redshift Universe

Direct observation of the [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] and [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] (ISM) during [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] and the epoch of [[first-star-formation|First star formation]] is fundamentally limited by cosmological distances. Consequently, local low-metallicity starbursts serve as indispensable high-redshift analogs. [[green-pea-galaxies|Green Pea galaxies]] and [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] are prototypical examples of such laboratories. Their extreme nebular emission features closely mirror the spectra of nascent galaxies at high redshift [Laseter et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935..150L/abstract). 

One of the most critical applications of these local analogs is understanding the sources of cosmic reionization, a key phase in [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. Observing these galaxies helps constrain the production of ionizing continuum photons. Theoretical models of low-metallicity [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] suggest that even moderately fast rotators can undergo quasi-chemically-homogeneous evolution. These objects may produce the high He II ionizing photon fluxes necessary to ionize the [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]].

Deep ultraviolet spectroscopy of nearby starbursts reveals that the hot [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] plays a crucial role in regulating photon escape via resonant scattering [Senchyna et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930..105S/abstract). However, there remains significant tension between the strong stellar wind profiles observed in extremely metal-poor systems and current models. This discrepancy highlights the necessity of using local laboratories, such as [[sextans-a|Sextans A]], to empirically calibrate fundamental massive star parameters [Telford et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021hst..prop16767T/abstract).

Additionally, these analogs replicate the [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|Galaxy chemical enrichment mechanisms]] and [[galaxy-enrichment-processes|Galaxy enrichment processes]] histories characteristic of the early universe. Recent JWST observations demonstrate metallicities substantially lower than predicted by standard fundamental scaling relations, indicating that their ISM is subject to continuous dilution. This same mechanism is directly observed in local [[ultra-low-metallicity-empgs|Ultra-low metallicity EMPGs]] (XMP) galaxies, where localized starbursts are fueled by recent accretion [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...810L..15S/abstract). 

Finally, low-metallicity dwarf galaxies serve as a local testbed for early supermassive black hole formation. Multiwavelength approaches in these local analogs provide vital templates for identifying the primordial seeds that eventually populated [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] [Reines et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...787L..30R/abstract), [Reefe et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946L..38R/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Frontiers with JWST

The advent of JWST has initiated a paradigm shift in our understanding of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] and the chemical enrichment of the pristine Universe. Standard cosmological models predicted a scarcity of luminous systems in the first 500 Myr. However, recent surveys have confirmed that substantial [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] was already underway merely 300 Myr after the Big Bang [Carniani et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240518485C/abstract).

A central open question is the exact trajectory of [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]] evolution in the early Universe. Observations of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] at high redshift reveal that many systems exhibit oxygen abundances roughly a factor of four lower than expected from local relations [Heintz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7.1517H/abstract). For [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], the relation is notably steeper than at lower redshifts [Chemerynska et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240717110C/abstract). This suggests that early chemical enrichment is frequently diluted by the continuous infall of pristine intergalactic gas.

Another major observational frontier is the definitive identification of Population III analogs. While completely metal-free galaxies remain elusive, the timeline of early metal production has been dramatically extended. The detection of significant oxygen production just 250 million years after the Big Bang highlights the importance of tracing early [[first-star-formation|First star formation]]. Furthering this search, gravitational lensing has recently uncovered AMORE6, an exceptionally pristine object that provide critical laboratories for testing theories of pristine [[massive-star-formation|Massive Star Formation]] near cosmic dawn [Morishita et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250710521M/abstract). 

Crucially, interpreting these high-redshift spectra relies heavily on [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] synthesis models calibrated in the local Universe. However, significant tensions emerge when applying these models to extremely metal-poor regimes. Ultra-deep ultraviolet spectroscopy reveals that current models fail to reproduce the strong stellar wind profiles of low-metallicity [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] [Senchyna et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930..105S/abstract). Resolving these discrepancies in stellar modeling is an urgent priority, as it directly impacts our ability to accurately measure the [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]] and interpret the ionizing flux of the earliest galaxies.
