## Article

**Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions** examines the morphological, dynamical, and chemical transformations of the Universe's most abundant galactic inhabitants. Defined by their [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|low stellar masses]] and exceptionally shallow gravitational potential wells, dwarf galaxies are highly [[subhalo-physics-and-galactic-substructure|dark matter-dominated systems]]. In the hierarchical paradigm of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], these [[low-mass-galaxies|low-mass halos]] are the first to collapse and serve as the fundamental building blocks of more massive cosmic structures [White et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982SAAS...12..291W/abstract), [Carlberg et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...397....5C/abstract). Historically viewed as simple, passively evolving remnants of a single primordial collapse [Tinsley et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980FCPh....5..287T/abstract), modern astrophysics recognizes [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] as highly dynamic entities. Their evolutionary trajectories are continuously reshaped by an intricate balance of intense internal [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|feedback mechanisms]] and violent environmental forces.

The physical properties and survival of a dwarf galaxy are largely dictated by its ability to retain its [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|baryonic mass]] against these competing forces. Internally, the shallow potential wells of dwarfs make them highly susceptible to [[wind-mechanical-energy|stellar winds]] and [[clustered-supernovae|supernova explosions]], which drive multi-phase [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]] that can efficiently eject gas and regulate the galaxy's overall [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]]. Externally, as dwarf galaxies infall into denser [[galaxy-environment|groups or clusters]], they are subjected to [[galactic-environmental-stripping|Galactic Environmental Stripping]]. Processes such as ram-pressure stripping, tidal harassment, and strangulation systematically remove their gas reservoirs, leading to rapid [[environmental-quenching|Environmental quenching]]. This environmental transformation chain underpins the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]], driving the [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]] of gas-rich, star-forming [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|dwarf irregulars]] into quiescent, gas-poor [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf spheroidals]]. Furthermore, extreme [[galaxy-interactions|Galaxy interactions]] and [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] between massive hosts can trigger the formation of a secondary class of [[dark-matter-free-galaxies|dark matter-deficient]], "recycled" [[secondary-galaxy-formation|tidal dwarf galaxies]] from expelled baryonic debris.

This article explores the synthesis of these internal and external drivers, detailing how they imprint themselves on the kinematics, [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|chemical abundance patterns]], and [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]] of dwarf systems. Despite significant observational and theoretical advancements, several critical open questions remain in the field. Researchers continue to debate whether ejective outflows or "preventive" pre-heating dominate the internal regulation of isolated dwarfs, and to what extent interactions universally flatten versus occasionally steepen internal metallicity gradients. Additionally, the exact cosmological ratio of primordial dwarf halos to tidally recycled systems remains uncertain. By leveraging next-generation facilities like JWST and ALMA alongside massive spectroscopic surveys, contemporary extragalactic astronomy aims to resolve these sub-galactic processes, tracking the continuous cycles of dwarf [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] and rejuvenation across cosmic time.

## Historical Development of Evolutionary Paradigms

Historically, the formation of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], including [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf spheroidals (dSphs)]] and dwarf ellipticals (dEs), was interpreted through the lens of the [[monolithic-collapse|Monolithic collapse]] model. In this framework, it was assumed that galaxies formed the bulk of their stellar mass in a single, rapid, [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|high-redshift]] dissipational collapse, followed by a long period of passive aging [Tinsley et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980FCPh....5..287T/abstract). Early theoretical frameworks also sought to explain morphological variations through internal processes; for example, the Stochastic Self-Propagating Star Formation (SSPSF) model historically illustrated how differences in dimensionality and overall galaxy size could dictate the morphological differences between extended disk galaxies and compact dwarf galaxies [Seiden et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982FCPh....7..241S/abstract). However, these isolated models struggled to reconcile the observed environmental dependence of galaxy morphologies and the extended, complex star formation histories of many local dwarfs.

The realization that galaxies do not evolve in strict isolation as static "island universes" catalyzed a paradigm shift in extragalactic astrophysics [Heckman et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..148..127H/abstract). A critical stepping stone was the Toomre hypothesis, which posited that [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] of disk galaxies could produce dynamically hot, spheroidal remnants. While originally applied to massive ellipticals, this concept established that [[galaxy-interactions|Galaxy interactions]] play a foundational role in shaping galaxy morphology. It suggested that the Hubble morphological sequence is not a strict condition of birth, but is largely driven by a galaxy's merger and interaction history [Schweizer et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000RSPTA.358.2063S/abstract).

This interaction-driven perspective naturally integrated with the emerging Cold Dark Matter ($\Lambda$CDM) cosmology, formalizing the framework of hierarchical structure formation. In this paradigm of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], low-mass dark matter halos collapse first, making dwarf galaxies the fundamental building blocks of the Universe [White et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982SAAS...12..291W/abstract). Rather than forming in a single rapid event, mass assembly is progressive; theoretical models of hierarchical assembly predict a [[merger-rate-density|merger rate]] that evolves rapidly, scaling approximately as $(1 + z)^4$ [Carlberg et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...399L..31C/abstract). Consequently, dwarf galaxies transitioned from being viewed as primordial, undisturbed relics to dynamic entities subjected to continuous environmental and internal evolutionary processes. Simulations demonstrated that these low-mass systems are profoundly dark-matter dominated, a consequence of their shallow potential wells which make them highly susceptible to [[mass-loss-processes|baryon loss]] via feedback and tidal stripping [Ikeuchi et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJ...312..485I/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract).

Modern evolutionary paradigms no longer treat dwarf galaxies as a distinct or disjoint class of objects. Instead, they are recognized as the low-mass extrema of a continuous sequence of transformation processes. Structural scaling relations reveal a parallel sequence where gas-rich, star-forming [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|irregulars]] transform into quenched, gas-poor dSphs and dEs through [[galactic-environmental-stripping|Galactic Environmental Stripping]] and [[environmental-quenching|Environmental quenching]] [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract), [Carraro et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..253C/abstract). This unified view dictates that dwarf galaxies are active participants in the cosmic web. Their evolutionary state is often quantified by the [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|specific star formation rate]], 
$$ \mathrm{sSFR} = \frac{\dot{M}_\star}{M_\star} $$
which reveals that rather than simply fading away passively, a major fraction of the population undergoes complex "on-off" cycles of quenching and [[rejuvenation-episodes|Rejuvenation episodes]] on timescales of $\sim 1$ Gyr [Fortun\'e et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320858F/abstract). 

Despite the consensus on hierarchical assembly, the literature remains explicitly conflicted regarding the primary mechanism responsible for the final quenching of dwarf galaxies. There is an active debate over whether internal [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|stellar feedback]] alone is sufficient to permanently deplete gas and halt star formation even in isolated environments [Hazenfratz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969...65H/abstract), or if external environmental effects—such as ram-pressure stripping—are strictly necessary to fully transition a dwarf galaxy onto the red sequence [Carraro et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..253C/abstract), [Xu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250321724X/abstract).

## Internal Drivers: Star Formation and Feedback Loops

[[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] possess exceptionally shallow gravitational potential wells, making their [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|baryon cycles]] highly sensitive to internal regulatory mechanisms. Consequently, their overall star formation efficiency is inherently low compared to that of [[spiral-galaxy-dynamics|massive spiral galaxies]], as galactic-scale star formation is heavily throttled by internal feedback loops. 

While theoretical models often invoke highly bursty star formation to explain the varied stellar populations and structural properties of low-mass galaxies, observations indicate that intense [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|global starbursts]] are relatively rare in the local Universe. A notable exception serving as an extreme case study is the central starburst in the [[low-metallicity-galaxies|low-metallicity dwarf]] II Zw 40, where high-resolution radio and optical observations reveal that half of the radio emission is diffuse and star formation surface densities far exceed those found in the [[milky-way-structure-and-components|Milky Way]] [Kepley et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....147...43K/abstract). The capacity of a galaxy to undergo such massive, interaction-induced starbursts depends heavily on its evolutionary state; the emergence of a coherent [[galactic-disk-evolution|rotating gas disk]] is a prerequisite for merger-induced starbursts, as the more turbulent interstellar medium characteristic of high redshift cannot react collectively to tidal perturbations [Segovia Otero et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.516.2272S/abstract). Currently, only $\sim 6\%$ of the dwarf galaxy population is undergoing a global starburst, and these events account for just $\sim 25\%$ of the total star formation within the low-mass regime [Lee et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...692.1305L/abstract). Most stars in isolated dwarfs instead form via a more continuous, quiescent mode. Nonetheless, long-term evolutionary tracking reveals that simple passive evolution is uncommon. Up to $86\%$ of field galaxies on the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] undergo continuous cycles of quenching and [[rejuvenation-episodes|Rejuvenation episodes]] on gigayear timescales, shifting the paradigm from steady [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|secular evolution]] to a sequence of active and passive phases [Fortun\'e et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320858F/abstract).

The energy injected into the interstellar medium (ISM) via [[clustered-supernovae|supernova feedback]] and [[wind-mechanical-energy|stellar winds]] is a primary driver of these cyclic variations. In dwarf galaxies, the kinetic energy deposited by [[massive-star-physics|massive star evolution]] can easily exceed the local binding energy of the gas, driving multi-phase [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]]. Multi-phase observational evidence, such as X-ray data from the low-metallicity galaxy I Zw 18, directly supports the existence of these hot, metal-enriched outflows escaping the shallow potential wells of low-mass dwarfs [Bomans et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..262..141B/abstract). Smoothed-particle hydrodynamics simulations demonstrate that internal stellar feedback alone can be sufficient to deplete gas reservoirs and quench star formation, reproducing the physical properties of isolated [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf spheroidals (dSphs)]] without necessitating external environmental stripping [Hazenfratz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969...65H/abstract). 

Despite the general success of ejective feedback models, relying exclusively on strong, supernova-driven outflows introduces theoretical tensions. Specifically, models that utilize only strong outflows fail to simultaneously reproduce both the [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass function]] and the [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]] of Milky Way satellite dwarfs [Lu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...66L/abstract). To reconcile this discrepancy, models increasingly require a combination of moderate, mass-independent outflows to govern metallicity, alongside "preventive feedback" mechanisms. Preventive feedback operates by pre-heating [[galaxy-and-igm-evolution|intergalactic gas]], thereby raising its entropy and preventing it from collapsing into the shallow dark matter halos of dwarf galaxies in the first place [Lu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...66L/abstract). Together, these internal drivers—ejective winds and preventive pre-heating—throttle the baryonic infall rate, govern subsequent chemical enrichment, and ultimately dictate the structural evolution of the dwarf galaxy population.

## Environmental Mechanisms of Quenching and Stripping

As dwarf galaxies accrete into denser environments, such as massive [[host-galaxies|Host galaxies]] or [[galaxy-environment|galaxy clusters]], they are subjected to a suite of external processes that deplete their gas reservoirs and suppress star formation. This environmental transformation chain is the primary driver of the observed [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]], efficiently converting gas-rich [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|dwarf irregulars]] into gas-poor dwarf spheroidals and ellipticals [Carraro et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..253C/abstract). The dominant external mechanisms—ram-pressure stripping, strangulation, and tidal harassment—operate on varying timescales and physical scales to halt star formation.

When a dwarf galaxy undergoes cluster infall, it must traverse a dense, hot [[intracluster-and-intragroup-phenomena|intracluster or intragroup medium]]. During this passage, hydrodynamical forces can exceed the galaxy's gravitational restoring force per unit area, resulting in [[galactic-environmental-stripping|ram-pressure stripping]]. This process removes the cold interstellar medium on relatively short dynamical timescales, rapidly shutting down star formation. Observational evidence confirms that ram-pressure stripping is a potent quenching mechanism not only in the local universe but also at [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|high redshifts]]; extended, elongated gas tails indicative of ram-pressure stripping have been directly observed in forming galaxy clusters as early as $z = 2.51$ [Xu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250321724X/abstract).

For many [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]], star formation ceases more gradually through strangulation (or starvation). In this scenario, the dwarf's loosely bound, extended [[galaxy-and-igm-evolution|hot gas halo]] is stripped upon entering a larger halo, but its dense [[galactic-disk-structure-and-dynamics|cold gas disk]] remains largely intact. Cut off from future cosmological gas accretion, the galaxy continues to form stars only until its existing cold gas reservoir is exhausted. The characteristic timescale for strangulation scales with the gas consumption time, $\tau_{\rm SF} = M_{\rm cold}/\dot{M}_\star$. Incorporating delayed stripping timescales of $\sim 1-2$ Gyr is critical in hierarchical [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]] to resolve the "over-quenching problem"—a historical tension where simulations overpredicted the fraction of red, passive satellites by assuming instantaneous gas loss upon accretion.

Beyond hydrodynamical effects, gravitational interactions drastically alter dwarf morphologies. Galaxy harassment occurs via repeated high-speed, impulsive encounters with massive cluster members and the global cluster potential. During these encounters, there is an important distinction between interaction-driven starbursts fueled by radial gas inflows, which strictly enhance central [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|nuclear star formation]], and those driven by triggered turbulence and fragmentation, which generate massive [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|star clusters]] across extended scales [Bournaud et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....51..107B/abstract). Similarly, [[galactic-tide|tidal forces]] acting within accreting sub-groups inside clusters are shown to be highly efficient drivers of galaxy evolution, transforming massive spirals into [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|S0 galaxies]]—a mechanism that scales down to profoundly affect the structural configuration of low-mass dwarfs [Moss et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..230..487M/abstract). These rapid tidal shocks drive secular mass loss, strip peripheral stars, and kinematically heat the dwarf's stellar disk into a spheroidal configuration. Even outside of rich clusters, tidal [[galaxy-interactions|Galaxy interactions]] between dwarfs can induce severe [[stellar-substructure|tidal tails]] and structural distortion. For instance, the Antlia dwarf exhibits tidal tails offset by $<10^\circ$ toward its neighbor NGC 3109, demonstrating that tidal forces dictate evolution even at the low-density edges of the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]]. While models that couple these minor interactions with small induced starbursts were originally developed to successfully reproduce the morphology-radius relation of bright cluster galaxies [Okamoto et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ogci.conf..534O/abstract), these same principles of tidal harassment scale down to explain the severe structural distortions observed in the dwarf regime.

Furthermore, the impact of external perturbations on a galaxy's interstellar medium can be systematically traced via the "fueling diagram," which links recent enhancements in star formation directly to interaction-driven changes in the [[h2-regulated-star-formation|molecular-to-atomic]] ($\text{H}_2/\text{H I}$) gas ratio [Stark et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...769...82S/abstract). Together, these mechanisms dictate the pathways for [[environmental-quenching|Environmental quenching]]. While isolated dwarfs rely heavily on internal stellar feedback to regulate star formation, the addition of stripping and harassment in dense environments ensures rapid baryon depletion. This multifaceted removal of gas and stars ultimately drives complete [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]], leaving the low-mass extremities of the cluster population heavily [[subhalo-physics-and-galactic-substructure|dark matter-dominated]] [Kormendy et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract). The foundational architecture of these dense cluster environments has been established for billions of years; observations demonstrating that the K-band luminosity function of clusters evolves passively suggest that cluster populations were already largely in place by $z \sim 1.5-2$ [Lin et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...650L..99L/abstract). The Centaurus A group serves as a key local environment where these effects are closely studied; using HST/ACS color-magnitude diagrams, researchers have directly measured the specific [[star-formation-history|star formation histories]] of its dwarf galaxies, confirming a strong environmental dependence in their evolution [Makarova et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..244..326M/abstract). These processes align with the broader synthesis of "cosmic quenching," which demonstrates that the overall fraction of quenched galaxies grows at later epochs and correlates strongly with both intrinsic mass (e.g., [[galactic-bulges|bulge-dominance]]) and local density [De Lucia et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250201724D/abstract).

## Morphological Transformations: From Irregulars to Spheroids

The observed [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]] extends to the lowest-mass galaxies, highlighting a fundamental evolutionary link between gas-rich [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|dwarf irregulars (dIrr)]] and gas-poor [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf spheroidals (dSph)]] or [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|dwarf ellipticals]]. Structural parameter correlations demonstrate that dSphs are essentially star-formation-quenched, "red and dead" versions of dIrrs. They form a continuous parallel sequence wherein dSphs act as the low-mass extensions of bulgeless S0 galaxies [Kormendy et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract). The transition between these states is often marked by a [[post-starburst-galaxies|"post-starburst"]] or [[e-a-galaxies|"E+A"]] phase, which serves as a critical, fast-evolving transition state between star-forming irregulars and fully quenched spheroids [French et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASP..133g2001F/abstract). Theoretical models suggest that a higher frequency of these E+A galaxies in the early universe may be attributed to the lower metallicities and higher burst strengths driven by the greater gas content typical of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|high-redshift dwarfs]] [Falkenberg et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.397.1940F/abstract).

In dense environments, this [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]] is primarily driven by external mechanisms such as [[galactic-environmental-stripping|ram-pressure stripping]], tidal harassment, and strangulation [Vollmer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013pss6.book..207V/abstract), [Carraro et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..253C/abstract). As a dIrr falls into a larger dark matter halo, hydrodynamic pressure from the intracluster or intragroup medium removes its cold gas reservoir, leading to rapid [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]]. Ram-pressure stripping is an efficient quenching pathway that operates over cosmic time, with direct observational evidence of extended gas tails caught in action as early as $z=2.51$ [Xu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250321724X/abstract). Tidal interactions further reshape the stellar component; even at the outskirts of the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]], dwarf galaxies like Antlia exhibit tidal tails from encounters with neighbors (e.g., NGC 3109). This indicates that multiple moderate interactions can severely alter a dwarf's morphology without completely disrupting it, provided its orbital pericenter is sufficiently large ($> 6$ kpc for Antlia) [Penny et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...758L..32P/abstract). When these interactions successfully trigger star formation, the enhancement is not always restricted to the galaxy center; rather, star formation can be elevated on extended spatial scales out to $\sim 6.7 \text{ kpc}$ following the first pericenter passage [Pan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881..119P/abstract).

However, whether dense environments are strictly required to form a dSph remains an active debate. Recent hydrodynamical models suggest that internal stellar feedback alone may be sufficient to deplete gas and quench star formation in [[low-mass-galaxies|isolated dwarf galaxies]] [Hazenfratz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969...65H/abstract). Additionally, much like how massive lenticular/S0 morphologies can form in isolation via [[violent-disk-instability|violent disk instabilities]]—providing a non-environmental pathway for morphological mutation in the field [Saha et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862L..12S/abstract)—isolated dwarfs might undergo analogous internal structural evolution. Because low-mass galaxies possess shallow gravitational potential wells, they are exceptionally sensitive to internal heating. There is ongoing tension regarding the exact nature of this feedback: while strong [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|supernova-driven outflows]] are traditionally invoked, some models indicate that "preventive feedback"—which pre-heats gas and prevents it from cooling into the halo—is required to simultaneously reproduce the observed [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|stellar mass and metallicity scaling relations]] of [[galactic-satellite-galaxies|Galactic satellite galaxies]] [Lu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...66L/abstract). 

The endpoint of either transformation pathway—environmental stripping or internal blowout—is a dynamically distinct system. Kinematic studies reveal a universal bimodality between cold rotating disks and hot, dispersion-dominated spheroids, governed by the intrinsic spin parameter of the stars, $\lambda_{R_e, {\rm intr}}$ [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..165W/abstract). As gas is removed and star formation ceases, the remaining collisionless [[stellar-and-galactic-core-structure|stellar system]] relaxes into a hot spheroid. However, this morphological transition is not always irreversible; the concept of "evolutionary cycling" posits that gas replenishment can occasionally trigger a transformation back to a star-forming state, even from a quiescent dwarf elliptical [Hensler et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASSP...28...75H/abstract). Furthermore, because the retained [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|baryon fraction]] $f_{\rm b} = M_{\rm baryon}/M_{\rm halo}$ drops sharply in these shallow potential wells, the extreme products of this morphological transformation are overwhelmingly dominated by dark matter [Kormendy et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract).

## Tidal Dwarf Galaxies and Recycled Systems

Tidal Dwarf Galaxies (TDGs) represent a distinct mode of [[secondary-galaxy-formation|Secondary galaxy formation]], emerging from the gravitational collapse of gaseous and stellar debris expelled during [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] and violent galaxy interactions. Unlike primordial [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] that assemble within isolated cosmological halos, TDGs condense directly within the gas-rich [[galactic-tide|tidal tails]] and Galactic bridges of interacting systems. Theoretical models and hydrodynamical simulations indicate that a single massive galaxy collision can spawn several of these self-gravitating systems from the expelled baryonic material [Okazaki et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...543..149O/abstract). The formation of these unique recycled systems involves a specific transformation from instabilities within expelled [[h-i-rich-galaxies|HI clouds]] into [[h2-regulated-star-formation|molecular gas]] prior to the onset of star formation [Duc et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002Ap&SS.281..347D/abstract).

The physical characteristics of TDGs distinguish them markedly from typical isolated or satellite dwarfs. Because the kinematics of interaction-driven tidal stripping preferentially ejects cold baryonic disk material rather than the extended, collisionless [[subhalo-physics-and-galactic-substructure|dark matter]], TDGs are born severely deficient in dark matter. They function essentially as [[dark-matter-free-galaxies|Dark matter-free galaxies]], lacking the deep, dark matter-dominated potential wells characteristic of standard dwarf irregulars and dwarf spheroidals [Duc et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASSP...28..305D/abstract). Furthermore, because TDGs are assembled through the recycling of the interstellar medium from massive progenitor spirals, they exhibit anomalous [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|chemical properties]] for their size. Specifically, they possess significantly higher metallicities than predicted by the standard mass-metallicity relation for typical dwarf galaxies, directly reflecting the pre-enriched state of their parent gas [Duc et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASSP...28..305D/abstract).

Despite their exotic origins, the global star formation physics within TDGs appears to mirror that of mature galactic disks. Ultraviolet and optical observations of TDGs in local interacting systems, such as NGC 7252 and NGC 5291, demonstrate that their star formation rates scale with gas surface density according to the standard [[star-formation-history|Kennicutt-Schmidt relation]], $\Sigma_{\rm SFR} \propto \Sigma_{\rm gas}^N$ [Santhosh et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250302777S/abstract). The intense, high-pressure environments within these tidal features frequently catalyze the formation of young massive star clusters. These massive clusters share masses and radii consistent with being the young progenitors of metal-rich [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]] [Anders et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003IAUJD..11E..13A/abstract), [de Grijs et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...29D/abstract). High-resolution studies of actively interacting systems, such as Arp 305, have identified candidate TDGs and associated star-forming clumps with exceptionally young [[late-type-galaxies-and-stellar-populations|stellar populations]] of $\sim 6 \text{ Myr}$, highlighting the ongoing nature of this recycling process [Hancock et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AJ....137.4643H/abstract).

The long-term survival and overall cosmological significance of TDGs remain subjects of active debate in the literature [Duc et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASSP...28..305D/abstract). It is currently uncertain whether the majority of TDGs endure as long-lived [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] or if they are predominantly transient phenomena that dissolve into the intragroup medium or rapidly re-merge with their host galaxies. Because identifying ancient, evolved TDGs among the general dwarf population is observationally challenging, the exact fraction of the present-day universe's low-mass galaxies formed via this tidal recycling mechanism remains ambiguous. However, the interactions shaping these recycled systems continue to be modeled in detail; for instance, hydrodynamical simulations of [[dwarf-galaxy-physical-processes|Magellanic-like]] dwarf-dwarf interactions demonstrate that such sub-galactic encounters can produce complex chemical structures within [[stellar-substructure|stellar streams]], even in the presence of strong ram pressure from a massive host galaxy [Williamson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...907....9W/abstract).

## Chemical Evolution and Enrichment Histories

Dwarf galaxies follow [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] between their total mass and chemical enrichment. Because of their uniquely shallow gravitational potential wells, dwarfs are highly sensitive to internal stellar feedback, which drives metal-enriched [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]]. However, regulating both the [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass function]] and the [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]] poses a theoretical challenge. While standard models often invoke strong supernova-driven winds as the primary mechanism for depleting gas and expelling metals, recent parameter space explorations indicate that outflows alone cannot simultaneously reproduce the mass and metallicity of Milky Way satellites [Lu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...66L/abstract). Instead, a combination of moderate, mass-independent outflows and "preventive feedback"—whereby intergalactic gas is pre-heated, preventing it from ever collapsing into low-mass halos—is required to match both observables [Lu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...66L/abstract).

The internal distribution of metals in dwarf galaxies also challenges simple predictions. A standard [[closed-box-model|Closed box model]] suggests that star formation should steadily increase the metallicity of the gas. However, many dwarf galaxies lack significant radial metallicity gradients, even when individual, isolated [[active-star-forming-regions|H II regions]] show localized metal enhancement. This discrepancy points to highly efficient [[galaxy-enrichment-processes|internal gas mixing]] processes. Chemodynamical models suggest that cycles of gas evaporation by hot [[clustered-supernovae|supernova ejecta]] followed by subsequent condensation can rapidly homogenize the interstellar medium. These internal gas mixing cycles operate over timescales ranging from roughly $10 \text{ Myr}$ locally to $\sim 1 \text{ Gyr}$ globally across the galaxy [Hensler et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004IAUS..217..178H/abstract), [Rieschick et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..215..130R/abstract). 

When dwarf galaxies undergo [[galaxy-interactions|Galaxy interactions]] or [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]], their chemical distributions are profoundly altered. Traditional textbook models predict that interaction-induced gravitational torques drive gas inflows, which efficiently mix the gas and flatten existing metallicity gradients. However, the literature presents conflicting evidence regarding the universality of this flattening. Recent spatially resolved observations reveal that while metallicity gradients often flatten shortly after the first pericenter passage due to radial mixing, interactions can also trigger centrally concentrated [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|starbursts]] that rapidly enrich the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|nuclear regions]]. Consequently, depending on the merger stage and the intensity of the induced star formation, the gas-phase metallicity gradients can actually steepen, directly contradicting simple hydrodynamical mixing prescriptions [Pan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982..130P/abstract).

The chemical enrichment histories of dwarf galaxies are permanently imprinted in their star cluster systems. Studies of [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]] reveal a bimodal color and metallicity distribution, broadly separating into metal-poor clusters formed in high-redshift protogalactic fragments and metal-rich clusters formed during later merger-induced starbursts [Kissler-Patig et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002IAUS..207..207K/abstract). The [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|alpha-element abundance ratios]] and mean metallicities further differentiate dwarf populations by their [[star-formation-history|star formation histories]] and environments. At a given age, globular clusters in early-type dwarfs exhibit higher mean metallicities than those in late-type dwarf irregulars, reflecting the more rapid, burst-dominated chemical evolution necessary to form the early-type systems before gas depletion occurs [Sharina et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010vsgh.conf..181S/abstract).

## Star Cluster Systems as Evolutionary Tracers

Because they are dense, luminous, and robust against mild tidal disruption, star clusters serve as fossil records of the violent [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and dynamical assembly of their host galaxies [Kissler-Patig et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000RvMA...13...13K/abstract). The study of [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]] and their younger counterparts provides crucial constraints on the chemical enrichment and environmental conditions of dwarf galaxies at their epoch of formation.

A defining feature of many globular cluster systems is [[ashman-zepf-model|cluster bimodality]], characterized by a distinct blue (metal-poor) population and a red (metal-rich) population. This bimodal color distribution is generally interpreted as the signature of a two-phase assembly history. The metal-poor clusters are thought to have formed at high redshifts within primordial [[early-galaxy-formation|protogalactic fragments]], while the metal-rich clusters represent a second generation formed during subsequent merger-induced starbursts. By measuring the ages, metallicities, and [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|$\alpha$-element abundance ratios]] of these clusters, observers can reconstruct the chemical evolution of the host. For instance, spectroscopic observations indicate that at a given age, the mean metallicity of globular clusters is systematically higher in early-type dwarf galaxies than in late-type dwarf irregulars [Sharina et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010vsgh.conf..181S/abstract).

In contemporary interacting and starbursting galaxies, observations reveal the ongoing star cluster formation of Young Massive Clusters (YSCs), also known as super star clusters. The extreme, high-pressure environments induced by [[galaxy-interactions|Galaxy interactions]] drive gas to densities that strongly favor this clustered mode of star formation [de Grijs et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...29D/abstract). The sizes, luminosities, and masses of YSCs are entirely consistent with the expected properties of young [[galactic-globular-cluster-system|Milky Way-type globular clusters]], strongly supporting the hypothesis that YSCs are the direct progenitors of globular clusters [de Grijs et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph..8232D/abstract). Because they are newly formed, YSCs act as unique probes of the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] and star formation efficiency under extreme interstellar medium conditions.

The violent encounters that trigger YSC formation often eject tidal debris, leading to the formation of recycled dwarf galaxies known as [[secondary-galaxy-formation|tidal dwarf galaxies]]. Multi-wavelength observations of highly disturbed systems, such as NGC 7252 and NGC 5291, reveal that the [[active-star-forming-regions|Active star-forming regions]] and YSCs within these tidal structures adhere to the same Kennicutt-Schmidt scaling relations, $\Sigma_{\rm SFR} \propto \Sigma_{\rm gas}^N$, as regular spiral galaxies [Santhosh et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250302777S/abstract). As these systems evolve, the survival of the clusters depends heavily on their initial mass. Simulations typically track clusters with masses $M \gtrsim 10^4\ M_\odot$ as they are subjected to two-body relaxation, [[massive-star-physics|stellar evolution]], and tidal shocks [Hoyer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250412079H/abstract). Furthermore, the redistribution and repositioning of these clusters during ongoing [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|mergers]] are primary drivers of the spatial evolution of the broader star cluster system [Hoyer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250412079H/abstract). Consequently, the spatial and chemical distributions of surviving cluster systems encode the deep history of galactic collisions, gas accretion, and morphological transformation experienced by the dwarf galaxy population.

## The Role of Dark Matter on Small Scales

Dwarf galaxies are fundamentally distinguished by their extreme [[subhalo-physics-and-galactic-substructure|dark matter domination]], typically characterized by baryon fractions well below the cosmic average. Kinematic and structural scaling relations demonstrate that as the total mass of a system decreases, it becomes increasingly dominated by dark matter [Kormendy et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract), [Meurer et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001sgnf.conf...81M/abstract). This scaling is a direct consequence of the shallow gravitational potential wells characteristic of low-mass dark matter halos, which severely limit the efficiency of [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]]. In these systems, the dark matter halo not only dictates the orbital kinematics but also sets the equilibrium surface brightness of the baryonic disk and steers its morphological evolution [Meurer et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001sgnf.conf...81M/abstract). For example, results from the [[munich-galaxy-formation-model|GAEA semi-analytic model]] show that applying mass-dependent timescales for the re-accretion of ejected gas into these halos is critical to successfully reproducing the galaxy stellar mass function up to redshift $z \sim 7$ [Fontanot et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...842L..14F/abstract).

The most immediate consequence of a shallow potential well is the galaxy's vulnerability to [[mass-loss-processes|Mass Loss Processes]]. Because the escape velocity $v_{\rm esc}$ in sub-galactic halos is small, kinetic and thermal energy injected by [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|starbursts]] can easily unbind the local interstellar medium. Consequently, extreme dwarfs exhibit remarkably low [[dense-stellar-systems|stellar densities]] because they retain only a small fraction of their primordial baryons against feedback [Kormendy et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract). Furthermore, the rapid, feedback-driven expulsion of this gas can cause a profound 'puffing-up' effect, significantly increasing the half-light radius of the remaining stellar component as the system kinematically adjusts to the sudden loss of central mass [Lapi et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...857...22L/abstract). While standard models of [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] traditionally rely on strong [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]] to regulate star formation in [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf spheroidals (dSphs)]], recent studies of Milky Way satellites have revealed a major tension in this paradigm. Theoretical investigations show that extreme, ejection-dominated feedback cannot simultaneously reproduce both the observed low stellar mass fractions and the mass-metallicity relation of local dwarf populations [Lu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...66L/abstract). Resolving this discrepancy requires a shift toward "preventive feedback" mechanisms—such as the pre-heating of gas—which prevent baryons from falling into the shallow gravitational potentials of low-mass halos in the first place, operating alongside moderate, weakly mass-dependent outflows [Lu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...66L/abstract). 

Beyond internal regulation, the depth of the dark matter potential well governs a dwarf's vulnerability to external forces, closely tying their evolution to [[subhalo-physics-and-galactic-substructure|Subhalo Physics and Galactic Substructure]]. When low-mass halos are accreted by larger structures to become [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]], their loosely bound outer gas is rapidly removed by ram-pressure stripping and tidal harassment. This mass loss drives [[environmental-quenching|Environmental quenching]], accelerating the morphological transformation from gas-rich dwarf irregulars into gas-poor dwarf spheroidals [Kormendy et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract). The long-term survival of these satellites depends entirely on their [[read-density-profile|dark matter profiles]]. While the diffuse baryonic component and extended dark matter halo are continuously stripped by the host's tidal field, subhalos with sufficiently dense, concentrated dark matter cores can survive complete tidal disruption. Even minor encounters with dark matter substructure can drive consequential secular evolution; interactions with dark matter subhalos have been shown to trigger long-lived [[spiral-galaxy-dynamics|stellar bars]] within galactic disks that can endure for up to $10 \text{ Gyr}$ [Romano-Díaz et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...687L..13R/abstract). Ultimately, it is the interplay between the shallow dark matter potential well and the combined forces of preventive feedback and environmental stripping that defines the mass assembly and stellar retention of the dwarf galaxy population.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite significant progress in characterizing [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], the precise balance between internal and external drivers of [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] remains unresolved. A major theoretical tension exists regarding the mechanisms of internal feedback. While standard models have long relied on stellar-driven outflows to regulate star formation, it is debated whether these outflows alone can simultaneously reproduce both the [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass function]] and the [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]] of dwarf galaxies. Recent inferences suggest that "preventive feedback"—such as the pre-heating of gas that prevents baryons from collapsing into shallow potential wells—may be required as the dominant regulatory mechanism [Lu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...66L/abstract). 

Furthermore, the impact of interactions on internal chemical gradients is disputed. While hydrodynamical mixing models generally predict a flattening of metallicity gradients during galactic encounters, recent spatially resolved observations indicate that interactions can occasionally steepen these gradients, contradicting simple mixing scenarios [Pan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982..130P/abstract). It also remains an open question what fraction of the local dwarf population originated within primordial dark matter halos versus as [[secondary-galaxy-formation|"recycled" tidal dwarf galaxies]] formed from the collisional debris of massive galaxy encounters [Duc et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASSP...28..305D/abstract).

The [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|redshift]] at which [[galactic-environmental-stripping|Galactic Environmental Stripping]] began to significantly influence dwarf galaxy evolution represents another major observational frontier. While dense local environments clearly drive morphological transformations, the redshift at which effects like ram-pressure stripping become dominant is contested. Recent joint observations utilizing ALMA and JWST have caught ram-pressure stripping in action in [[nascent-galaxy-clusters|Nascent galaxy clusters]] as early as $z=2.51$ [Xu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250321724X/abstract). In these distant systems, ALMA detected extended, stripped gas tails, while JWST imaging confirmed that the underlying [[galaxy-subcomponents|stellar components]] remained morphologically undisturbed by mergers, providing unambiguous evidence for environmental gas removal [Xu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250321724X/abstract). Looking forward, JWST is uniquely positioned to observe warm [[h2-regulated-star-formation|$\text{H}_2$ line emission]], which will test theories of turbulent energy dissipation and gas cooling in high-redshift environments [Guillard et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015sf2a.conf...81G/abstract).

In the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]], upcoming large-scale surveys will provide unprecedented constraints on dwarf galaxy assembly. The 4MOST facility, specifically through the 4DWARFS survey, will map the chrono-chemo-kinematics of approximately $140,000$ stars in dwarf galaxies and [[stellar-substructure|Stellar substructure]] streams, offering a highly resolved view of hierarchical [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] on the smallest scales [Skúladóttir et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Msngr.190...19S/abstract). In the radio regime, the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) will revolutionize the study of [[h-i-rich-galaxies|atomic ($\text{H I}$)]] and molecular gas dynamics. By tracing the neutral-to-molecular gas transition via 21-cm absorption lines, the SKA will provide critical insights into the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and chemical enrichment of gas-rich dwarfs across cosmic time [Takeuchi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160301938T/abstract). Finally, the planned generation of 30-40m optical telescopes is expected to extend spatially resolved spectroscopy to ultra-low-mass dwarfs ($M_\star \sim 10^5-10^7 M_\odot$), providing definitive tests for models of dark matter and early-universe feedback across diverse cosmic environments [Aguerri et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251214783A/abstract).
