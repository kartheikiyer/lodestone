**Earendel** is a highly magnified astrophysical source located within the Sunrise Arc, a [[background-galaxies|background galaxy]] at a spectroscopically confirmed redshift of $z = 5.926 \pm 0.013$. Initially discovered using the Hubble Space Telescope and subsequently characterized with high-resolution James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) imaging and spectroscopy, Earendel represents the highest-redshift highly magnified point-like source detected to date [Meena et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944L...6M/abstract). Its discovery challenged classical tenets of [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], which traditionally maintained that individual stars could only be resolved within relatively nearby galaxies. Instead, the precise spatial alignment of the Sunrise Arc behind the massive foreground galaxy cluster WHL J013719.8-08284 ($z = 0.566$) subjects the source to extreme [[strong-gravitational-lensing|Strong gravitational lensing]]. By intersecting the cluster's critical curve, Earendel experiences immense gravitational magnification, rendering parsec-scale emission regions visible across cosmological distances.

The precise astrophysical classification of Earendel is currently the subject of an active debate in the literature, hinging primarily on discrepancies in estimating its gravitational magnification factor ($\mu$). Initial macroscopic lens models calculated extreme magnification values ranging from $\mu = 2,000$ to $17,500$ (reported as $2\mu = 4,000\text{--}35,000$), which stringently constrained the source's physical radius to $\lesssim 0.3 \text{ pc}$ [Scofield et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..226S/abstract), [Ji et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..190J/abstract). Under this regime, Earendel is best interpreted as a single, extraordinarily massive star or a tightly bound binary system. However, more recent joint strong- and weak-lensing reconstructions using deep JWST data suggest drastically lower magnification values of $\mu = 43\text{--}67$. At these lower magnifications, the intrinsic luminosity required to match observations suggests that Earendel may instead be a compact, metal-poor ($Z_\star \lesssim 0.1 Z_\odot$) proto-globular [[star-clusters|star cluster]] with an intermediate age of $30\text{--}150 \text{ Myr}$ [Pascale et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988L..76P/abstract), [Scofield et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..226S/abstract). This star cluster hypothesis is bolstered by theoretical models demonstrating that small-scale substructures in the lens—specifically dark matter subhalos—can perturb the lensing caustics and relax initial source-size constraints by up to a factor of ten [Ji et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..190J/abstract). 

This article explores the observational history, lensing mechanics, and physical characterization of Earendel. It examines how the granularity of the foreground lens complicates the derivation of the source's intrinsic properties, places Earendel in comparative context with other cosmologically distant lensed stars, and outlines the open questions surrounding its definitive classification.

## Discovery and the Sunrise Arc Host Galaxy

Earendel was identified within the Sunrise Arc, a strongly lensed high-redshift galaxy situated behind the massive foreground galaxy cluster WHL J013719.8-08284 at $z = 0.566$ [Scofield et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..226S/abstract). Early photometric estimates placed the arc at a redshift of $z \approx 6.2$, serving as a baseline when comparing Earendel to other extreme high-redshift objects, such as [[lyman-break-selection-and-galaxies|dropout galaxies]] found behind other clusters [Meena et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944L...6M/abstract). The initial detection of the point-like source was made using the Hubble Space Telescope, establishing Earendel as the most distant highly magnified object of its kind.

The discovery of such distant sources adds to a broader ongoing search for early-universe objects. By way of context, large-scale pure-parallel programs like the [[borg-survey|BoRG-JWST survey]] are concurrently mapping the high-redshift universe, recently confirming some of the most intrinsically luminous [[bright-galaxies|UV-bright galaxies]] in the reionization era at $z > 7$, with absolute magnitudes of $-22.4 < M_{\text{UV}} < -20.4$ [Roberts-Borsani et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240717551R/abstract). 

Following its initial discovery, Earendel and its host galaxy became primary targets for high-resolution follow-up using JWST's NIRCam and NIRSpec instruments. Deep NIRSpec PRISM spectroscopy definitively refined the redshift of the host Sunrise Arc to a spectroscopic value of $z = 5.926 \pm 0.013$ [Pascale et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988L..76P/abstract). The immense magnification of this source is driven by the spatial alignment of the source with the foreground cluster's critical curve. While foundational textbooks have historically maintained that individual stars can only be resolved in relatively nearby systems, the discovery of the Sunrise Arc demonstrates that caustic crossings can render extremely luminous, highly compact sources visible across the Universe.

## Gravitational Lensing Mechanics and Substructure

The astrophysical interpretation of Earendel is heavily dependent on its exact gravitational magnification factor ($\mu$), which has been subject to significant revisions. Early macroscopic lens models predicted extreme magnification factors in the range of $\mu = 2,000$ to $17,500$ (where $2\mu = 4,000\text{--}35,000$) [Scofield et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..226S/abstract). Such high magnifications are mathematically necessary to explain the observed flux if Earendel is an individual, highly magnified star.

However, recent joint strong- and weak-lensing analyses using deep JWST imaging have challenged these initial models. By incorporating newly identified multiple-image systems to achieve a root-mean-square lens-plane scatter of less than $0.\!\!^{\prime\prime}3$, and utilizing weak-lensing constraints from approximately 100 [[background-galaxies|Background galaxies]] per square arcminute, updated mass reconstructions derive a significantly lower macroscopic magnification of $\mu = 43\text{--}67$ [Scofield et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..226S/abstract). 

The derivation of Earendel's properties is further complicated by the granularity of the lensing mass distribution. Smooth macroscopic critical curves are often fractured by local substructures, creating a complex network of microscopic caustics. Analytical models indicate that the high-magnification tail of the probability distribution function for such events is directly proportional to the number of independent microlens critical curves generated by intracluster stars, with the finite size of the background source suppressing formal magnification singularities [Kawai et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.110h3514K/abstract). 

Beyond stellar microlenses, cold dark matter models predict the existence of sub-galactic dark matter subhalos. High-resolution numerical techniques, such as adaptive ray-shooting, demonstrate that accounting for a realistic subhalo mass function introduces astrometric perturbations up to $0.\!\!^{\prime\prime}5$ [Ji et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..190J/abstract). Crucially, these subhalos can relax the original source-size upper limits (initially constrained to $\lesssim 0.3 \text{ pc}$ to survive as a single point-like image) by a factor of three to ten. This expansion in permissible physical size provides robust theoretical support for the hypothesis that Earendel may not be an isolated stellar object.

## Physical Nature: Star vs. Star Cluster

Driven by the disparate magnification estimates and relaxed size constraints, the true physical classification of Earendel is actively debated. In the initial discovery framework relying on $\mu = 2,000\text{--}17,500$ and a radius of $< 0.3 \text{ pc}$, Earendel was primarily interpreted as a single, extraordinarily massive star or a tightly bound binary system undergoing a caustic crossing [Scofield et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..226S/abstract), [Ji et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..190J/abstract). 

Conversely, the lower magnification estimate of $\mu = 43\text{--}67$ creates significant tension with the single-star interpretation. At this lower level of magnification, an individual star would not be sufficiently luminous to match the observed apparent magnitude, pointing instead toward a massive stellar association [Scofield et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..226S/abstract). With subhalo modeling permitting a source size up to ten times larger than initially thought, the geometric limits comfortably accommodate a compact [[star-clusters|star cluster]] [Ji et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..190J/abstract).

Recent spectroscopic observations strongly favor this star cluster hypothesis. Fits to the rest-frame ultraviolet and optical continuum using simple stellar population (SSP) models reveal that Earendel's physical properties behave nearly equivalently to another confidently identified star cluster ("1b") within the same host galaxy. These fits indicate that, if Earendel is a stellar cluster, it possesses an intermediate age of $t_{\text{age}} \approx 30\text{--}150 \text{ Myr}$ and is extremely metal-poor, with a stellar metallicity $Z_\star \lesssim 0.1 Z_\odot$ [Pascale et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988L..76P/abstract). These physical parameters map closely onto the expected age-metallicity relations of proto-globular star clusters forming in the early Universe. Note that while Earendel's population is estimated to be young, recent JWST studies of [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|high-redshift lensed galaxies]] caution that rest-frame optical/NIR continuum observations cannot yet place strict upper limits on the mass contributed by older stellar populations (age $> 100 \text{ Myr}$) [Nianias et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241021788N/abstract).

## Comparative Astrophysics of Lensed Stars

Placing Earendel in context with other lensed stars highlights the varying physical mechanisms and observational signatures of extreme magnification events. At lower redshifts, caustic-crossing stars typically manifest as transient events. A prominent example is "Warhol", a $z=0.94$ blue supergiant located behind the galaxy cluster MACS J0416.1-2403. Warhol was identified as a transient microlensing event with a peak duration of at most four weeks and an estimated temporary magnification of at least several thousand [Chen et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881....8C/abstract). 

The field has rapidly advanced from single detections to statistical samples; recent observations of the Dragon Arc at $z=0.725$ behind Abell 370 have revealed more than 40 microlensed star candidates. These transient events are primarily consistent with red giants or supergiants magnified by factors of hundreds to thousands, demonstrating the power of time-domain observations to probe the granularity of intracluster mass [Fudamoto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240408045F/abstract), [Fudamoto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..428F/abstract).

Another dramatic example of a highly magnified, but fundamentally different, high-redshift source is 'Orochi' (AzTEC-ASTE-SXDF1100.001) at $z \sim 3.4$. Unlike Earendel's precise, point-like emission from stellar populations, Orochi is an optically dark, extremely luminous lensed submillimeter galaxy exhibiting apparent star formation rates exceeding 11,000 solar masses per year [Ikarashi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.3081I/abstract). 

Moving to even higher redshifts, extreme point-like highly magnified targets become rarer but profoundly informative. Behind the cluster MACS J0647.7+7015, two B-type star candidates have been identified in a giant arc at $z \simeq 4.8$. Lacking clear counter-images, they are likely stars undergoing caustic crossings with effective magnifications reaching $\mu \sim 10^3\text{--}10^5$. Their spectral energy distributions indicate surface temperatures of $\sim 10,000 \text{ K}$ and $\sim 12,000 \text{ K}$, corresponding to masses of $\gtrsim 20 M_\odot$ and $\gtrsim 50 M_\odot$, respectively [Meena et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944L...6M/abstract). JWST observations of other massive clusters like MACS0647 have revealed similar populations of $z > 6$ [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|galaxy candidates]] and spectroscopic confirmations, establishing a demographic context for Earendel's environment [Worku et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251211985W/abstract). 

Note that other high-redshift systems like HFLS3 at $z=6.34$, initially interpreted as [[extreme-starbursts|Extreme starbursts]], have been resolved by JWST into merging groups of star-forming galaxies, further illustrating the complexities of distinguishing compact from grouping sources at this epoch [Jones et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...682A.122J/abstract). Researchers must also carefully distinguish between highly magnified stars and other inherently luminous point-like sources; for example, the discovery of GHZ9, a confirmed X-ray detected AGN at $z = 10.145$ in the GLASS field, highlights a demographic of remarkably bright, high-redshift point sources that are intrinsically powerful, presenting morphological similarities that must be untangled from lensed star profiles [Napolitano et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241010967N/abstract). Earendel—whether the most distant individual star or an ancient proto-globular [[star-clusters|cluster]]—serves as the high-redshift extreme of this dynamically emerging demographic.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The fundamental open question regarding Earendel remains its definitive classification. Accounting for both macromodel uncertainties and sub-galactic substructure makes it difficult to definitively distinguish between an isolated hypergiant star and a dense, ancient [[star-clusters|star cluster]]. Future ultra-deep programs such as [[glimpse-survey|GLIMPSE]] will provide high-resolution surveys of early star clusters, aiding in the statistical differentiation between individual lensed stars and compact stellar associations [Atek et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251107542A/abstract).

Looking ahead, resolving the exact metallicity and abundance patterns of Earendel and similar caustic-crossing targets will be critical for testing theories of early star formation. In this era of [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], it remains paramount to analytically separate high-redshift targets whose apparent luminosity is strictly driven by gravitational lensing (like Earendel) from sources that are intrinsically massive. The robust verification of intrinsically massive high-redshift galaxy candidates challenges standard $\Lambda$CDM halo mass functions and provides vital constraints on the primordial power spectrum, potentially favoring a blue tilt to explain [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|early structure formation]]—a distinct cosmological track from the constraints supplied by low-mass microlensed stellar sources [Parashari et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526L..63P/abstract). 

Furthermore, the high-magnification tail of these stellar lensing events depends sensitively on the mass fraction and distribution of intracluster microlenses and sub-galactic halos [Kawai et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.110h3514K/abstract). As statistical samples of highly magnified stars grow—such as the recent discovery of over 40 microlensed stars in the $z = 0.725$ Dragon Arc [Fudamoto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..428F/abstract)—they will yield increasingly precise dark matter constraints. By analyzing the statistics of these extreme magnification events, future studies will be able to probe the granularity of small-scale dark matter structures and test cold dark matter predictions at sub-galactic scales.
