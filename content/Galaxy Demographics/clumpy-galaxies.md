## Article

**Clumpy galaxies** are a major demographic of massive [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] predominantly observed during "cosmic noon" ($z \sim 1-3$), the epoch corresponding to the peak of the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]]. Unlike the smooth, rotationally supported spiral structures typical of the local Hubble sequence, these high-redshift systems exhibit highly irregular, chaotic morphologies. They are characterized by massive, kiloparsec-scale knots of intense ultraviolet emission embedded within thick, gas-rich, and highly turbulent [[clumpy-disks|Clumpy disks]] [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.392..253N/abstract), [Elmegreen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...701..306E/abstract). In traditional galactic dynamics, starbursts are often confined to compact nuclear regions triggered by external interactions; however, clumpy galaxies host widespread, distributed starbursts across their disks. These individual clumps can encompass up to $10^9 M_\odot$ in stellar mass and drive powerful multiphase outflows, contributing substantially to the global star formation and feedback profile of the host galaxy.

The primary theoretical framework for the formation of these giant substructures is [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] (VDI). In the early universe, continuous cold-mode accretion sustains disk gas fractions approaching 50%, pushing the local gas surface density past the Toomre critical threshold for gravitational fragmentation [Elmegreen et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..235..376E/abstract). Standard secular evolution models propose that these massive in-situ clumps subsequently undergo [[clump-migration|Clump migration]], losing angular momentum via dynamical friction and tidal torques to spiral inward. This process provides an internal pathway for the assembly of [[clump-origin-bulges|Clump-origin bulges]], contrasting sharply with classical cosmological models that attribute the formation of central [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] almost entirely to dissipational [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514...77N/abstract). Over cosmic time, as galaxies mature and their global gas fractions decline, these turbulent disks kinematically stabilize. Consequently, the fraction of clumpy galaxies drops precipitously, confining such fragmented morphologies in the modern universe to a small subset of analogs like [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] [Elmegreen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...747..105E/abstract).

Despite decades of study, the physical nature and evolutionary significance of giant clumps remain the subject of intense debate, particularly regarding their survivability. It is a major open question whether these structures represent gravitationally bound, long-lived "clumpy mass" capable of surviving the migration to the galactic center, or merely transient "clumpy light" regions that are rapidly disrupted by intense stellar feedback. The advent of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has revolutionized this field, pushing the detection of rest-frame near-infrared clumps into the epoch of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]]. Recent high-resolution observations have not only confirmed the presence of mature stellar populations within some high-redshift clumps, but have also revealed enigmatic, overmassive systems like [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]] at $z > 7$. These findings continuously challenge existing paradigms regarding the interplay between in-situ disk fragmentation, the fueling mechanisms of active galactic nuclei, and the earliest stages of [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] assembly.

## Morphological Classification and Properties

High-redshift [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] frequently exhibit a **chaotic morphology** dominated by massive, discrete star-forming regions. Unlike the smooth, symmetric Hubble sequence galaxies prevalent in the local universe, these systems are characterized by large, **UV-bright clumps** embedded within gas-rich, highly turbulent [[clumpy-disks|Clumpy disks]] [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.392..253N/abstract), [Elmegreen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...701..306E/abstract). Furthermore, deep learning models can now distinguish [[post-merger-galaxies|Post-merger galaxies]] from clumpy star-forming galaxies with roughly $80\%$ accuracy, confirming that many highly asymmetric systems observed at high redshift are inherently clumpy disks rather than collision remnants [Ferreira et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931...34F/abstract).

These substructures present as **kpc-scale knots** of intense star formation. Spectroscopic studies of analogous structures in clumpy irregulars reveal that these regions contain [[giant-h-ii-regions|Giant H II regions]] with electron temperatures ranging from $7000$ to $9000\text{ K}$ and slightly sub-solar oxygen and nitrogen abundances [Boesgaard et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982ApJ...252..487B/abstract). Furthermore, radio observations of clumpy irregulars like Mrk 325 show nonthermal spectra, indicating that supernova remnants from massive stars remain tightly contained within these giant star-forming regions [Condon et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ApJ...357...97C/abstract). Individual clump masses typically span $10^7$ to $10^9 M_\odot$ [Ribeiro et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...608A..16R/abstract), [Dessauges-Zavadsky et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479L.118D/abstract). Despite their visual prominence, giant clumps typically represent a minority of the host galaxy's total stellar mass—averaging $\sim 4\%$, though in rare extreme cases their contribution can be much higher [Soto et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837....6S/abstract). However, they are highly luminous, contributing up to $\sim 20\%$ of the galaxy's rest-frame UV flux and roughly $4\%-10\%$ of its total global star formation rate [Guo et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800...39G/abstract), [Soto et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837....6S/abstract).

Statistical analyses across multiple deep-field surveys indicate that the clump stellar mass function (cSMF) can be modeled as a power law:
$$ \frac{dN}{dM} \propto M^{\alpha} $$
where the slope $\alpha$ is found to be roughly $-1.7$ to $-2.0$ [Dessauges-Zavadsky et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479L.118D/abstract), [Sok et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250925363S/abstract). This slope is theoretically consistent with models where clumps form *in situ* via [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] driven by turbulence in high-gas-fraction disks [Dessauges-Zavadsky et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479L.118D/abstract). 

The measured physical properties of clumps are highly sensitive to observational **spatial resolution**. While typical non-lensed HST and JWST observations resolve clumps as monolithic kpc-scale structures, strong gravitational lensing studies reveal that these giant clumps exhibit significant sub-structure. When the effective spatial resolution is enhanced to $R \lesssim 100$ pc, clumps are observed to fragment into smaller entities with masses $M_* \lesssim 10^7 M_\odot$ and star formation rates $\lesssim 0.5 M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$. This fragmentation approaches the physical scales of compact stellar complexes and high-redshift [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] [Meštrić et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.516.3532M/abstract). Rest-frame UV spectra of these lensed clumps reveal significant variations in P-Cygni line profiles (such as C IV and Mg II), tracing the complex dynamics of outflows on sub-galaxy scales [Bayliss et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..309..251B/abstract).

Morphological analyses of clump populations reveal distinct radial gradients: clumps located in the galaxy outskirts are typically younger, bluer, and exhibit [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|Unusually high specific star formation rates]] than the older, redder, and more massive clumps found near the galactic center [Soto et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837....6S/abstract), [Guo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...853..108G/abstract). This is often interpreted as evidence for [[clump-migration|Clump migration]], where long-lived clumps spiral inward due to dynamical friction to eventually assemble central [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514...77N/abstract), [Elmegreen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...747..105E/abstract). 

However, the literature remains conflicted over the longevity and true structural nature of these features. Some high-resolution cosmological simulations suggest that many observed UV-bright clumps correspond strictly to "clumpy light"—transient, highly luminous star-forming regions that lack significant underlying stellar mass overdensities. In such models, rapid stellar feedback disperses the clumps on timescales of $\sim 100$ Myr, well before they can migrate inward, rendering their contribution to bulge assembly negligible [Buck et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3628B/abstract). Conversely, recent JWST rest-frame near-infrared observations have confirmed the presence of mature stellar populations in some clumps, supporting their status as genuine mass-bearing substructures rather than purely transient illumination [Mercier et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250613881M/abstract).

## Formation via Violent Disk Instability

The irregular, knotty morphologies of massive [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] at cosmic noon ($z \approx 1-3$) are primarily driven by [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] (VDI) within gas-rich, turbulent disks [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.392..253N/abstract). Unlike local spiral galaxies, which typically possess cold gas fractions of a few percent, high-redshift [[clumpy-disks|Clumpy disks]] exhibit gas fractions approaching $\sim 50\%$ [Garland et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...807..134G/abstract). This massive gas reservoir, combined with continuous smooth accretion, renders the disk susceptible to widespread gravitational fragmentation [Elmegreen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ASPC..419...23E/abstract). The presence of undisrupted rotation in typical $z \sim 3$ galaxies suggests that this gas accretion, rather than [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]], is the dominant mode of mass assembly powering this internal fragmentation [Stark et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455..775S/abstract). Furthermore, simulations of the circumgalactic medium indicate that O VI emission likely traces clumpy, inflowing recycled gas from galactic fountains, providing the fuel necessary to sustain this high-redshift [[clumpy-star-formation|Clumpy star formation]] [Lochhaas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251025844L/abstract).

The stability of these multiphase systems is typically characterized by the Toomre $Q$ parameter. Instabilities arise when the local gas surface density exceeds the critical threshold for fragmentation, overcoming the stabilizing effects of rotation and thermal/turbulent pressure. Observations of intermediate-redshift galaxies ($z \sim 0.6$) indicate that while their isolated gaseous phases might appear marginally stable, the combined dynamic contribution of the stellar and gaseous phases causes the system to become globally unstable [Puech et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..535P/abstract). This instability efficiently fragments the turbulent medium into giant, kpc-scale stellar nurseries.

The mass distribution of these *in situ* formed clumps provides a critical diagnostic for the VDI model. Statistical analyses across multiple deep surveys (such as CANDELS and JADES) demonstrate that the clump stellar mass function follows a power-law distribution:
$$ \frac{dN}{dM} \propto M^{\alpha} $$
where the slope $\alpha$ is consistently measured between $-1.5$ and $-2.0$ [Dessauges-Zavadsky et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479L.118D/abstract), [Zhu et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115965Z/abstract). This slope theoretically mirrors the expectations of turbulence-driven [[gas-clumping|Gas clumping]], yielding typical individual clump masses ranging from $10^7$ to $10^9 M_\odot$ [Zhu et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115965Z/abstract).

Despite the widespread acceptance of the VDI paradigm for $z \gtrsim 2$ galaxies [de la Vega et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250814972D/abstract), the literature reflects active debate regarding the relative importance of internal disk fragmentation versus external triggers like major mergers. While VDI accurately predicts the ubiquitous presence of lower-mass clumps, a high major merger fraction ($\approx 20\%$) is observed for galaxies dominated by two particularly bright and massive clumps (up to $10^{10} M_\odot$), implying that the most massive clumps often have an *ex situ* origin [Ribeiro et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...608A..16R/abstract). Furthermore, because the cumulative merger fraction at early epochs is high, nearly every galaxy at $z \sim 2$ may have experienced a merger within the preceding $1\text{ Gyr}$, significantly complicating the observational distinction between pure VDI and merger-induced clump formation [Puech et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..535P/abstract).

A separate, profound tension exists regarding the physical nature and longevity of these clumps. Traditional VDI models hypothesize that giant clumps are gravitationally bound, long-lived structures that eventually migrate inwards via dynamical friction to form classical bulges. However, high-resolution cosmological simulations present conflicting evidence. Models implementing strong stellar feedback (e.g., the NIHAO simulations) suggest that clumps may be rapidly disrupted on timescales of $\sim 100\text{ Myr}$ [Buck et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3628B/abstract). If true, these substructures predominantly represent "clumpy light"—highly luminous, transient [[active-star-forming-regions|Active star-forming regions]] superimposed on a smoother mass distribution—rather than long-lived "clumpy mass" capable of surviving the migration process. Resolving this tension remains a primary objective of ongoing JWST observations.

## Clump Migration and Bulge Assembly

In the framework of secular evolution, the massive star-forming clumps formed via [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] in high-redshift [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] are hypothesized to be long-lived structures that significantly influence [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514...77N/abstract). Because these individual clumps can encompass a substantial fraction of the host galaxy's mass (typically $10^7$ to $10^9 \ M_\odot$), they are subject to strong gravitational forces. Through dynamical friction against the surrounding disk, dark matter halo particles, and halo stars, as well as tidal torques originating from clump-clump interactions, these massive structures lose angular momentum [Elmegreen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...747..105E/abstract). This loss of angular momentum causes the clumps to spiral inward toward the galactic center on timescales of approximately $1$ to a few Gyr [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514...77N/abstract), [Elmegreen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...747..105E/abstract). 

As the migrating clumps reach the central regions of the galaxy, they coalesce to form or grow a central bulge [Elmegreen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ASPC..419...23E/abstract). This mechanism provides a secular channel for bulge assembly (yielding [[clump-origin-bulges|Clump-origin bulges]]) that contrasts with the traditional textbook model of bulge formation via dissipational [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]]. Hydrodynamical simulations have demonstrated that the inward transport and coalescence of clump matter can generate [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] with steep central density profiles, analogous to "classical" bulges characterized by a Sérsic index $n > 2$ [Perez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436..259P/abstract). This clump-driven evolution is consistent with the inside-out growth paradigm, wherein central proto-bulges assemble rapidly while extended [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] stabilize later [Baker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..141B/abstract). The timeline of this transformation is environmentally dependent; recent Euclid findings reveal that morphological transformation usually precedes quenching in field galaxies, whereas quenching precedes bulge formation in dense cluster environments [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251102964E/abstract). Observations have identified candidate galaxies caught in this precise core formation phase; for instance, GOODS-N-774 represents a massive galaxy three billion years after the Big Bamg displaying gas dynamics consistent with the massive elliptical descendants of clumpy disks [Nelson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.513..394N/abstract).

Observational support for clump migration is primarily derived from radial gradients in clump physical properties. In both high-redshift star-forming systems and their rare local analogs, clumps located in the galaxy outskirts are typically found to be younger, bluer, and exhibit [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|Unusually high specific star formation rates]] [Soto et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837....6S/abstract), [White et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926...32W/abstract). Conversely, clumps situated closer to the galactic center are generally older, redder, and more massive [Soto et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837....6S/abstract), [Guo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...853..108G/abstract). Furthermore, rest-frame optical observations utilizing the James Webb Space Telescope reveal a deficit of young clumps near galaxy centers and a distinct radial transition in the clump size-mass relation. Outer clumps tend to follow a steep, near-virial scaling ($R_e \propto M_*^{0.3}$), while inner clumps exhibit a flatter relation ($R_e \propto M_*^{0.2}$), which is consistent with structural evolution via [[clump-migration|Clump migration]] or selective disruption [Zhu et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115965Z/abstract). 

Despite the observational evidence for these gradients, the migration scenario remains a subject of active debate, hinging primarily on the survival timescale of the clumps. For migration to successfully assemble a bulge, clumps must remain gravitationally bound for several dynamical times. However, strong stellar feedback—such as energy and momentum injected by supernovae and stellar winds—can rapidly expel gas from the clumps. Several high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations suggest that this feedback causes clumps to disperse into the interstellar medium within $\sim 100$ Myr, long before they can migrate to the center [Buck et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3628B/abstract). In this competing view, the observed structures represent transient "clumpy light"—regions of heightened, unobscured star formation—rather than persistent mass concentrations, making their overall contribution to bulge assembly negligible [Buck et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3628B/abstract). Resolving this tension requires determining whether the observed mass and color gradients are exclusively the result of physical migration, or if they instead reflect inside-out disk quenching and the preferential disruption of low-mass clumps by feedback [Sok et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250925363S/abstract), [Zhu et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115965Z/abstract).

## Redshift Evolution and Demographics

The fraction of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] that exhibit a heavily fragmented morphology, denoted $f_{\rm clumpy}$, is strongly tied to the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]]. Clumpiness peaks during the "cosmic noon" epoch at $z \approx 1-3$. Rest-frame ultraviolet observations from the Hubble Space Telescope indicate that $f_{\rm clumpy}$ reaches approximately $50\%-65\%$ at $z \sim 1.5-2$ [Sok et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...924....7S/abstract), [Sattari et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951..147S/abstract). Recent observations using the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), which offer superior sensitivity and rest-frame near-infrared resolution, suggest $f_{\rm clumpy}$ may be as high as $\sim 80\%$ at $z \approx 2.75$ for galaxies with stellar masses $\log(M_\star/M_\odot) \geq 9$ [de la Vega et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250814972D/abstract). In these high-redshift systems, giant clumps typically contribute $4\%-10\%$ of the total host galaxy star formation rate [Guo et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800...39G/abstract) and up to $\sim 20\%$ of the rest-frame ultraviolet luminosity [Soto et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837....6S/abstract), [de la Vega et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250814972D/abstract).

The redshift evolution of $f_{\rm clumpy}$ exhibits a strong dependence on stellar mass, a phenomenon closely linked to the progressive stabilization of galactic disks. [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] ($\log(M_\star/M_\odot) > 10.5$) show a sharp decline in clumpiness over time, dropping from $\sim 55\%$ at $z \sim 3$ to $\sim 15\%$ at $z \sim 0.5$ [Guo et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800...39G/abstract). Conversely, [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] ($\log(M_\star/M_\odot) < 9.8$) maintain a roughly constant clumpy fraction of $\sim 60\%$ over the same redshift range [Guo et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800...39G/abstract). This disparity persists into the intermediate redshift regime ($0.5 \leq z \leq 1$), where low-mass systems continue to exhibit higher clumpy fractions ($\sim 35\%$) compared to their high-mass counterparts ($\sim 22\%$) [Martin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...955..106M/abstract). Morphological studies of dense environments support this secular progression; cluster [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] galaxies at $z \sim 1.25$ are significantly discier than their local analogs, implying they arrive on the sequence as disks that subsequently fade or evolve secularly over time [De Propris et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.1268D/abstract). This mass-dependent evolution is generally attributed to the earlier kinematic stabilization of massive disks, which suppresses the fragmentation predicted by [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] models [Guo et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800...39G/abstract).

Following cosmic noon, $f_{\rm clumpy}$ drops precipitously alongside the global decline in cosmic gas fractions and star formation rates. In the local universe ($0.02 < z < 0.15$), the fraction of star-forming galaxies hosting at least one giant clump is estimated to be between $3.2\%$ and $12.7 house$, depending on the specific flux threshold used to define a clump [Adams et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931...16A/abstract). This sharp decline tightly tracks the cosmic decrease in galaxy turbulence, providing empirical support that local clumpy systems—often studied as analogs to high-redshift galaxies—are driven by similar internal instabilities [Garland et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...807..134G/abstract), [Adams et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931...16A/abstract).

Pushing beyond $z \sim 3$ into the epoch of reionization, $f_{\rm clumpy}$ gradually decreases but remains a significant feature of early [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]]. JWST surveys indicate that $f_{\rm clumpy}$ drops to $\sim 20\%$ at $z > 6.5$ [de la Vega et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250814972D/abstract). Rest-frame near-infrared observations, which trace stellar mass rather than just young starbursts, confirm that the fraction of galaxies hosting massive substructures steadily decreases from $40\%$ at $z = 4$ to $10\%$ at $z = 1$ [Mercier et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250613881M/abstract). Despite the lower overall fraction at extreme redshifts, clumpy star formation remains a critical assembly mode in the early universe, with distinct clumps and compact cores detected in star-forming disks as early as $z > 7$ [Baker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..141B/abstract). While internal violent disk instability is widely accepted as the dominant mechanism driving clump formation at $z \lesssim 6$, the compression of gas during mergers is thought to play an increasingly dominant role in generating clumps at the highest redshifts and lowest stellar masses [de la Vega et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250814972D/abstract). Additionally, theoretical models suggest that low-mass clumpy galaxies at $z > 3$ may possess episodic star formation histories, a notion supported by the relatively shallow slope of their stellar mass function when compared to the UV luminosity function [Lee et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...752...66L/abstract).

## Feedback and Stellar Population Scaling

Observationally, the clump stellar mass function (cSMF) follows a power-law distribution $dN/dM \propto M^{\alpha}$, with the slope $\alpha$ typically measured between $-1.7$ and $-2.0$ [Dessauges-Zavadsky et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479L.118D/abstract), [Zhu et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115965Z/abstract). This slope is theoretically consistent with models of turbulence-driven in situ fragmentation, characteristic of [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] in gas-rich systems. Individual clumps host stellar masses ranging from $10^7$ to $10^9\ M_\odot$, with lensed observations revealing that these structures can fragment into even smaller entities approaching the scales of compact [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] ($R_{\rm eff} < 100$ pc) [Meštrić et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.516.3532M/abstract). The cSMF slope is observed to be dependent on the host galaxy mass and clump age; younger clump populations ($< 300$ Myr) in gas-rich systems exhibit flatter mass functions, while the preferential disruption of low-mass clumps by stellar feedback further flattens the cSMF over time [Sok et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250925363S/abstract).

Detailed photometric studies of high-redshift clumpy disks reveal pronounced radial age gradients. Clumps located in the outskirts of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] tend to be younger, bluer, and exhibit [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|Unusually high specific star formation rates]] [Soto et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837....6S/abstract), [Guo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...853..108G/abstract). Conversely, clumps situated closer to the galactic center are generally older, redder, and more massive. These age gradients are widely interpreted as evidence for [[clump-migration|Clump migration]], where off-center clumps lose angular momentum through dynamical friction and spiral inward to assemble or grow [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] [Soto et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837....6S/abstract), [White et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926...32W/abstract), [Zhu et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115965Z/abstract). However, this migration scenario remains actively debated in the literature. Certain high-resolution hydrodynamic simulations argue that giant clumps undergo rapid disruption due to intense stellar feedback on timescales of $\sim 100$ Myr, rendering them transient "clumpy light" features rather than long-lived mass concentrations capable of reaching the nucleus [Buck et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3628B/abstract). In contrast to high-redshift observations, some studies of clumpy galaxies in the local universe also find no evidence of age or mass gradients as a function of galactocentric distance, further complicating the universality of the inward migration model [Mehta et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912...49M/abstract).

Galactic-scale feedback plays a crucial role in the evolution of clumpy systems. Observations of strongly lensed dusty star-forming systems at $z > 4$ indicate that massive molecular outflows are ubiquitous, and these outflows themselves often display clumpy substructures on 500 pc scales [Spilker et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...905...85S/abstract). Furthermore, when an active galactic nucleus is present, such as in high-redshift radio-loud quasar hosts, quasar jets can drive clumpy, multiphase outflows where the gas outflow rates significantly exceed the host galaxy's global star formation rate [Vayner et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923...59V/abstract). Within the galactic disk, the survival of individual star-forming clumps depends critically on the balance between self-gravity and the disruptive forces of stellar feedback, such as supernova-driven winds and radiation pressure. Direct evidence of this feedback is observed via UV interstellar absorption lines in starbursts, which directly trace superwinds carrying metal-rich gas at velocities comparable to the host galaxy's escape velocity [Heckman et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999AIPC..470..322H/abstract). The intense radiation and winds also heavily influence the local dust geometry; theoretical models show that the standard Calzetti attenuation law can be effectively reproduced by clumpy, shell-type dust distributions directly surrounding these compact starburst regions [Witt et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528..799W/abstract). Tracking this complex feedback via Lyman-alpha emission requires specialized radiative transfer, but models demonstrate that Lyman-alpha spectra from realistic clumpy geometries converge to simpler homogeneous model predictions when the number of clumps along the line of sight is high [Gronke et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833L..26G/abstract). While low-mass clumps are highly susceptible to early destruction, massive clumps ($\sim 10^8\ M_\odot$) may withstand radiative feedback, allowing them to evolve, migrate, and contribute significantly to the secular evolution of the host galaxy [White et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926...32W/abstract), [Sok et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250925363S/abstract).

## Local Analogs and Environmental Influences

While the clumpy morphology dominates [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] at cosmic noon, its prevalence sharply declines over cosmic time. In the local Universe, the fraction of star-forming galaxies hosting at least one giant clump ($f_{\rm clumpy}$) falls to between $\sim 3.2\%$ and $12.7\%$, depending on the relative flux threshold adopted [Adams et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931...16A/abstract). Despite their rarity, local systems such as luminous compact blue galaxies and [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] serve as high-resolution laboratories for studying the microphysics of high-redshift clump formation. These local analogs possess atomic gas fractions of $\sim 50\%$, mimicking the gas-rich, highly turbulent conditions of early disks [Garland et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...807..134G/abstract). Detailed studies of local turbulent disks, such as the DYNAMO survey galaxy G04-1 at $z=0.13$, reveal kpc-scale clumps with masses of $10^7-10^8 M_\odot$ that exhibit radial mass and color gradients consistent with the inward [[clump-migration|Clump migration]] hypothesized for high-$z$ bulge assembly [White et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926...32W/abstract). However, the literature remains conflicted; other large-scale local surveys utilizing Galaxy Zoo classifications find no significant evidence for radial age or mass gradients in $z < 0.06$ clumps, challenging the universality of the migration scenario in the modern epoch [Mehta et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912...49M/abstract).

Although [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] is the favored formation mechanism for clumps at high redshift [Sattari et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951..147S/abstract), kinematic studies suggest that external triggers like [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] and minor interactions become increasingly important drivers of clumpiness at $z < 1$ [Puech et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..535P/abstract). This is particularly evident in local [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|Star-forming dwarf galaxies]] and dwarf irregulars. Rather than funneling gas directly into a compact nuclear starburst, interactions between gas-rich dwarfs frequently trigger widespread, distributed clumpy star formation across the system [Privon et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...74P/abstract). For instance, the interaction in the dwarf-dwarf merger Mrk 709 triggered a $\sim 10$ kpc string of star clusters (with masses $> 10^5 M_\odot$), while the southern galaxy (Mrk 709 S) specifically exhibits a clumpy star formation mode featuring massive clumps of $10^7 - 10^8 M_\odot$ [Kimbro et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912...89K/abstract). Even at high redshift, merger-induced clumps are invoked to explain the most massive sub-galactic structures; systems dominated by two exceptionally bright, massive clumps ($M_* \sim 10^9-10^{10} M_\odot$) are strongly associated with major mergers bringing in ex situ matter, rather than in situ fragmentation [Ribeiro et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...608A..16R/abstract).

The broader environmental dependence of clump formation varies significantly with redshift and mass. At $0.5 \leq z \leq 3$, $f_{\rm clumpy}$ shows no strong correlation with local galaxy overdensity, reinforcing the notion that internal disk physics largely governs high-$z$ clumpiness in the field [Sattari et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951..147S/abstract). Conversely, extreme local environments like [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] provide unique pathways for clump formation. Stripping mechanisms can induce clump formation entirely devoid of an underlying stellar disk, as seen in jellyfish galaxies undergoing intense ram-pressure stripping. High-spatial-resolution observations of GASP jellyfish galaxies reveal ultra-compact, H$\alpha$-bright star-forming clumps embedded directly within the stripped gaseous tails [Gullieuszik et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...54G/abstract). These systems offer a unique extreme environment to probe star formation occurring directly within the hot intracluster medium, demonstrating that while the standard violent disk instability drives clumpiness globally, local perturbations can still trigger violent fragmentation locally. Furthermore, the broader environmental context at high redshift can drastically alter clump visibility; studies show that protocluster groups at $z=1.6$ can suppress star formation and shift its mode from unobscured to dusty before cluster collapse, heavily impacting the observability of clumps in dense early structures [Hatch et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464..876H/abstract).

## Historical Development of the Clumpy Model

In the early study of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]], the irregular, knotty morphologies commonly observed at cosmic noon ($z \approx 1-3$) were widely interpreted through the lens of [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]]. Within the standard framework of hierarchical assembly, galaxies grow by continuously merging with one another, and the chaotic structures seen in distant galaxies were assumed to be the observational signatures of these frequent collisions. While monolithic collapse was invoked to explain the rapid, early formation of smooth elliptical galaxies, merger-driven starbursts remained the default explanation for the disturbed appearances of high-redshift star-forming systems. Under this early paradigm, giant star-forming clumps were viewed primarily as ex-situ components—either incoming [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] or [[protogalaxies|Protogalaxies]].

A major paradigm shift occurred with the introduction of the Noguchi model [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.392..253N/abstract), [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514...77N/abstract). Through numerical simulations of protogalactic collapse, Masafumi Noguchi demonstrated that the peculiar morphologies of high-redshift galaxies did not necessarily require external triggers. Instead, they could arise naturally as an in-situ product of internal disk physics. If a young galactic disk has a sufficiently high gas fraction relative to its total mass, it becomes gravitationally unstable. This instability causes the disk to fragment into discrete, massive clumps, individual masses of which can reach $\sim 10^9 M_\odot$. This internal fragmentation process is now widely recognized as [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]].

The viability of the clumpy disk model over cosmological timescales requires a continuous supply of cold gas. While early models relied on the initial collapse of primordial gas, subsequent theoretical work demonstrated that cold streams from the intergalactic medium violently feed fresh gas to young galaxies, producing massive UV-luminous systems [Devriendt et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.403L..84D/abstract). The theoretical link establishing that smooth gas accretion is necessary to sustain the clumpy model by maintaining high turbulence and instability was explicitly formalized by Elmegreen et al., contrasting sharply with the episodic, short-lived nature of major mergers [Elmegreen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ASPC..419...23E/abstract). Consequently, clumps are recognized as massive, bound star-forming regions native to the disk rather than accreted external bodies.

The recognition of in-situ clump formation profoundly influenced theories regarding the establishment of the Hubble sequence. In the traditional hierarchical model, [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] are primarily assembled via dissipational major mergers. However, the clumpy model provided a secular, internal pathway for bulge formation, leading to what are sometimes called [[clump-origin-bulges|Clump-origin bulges]]. In this scenario, massive clumps interact with one another and the surrounding dark matter halo, losing angular momentum through dynamical friction. This causes them to undergo [[clump-migration|Clump migration]] toward the galactic center, where they coalesce to form a central spheroidal bulge [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.392..253N/abstract), [Elmegreen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ASPC..419...23E/abstract), [Perez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436..259P/abstract). However, the literature remains explicitly conflicted on the efficiency of this process. While early models and some simulations support long-lived clumps that successfully migrate, other high-resolution simulations suggest that strong stellar feedback may disrupt clumps within $\sim 100$ Myr [Buck et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3628B/abstract). If clumps are rapidly destroyed, their contribution to bulge growth would be negligible, representing transient "clumpy light" rather than long-lived mass concentrations [Buck et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3628B/abstract).

Today, the in-situ fragmentation of turbulent, gas-rich disks is widely accepted as the dominant mechanism for clump formation in massive [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] at high redshift [Guo et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800...39G/abstract), [Sattari et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951..147S/abstract). While external mergers are still recognized as important drivers—particularly for triggering starbursts in the local universe or delivering the most massive clumps—the historical development of the clumpy model has firmly established internal disk instabilities and cold-mode accretion as cornerstones of early galaxy assembly.

## Open Questions and the JWST Era

Prior to the *James Webb Space Telescope* (JWST), studies of high-redshift clumpy [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] relied predominantly on rest-frame ultraviolet imaging, which traces unobscured, recent star formation. This observational bias historically missed significant demographics; sub-millimeter observations have revealed a dominant population of optically invisible, massive star-forming galaxies at $z > 3$, representing the bulk of massive galaxy formation missed by traditional optical surveys [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.572..211W/abstract). This bias also fueled a persistent theoretical debate regarding clump survivability: are giant clumps genuine, gravitationally bound mass concentrations ("clumpy mass"), or merely transient, UV-bright starbursts superimposed on a smooth underlying disk ("clumpy light")? Simulations have historically offered conflicting predictions, with some suggesting that clumps disperse within $\sim 100 \text{ Myr}$ due to intense stellar feedback, contributing negligibly to bulge growth [Buck et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3628B/abstract). However, JWST's near-infrared sensitivity has directly detected **rest-frame NIR substructures** at $1\text{--}2\mu\rm m$, confirming that many clumps contain older stellar populations and represent true physical mass overdensities rather than just patchy illumination [Mercier et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250613881M/abstract). 

High-resolution JWST data has also refined the demographics of clumpiness over cosmic time. For galaxies with stellar masses $\log(M_*/M_\odot) \geq 9$, the clumpy fraction ($f_{\rm clumpy}$) peaks at nearly $80\%$ around $z \approx 2.75$ and remains substantial even into the Epoch of Reionization [de la Vega et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250814972D/abstract). Analysis of the clump stellar mass function across $0.5 < z < 5$ reveals a power-law slope of $\alpha \approx -2$, which broadly supports [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] driven fragmentation [Sok et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250925363S/abstract). Despite this, the relative importance of **in-situ vs ex-situ** formation mechanisms remains ambiguous in the literature. While disk instability appears to dominate clump formation in intermediate-to-high mass galaxies at $z \lesssim 6$, morphological analyses suggest that external triggers—such as gas compression during minor and major mergers—may be the primary drivers of clumpiness in low-mass systems at the highest redshifts [de la Vega et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250814972D/abstract), [Mercier et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250613881M/abstract). In support of stable in situ growth, standard morphological statistics derived from the GLASS-JWST survey show no dramatic variations between rest-frame UV and optical wavelengths at $z = 7-12$ [Treu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942L..28T/abstract).

Observations deep into the early Universe reveal that clump-driven galaxy assembly began exceptionally early. JWST has identified luminous galaxies already in place by $z \sim 14$ [Carniani et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240518485C/abstract), alongside morphologically mature systems like JADES-GS+53.18343, which existed within the first $700\text{ Myr}$ of the Universe's history. The latter exhibits a highly compact core ($<100\text{ pc}$), an active disc, and a star-forming clump, providing direct evidence for inside-out growth and proto-bulge formation [Baker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..141B/abstract). Such discoveries challenge textbook models of gradual, merger-dominated assembly, suggesting instead that in-situ clumps play an immediate, critical role in early galactic structural evolution.

A major open question in the JWST era is the exact relationship between clumpy substructures, quenching, and central supermassive black holes. This tension has been highlighted by the discovery of a compact population of galaxies known as **[[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]]** at $z \gtrsim 7$. These objects exhibit both evolved stellar populations and broad emission lines indicative of overmassive active galactic nuclei (AGN) coexisting with early star formation [Loeb et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RNAAS...8..182L/abstract). Theoretical models propose that these systems may represent a low-spin tail of the galaxy population, where a compact, clumpy gaseous disk efficiently feeds both rapid starbursts and a central black hole [Loeb et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RNAAS...8..182L/abstract). However, it remains an active debate whether the inward migration of massive clumps provides the primary fuel for these early AGN, or if clumpy disks simply coexist with secular feeding mechanisms without preferentially triggering nuclear activity. Supporting the latter, X-ray observations have shown nearly indistinguishable AGN fractions between clumpy and smooth galaxies at $z \sim 2$, suggesting that violent disk instabilities and secular processes are equally efficient at fueling black holes [Trump et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793..101T/abstract). Conversely, recent JWST/NIRSpec findings highlight the extreme environments of some AGN hosts, revealing extreme clumpy star formation rates (at least $200 M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$) and high electron densities in the host galaxies of extremely red quasars at $z \sim 3$, alongside powerful ionization cones and shocks [Vayner et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...955...92V/abstract).
