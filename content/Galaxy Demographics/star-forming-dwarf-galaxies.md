**Star-forming dwarf galaxies** are among the most abundant yet physically dynamic stellar systems in the Universe. Broadly defined as gas-rich, [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] (typically $M_\star \lesssim 10^9 M_\odot$) with sub-solar gas-phase metallicities, they serve as pristine astrophysical laboratories for studying galaxy assembly, chemical enrichment, and the mechanics of stellar feedback. Morphologically, this class spans a diverse range of structures, from extended, low-surface-brightness dwarf irregulars to highly concentrated [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCDs) [Papaderos et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996A&A...314...59P/abstract). Unlike [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], which generally maintain steady-state star formation within rotationally supported disks, star-forming dwarfs frequently experience highly stochastic, episodic bursts. These intense periods of star formation are rarely secular; rather, they are typically triggered by external environmental factors such as dwarf-dwarf mergers, tidal interactions, or the direct accretion of cold cosmological gas streams. 

Because of their remarkably shallow gravitational potential wells, the interstellar media of star-forming dwarfs are acutely sensitive to energy and momentum injection. During [[violent-starbursts|Violent starbursts]], the combined mechanical energy from [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] and subsequent supernovae drives powerful galactic winds that regulate star formation and preferentially expel heavy elements before they can cool and mix into the gas disk [De Young et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...431..598D/abstract). This mass-dependent feedback mechanism governs fundamental scaling laws, such as the mass-metallicity relation, which dictates that dwarfs are significantly more metal-depleted than $L^*$ galaxies [Tremonti et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...613..898T/abstract). More recently, demographic studies have revolutionized traditional feedback models by revealing that these low-mass systems frequently host massive [[dwarf-black-holes|Dwarf black holes]]. The emerging evidence suggests that active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback in dwarfs can rival or even exceed stellar feedback in driving metal-enriched outflows and regulating the host's interstellar medium.

The evolutionary trajectory of star-forming dwarfs and their ultimate structural fate remain the subject of active debate. While it is well-established that environmental mechanisms like ram-pressure stripping and tidal harassment quench these systems into passive [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]] when they become [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] in cluster environments, observations of active stripping in highly isolated field dwarfs challenge strictly environment-dependent quenching paradigms. Furthermore, the extreme central starbursts characterizing some dwarfs are intimately linked to the formation of dense nuclear star clusters and the origins of ultra-compact dwarfs (UCDs) left behind after tidal disruption [Drinkwater et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Natur.423..519D/abstract). This article explores the morphological diversity and physical processes governing star-forming dwarfs, detailing their chemical evolution, the environmental drivers of their transformation into [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]], and major open questions—including the universality of the initial mass function in extreme starbursts, the detection of elusive [[wandering-black-holes|Wandering black holes]], and the contribution of bursting dwarfs to multi-messenger high-energy backgrounds.

## Classification and Morphological Diversity

Star-forming dwarf galaxies exhibit significant morphological diversity and are broadly divided into two primary structural classes: gas-rich dwarf irregulars (dIrrs) and [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCDs). While both classes are characterized by low metallicities and ongoing star formation, their spatial distributions of star formation and underlying mass concentrations differ markedly.

Dwarf irregulars are typically extended, low-surface-brightness systems with globally distributed, stochastic, or continuous star formation histories. Their radial light profiles are generally well-described by a simple exponential disk, corresponding to a Sérsic index $n \approx 1$. Furthermore, Bayesian modeling of low-surface-brightness dwarfs indicates that the stellar mass-radius relation for these systems is significantly steeper (slope $\beta \approx 0.4$) than extrapolated canonical models for [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] suggest [Prole et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506L..59P/abstract).

In contrast, BCDs are defined by extraordinarily high, centrally concentrated star formation rates. Deep photometric decompositions reveal that BCDs cannot be fit by a single structural profile. Instead, they require multi-component modeling—typically a two-component Sérsic profile that separates the young, high-surface-brightness nuclear starburst from an older, extended, low-surface-brightness host galaxy [Papaderos et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996A&A...314...59P/abstract), [Chhatkuli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.4953C/abstract). Because the central starburst dominates the optical emission, BCDs exhibit a very young luminosity-weighted age, which observationally masks the mature underlying stellar population [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...685..194S/abstract). 

The structural scaling relations of the underlying hosts in BCDs distinguish them fundamentally from the broader dIrr population. At a given optical luminosity, the host galaxy of a BCD exhibits an exponential scale length approximately a factor of $2$ smaller, and a central surface brightness $\sim 1.5$ magnitudes brighter, than a typical dIrr [Papaderos et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996A&A...314...59P/abstract), [Janowiecki et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793..109J/abstract). Because BCDs possess such distinct, highly concentrated mass distributions, it is considered physically unlikely that standard dIrrs routinely evolve into BCDs or vice versa [Janowiecki et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793..109J/abstract). Instead, BCDs appear to be a long-lived class of compact dwarfs that undergo episodic bursts; statistical comparisons of active and [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] suggest that the quiescent BCD (QBCD) phase lasts roughly $30$ times longer than the active starburst phase [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...685..194S/abstract). Structural parameter comparisons have also been extended to distant environments, such as the Hydra I and Centaurus clusters, further highlighting the morphological nuances between star-forming dwarfs and quiescent dEs/dSphs across varying cluster conditions . Moreover, recent JWST observations have revealed that dwarf galaxies appear smaller at longer wavelengths, providing strong structural evidence for inside-out stellar assembly on short timescales [Jia et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..165J/abstract).

There is an active debate in the literature regarding whether star-forming dwarfs ultimately fade into early-type [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]] (dEs/dSphs) after star formation ceases. Studies of neutral gas dynamics have historically argued against a direct evolutionary link, suggesting that BCDs are heavily rotationally supported and possess too much angular momentum to transform into dynamically hot dEs without a major angular momentum loss mechanism [van Zee et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....122..121V/abstract). Conversely, recent spatially resolved stellar kinematic studies reveal a conflicting picture: the stellar velocity-to-dispersion ratio ($v/\sigma$) of some BCDs is $\sim 1.5$, which is highly consistent with the stellar kinematics of dEs. This suggests that BCDs already share the kinematics of early-type dwarfs and could passively fade into dEs upon gas exhaustion without requiring further dynamical transformation [Koleva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441..452K/abstract). Adding to this complexity, while recent studies of [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] indicate they typically possess lower specific angular momentum than normal star-forming galaxies, implying they must lose further momentum to fully evolve into [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], the exact application of this mechanism to the dwarf regime remains uncertain.

## Triggers of Episodic and Stochastic Star Formation

Star formation in [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] frequently deviates from the steady-state models applicable to massive spirals, instead manifesting as episodic or stochastic bursts. These intense starbursts require the rapid accumulation of large quantities of cold gas into a small central volume. The mechanisms that trigger this process in [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]]—particularly the extreme bursts seen in [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCDs)—are primarily driven by external environmental factors, including dwarf-dwarf mergers, tidal interactions, and gas accretion.

**Dwarf-Dwarf Mergers and Tidal Interactions**
Dwarf-dwarf mergers have been robustly identified as a primary catalyst for intense, centrally concentrated star formation. Deep optical imaging reveals that a significant fraction of strongly bursting dwarfs exhibit extended stellar shells and asymmetrical tidal debris, confirming a merger origin [Chhatkuli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.4953C/abstract). The fraction of dwarf galaxies hosting visible tidal features rises steeply with star formation activity; while the baseline tidal detection rate is $\sim 3.3\%$, it increases to $15\% - 20\%$ for systems with extreme H$\alpha$ equivalent widths ($> 250$ \AA) [Kado-Fong et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....159..103K/abstract). In extreme low-density environments such as void regions, interacting gas-rich dwarf galaxies exhibit a star formation rate enhancement of approximately $3.4$ times compared to isolated single dwarfs [Subramanian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...681A...8S/abstract). Furthermore, star-forming regions in tidally perturbed galaxies tend to exhibit flatter [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] (SFMS) slopes, potentially indicating a lower dependence of star formation on local stellar mass density [Morales-Vargas et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.4370M/abstract).

Spatially resolved kinematics of merging dwarf systems, such as NGC 4809/4810, show that the interacting regions host the highest specific star formation rates and chemically inhomogeneous, metal-poor gas [Gao et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...677A.179G/abstract). In these systems, star formation triggered by the initial interaction often persists long after the final coalescence [Kado-Fong et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....159..103K/abstract). Furthermore, minor mergers—including interactions with predominantly dark matter satellites—can induce strong, stochastic bursts of star formation. Hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate that such encounters can drive star formation rates up by an order of magnitude while simultaneously inducing morphological transformations from disky to spheroidal structures, even in gas-rich systems [Starkenburg et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...587A..24S/abstract).

**Cold Gas Accretion**
For isolated dwarf galaxies lacking massive companions, cold gas accretion from the cosmic web provides a critical mechanism for fueling episodic star formation. Isolated field dwarfs tend to be generally non-clumpy, as their starbursts are likely fed by clumpy cold streams that can disrupt stable disk formation. Instead, highly clumpy morphologies analogous to high-redshift systems are typically found in denser clustered environments, where dwarf galaxies smoothly accrete gas from tidally disrupted companions [Garland et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...807..134G/abstract). Recent models also highlight that continuous circumgalactic medium (CGM) accretion contributes up to 50% of the gas reservoir required for ongoing star formation in these dwarf systems [Tung et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241216440T/abstract). At the faintest extremes of the gas-dominated regime, such as in the Local Group dwarfs Leo T and Leo P, star formation is additionally regulated by the ambient ultraviolet radiation from the [[first-star-formation|First star formation]] event, which dramatically shapes the evolution of their primordial cold gas supplies [Verbeke et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...815...85V/abstract).

**Internal Dynamics and Ignition Thresholds**
While external triggers provide the fuel and angular momentum loss necessary for gas inflow, the underlying structure of the host galaxy dictates the intensity of the resulting burst. Studies of the underlying hosts of BCDs indicate that they possess significantly more concentrated mass distributions—characterized by smaller scale lengths and brighter central surface brightnesses—than typical dwarf irregulars [Janowiecki et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793..109J/abstract). This compact gravitational potential facilitates the rapid central accumulation of gas, allowing the system to cross the threshold for vigorous star formation. Once ignited, these extreme bursts are relatively short-lived ($\sim 10$ Myr) [Chhatkuli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.4953C/abstract), with the galaxy spending the vast majority of its evolutionary timeline—perhaps $30$ times longer—as [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] before the next stochastic burst is triggered [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...685..194S/abstract). Recent analyses have also uncovered a distinct symmetry in structural parameters for galaxies lying above and below the Star-Forming Main Sequence, suggesting that episodic star formation rate variations originate from accretion flow oscillations regulated by the stellar surface density [He et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250506868H/abstract).

## Chemical Evolution and Scaling Relations

Star-forming dwarf galaxies serve as pristine laboratories for understanding sub-solar [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|Galaxy chemical enrichment mechanisms]]. Because of their shallow gravitational potential wells, [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] are highly susceptible to feedback mechanisms that drive galactic winds, which profoundly shape their chemical evolution. 

This physical reality underpins the gas-phase Mass-Metallicity Relation (MZR), a tight empirical scaling law spanning multiple orders of magnitude in stellar mass. Observations of the local Universe demonstrate that low-mass dwarfs are approximately $5$ times more metal-depleted than $L^*$ galaxies [Tremonti et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...613..898T/abstract). The MZR is relatively steep in the mass range of $10^{8.5}$ to $10^{10.5} M_\odot$, reflecting the efficiency with which mass-dependent outflows successfully remove newly synthesized metals from the shallow potential wells of dwarf systems [Tremonti et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...613..898T/abstract). Structurally, the interplay between their potential, H I line width, and surface brightness forms a distinct "dwarf fundamental plane," indicating that the predominantly disordered gas motions in dwarfs are fundamentally governed by gravity rather than internal feedback alone [McCall et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...540A..49M/abstract).

While primordial dwarfs tightly adhere to the MZR, distinct classes of outliers exist. [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCDs) are notably metal-poor for their luminosity, whereas tidal dwarf galaxies—formed from the pre-enriched tidally stripped debris of major gas-rich disc-disc interactions—remain anomalously metal-rich outliers significantly above the canonical relation [Grebel et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AIPC.1480..172G/abstract), [Dumont et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.2866D/abstract). Alternative chemical evolution models argue that ancient tidal dwarf galaxies formed from initially very metal-poor gas would naturally build up and align with the canonical MZR over time [Recchi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.2367R/abstract). The MZR is also sensitive to environmental density; comparisons of star-forming dwarfs in the Fornax and Hydra clusters to those in the Virgo cluster reveal subtle differences in their chemical enrichment histories depending on the specific cluster overdensity [Vaduvescu et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...533A..65V/abstract).

Recent JWST observations have extended constraints on the MZR to cosmic noon ($z \sim 2-3$) for dwarfs with stellar masses down to $M_* \approx 10^{6.5} M_\odot$. In this low-mass regime, the MZR exhibits a shallower slope below a turnover point of $M_* \approx 10^9 M_\odot$, suggesting that the dominant feedback processes in the smallest galaxies may scale differently than those in [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...955L..18L/abstract). Furthermore, these high-redshift dwarfs conform to the Fundamental Metallicity Relation (FMR)—a three-dimensional plane connecting gas-phase metallicity, stellar mass, and star formation rate. The FMR for dwarf galaxies shows no significant redshift evolution from $z=3$ to the present day, indicating that the self-regulated baryonic cycling governing the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] has been operating consistently for over 10 billion years [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...955L..18L/abstract).

Detailed chemical abundances, particularly $[\alpha/\rm Fe]$ ratios and spatial metallicity gradients, provide further constraints on the enrichment histories of dwarfs. High-resolution near-infrared spectroscopy from APOGEE shows that $\alpha$-element distributions in Milky Way satellites correlate strongly with host luminosity, confirming the dominant role of mass in shaping chemical enrichment [Xu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251106820X/abstract). The integrated galactic initial mass function (IGIMF) theory predicts that the characteristically low global star formation rates in dwarfs lead to a steeper initial mass function, resulting in lower $[\alpha/\rm Fe]$ plateaus compared to more massive galaxies like the Milky Way [Recchi et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.437..994R/abstract). Additionally, analysis of N/O ratios versus O/H metallicity in star-forming dwarfs provides a powerful diagnostic for galaxy assembly, as nitrogen and oxygen are ejected on different timescales by intermediate and massive stars [Stephenson et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023RNAAS...7...31S/abstract).

Internal chemical homogeneity is strongly influenced by stellar mass; radial gas-phase metallicity gradients show a significant negative correlation with stellar mass, with lower-mass systems exhibiting flatter or even positive gradients (a central suppression of metallicity) compared to their more massive counterparts [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250417541L/abstract). This implies that metal mixing and transport processes—such as feedback-driven outflows—are more important than localized in-situ enrichment in shaping the metallicity distribution of the lowest-mass galaxies [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250417541L/abstract). Conversely, Ca-by photometry reveals that some [[spheroidal-galaxies|Spheroidal Galaxies]], such as [[sextans-dwarf-spheroidal-galaxy|Sextans Dwarf Spheroidal Galaxy]] and Canes Venatici I, host centrally concentrated metal-rich populations, resulting in negative radial metallicity gradients that contradict earlier spectroscopic results [Han et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..247....7H/abstract). In addition to spatial gradients, dwarf galaxies show a distinct correlation between their mean metallicity and intrinsic metallicity spread; this distinct chemical signature can be utilized to successfully separate dwarf galaxies from [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] even without prior knowledge of their mass [Leaman et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....144..183L/abstract). At the faintest extremes, chemical enrichment in ultra-faint dwarfs (UFDs) often follows a 'one-shot' event pattern where only a single stellar generation forms after the initial [[first-star-formation|First star formation]] events, preserving the nucleosynthetic fossil record of the first stars [Frebel et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759..115F/abstract).

## Feedback Mechanisms and the Interstellar Medium

In [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] with low total masses (typically $M_\star < 10^9 M_\odot$), the shallow gravitational potential wells make the interstellar medium (ISM) highly susceptible to kinetic and thermal energy injection. The classical paradigm for regulating star formation in these environments relies heavily on supernova feedback. Direct measurements indicate that star-forming dwarfs host a substantial total supernova rate of approximately $4.0$ per millennium, providing a steady baseline of kinetic energy injection across the galaxy [Chakrabarti et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...863L...1C/abstract). During intense bursts of star formation, the combined mechanical energy from the stellar winds of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] and subsequent supernova explosions creates expanding superbubbles. If the kinetic energy exceeds the local binding energy of the ISM, these bubbles can rupture and drive galaxy-scale galactic winds. Hydrodynamical models demonstrate that while a complete "blowaway" of the entire ISM is relatively rare, localized "blowouts" along the minor axis are common, allowing hot, metal-enriched gas to readily escape into the circumgalactic medium [De Young et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...431..598D/abstract).

The efficiency of these supernova-driven winds plays a critical role in the chemical evolution of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]]. Observational scaling relations, such as the mass-metallicity relation, indicate that mass-loss rates are strongly anti-correlated with baryonic mass. Due to their weaker gravity, dwarf galaxies are significantly more efficient at ejecting heavy elements than more massive systems; for instance, star-forming dwarfs can be up to five times more metal-depleted than typical $L^*$ galaxies [Tremonti et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...613..898T/abstract). This severe metal depletion arises because galactic winds preferentially expel the freshly synthesized, metal-enriched supernova ejecta before it can fully cool and mix with the colder, neutral ISM. Consequently, feedback-driven mixing dictates internal abundance patterns, often flattening internal metallicity gradients which show a negative correlation with stellar mass [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250417541L/abstract). 

Beyond traditional supernova models, other energetic stellar byproducts also influence the ISM. The intense, hard radiation fields characteristic of these feedback environments result in a significant deficit of $8.0\ \mu\text{m}$ polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) emission in dwarfs compared to massive galaxies [Hong et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...717..503H/abstract). Furthermore, the high-mass X-ray binary (HMXB) luminosity function in dwarf galaxies exhibits a shallower power-law slope and significant stochastic sampling at high luminosities compared to massive galaxies, injecting additional variable high-energy feedback [Geda et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...965...67G/abstract). Feedback processes may also drive significant magnetogenesis; theoretical models suggest that a cosmic-ray-driven dynamo can generate $\mu$G-scale ordered magnetic fields in star-forming dwarf galaxies, despite their characteristically slow rotation [Siejkowski et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...562A.136S/abstract).

While supernova feedback has traditionally been considered the sole driver of gas removal in dwarfs, emerging evidence points to the substantial role of [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]] (AGN) feedback. The recent discovery of [[dwarf-black-holes|Dwarf black holes]] in low-mass galaxies has prompted a re-evaluation of feedback models. Observations of [[active-dwarf-galaxies|Active dwarf galaxies]] reveal that AGN-driven outflows are significantly more energetic than those driven purely by star formation. Compared to starburst-driven winds, AGN outflows exhibit higher blueshifts, greater mass-loss rates, and higher momentum fluxes [Aravindan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...950...33A/abstract). Furthermore, radio jets from AGNs in dwarfs can be remarkably powerful, reaching jet efficiencies of $>10\%$ and injecting mechanical power on the order of $10^{42}$ to $10^{44} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$ into the ISM [Mezcua et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488..685M/abstract).

The relative dominance of AGN versus stellar feedback remains a subject of active debate in the literature. Analytical frameworks suggest there is a critical halo mass threshold below which AGN feedback can unbind the host's gas more efficiently than supernovae, providing a potent source of negative feedback even in the dwarf regime [Dashyan et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.5698D/abstract). Conversely, star-formation-driven outflows are often found to be more spatially extended than their AGN counterparts, affecting a larger volume of the ISM [Aravindan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...950...33A/abstract). It is increasingly likely that a combination of both feedback mechanisms regulates the ISM, driving metal-enriched outflows and ultimately dictating the structural evolution of star-forming dwarf galaxies.

## Environmental Influence and Pre-processing

The cessation of star formation in [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] is almost exclusively an environmentally driven process. For systems with a stellar mass $M_{\star} < 10^9 M_{\odot}$, quenched field galaxies are virtually non-existent, constituting less than $0.06\%$ of the isolated dwarf population [Geha et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757...85G/abstract). Consequently, the transition of gas-rich [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] into [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] requires the presence of a massive host halo or a dense local environment to facilitate gas removal and suppress star formation. 

This environmental dependence manifests as a well-defined morphology-density relation: gas-rich dwarf irregulars populate the field, while gas-poor dwarf ellipticals and spheroidals cluster heavily around massive hosts [Weisz et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...743....8W/abstract). Upon entering a denser environment, [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] are subjected to a combination of strangulation (the truncation of cosmological gas accretion), ram-pressure stripping (the removal of cold interstellar gas via interaction with the intracluster medium), and tidal harassment (repeated rapid gravitational encounters that dynamically heat the galaxy and alter its morphology) [Sabatini et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.357..819S/abstract), [Venhola et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...625A.143V/abstract). Often, a substantial fraction of dwarf satellites may enter these massive halos not individually but as part of smaller groups, such as the [[large-magellanic-cloud-lmc|Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC)]] group. This group infall mechanism naturally explains planar orbital configurations and the non-stochastic distribution of satellites observed around massive hosts [D'Onghia et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IAUS..256..473D/abstract). In the most extreme cases, environmental pre-processing can occur remarkably early; it is theorized that tidal shocks and rapid ram-pressure stripping removed gas from the progenitors of ultra-faint dwarfs up to 10 billion years ago, permanently halting star formation and creating the heavily dark-matter-dominated satellites observed in modern galactic halos [Mayer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Natur.445..738M/abstract).

Observations of the Virgo cluster provide a canonical laboratory for these physical processes. For instance, star-forming dwarfs in the central regions of Virgo exhibit anomalously high [C II] to total infrared continuum ratios compared to their field counterparts. This [C II] excess is strongly correlated with the local ram pressure felt by the galaxy, suggesting that ram-pressure shocks actively excite [C II] emission during the stripping process [Minchin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AJ....164...44M/abstract). Complementing these cluster observations, semi-analytic modeling of satellite systems orbiting Centaurus A reinforces the universality of star formation and quenching processes in dwarf galaxies across vastly different massive host halos [Weerasooriya et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968...78W/abstract). Furthermore, detailed star formation histories of transitioning dwarf lenticular galaxies (dS0s) reveal two distinct bursts of star formation, with recent episodes likely representing temporary rejuvenations triggered by minor mergers  or late gas accretion prior to their final quenching in a dense environment [Ann et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530..210A/abstract).

However, environmental quenching is not restricted to the cluster core; galaxies undergo significant pre-processing in large-scale filamentary structures prior to virialization. Spectroscopic studies of filaments feeding the Virgo cluster (such as the Virgo III filament) reveal populations of star-forming galaxies that already exhibit suppressed specific star formation rates and enhanced gas-phase metallicities. This chemical pre-processing is likely driven by local dwarf-dwarf interactions and local overdensities within the filament [Chung et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923..235C/abstract). Furthermore, major mergers in cluster outskirts, such as the coalesced system VCC 479, demonstrate that the combined effects of environmental gas stripping and tidal interactions can rapidly drive a dwarf's remaining gas to its nucleus, triggering a central starburst and transforming late-type dwarfs into transitioning "blue-core" systems [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250615268S/abstract). In fact, approximately $10\%$ of Virgo early-type dwarfs exhibit such blue, metal-enriched cores, likely representing recent central star formation (within the last few $100$ Myr) right before complete environmental quenching takes hold [Urich et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...606A.135U/abstract).

Despite the strong consensus that ram-pressure stripping is primarily a cluster- or group-specific mechanism, recent observations present a direct challenge to this paradigm. The isolated dwarf galaxy WLM, located approximately $900$ kpc from both the Milky Way and the Andromeda galaxy, displays trailing extended H I gas clouds and spatial offsets between its gas and stellar components that are uniquely characteristic of active ram-pressure stripping [Yang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...660L..11Y/abstract). This tension in the literature indicates that ram pressure from diffuse intergalactic gaseous reservoirs may occasionally influence the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] of ostensibly isolated field dwarfs, a phenomenon not fully accounted for in standard models of secular dwarf galaxy evolution.

## Massive Black Holes and Nuclear Activity

Historically, massive black holes (MBHs) and intermediate-mass black holes were presumed to reside almost exclusively within the bulges of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]]. However, recent demographic studies demonstrate a paradigm shift: [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] in the dwarf regime ($M_\star \lesssim 10^{9.5}\ M_\odot$) frequently host these compact objects. High-resolution cosmological simulations predict a black hole occupation fraction of at least 50% for dwarf galaxies with stellar masses down to $10^6\ M_\odot$ [Beckmann et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.5610B/abstract). The presence of an active galactic nucleus in these systems correlates strongly with host morphology; whereas non-active dwarfs are often pure disk galaxies, dwarf AGN hosts almost ubiquitously possess detectable pseudo-bulges and highly luminous central point sources [Kimbrell et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958..115K/abstract). 

A major observational challenge in constraining the true dwarf AGN population is the spatial displacement of their black holes. Following dwarf-dwarf mergers, dynamic friction is highly inefficient in the shallow potential wells of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]]. Consequently, approximately 50% of MBHs in dwarf galaxies do not sink to the galactic center within a Hubble time, instead existing as [[wandering-black-holes|Wandering black holes]] located several kiloparsecs from the nucleus [Bellovary et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.2913B/abstract), [Bellovary et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.5129B/abstract). Recent observations confirm this wandering population: based on eROSITA centroids, approximately $50\%$ of accreting massive black holes in local dwarf galaxies are identified as off-nuclear, consistent with these theoretical predictions [Sacchi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240601707S/abstract). Because these off-center MBHs lack access to the dense [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]] required for sustained feeding, their accretion luminosities remain extremely low. This displacement largely explains why current theoretical models underpredict the observed AGN luminosity function when only centrally-located black holes are considered [Beckmann et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.5610B/abstract).

Despite these low average accretion rates, high-energy surveys successfully identify nuclear activity in a subset of the population. Using eROSITA X-ray data, the fraction of nearby dwarfs hosting an AGN accreting at $\gtrsim 1\%$ of the Eddington limit is estimated to have an upper bound of $\le 1.8\%$, characterized by X-ray luminosities of $L_{\rm 0.5-8\ keV} \sim 10^{39}-10^{40}$ erg s$^{-1}$ [Latimer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922L..40L/abstract). Conversely, multi-wavelength spectral energy distribution fitting suggests that up to $\sim 32\%$ of certain dwarf populations exhibit radiatively efficient AGN signatures [Bichang'a et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532..613B/abstract). However, the interpretation of some accretion signatures warrants caution; recent GMOS-IFU observations of dwarf black hole candidates suggest that highly ionized features like [Fe X] emission lines may be transient, possibly linked to past tidal disruption events rather than continuous active accretion [Heckler et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.2111H/abstract). In extreme cases, active MBHs of $\sim 10^5\ M_\odot$ have been identified even in very young, metal-poor systems (such as SBS 0335-052E) via infrared temporal flux variability and exceptionally broad H$\alpha$ emission components ($v \sim 1.24 \times 10^4$ km s$^{-1}$) [Hatano et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230403726H/abstract).

The discovery of active MBHs in these environments fundamentally alters models of [[dwarf-galaxy-physical-processes|Dwarf Galaxy Physical Processes]]. It is actively debated whether gas removal and quenching are driven exclusively by stellar feedback or if AGN feedback plays a dominant role. Observational comparisons reveal that AGN-driven outflows in dwarf galaxies exhibit significantly higher blueshifts and kinetic energy rates than star-formation-driven outflows [Aravindan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...950...33A/abstract). Furthermore, a subset of dwarf galaxies has been found to host powerful radio jets with mechanical powers ranging from $Q_{\rm jet} \sim 10^{42}$ to $10^{44}$ erg s$^{-1}$ and jet efficiencies exceeding $10\%$, comparable to massive radio galaxies [Mezcua et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488..685M/abstract). Theoretical models corroborate that in the most massive dwarf halos, an AGN provides a more successful source of negative feedback than supernovae, capable of unbinding the interstellar medium and fundamentally regulating the overall [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] [Dashyan et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.5698D/abstract).

## Nuclear Star Clusters and Compact Stellar Systems

Nuclear star clusters (NSCs) represent some of the densest known [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] in the Universe and are commonly found at the photometric and kinematic centers of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]]. In star-forming dwarfs, these highly concentrated stellar systems typically possess stellar masses in the range of $M_{\rm NSC} \approx 6 \times 10^4 - 6 \times 10^6 M_\odot$ and effective radii of $r_h \sim 5$ pc [Fahrion et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...667A.101F/abstract). 

The assembly of NSCs in dwarf galaxies operates through two primary, host-mass-dependent channels. In the lowest-mass regime ($M_{\rm gal} < 10^9 M_\odot$), nuclear clusters are thought to form predominantly via the orbital decay and subsequent merger of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] driven by dynamical friction [Oh et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...543..620O/abstract), [Fahrion et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...667A.101F/abstract). Spectroscopic observations of these specific systems often reveal old, metal-poor stellar populations that are consistent with a globular cluster origin. However, globular cluster populations are highly stochastic in the low-mass regime; approximately 50% of dwarf galaxies with stellar masses below $2 \times 10^7 M_\odot$ lack massive clusters entirely, complicating this formation pathway for the faintest dwarfs [Chen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.5638C/abstract). Recent Keck/KCWI spectroscopy of globular cluster candidates in local volume dwarf galaxies like NGC 247 and DDO 190 has confirmed the presence of both expected old, metal-poor populations and unexpectedly young clusters, highlighting the highly stochastic nature of cluster formation in small galaxies [Forbes et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PASA...41...44F/abstract).

In more massive dwarf galaxies, central gas inflows can trigger in-situ star formation. This active star-forming mechanism contributes significantly to the mass growth of the NSC, resulting in the presence of younger, more chemically enriched stellar populations within the galactic nucleus [Fahrion et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...667A.101F/abstract). To explain the observed spread in internal cluster kinematics and chemistry within these dynamic environments, recent models propose the "Celestial Buffet" hypothesis, suggesting that globular clusters form centrally during intense starbursts and are subsequently pumped to wider orbital radii by fluctuating galactic potentials driven by stellar feedback [Maxwell et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.439.2043M/abstract). In dense cluster environments, the relationship between dwarf galaxies and compact stellar systems is further illuminated by recent Euclid early release observations in the Perseus cluster. These observations show that ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs) host $3-4$ times more globular clusters than non-UDGs at a given stellar mass, suggesting that UDGs possess fundamentally distinct dark matter halo properties and assembly histories compared to standard dwarfs [Saifollahi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250316367S/abstract).

The formation and evolution of NSCs are intimately linked to the origin of ultra-compact dwarf galaxies (UCDs). UCDs are extraordinarily dense stellar systems that bridge the parameter space between massive globular clusters and [[compact-galaxies|Compact Galaxies]], featuring half-light radii of $r_h \approx 10 - 100$ pc and stellar masses of $M_* \approx 10^6 - 10^8 M_\odot$ [Hilker et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..5447H/abstract), [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Natur.623..296W/abstract). Recent large-scale surveys of cluster environments, such as the Virgo and Fornax clusters, have revealed a continuous morphological transition from nucleated dwarf galaxies to isolated UCDs [Drinkwater et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Natur.423..519D/abstract), [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Natur.623..296W/abstract). This continuum provides strong observational evidence that many UCDs are the remnant nuclei of tidally stripped [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]]. As the progenitor dwarf galaxy falls into a dense cluster environment, its extended dark matter halo and outer stellar envelope are systematically removed by tidal forces and ram-pressure stripping, ultimately leaving behind only the robust nuclear star cluster [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Natur.623..296W/abstract).

Despite their morphological similarities to massive globular clusters, UCDs exhibit physical properties that challenge standard stellar population models. Notably, the literature is currently in tension regarding their internal kinematics: the dynamical mass-to-light ratios ($\Upsilon$) of UCDs are frequently observed to be significantly elevated compared to typical clusters of the same metallicity:
$$ \Upsilon_{\rm UCD} \approx 2 \Upsilon_{\rm GC} $$
This elevated ratio of approximately a factor of two [Hilker et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009RvMA...21..199H/abstract) presents a physical anomaly, and the driver of this excess mass remains a subject of active debate [Dabringhausen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..440..261D/abstract). While some theoretical models suggest that UCDs may retain a tightly bound fraction of their progenitor's central dark matter, an alternative explanation posits that the extreme gas densities present during the UCD's central starburst may have induced a top-heavy [[stellar-mass-function|Stellar Mass Function]] (IMF). A top-heavy IMF would naturally account for the "missing" dynamical mass by producing an overabundance of massive stellar remnants—such as neutron stars and [[dwarf-black-holes|Dwarf black holes]]—without requiring the presence of a dark matter component [Dabringhausen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..440..261D/abstract).

## Evolution of Theoretical Frameworks

The theoretical understanding of star-forming dwarf galaxies has undergone a profound shift over the past few decades. Early models of galaxy formation often relied on the monolithic collapse scenario, which posited that galaxies formed in a single, high-redshift burst of intense star formation followed by a lifetime of passive evolution. Under this framework, researchers proposed the "fading dwarf hypothesis" to explain the origin of low-mass early-type systems [Davies et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989Ap&SS.157..291D/abstract). In this evolutionary sequence, a gas-rich dwarf irregular undergoes a period of vigorous star formation—appearing observationally as one of the [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCDs)—before depleting its primordial gas reservoir and passively fading into the [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]] we observe locally.

However, the fading dwarf hypothesis faced significant observational and theoretical challenges. Studies of intermediate-redshift [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] revealed that their gas-phase metallicities were too high for them to simply fade into the metal-poor dwarf populations of the local universe, lending more support to "downsizing" models of galaxy evolution rather than a fading dwarf picture [Lilly et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...597..730L/abstract). Furthermore, the morphological transformation from a rotation-supported, star-forming dwarf to a pressure-supported elliptical system requires structural and dynamical changes that simple fading cannot explain. The literature remains explicitly conflicted on whether a bursting BCD can dynamically transition into a dwarf elliptical. Some gas dynamics studies argue that while BCDs possess lower specific angular momenta than typical diffuse dwarfs, they remain heavily rotationally supported and still require a significant loss of angular momentum to match the kinematics of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. Conversely, more recent full-spectrum fitting of stellar kinematics suggests that BCDs already exhibit stellar velocity-to-dispersion ratios ($v/\sigma \sim 1.5$) matching those of dwarf ellipticals, implying they could theoretically fade into early-type dwarfs without shedding angular momentum [Koleva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441..452K/abstract). To reconcile dynamical differences, theoretical models have demonstrated that baryonic mass loss triggered by stellar feedback during the galaxy's initial collapse can effectively reduce the asymptotic velocity dispersion of dwarf spheroidals, allowing them to match the values observed in the Local Group [Gritschneder et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...765...38G/abstract).

With the establishment of the $\Lambda$CDM cosmological paradigm, monolithic collapse was largely supplanted by hierarchical clustering. In this framework, dwarf galaxies act as the fundamental building blocks of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], and their evolution is dictated heavily by their surroundings rather than isolated, internal gas depletion [Carraro et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..253C/abstract). Interestingly, despite early theoretical assumptions, studies of ancient stellar populations in Local Group dwarfs show no evidence of a widespread, dominant quenching effect strictly from cosmic reionization, indicating that internal gas dynamics and later environmental interactions play the more definitive role in terminating their global star formation [Grebel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...610L..89G/abstract). Supporting this merger-driven assembly, recent hydrodynamical simulations from the Marvelous Massive Dwarfs suite demonstrate that rotation-supported stellar disks in dwarf galaxies are often formed via mergers with high orbital angular momentum satellites, rather than exclusively through smooth gas accretion [Geda et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251026875G/abstract). 

A major paradigm shift regarding morphological transformation was driven by the observation that below a stellar mass threshold of $M_{\star} < 10^9 M_{\odot}$, fully [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] are virtually nonexistent in the field (accounting for $<0.06\%$ of the population) [Geha et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757...85G/abstract). This firmly established a critical mass threshold between $10^8$ and $10^9 M_\odot$, below which environment-driven quenching becomes the decisively dominant factor in galaxy evolution [Romero-Gómez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.9715R/abstract). Rather than passively fading in isolation, gas-rich dwarfs require the proximity of massive [[host-galaxies|Host galaxies]] to facilitate gas removal via ram-pressure stripping or tidal harassment. 

While the necessity of environmental influence for quenching low-mass [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] is now well established, the physical boundaries of these environments are currently being re-evaluated. Standard models associate ram-pressure stripping strictly with the dense intra-cluster or intra-group medium. However, recent observations have detected trailing gas clouds and spatial offsets indicative of ram-pressure stripping in the completely isolated dwarf WLM, located roughly $900$ kpc from the nearest massive hosts [Yang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...660L..11Y/abstract). Such discoveries challenge the strict dichotomy between cluster and field mechanisms, suggesting that intergalactic gas reservoirs can drive the morphological transformation of dwarf galaxies well outside of standard virial radii.

## Extreme Environments: Ultra-Diffuse and Metal-Poor Dwarfs

At the extremes of the galaxy parameter space lie [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] that act as local laboratories for understanding primordial interstellar medium physics and star formation. These extreme environments are broadly characterized either by extraordinarily low stellar mass surface densities, as seen in ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs), or by exceptionally low heavy-element abundances, typical of ultra-metal-poor [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCDs).

Although originally identified in cluster environments as passively evolving objects, a population of H I-rich, star-forming UDGs exists in the field. These systems possess substantial atomic gas reservoirs but exhibit unusually low star formation efficiencies as a function of their atomic gas mass, a trend that persists down to spatial scales of $\sim 500$ pc [Kado-Fong et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...11K/abstract). Their low star formation rate surface densities ($\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$) can be naturally explained within the pressure-regulated, feedback-modulated (PRFM) theory of star formation: the diffuse structure of UDGs results in low midplane pressures, which severely suppresses the conversion of H I to molecular hydrogen (${\rm H_2}$) [Kado-Fong et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...939..101K/abstract). Consequently, these galaxies are heavily ${\rm H_2}$-poor compared to the general dwarf population. While some kinematic models of specific gas-rich UDGs like WALLABY J125956-192430 (KK176) place them squarely on the standard baryonic Tully-Fisher relation, indicating they are normal dark matter-dominated systems [Dudley et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250915340D/abstract), broader surveys of H I-selected ultra-diffuse galaxies reveal significant offsets from this relation compared to normal dwarfs, underscoring their vast structural heterogeneity [Šiljeg et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...692A.217S/abstract). Beyond UDGs, ultra-faint dwarf galaxies also display remarkable structural extensions; recent observations reveal that at least $33\%$ of ultra-faint dwarf galaxies containing RR Lyrae populations possess extended stellar halos that reach beyond four times their half-light radius [Tau et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167...57T/abstract).

At the opposite structural extreme are highly concentrated, ultra-metal-poor systems such as [[i-zw-18|I Zw 18]] and SBS 0335-052E. Often termed "living fossils," these [[low-metallicity-galaxies|Low-metallicity galaxies]] host gas-phase metallicities ranging from $1/3$ down to $\sim 1/40 \ Z_\odot$, making them the most metal-deficient star-forming galaxies known in the local Universe [Thuan et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..255..348T/abstract). Because of their nearly pristine interstellar media, they serve as critical test-beds for understanding [[first-star-formation|First star formation]] and stellar evolution prior to substantial cosmic chemical enrichment [Izotov et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ASPC..353..349I/abstract). The lack of metals alters local cooling pathways, resulting in higher typical gas temperatures and influencing the fragmentation of molecular clouds.

Star formation in these extremely metal-poor environments yields distinct stellar populations. Photometric studies of nearby metal-poor dwarfs reveal that [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] in these environments are frequently extreme rotators, with OBe star fractions reaching $20\%-30\%$ in environments with $0.1-0.5 \ Z_\odot$ [Schootemeijer et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221001453S/abstract). Furthermore, some of these supposedly pristine "nascent" galaxies have been found to host active [[dwarf-black-holes|Dwarf black holes]]. For example, infrared and spectroscopic variability in the metal-poor dwarf SBS 0335-052E suggests the presence of an accreting massive black hole of $\sim 10^5 \ M_\odot$ [Hatano et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230403726H/abstract). This highlights that even the most metal-deficient and structurally unevolved dwarfs can harbor complex, multi-messenger feedback mechanisms, wherein both vigorous stellar winds and active galactic nuclei regulate the assembly of their stellar mass.

## Open Questions and Future Observational Frontiers

Despite decades of intensive study, the evolutionary destiny of star-forming dwarf galaxies remains a subject of active debate. While environmental mechanisms like ram-pressure stripping are widely accepted drivers of quenching in dense cluster regions, recent observations of ram-pressure stripping in the isolated dwarf WLM challenge the paradigm that such processes are strictly confined to massive host environments [Yang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...660L..11Y/abstract). Furthermore, the literature exhibits tension regarding whether [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCDs) can directly transition into passive dwarf ellipticals. Some kinematic studies indicate that BCDs possess stellar velocity-to-dispersion ratios ($v/\sigma \approx 1.5$) comparable to dwarf ellipticals, suggesting they can fade into early-type systems without requiring significant angular momentum loss [Koleva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441..452K/abstract), a finding that directly contradicts earlier evolutionary models based solely on gas kinematics. Additionally, the kinematic signatures of transitioning galaxies continue to puzzle observers; for instance, the detection of prolate rotation in transitioning dwarfs like Phoenix serves as a unique indicator of past interactions or distinct accretion events [Kacharov et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.2006K/abstract).

A rapidly evolving frontier is the demographic study of massive black holes in the low-mass galaxy regime. Hydrodynamical simulations indicate that massive black holes in dwarf galaxies frequently occupy off-center orbits as [[wandering-black-holes|Wandering black holes]] following dwarf-dwarf mergers [Bellovary et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.2913B/abstract), [Bellovary et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.5129B/abstract). Because their accretion luminosities are typically low, they remain observationally elusive. However, theoretical models suggest that active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback in dwarfs may rival or exceed stellar feedback in driving gas outflows, possessing sufficient energy to unbind the interstellar medium entirely [Dashyan et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.5698D/abstract). Upcoming wide-field X-ray surveys are poised to revolutionize this subfield; extrapolations from the eROSITA Final Equatorial Depth Survey (eFEDS) predict that the full-sky eROSITA survey will identify approximately 1,350 AGN candidates in local dwarf galaxies, drastically expanding the known population of accreting [[dwarf-black-holes|Dwarf black holes]] [Latimer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922L..40L/abstract). While active nuclei can be identified through these high-energy signatures, finding inactive black holes requires precise kinematic measurements. Looking forward, the planned Habitable Worlds Observatory holds the potential to dynamically detect quiescent massive black holes with masses as low as $10^{4.5} M_\odot$ in nearby dwarf galaxies by spatially resolving their central stellar velocity dispersions [Pacucci et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250701523P/abstract).

Extending dwarf galaxy studies to earlier cosmic epochs represents another critical observational frontier. With the advent of JWST, it is now possible to spatially resolve and spectroscopically characterize high-redshift analogs of local dwarf galaxies during Cosmic Noon ($z \sim 2-3$). Recent JWST/NIRISS observations have successfully traced the mass-metallicity relation in dwarf galaxies down to stellar masses of $M_\star \approx 10^{6.5} M_\odot$ [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...955L..18L/abstract). These data reveal a turnover and shallower slope in the relation below $10^9 M_\odot$, implying that the dominant feedback mechanisms operating in early, low-mass systems may differ substantially from those operating in [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]]. Importantly, JWST has confirmed that the fundamental metallicity relation—connecting stellar mass, gas-phase metallicity, and star formation rate—holds for these distant systems, cementing their utility as benchmarks for understanding primordial [[star-formation-processes|Star Formation Processes]] and chemical enrichment across cosmic time [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...955L..18L/abstract).

Finally, the universality of the initial mass function (IMF) in extreme dwarf environments remains an open question. Anomalously high mass-to-light ratios and an overabundance of neutron stars in some ultra-compact dwarfs have led to suggestions of a top-heavy IMF during periods of [[violent-starbursts|Violent starbursts]] [Dabringhausen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..440..261D/abstract). Constraining this requires deeper spectroscopic surveys and multi-messenger campaigns. Multi-messenger astronomy stands to gain significantly from these low-mass environments: extreme dwarf galaxies are actively theorized to be the primary hosts for binary black hole mergers producing multi-messenger gravitational wave signals (such as GW150914-like events) [Marassi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3219M/abstract). Moreover, star-forming and starburst galaxies in general are theorized to be primary sources of the diffuse high-energy neutrino background observed by IceCube, heavily dependent on the efficiency of cosmic ray acceleration in these extreme environments [Tamborra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014JCAP...09..043T/abstract). In the electromagnetic regime, the abundant high-mass X-ray binary populations within star-forming dwarfs may additionally provide a significant contribution to the unresolved cosmic diffuse X-ray background [Griffiths et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ApJ...360..483G/abstract).
