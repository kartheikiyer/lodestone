**Disk galaxies** are gravitationally bound, multicomponent systems characterized by a flattened, rotationally supported distribution of stars, gas, and dust. Unlike [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], which are primarily supported by the random velocity dispersion of their [[stellar-dynamics-and-kinematics|stellar orbits]], the [[galactic-disk-structure-and-dynamics|internal dynamics]] of disk galaxies are governed by centrifugal equilibrium and angular momentum conservation. Structurally, these galaxies typically consist of a vertically compact [[thin-disks|thin disk]] embedded within a more extended, older [[thick-disks|thick disk]] [van der Kruit et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ARA&A..49..301V/abstract). At their centers, many host spheroidal [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]], while the entire baryonic structure is enveloped by a diffuse [[stellar-halos|stellar halo]] and an extended dark matter halo. The relative admixture of these components dictates a galaxy's morphological classification along the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]]. The foundational signature of disk galaxy kinematics is the observation of flat outer [[universal-rotation-curves|rotation curves]], which historically provided the primary dynamical evidence for massive dark matter halos [Navarro et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..7084N/abstract) and established fundamental empirical scaling laws such as the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]].

The standard cosmological paradigm for [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] posits that these systems form through the dissipational collapse of gas within virialized dark matter halos. As the gas radiatively cools, it conserves its specific angular momentum and settles into an exponential [[stellar-density-profile|surface density profile]] [Kormendy et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982SAAS...12..115K/abstract), [Dalcanton et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...482..659D/abstract). Disk growth generally proceeds "inside-out" via continuous cold [[gas-accretion-modes|gas accretion]], while internal [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] is driven by [[galaxy-structure|non-axisymmetric structures]] like bars and [[spiral-galaxy-dynamics|spiral arms]], classically modeled via [[density-wave-theory|Density wave theory]] [Lin et al. (1964)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1964ApJ...140..646L/abstract). Although traditional hierarchical models assumed that frequent early [[major-mergers|major mergers]] would easily destroy fragile, rotationally supported disks, recent observations from JWST and ALMA have revealed massive, ordered, and occasionally [[clumpy-disks|Clumpy disks]] in place as early as $z > 4$. This paradigm-shifting discovery suggests that high [[gas-fraction|gas fractions]] and rapid stellar recycling mechanisms in the early universe can buffer disks against disruptive dynamical heating, enabling them to survive the peak epoch of cosmic assembly.

This article explores the physical properties and evolutionary pathways of disk galaxies, bridging classic analytical models with modern observational and computational constraints. It details the morphological subcomponents, the kinematic interplay between baryons and dark matter, and the environmental mechanisms—such as ram-pressure stripping and [[galaxy-harassment|harassment]] within [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] and [[galaxy-groups|Galaxy groups]]—that can quench and transform disks over cosmic time. Finally, it addresses major open questions at the frontier of extragalactic astrophysics. Foremost among these is the "bulgeless galaxy" problem—the unexpected ubiquity and survival of massive pure disk galaxies in a hierarchical universe—alongside ongoing debates regarding the physical origin of outer disk truncations, the influence of galactic magnetic fields, and whether the inner regions of these galaxies represent baryon-dominated [[maximum-disks|Maximum disks]] or dark matter-dominated [[submaximal-discs|Submaximal discs]].

## Morphological Components and the Hubble Sequence

Disk galaxies are complex, multicomponent systems fundamentally characterized by a rotationally supported disk of stars, gas, and dust. This disk is frequently subdivided into dynamically distinct [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] and [[thick-disks|Thick disks]]. Embedded within this structure are a central spheroidal component known as the [[galactic-bulges|bulge]], an extended [[stellar-halos|stellar halo]], and a dominant dark matter halo. The relative proportions and physical properties of these components vary systematically, defining a galaxy’s position along the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]].

Along the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]], disk galaxies range from early-type spirals (Sa) to [[late-type-galaxies-and-stellar-populations|late-type galaxies]] (Sc/Sd) and [[irregular-morphology|irregulars]]. This classification is driven primarily by the admixture of the spheroidal and disk components, parameterized by the bulge-to-disk ratio. Moving from early to late types, the bulge-to-disk ratio decreases, [[spiral-galaxy-dynamics|spiral arms]] become more loosely wound, and the overall [[gas-fraction|gas fraction]] increases. Analytical models suggest that a galaxy's location on this sequence is heavily regulated by its mass and initial angular momentum (spin parameter $\lambda$), which together dictate the resulting disk thickness and bulge fraction [Hernandez et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006RMxAC..26...97H/abstract).

The central components of disk galaxies—[[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]]—exhibit significant morphological and kinematic diversity and are broadly divided into "classical" bulges and [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]]. Classical bulges resemble scaled-down [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|elliptical galaxies]]; they are dynamically hot, have steep [[density-profiles|Sérsic surface brightness profiles]] ($n \gtrsim 2$), and are generally understood to be the remnants of [[major-mergers|major mergers]]. In contrast, pseudobulges ($n \lesssim 2$) are dynamically colder, rotationally supported structures that retain a memory of their disky origin. They are built slowly through the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] of the disk, driven by the redistribution of mass and angular momentum by non-axisymmetric [[galaxy-structure|structures]] like bars and spiral arms [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). Distinguishing observationally between these two formation pathways can be difficult, as their photometric properties often overlap, making kinematic measurements the most robust discriminant. The distinct inner structural properties of these late-type galaxies reliably distinguish them from the dynamically hot cores of [[post-merger-galaxies|merger-remnant ellipticals]] [Carollo et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004cbhg.symp..231C/abstract). Furthermore, the detailed [[galactic-kinematics-and-rotation|kinematics]] of the broader disk itself can reveal complex assembly histories; for instance, the discovery of counter-rotating stellar populations in galaxies like [[notable-ngc-galaxies-n-o-sector|NGC 7217]] suggests substantial secondary infall of material carrying variable or misaligned angular momentum [Kuijken et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PASP..105.1016K/abstract).

At the extreme late-type end of the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] lie "pure disk" or bulgeless galaxies, which entirely lack a classical bulge. Observations indicate that a substantial fraction of massive disk galaxies—approximately $15\%$ to $18\%$ in both the local universe and out to $z \sim 1$—are pure disk systems [Sachdeva et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...820L...4S/abstract). Within $8 \text{ Mpc}$ of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]], more than half of the galaxies with circular velocities $V_{\rm circ} > 150 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ show no evidence of a classical bulge [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). The ubiquity and survival of these pure disk galaxies pose a significant, unresolved tension for hierarchical $\Lambda$CDM models, which predict that frequent minor and major mergers should inevitably produce massive classical bulges [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). Recent cosmological simulations suggest that continuous gas recycling from stellar mass loss may help massive late-type spirals maintain their disk dominance and suppress bulge growth during interactions [Martig et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract), though the persistence of bulgeless disks remains an active area of debate.

Enveloping the disk and bulge is the diffuse [[stellar-halos|stellar halo]]. Though it typically contributes only a small fraction to the total galactic light, the halo contains crucial fossil records of the galaxy's early assembly. It is populated by old, metal-poor stars primarily deposited through the tidal disruption of [[satellite-galaxies|accreted satellite galaxies]] in the galaxy's outskirts. Observations show that massive galaxies with circular velocities above $200 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ often possess extended stellar envelopes with power-law density profiles that correlate with Hubble type and bulge-to-disk ratio [de Jong et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..187D/abstract). Additionally, beyond the primary stellar structure, deep surveys of neutral hydrogen reveal the presence of extended high-velocity clouds (HVCs) in external disk galaxies. Detectable as high-velocity wings in [[h-i-rich-galaxies|H I emission profiles]], these HVC complexes can contribute $4\%$ to $14\%$ of a galaxy's total H I mass, tracing either ongoing cosmic [[gas-accretion-modes|gas accretion]] or substantial galactic fountain activity [Schulman et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...423..180S/abstract).

## Historical Development of Disk Models

From a historical perspective, the modern understanding of disk galaxies emerged from the early 20th-century debate over the nature of "spiral nebulae." Following Edwin Hubble's identification of Cepheid variables in the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Nebula]] in the 1920s, it became universally accepted that these nebulae were extragalactic "island universes." This discovery prompted the creation of the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]], a morphological classification scheme that ordered galaxies by their relative admixture of a rotationally supported disk and a pressure-supported spheroidal component, or bulge.

A watershed moment for galactic astrophysics occurred during World War II. Utilizing the Mount Wilson Observatory under the unusually dark skies of wartime blackouts, Walter Baade successfully resolved individual stars in the central regions of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] and nearby galaxies. Baade realized that a galaxy's structural components are characterized by fundamentally different [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]]. He coined the terms Population I and II to describe them: Population I stars, which reside primarily in the disk, span a wide range of ages and exhibit near-solar heavy element abundances (ranging from $0.2$ to $1.0 \, Z_\odot$). Conversely, Population II stars, which dominate the halo and [[galactic-bulges|bulge]], are predominantly old and [[low-metallicity-galaxies|metal-poor]]. This realization permanently linked a galaxy's spatial and kinematic structure to its chemical enrichment and star formation history.

As the field matured, qualitative morphological classifications were supplemented by quantitative surface photometry. Observers found that the radial light [[density-profiles|profiles]] of galactic disks could be reliably modeled by an exponential decay:
$$ \Sigma(R) = \Sigma_0 \exp\left(-\frac{R}{h_R}\right) $$
where $\Sigma(R)$ is the surface brightness at radius $R$, $\Sigma_0$ is the central surface brightness, and $h_R$ is the [[stellar-distributions|disk scale length]] [Kormendy et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982SAAS...12..115K/abstract). 

In 1970, Ken Freeman conducted a seminal analysis of these photometric profiles, leading to what became known as the [[freeman-stellar-disk|Freeman Law]]. He posited that the extrapolated central surface brightness of typical spiral disks is remarkably uniform, clustering tightly around $\mu_0 \approx 21.65$ B-mag arcsec$^{-2}$ [van der Kruit et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ARA&A..49..301V/abstract). This perceived uniformity provided a crucial boundary condition for early [[galactic-disc-models|Galactic Disc Models]] and theories of angular momentum conservation during protogalactic collapse.

However, subsequent deep imaging surveys demonstrated that the Freeman Law is not a universal constant, but rather an observational upper limit influenced by selection effects. Analyses of tens of thousands of local disks reveal that while the brightest disks peak near the Freeman value, late-type spirals routinely exhibit significantly fainter central surface brightnesses [Fathi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1101.1339F/abstract). Furthermore, the discovery of [[giant-low-surface-brightness-galaxies|Giant low surface brightness galaxies]], such as Malin 1, proved that massive disks can exist at surface densities far below the canonical threshold [Bothun et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ApJ...360..427B/abstract). 

Together, the identification of distinct [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]] and the development of rigorous photometric decomposition via tools like [[diskfit|DISKFIT]] laid the groundwork for contemporary astrophysics. They allowed theorists to separate the luminous baryonic disk from the dark matter halo and prompted the later discovery of vertically extended subcomponents, such as [[thick-disks|Thick disks]], which remain critical to our understanding of disk assembly today.

## Kinematics and Rotation Curve Dynamics

The dynamical state of cold gas and stars in the midplane of disk galaxies is heavily supported by rotation and governed by [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|internal kinematics]]. In this state, the inward gravitational pull is balanced by the outward centrifugal force, producing roughly circular orbits via [[centrifugal-and-centripetal-dynamics|Centrifugal and Centripetal Dynamics]]. The orbital speeds, or circular velocity, as a function of galactocentric radius $R$ are described by the [[universal-rotation-curves|rotation curve]], $V_{\rm rot}(R)$. Observational techniques—ranging from optical spectroscopy of H II regions to 21-cm radio interferometry of the extended [[outer-hi-disk|neutral hydrogen (H I) disk]]—demonstrate that rotation curves typically rise rapidly in the central regions and remain remarkably flat across the outer disk. The exact shape is morphology-dependent; massive galaxies exhibit a sharp central rise, whereas low-surface brightness and low-mass galaxies show a much slower rise [Navarro et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..7084N/abstract). At the extreme of kinematic measurements, the S0/Sa galaxy UGC 12591 represents one of the most rapidly rotating disk systems known, with a rotational velocity of $500 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Giovanelli et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...301L...7G/abstract).

The observation of flat rotation curves at large radii implies that the enclosed dynamical mass, $M(R)$, does not converge at the optical edge of the galaxy but instead grows linearly with radius:
$$M(R) \propto R$$
Because the luminous stellar and gaseous components exponentially decline, this kinematic signature provides the foundational evidence that disk galaxies reside within massive, extended dark matter halos [Navarro et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..7084N/abstract). While circular velocity alone serves as a poor indicator of the total virial mass, the product of the disk scale length and the square of the rotational velocity provides a much more robust mass estimator for these systems [van den Bosch et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003mglh.conf..250V/abstract). The tight scaling relation linking the amplitude of the flat rotation velocity to the total baryonic luminosity of the disk is known as the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] [McGaugh et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..197..153M/abstract). While the presence of dark matter is the consensus interpretation, some alternative theories argue that the observed flat rotation curves can be explained without dark halos by invoking modified Newtonian dynamics (MOND) [Wittenburg et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...890..173W/abstract) or radial outflows of unbound material in the outer disk [Schulz et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230908930S/abstract). Furthermore, the identification of a unique, universal acceleration scale across rotation curve data continues to pose a significant theoretical challenge to standard CDM-based galaxy formation theories [McGaugh et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..182..528M/abstract).

Although outer rotation curves clearly require an unseen mass component in the standard cosmological paradigm, determining the relative contributions of baryonic mass and dark matter in the inner galaxy is complicated by the "disk-halo degeneracy." Because the density profile of the dark halo is not known *a priori*, multiple combinations of stellar mass-to-light ratios and halo profiles can reproduce the same inner rotation curve. Some rotation curve decompositions invoke the [[maximum-disks|Maximum disks]] hypothesis, assuming that the baryonic matter entirely dominates the gravitational potential in the inner few scale lengths [Sackett et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...483..103S/abstract), [Bosma et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..182..339B/abstract). Supporting this view, analyses of large-scale dynamics often indicate that dark matter has a surprisingly low density in the inner regions of bright galaxies, leaning in favor of a baryon-heavy disk model [Sellwood et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998HiA....11..410S/abstract). However, it is increasingly clear that the degeneracy exhibits a mass dependence. While high-mass galaxies may lean toward maximal disks, dwarf and low surface brightness galaxies are decidedly [[submaximal-discs|Submaximal discs]], meaning dark matter makes a significant contribution to the potential even at their centers [Navarro et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..7084N/abstract), [Courteau et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...801L..20C/abstract).

To break the disk-halo degeneracy, efforts such as the Disk Mass Project use integral field spectroscopy to measure the [[stellar-dynamics-and-kinematics|vertical velocity dispersion]] of stars. This provides a direct kinematic measurement of the stellar surface density, independent of the global rotation curve [Verheijen et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.10360V/abstract), [Verheijen et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASSP....3...95V/abstract). Combining these vertical kinematics with radial rotation profiles suggests a structural transition within disk galaxies: the interior regions are generally baryon-dominated, while the transition to a dark-matter-dominated potential typically occurs within or near 2.2 disk scale lengths ($R_{2.2}$) for low-mass systems, and beyond this radius for massive galaxies [Courteau et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...801L..20C/abstract).

## Vertical Structure: Thin and Thick Disks

The three-dimensional distribution of stars in [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]] is classically modeled under the assumption of an isothermal sheet in hydrostatic equilibrium. This yields a vertical density distribution that follows a [[stellar-density-profile|sech^2 law]], often combined with a radial exponential decline:
$$ \rho(R, z) = \rho_0 \exp\left(-\frac{R}{h_R}\right) \text{sech}^2\left(\frac{z}{h_z}\right) $$
where $R$ is the galactocentric radius, $z$ is the height above the midplane, $h_R$ is the radial scale length, and $h_z$ is the vertical scale height. Furthermore, the equilibrium gas scale height increases significantly with radius, rising from less than $100 \text{ pc}$ in the inner disk to over $500 \text{ pc}$ at large radii in massive disk systems [Vijayakumar et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...989...66V/abstract). 

However, deep surface photometry of [[nearby-edge-on-galaxies|Nearby edge-on galaxies]] reveals that a single component is usually insufficient. Instead, galactic disks generally possess at least two distinct subcomponents: a vertically compact [[thin-disks|thin disk]] and a more extended [[thick-disks|thick disk]] [van der Kruit et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ARA&A..49..301V/abstract). In edge-on systems, fast-rotating spirals often host an additional [[super-thin-disk-components|super-thin disk component]] contributing roughly $25\%$ of the total K-band light, which correlates with regions of ongoing star formation [Schechtman-Rook et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...795..136S/abstract). Additionally, 3D maps based on classical Cepheids demonstrate that the Galactic stellar disk follows the gas disk's [[warped-galaxies|warped morphology]], extending out to at least $20 \text{ kpc}$ with a leading spiral pattern [Chen et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NatAs...3..320C/abstract).

Thick disks appear to be ubiquitous across the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] [Comerón et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...729...18C/abstract). Observations from the Spitzer Survey of Stellar Structure in Galaxies (S$^4$G) indicate an average thick-to-thin disk scale height ratio of approximately 2.65 [Ranaivoharimina et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977...66R/abstract). The relative stellar mass fraction of the thick disk varies considerably; cosmological simulations and local observations suggest it accounts for $35\% \pm 15\%$ of the total disk mass in massive galaxies [Yi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271....1Y/abstract), while the thick components in low-mass galaxies tend to be even more structurally prominent relative to their embedded thin disks [Dalcanton et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..9700D/abstract).

Kinematically, thick disk stars exhibit a higher vertical velocity dispersion and typically rotate more slowly than those in the thin disk. In the [[milky-way|Milky Way]], the thick disk displays a rotational lag of $\sim 30 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ to $50 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ and harbors a distinct metal-weak component with an even higher velocity dispersion [Carollo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887...22C/abstract). Chemically, thick disk populations are generally older ($>10$ Gyr) and more [[high-metallicity-galaxies|metal-rich in alpha-elements]], showing strong enhancements that imply a history of rapid, early star formation [Bensby et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..298...17B/abstract). Nevertheless, the thick disk is not a uniformly old structure; recent spectroscopic surveys of the Milky Way's geometrically defined thick disk reveal a [[steep-age-and-metallicity-gradients|strong radial age gradient]], with the median stellar age decreasing from $\sim 9$ Gyr in the inner Galaxy to $\sim 5$ Gyr in the outer disk [Martig et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..139M/abstract). 

The physical origin of the thick disk remains a subject of active debate, with the literature broadly divided among three primary formation channels:
1. **Accretion and Minor Mergers:** The thick disk may be a fossil record of early hierarchical assembly. In this scenario, it is formed either through the direct disruption and accretion of [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] [Navarro et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..655N/abstract) or through the violent dynamical heating of a pre-existing thin disk during minor mergers [Bekki et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...566..245B/abstract).
2. **Secular Stellar Heating and Radial Migration:** Continuous kinematic heating by [[molecular-clouds-and-gas-clumping|molecular clouds]] and transient spiral arms naturally increases the vertical velocity dispersion of stars over time. Furthermore, radial migration can efficiently move older, dynamically hotter stars from the inner disk to the outer disk, naturally inflating the scale height and creating a flaring, geometrically thick structure without requiring violent merger events [Loebman et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...737....8L/abstract). Alternative secular mechanisms include "levitation," where stars are adiabatically captured into vertical resonances as the galactic potential evolves [Sridhar et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996Sci...271..973S/abstract).
3. **In-situ Formation and Early Mergers:** Thick disks may have formed *in situ* during early gas-rich minor mergers, which kinematically heat the existing stellar populations and regulate ongoing star formation, naturally producing an extended thick disk component before the remaining gas settles into the thin disk [Brook et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASSP....3..551B/abstract), following the standard model of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]].

It is highly probable that a combination of these mechanisms operates in nature, with their relative importance varying depending on the mass and specific merger history of the host galaxy.

## Non-Axisymmetric Features: Bars and Spiral Arms

By breaking the axisymmetry of the [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]], spiral arms and bars act as the primary drivers of the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). These structures exert [[galactic-torques|Galactic torques]] that systematically redistribute mass and angular momentum throughout the stellar and gaseous disks. This ongoing angular momentum transfer heats the stellar disk kinematically and funnels cold gas into the central regions, where it frequently fuels nuclear [[extreme-starbursts|starbursts]] and contributes to the slow, secular assembly of [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract).

The persistence and kinematics of spiral arms are widely analyzed within the framework of [[density-wave-theory|Density wave theory]], which posits that spiral patterns represent quasi-stationary density waves propagating through the rotating disk [Lin et al. (1964)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1964ApJ...140..646L/abstract). As interstellar gas flows through this perturbed potential, it is compressed into narrow arms, locally triggering star formation. The generation of these perturbations often relies on swing amplification, a mechanism sensitive to the local kinematic stability of the disk. Recent numerical models indicate that the onset of bar formation via repeated swing amplification is well-described by a two-parameter stability criterion involving the Toomre parameter $Q_T$ and the dimensionless wavelength $X$. Specifically, bar formation requires $Q_T + 0.4(X - 1.4)^2 \le 1.8$, a condition corresponding to a swing amplification factor of $\Gamma = 10$ [Jang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250907353J/abstract). Furthermore, recent results from cosmological analogs indicate that the available gas content and the dynamical 'coldness' of the disk jointly regulate the morphology and growth of these two-armed spiral perturbations [Ghosh et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251025848G/abstract). 

The geometry of spiral structure is typically quantified by the spiral pitch angle. Observational surveys reveal that pitch angles decrease (i.e., the arms become more tightly wound) in galaxies with earlier [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] classifications, redder colors, and higher [[structural-concentration-and-profiles|central mass concentrations]] [Yu et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...900..150Y/abstract). The distribution of pitch angles in nearby disks is bimodal, peaking at approximately $12^\circ$ and $23^\circ$ [Yu et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...900..150Y/abstract). The tangent of the pitch angle has also been shown to correlate strongly with both the disk's [[cold-gas-surface-density|neutral atomic hydrogen density]] and the central bulge mass, defining a tight "Fundamental Plane of Spiral Structure" [Davis et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...802L..13D/abstract). Furthermore, stronger spiral arms are empirically linked to elevated star formation efficiencies. Gas damping facilitates the maintenance of the spiral structure, which in turn boosts the star formation rate of the available cold gas reservoir [Yu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...88Y/abstract).

The global strength of any [[non-axisymmetric-pattern|Non-axisymmetric pattern]] can be quantified by the maximum relative gravitational torque, $Q_g$, defined as the maximum ratio of the tangential force to the mean background radial force [Buta et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127..279B/abstract). Observations indicate a morphological dependence for this parameter, with $Q_g$ generally being lower in early-type spirals than in [[late-type-galaxies-and-stellar-populations|late-type spirals]]. Stronger torque forces, whether driven by prominent bars or grand-design spirals, are directly correlated with a more centrally concentrated neutral gas distribution over the disk scale [Liang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241013595L/abstract). This provides direct kinematic evidence that strong non-axisymmetric structures efficiently drive the dissipative processes necessary to transport gas inward, sustaining both inner disk growth and long-term nuclear evolution.

## Scaling Relations and the Tully-Fisher Relation

Disk galaxies obey tight [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] that link their fundamental structural, kinematic, and luminous properties. These correlations place stringent constraints on models of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], reflecting the efficiencies of gas cooling, angular momentum retention, and star formation inside dark matter halos.

The most prominent of these is the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]], which tightly correlates a galaxy's total luminosity (or stellar mass) with its characteristic rotation velocity, $V_{rot}$ [McGaugh et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..197..153M/abstract). In the context of hierarchical clustering, this relationship is interpreted as an imprint of the mass-velocity scaling of cosmological dark matter halos [Firmani et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..197..445F/abstract). However, accurately reproducing the zero-point of the observed relation has historically posed a challenge for $\Lambda$CDM models, as simulated dark matter halos are often too centrally concentrated compared to observational inferences [Steinmetz et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..197..165S/abstract). Extending this to the baryonic relation—which accounts for both stellar and [[gas-fraction|cold gas mass]]—reveals an underlying, uniform proportionality between baryonic mass and total dark matter mass across a wide range of galaxy masses [van der Kruit et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ARA&A..49..301V/abstract). [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf]] and disk galaxies are separated into two unique sequences in the mass-scale length plane, suggesting that dwarfs are inherently more diffuse than disk galaxies despite following the same baryonic Tully-Fisher relation [Schombert et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..296S/abstract).

These scaling laws exhibit surprisingly mild evolution over cosmic time. Between $z \sim 1$ and the present day, the [[galactic-kinematics-and-rotation|rotation velocity]] at a fixed stellar mass is estimated to have decreased by only $\sim 10\%$ [Somerville et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...672..776S/abstract). Modern cosmological models successfully predict this mild evolution of the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] from $z \sim 1$ to the present day [Sommer-Larsen et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASSP....3..545S/abstract). The [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] likewise evolves gradually; models and observational surveys indicate that at $z \sim 2$, disk galaxies at a given stellar mass were approximately $60\%$ as large as their present-day counterparts, growing "inside-out" over cosmic time [Somerville et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...672..776S/abstract). Furthermore, [[globular-clusters|globular clusters]] may represent the surviving nuclei of early [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|dwarf galaxies]] that served as building blocks for massive galaxies. This is supported by the observation that nuclear star clusters and globular cluster systems share a remarkably consistent mass fraction ($\sim 0.25\%$) relative to their hosts in typical [[spheroidal-galaxies|spheroidal galaxies]] [Böker et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...672L.111B/abstract).

The distribution of stellar light in disk galaxies is typically characterized by an exponential [[stellar-surface-density|stellar surface density profile]], which naturally arises from the specific angular momentum of the initial cooling protogalactic cloud [Dalcanton et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...482..659D/abstract). To systematically classify the morphology of these varying disk structures, numerical simulations often employ the Concentration, Asymmetry, and Clumpiness (CAS) parameters. These quantitative metrics serve as robust structural indicators of a disk's initial kinematical state—such as its Toomre Q stability—and can trace its evolutionary history across cosmic time [Chantavat et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...965...77C/abstract). Despite this overarching regularity, many disks exhibit radial breaks. These include outer truncations ([[type-ii-disk-break-radius|Type II profiles]]) or antitruncations (Type III profiles), which are thought to result from gas density thresholds for star formation or the secular redistribution of mass by non-axisymmetric structures [Erwin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..207E/abstract).

Determining the exact baryonic mass fraction in these systems is complicated by the "disk-halo degeneracy," which makes it difficult to uniquely separate the gravitational contributions of baryons and dark matter using one-dimensional rotation curves alone [Verheijen et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASSP....3...95V/abstract). The literature reflects ongoing debate over whether the inner regions of disk galaxies are "maximal" (where baryons provide the vast majority of the rotational support) or "sub-maximal" [Sackett et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...483..103S/abstract). Current consensus leans toward a mass-dependent trend in [[galaxy-mass-fractions|Galaxy mass fractions]]: high-mass disk galaxies ($V_{tot} \gtrsim 200 \text{ km s}^{-1}$) typically host [[maximum-disks|Maximum disks]] within the characteristic optical radius $R_{2.2} = 2.2 R_d$ (the radius at which an exponential disk's rotational velocity peaks) but become dark matter-dominated in their outskirts [Courteau et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...801L..20C/abstract). Conversely, low-mass disks and [[giant-low-surface-brightness-galaxies|Giant low surface brightness galaxies]] appear to be definitively sub-maximal, with dark matter dominating the gravitational potential even in their central regions [Navarro et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..7084N/abstract), [Courteau et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...801L..20C/abstract).

## Formation in a Cosmological Context

The formation of disk galaxies is fundamentally governed by the dissipative collapse of baryons within cold dark matter (CDM) halos. According to the standard paradigm of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], gas falls into a virialized dark matter potential well, is shock-heated, and subsequently cools radiatively. Because the radiated photons carry away binding energy but negligible angular momentum, the collapsing gas undergoes angular momentum conservation. To reach the lowest possible energy state for a given total angular momentum $J$, the gas naturally settles into a rotationally supported, flattened structure.

The classical analytical framework assumes that the specific angular momentum of the initial gas is roughly equivalent to that of its host dark matter halo, which is parameterized by the dimensionless spin parameter. In the "strong" form of angular momentum conservation, the specific angular momentum of each individual mass element is strictly preserved during the collapse. This maps the initial angular momentum distribution of the halo directly onto the structural properties of the resulting stellar disk, naturally producing exponential surface brightness profiles and extended rotation curves [Dalcanton et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...482..659D/abstract). Additionally, early [[galactic-torques|tidal torques]] exert a lasting influence on the macroscopic orientation of these systems; observations indicate that nearby disk galaxies tend to align their rotation axes perpendicular to the minor axis of their host large-scale structures, such as the Supergalactic Plane [Navarro et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...613L..41N/abstract). The natural variance in halo spin and mass successfully explains the wide range of observed disk scale lengths and central surface densities.

Cosmological simulations and chemical evolution studies strongly support an "inside-out" assembly process driven by continuous gas accretion. In these infall models, the dense central regions of [[protogalaxies|Protogalaxies]] collapse and initiate star formation rapidly. The outer disk is built up more gradually over cosmic time through the steady accretion of gas with higher specific angular momentum [Avila-Reese et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..215...31A/abstract), [Chang et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...722..380C/abstract). This progressive growth consistently yields the [[steep-age-and-metallicity-gradients|negative metallicity gradients]] and positive age gradients typically observed across galactic disks. Interestingly, this assembly exhibits a "star formation hierarchy"—or [[morphological-downsizing|downsizing]]—in which massive disks appear to have formed the bulk of their stars earlier than their low-mass counterparts. This behavior seemingly proceeds in the opposite direction to the bottom-up assembly of their host dark matter halos [Prantzos et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003RMxAC..17..121P/abstract). Furthermore, disk formation remains an ongoing process even in lower-mass systems that lack trapped, virialized gas atmospheres; these galaxies are continuously fed by cold, skew-rotating cosmic infall directly from the intergalactic medium [Binney et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..331..131B/abstract).

A long-standing theoretical tension within hierarchical CDM models is the survival of fragile disks against frequent galaxy mergers, which tend to dynamically heat the system and funnel material centrally to build classical [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]]. However, high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate that the continuous recycling of gas from stellar mass loss, combined with the inherently high gas fractions of early mergers, can buffer the disk against violent disruption and promote its rapid regrowth [Domínguez-Tenreiro et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...508L.123D/abstract), [Martig et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract). 

While the standard framework typically views well-ordered disks as late-forming structures that settle only after major mergers cease, recent observations in the context of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] challenge this timeline. Advanced imaging has uncovered fully formed, rotationally supported disk galaxies in the very early Universe, including a system with distinct [[spiral-galaxy-dynamics|spiral arms]] at $z = 4.41$ [Tsukui et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...372.1201T/abstract) and a giant disk with a half-light radius of $\sim 9.6$ kpc at $z = 3.25$ [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240917956W/abstract). These discoveries indicate that favorable physical conditions for large-disk formation—such as the rapid, smooth accretion of cold gas carrying coherent angular momentum—can occur much earlier in cosmic history than historically anticipated.

## Internal Secular Evolution and Radial Migration

While hierarchical merging and continuous gas accretion dominate the early assembly of galaxies, the long-term structural development of isolated disk galaxies is governed by internal [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]. This slow, steady phase of evolution is driven primarily by non-axisymmetric [[galaxy-structure|structures]] such as bars, transient spiral arms, and ovals [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). In our own [[milky-way|Milky Way]], recent dynamical models of the barred inner region suggest a revised pattern speed that places the corotation resonance at approximately $6 \text{ kpc}$ [Gerhard et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..334...73G/abstract).

These non-axisymmetric features exert strong [[galactic-torques|dynamical torques]] on the surrounding material, facilitating the exchange of angular momentum between the gas, stars, and dark matter halo [Combes et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..325C/abstract). By transferring angular momentum outward, these torques drive cold gas inward toward the galactic center. Over timescales of several billion years, this funneled gas fuels central [[massive-star-formation|star formation]] and gradually builds up dense central mass concentrations known as [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract). 

Unlike "classical" bulges, which are primarily the remnants of violent mergers, pseudobulges retain a strong structural and kinematic memory of their disky origins. Observationally, they are characterized by nearly exponential surface brightness profiles with Sérsic indices $n \lesssim 2$, higher ratios of ordered rotation to random velocity dispersion ($V/\sigma$), and the frequent presence of nuclear bars or spiral structure [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). Furthermore, when viewed edge-on, pseudobulges often exhibit boxy or peanut-shaped isophotes, a morphology that arises when the central disk material kinematically heats and buckles vertically via bending instabilities.

Spiral arms also act as major agents of secular evolution across the broader disk. The strength of spiral arms correlates positively with the local star formation rate and the molecular gas fraction, suggesting that the arms are maintained by dynamical cooling provided by gas damping [Yu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...88Y/abstract). As transient spiral density waves repeatedly emerge and decay, they scatter stars at resonances. These recurrent transient spiral patterns act as agents of heating that systematically increase the random speeds of stars over time while simultaneously reducing radial metallicity gradients [Sellwood et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..341S/abstract), thereby kinematically heating the stellar disk as a whole [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). 

A distinct and critical mechanism of secular evolution is radial migration. Disk galaxies undergo a smooth transition from an early phase dominated by [[violent-disk-instability|violent disk instabilities]] and large clumps to a long-term phase of secular evolution governed by radial gas transport [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.1552F/abstract). Driven largely by the scattering of stars at the corotation resonance of transient spiral modes, radial migration allows stars to undergo significant changes in their guiding center radii without substantially increasing their orbital eccentricity [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). Because it rearranges stars without kinematically heating the disk, radial migration profoundly flattens radial metallicity gradients and mixes stellar populations of different ages. 

Radial migration is also heavily implicated in the formation of thick disks. As older stars migrate outward into regions of lower disk surface density, they experience a decreased vertical restoring force and naturally flare to larger scale heights, creating a geometrically thick component [Loebman et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...737....8L/abstract). However, the literature remains actively divided on whether internal radial migration is the dominant formation mechanism for thick disks, or if they are primarily fossil records of abrupt early-universe events, such as the direct accretion of satellite stars and minor mergers [Bekki et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...566..245B/abstract).

## Chemical Evolution and Star Formation History

Disk galaxies undergo a prolonged assembly process governed by continuous gas accretion and star formation. A foundational paradigm for [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]] is "inside-out" growth, a process whereby the dense central regions of a disk assemble and form stars earlier than the extended [[outer-disks|Outer disks]] [Chang et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753L..10C/abstract), [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...34W/abstract). This formation history leaves a distinct fossil record in the stellar populations, typically manifesting as [[steep-age-and-metallicity-gradients|negative radial metallicity gradients]] and positive age gradients [Stanghellini et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190504096S/abstract). Observations of high-redshift samples indicate that central metallicities in disk galaxies evolve steadily, increasing at a rate of approximately $0.15$ dex per unit redshift between $z=0$ and $z=3$ [Kewley et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASSP....3..435K/abstract). For instance, the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] exhibits a strong radial age gradient within its geometrically thick disk, with the median stellar age declining from $\sim 9$ Gyr in the inner regions to $\sim 5$ Gyr in the outer disk [Martig et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..139M/abstract). 

The radial buildup of metals can be parameterized by "leaky accretion disk" models, which balance coplanar gas inflow, ex-planar galactic outflows, and *in situ* star formation [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...34W/abstract). These models successfully reproduce the [[o-h-radial-gradient|magnesium abundance profiles]] ($[\text{Mg}/\text{H}]$) of the Milky Way across different lookback times [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...34W/abstract). However, the chemical structure of the outermost regions can be complex; in the extreme outskirts of some pure disk galaxies (e.g., NGC 7793), abundance gradients are observed to flatten or even reverse, reflecting an interplay between pristine gas accretion and radial mixing [Vlajić et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732....7V/abstract), [van der Kruit et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ARA&A..49..301V/abstract).

The conversion of cold gas into stars within the disk is empirically described by the Kennicutt-Schmidt law, a power-law scaling relation between the star formation rate surface density ($\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$) and the [[cold-gas-surface-density|Cold gas surface density]] ($\Sigma_{\rm gas}$). This relationship is typically parameterized as $\Sigma_{\rm SFR} \propto \Sigma_{\rm gas}^N$, with the index $N$ ranging from 1 to 2. This continuous, low-efficiency star formation yields long global gas consumption timescales, defined as $\tau_{\rm SF} \equiv M_{\rm gas}/\dot{M}_{\rm gas} \simeq (1-5) \times 10^9$ yr for normal spiral galaxies. Moreover, local formulations of the Schmidt law in individual galaxies show clear evidence of star formation thresholds that are driven by the nonlinear development of [[violent-disk-instability|gravitational instability]] [Li et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...639..879L/abstract). When gas densities fall below these disk stability thresholds, star formation is significantly suppressed, leading to an "undead" population of [[passive-galaxies|quiescent disk galaxies]] that maintain star formation rates well below the main sequence [Oemler et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844...45O/abstract). In extreme environments, however, interactions can drive intense bursts of star formation that rapidly deplete or expel the gas reservoir. For example, M82 serves as a prototype for disks whose star formation was globally quenched following a massive, disk-wide starburst triggered by tidal interactions, drastically altering both its morphology and chemical evolution [Divakara Mayya et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0906.0757D/abstract).

In the far outer disk, observations reveal an abrupt truncation in $H\alpha$ emission, implying a sharp cutoff in massive star formation despite the extended presence of [[h-i-rich-galaxies|neutral hydrogen]]. While the physical origin of these star formation thresholds has been attributed to disk stability limits, local formulations of clustered star formation also naturally reproduce the steep radial decrease in $H\alpha$ luminosity relative to the underlying gas density [Pflamm-Altenburg et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455..641P/abstract). Moreover, [[type-ii-disk-break-radius|Type II disk profiles]] (truncations) are typically associated with a color profile minimum at the break radius, likely resulting from the redistribution of stellar mass by secular processes near the star-formation cutoff [Azzollini et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...679L..69A/abstract).

A critical factor in the long-term survival and chemical evolution of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] is gas recycling. Mass loss from low- and intermediate-mass stars returns roughly half of a stellar population's initial mass to the interstellar medium over a 10 Gyr period [Martig et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract). Cosmological simulations demonstrate that this continuous return of gas buffers the disk against dynamical heating and helps rebuild rotationally supported structures following disruptive interactions. By continuously replenishing the cold gas reservoir, stellar recycling effectively regulates secular bulge growth. This mechanism can reduce the resulting bulge-to-disk ratio by up to a factor of 3, helping to explain the abundance and survival of massive, late-type disk-dominated galaxies in a universe characterized by hierarchical merging [Martig et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract). Additionally, the chemical and energetic evolution of star-forming galaxies is heavily influenced by galactic winds, which transport cosmic rays from the disk into the halo, indicating significant convective transport that regulates both star formation and disk chemistry [Dogiel et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0905.3071D/abstract).

## High-Redshift Disks and Early Universe Assembly

In the traditional hierarchical model of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], large, dynamically cold stellar disks were thought to form relatively late, as frequent [[major-mergers|major mergers]] in the early universe were expected to continuously disrupt fragile, rotationally supported structures. However, recent observations using the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have necessitated a paradigm shift, revealing that massive, rotationally supported disk galaxies were already in place at redshifts $z > 2$, and in some cases, as early as $z > 4$ [Tsukui et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...372.1201T/abstract), [Yan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240704209Y/abstract), [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240917956W/abstract).

**Observation of Early Disks**
Recent high-resolution imaging has identified well-defined disk morphologies remarkably early in cosmic history. For instance, observations of the galaxy BRI 1335-0417 at $z = 4.41$ exhibit a distinct two-armed [[galaxy-structure|spiral structure]] extending from $2$ to $5 \text{ kpc}$ in radius, tracing [C II] gas kinematics that indicate a rapidly rotating disk formed within $1.4$ billion years of the Big Bang [Tsukui et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...372.1201T/abstract). Similarly, JWST has uncovered highly regular, [[nearby-edge-on-galaxies|edge-on stellar disks]] such as D-CEERS-z5289 at $z = 5.289$, which has an effective radius of $1.3-1.4 \text{ kpc}$ and a stellar mass of $10^{9.5}-10^{10} M_\odot$ [Yan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240704209Y/abstract). At slightly later epochs ($z = 3.25$), "giant" disk galaxies have been observed with half-light radii reaching $9.6 \text{ kpc}$ and stellar masses around $3.7 \times 10^{11} M_\odot$, sizes comparable to the largest local disk galaxies [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240917956W/abstract). The existence of such massive, extended structures poses a challenge to early hierarchical assembly models, suggesting that favorable conditions for disk formation—such as the efficient accretion of high-angular-momentum cold gas—can exist even in dense early-universe environments [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240917956W/abstract). Following this early epoch, statistical analysis of [[galaxy-shapes|axial ratios]] indicates that 'round' disk structures were established at approximately $z \sim 0.9$, following a gradual morphological change from bar-like structures at higher redshifts [Takeuchi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...801....2T/abstract).

**Violent Disk Instability and Clumpy Morphologies**
While early disks exhibit ordered rotation, their structural properties often differ markedly from local [[thin-disks|Thin disks]]. High-redshift disk galaxies are typically gas-rich and highly turbulent, characterized by large, irregular regions of [[clumpy-star-formation|Clumpy star formation]] [Elmegreen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ASPC..419...23E/abstract), [Orr et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241023337O/abstract). These [[clumpy-disks|Clumpy disks]] are thought to arise through a process known as [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] (VDI). In this regime, the high gas surface densities ($\Sigma_{\rm gas}$) drive the Toomre stability parameter $Q$ close to unity, leading to widespread gravitational fragmentation [Orr et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241023337O/abstract). 

The giant star-forming clumps produced via VDI are massive enough to resist immediate disruption by stellar feedback. Through dynamical friction, these clumps interact, lose angular momentum, and can migrate toward the galactic center to assemble [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] [Elmegreen et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...688...67E/abstract), [Elmegreen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ASPC..419...23E/abstract). Furthermore, the fragmentation and interactions of these massive clumps can increase the stellar velocity dispersion, driving the morphological transformation of isolated disks into lenticular ([[s0-ring-galaxy|S0]]) galaxies [Saha et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862L..12S/abstract).

**Kinematics and Disk Survival**
Integral field spectroscopy of $z \sim 2$ galaxies has confirmed that despite their clumpy and turbulent visual morphologies, many of these systems are fundamentally supported by rotation, with kinematic ratios of $V/\sigma > 1$. The survival of these early disks through the epoch of peak merger activity remains an area of active debate in the literature. While traditional models assumed that mergers primarily destroy disks, modern hydrodynamical simulations propose that [[gas-rich-galaxy-interactions|Gas-rich galaxy interactions]] and minor mergers, combined with the continuous recycling of gas from stellar populations, can quickly rebuild or preserve rotationally supported disks [Martig et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract), [Robertson et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...685L..27R/abstract). The high gas fractions act to dampen merger-induced heating and rapidly re-establish a stable, rotating gas disk, allowing spiral and disk structures to persist across cosmic time. Minor collisions involving clumpy galaxies at high redshift can also produce distinct empty ring structures, such as those observed in the galaxy R5519 at $z = 2.19$ [Guo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926..224G/abstract).

## Environmental Influences and Transformations

The evolutionary trajectory of disk galaxies is strongly modulated by their host environment, an effect encapsulated by the well-known [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]]. In dense environments such as [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] and massive [[galaxy-groups|Galaxy groups]], disk galaxies are subjected to a variety of dynamical and hydrodynamic processes that deplete their gas reservoirs and alter their structural properties. Consequently, dense environments host a higher fraction of passive, early-type galaxies, while late-type disk galaxies are more frequently found in the field [Ann et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014JKAS...47....1A/abstract).

When gas-rich [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] fall into a cluster potential, they interact with the hot intra-cluster medium (ICM). For a typical cluster velocity dispersion of $V \sim 1000$ km/s and an ICM density of $\rho_{\rm ICM} \sim 10^{-27}$ g cm$^{-3}$, the hydrodynamic pressure exerted on the galaxy often exceeds the gravitational restoring force of its disk. This results in ram-pressure stripping, which rapidly removes the cold gas supply from the outer disk. The cessation of cosmological gas accretion combined with this stripping leads to "strangulation," fundamentally quenching star formation and transforming the galaxy's photometric colors from blue to red.

In addition to hydrodynamic effects, disk galaxies in clusters undergo repeated, high-speed tidal encounters with other cluster members and the global cluster potential—a process known as [[galaxy-harassment|Galaxy harassment]]. These impulsive interactions dynamically heat the stellar disk, stripping mass and driving morphological transformations. Fragile, late-type disks are particularly susceptible to harassment; numerical models indicate that these interactions can effectively transform spiral galaxies into [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|dwarf spheroidal]] or dwarf elliptical systems. Nevertheless, remnants of their disk origins can persist; advanced imaging techniques have shown that faint disk features, such as spiral arms and bars, can still be extracted from the dominant diffuse light of dwarf early-type galaxies in clusters. Interestingly, while these environments are largely destructive, tidal forces in cluster environments can also initially trigger the formation of two-armed spiral patterns in early-type dwarf disk galaxies even before classical bars develop [Kwak et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887..139K/abstract). The vulnerability of pure, bulgeless disks to such tidal forces largely explains their rarity in high-density environments [Kautsch et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AN....330.1056K/abstract). 

The combination of gas stripping and galaxy harassment is widely invoked to explain the morphological transformation of star-forming spirals into passive lenticular ([[s0-ring-galaxy|S0]]) galaxies. However, the literature presents conflicting views on whether all S0 galaxies originate from such environmental transformations. Some models suggest that isolated, cold gas disks can undergo [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]], forming stellar clumps that build a central bulge and heat the disk, resulting in an S0-like remnant completely independent of environmental density [Saha et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862L..12S/abstract). 

Merger events and satellite accretion also leave distinct environmental signatures on disk structure. Tidal interactions during major mergers can redistribute accreted satellite galaxies, potentially aligning them into a flattened, corotating [[disk-of-satellites|Disk of Satellites]] around the remnant [Smith et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...818...11S/abstract). Observational evidence highlights a wide variety of tidal signatures from these interactions—including extended tidal bridges and tails—and their role in producing protoellipticals and oddly rotating stellar cores [Schweizer et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998giis.conf..105S/abstract). Furthermore, although interactions often trigger starbursts, close tidal encounters can actually suppress star formation in some cases due to the rapid, gravitationally induced loss of gas from the inner disk [Mihos et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...377...72M/abstract). Finally, while dense clusters are hostile to disk survival, lower-density environments can actually facilitate disk assembly. Observations indicate that group halos with masses below $\sim 10^{11.5} M_\odot$ may represent a preferred regime for the significant regrowth of late-type disks via cold [[gas-accretion-modes|gas accretion]] [Moffett et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812...89M/abstract). Thus, the observable properties of a disk galaxy reflect a continuous competition between internal secular growth and environmentally driven morphological disturbance.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite significant progress in understanding the assembly and secular evolution of disk systems, several fundamental challenges remain unresolved, particularly regarding the cosmological context of their formation and the detailed physics of their outer structures. 

One of the most pressing theoretical tensions is the survival of **Bulgeless galaxies** (also known as "pure disk" galaxies). In standard hierarchical $\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}$ models, frequent major and minor mergers are expected to destroy fragile disks or inevitably build classical [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract). However, observations show that a substantial fraction of massive disk galaxies—such as M101 and NGC 6946—lack classical bulges entirely [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). Furthermore, observations out to $z \sim 1$ indicate that many of these pure disk systems have survived intact for at least 8 Gyr without undergoing disruptive mergers [Sachdeva et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...820L...4S/abstract). Resolving this problem may require continuous gas recycling from stellar populations and rapid, smooth cold gas accretion to promote disk survival and suppress bulge formation [Martig et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract).

The physical nature of **Disk truncations** represents another active debate. While the [[stellar-distributions|Stellar distributions]] of disks are often characterized by exponential profiles, many display a sharp downward break or truncation in their outer regions ([[type-ii-disk-break-radius|Type II profiles]]) [Erwin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..207E/abstract). The literature is currently divided on the origin of these features. Some authors argue that truncations trace the maximum specific angular momentum of the initial protogalactic cloud from which the disk collapsed [Dalcanton et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...482..659D/abstract), [Pohlen et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..713P/abstract). Others contend they represent a global threshold in gas density required for star formation or are an artifact of the clustered nature of star formation causing sharp $\mathrm{H}\alpha$ cut-offs [Pohlen et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..713P/abstract), [Pflamm-Altenburg et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455..641P/abstract). Another significant challenge lies in connecting the circumgalactic medium to the cold gas of the outer disk. It has been proposed that "clumpy" outer disks composed of dense clouds may bridge this transition and naturally explain the observed Mg II absorption forest in background quasar spectra [Bland-Hawthorn et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...849...51B/abstract).

The coupling between baryons and dark matter in the inner disk also remains ambiguous due to the "disk-halo degeneracy." It is difficult to determine observationally whether the inner regions of disks are baryon-dominated ([[maximum-disks|Maximum disks]]) or dark matter-dominated (sub-maximal). Current evidence suggests a mass-dependent trend: massive galaxies often appear maximal within $\sim 2.2$ scale lengths, whereas low-mass systems are firmly sub-maximal [Courteau et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...801L..20C/abstract). Some researchers have pushed the boundaries of this problem to explore whether radial gas outflows [Schulz et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230908930S/abstract) or modified Newtonian dynamics (MOND) [Wittenburg et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...890..173W/abstract) might entirely replace the need for dark matter halos. Conversely, to explain these dynamically anomalous or [[dark-matter-free-galaxies|Dark matter-free galaxies]], some researchers offer a purely Newtonian counterexample to MOND: standard Newtonian physics acting on an exceptionally massive baryonic disk—potentially rich in unseen molecular hydrogen—can fully account for flat rotation curves without requiring any universal force law modification or dark matter [Byrd et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240400944B/abstract).

Another frontier in disk evolution is the role of galactic **Magnetic fields**. Historically neglected in models of galaxy assembly, magnetic fields are now understood to reach equipartition with thermal energy and cosmic rays in the interstellar medium. High-resolution cosmological simulations indicate that tiny primordial seed fields can be exponentially amplified by small-scale dynamos, eventually saturating into an ordered, large-scale field of $\sim 6\,\mu\mathrm{G}$ in the disk plane by $z \sim 2$ [Pakmor et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783L..20P/abstract). The extent to which these fields regulate star formation and provide pressure support in the outer disk remains an open question.

On a related observational front, the advent of precision astrometry from the *Gaia* mission has spurred the burgeoning field of "Galactoseismology." This approach provides a novel method to interpret the complex, non-equilibrium structures and kinematic distributions recently revealed in the stellar disk [Kawata et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241212252K/abstract). 

Finally, the formation mechanisms of [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] are heavily contested. While they are a ubiquitous feature of disk galaxies, their origins are attributed to competing mechanisms: violent accretion of [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] [Navarro et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..655N/abstract), adiabatic resonant levitation of stars [Sridhar et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996Sci...271..973S/abstract), or secular radial migration driving older stars outward and upward [Loebman et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...737....8L/abstract). The existence of radial age gradients in the Milky Way's thick disk suggests that these structures might be complex amalgams formed by multiple physical processes operating across different epochs [Martig et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..139M/abstract).
