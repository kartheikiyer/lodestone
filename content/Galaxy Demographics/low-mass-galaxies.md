**Low-mass galaxies** are a ubiquitous class of extragalactic objects typically defined by stellar masses below $10^9 M_\odot$, historically encompassing systems with absolute blue magnitudes $M_B \gtrsim -18$. As the most numerically abundant building blocks in the [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] ($\Lambda$CDM) cosmological framework, they dominate the faint end of the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]]. Morphologically, the low-mass regime spans a continuum from gas-rich, actively star-forming dwarf irregulars to gas-poor, passive [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]]. Because their shallow gravitational potential wells are highly inefficient at retaining baryons against energetic feedback, these systems are fundamentally characterized by low gas-phase metallicities, extreme mass-to-light ratios, and have historically been recognized as the most dark matter-dominated objects in the Universe [Tinsley et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981MNRAS.194...63T/abstract), [Bothun et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PASP..109..745B/abstract). 

The internal evolution of low-mass galaxies is governed by a complex interplay of environmental interactions and internal feedback. Unlike the continuous star formation and secular evolution seen in [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] of isolated [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|Star-forming dwarf galaxies]] is highly stochastic. Repeated cycles of rapid gas accretion, intense starbursts, and subsequent gas ejection via core-collapse supernova feedback severely regulate their growth, resulting in "bursty" formation cycles [Dekel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004cfa..collE..16D/abstract). While stellar feedback was historically considered the sole regulatory mechanism at this mass scale, recent observations of low-mass [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] driving ionized gas outflows indicate that AGN feedback also operates in this regime. Furthermore, their evolutionary trajectory is heavily environment-dependent; while isolated field dwarfs often sustain prolonged star formation, [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] falling into dense clusters are rapidly transformed into red, [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] via ram pressure and tidal stripping.

Due to their extreme dark matter content and sensitivity to feedback, low-mass galaxies serve as critical astrophysical laboratories for testing small-scale cosmological predictions. However, their observed properties frequently clash with standard $\Lambda$CDM expectations. High-resolution kinematic studies reveal that many low-mass systems exhibit [[cored-dark-matter-halos|Cored dark matter halos]] with constant central densities, contradicting the steeply cusped density profiles predicted by dark matter-only simulations [Kuzio de Naray et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741L..29K/abstract). Additionally, the observed abundance of low-mass halos in the field is significantly lower than theoretical predictions, a tension known as the void phenomenon or the field faint-end problem [Roberts et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.352..478R/abstract), which is distinct from the related 'missing satellites' problem observed in massive host halos. The paradigm of universal dark matter dominance has also been recently upended by the discovery of [[deficient-dark-matter-galaxies|Deficient dark matter galaxies]]—extreme remnants of tidal stripping that appear to lack a dark matter halo entirely [Moreno et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6..496M/abstract).

This article explores the physical properties, formation pathways, and cosmological significance of low-mass galaxies. It reviews the historical development of dwarf galaxy theory, details their chemical scaling relations, and examines anomalous populations such as ultra-diffuse galaxies. Finally, it addresses the most pressing open questions in the field, including the true impact of the Epoch of Reionization on delaying the formation of early [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] [Babul et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...458..100B/abstract), [Grebel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...610L..89G/abstract), the resolution of the small-scale cosmological crisis, and the theoretical challenge posed by unexpectedly luminous, early low-mass galaxies recently identified at 'Cosmic Dawn' by the James Webb Space Telescope.

## Defining the Low-Mass Regime

The low-mass galaxy regime is broadly characterized by systems with stellar masses $M_* \lesssim 10^9 M_\odot$, historically encompassing objects with absolute blue magnitudes of $M_B \gtrsim -18$. While they numerically dominate the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and the low-mass end of the stellar mass function, they contain only a fraction of a percent of the Universe's total stellar mass. 

Classification of these [[faint-stellar-systems|Faint stellar systems]] traditionally falls into two broad morphological categories based on their gas content and star formation activity. Gas-rich systems with ongoing, often bursty star formation are classified as dwarf irregulars (dIrr). These galaxies are characterized by chaotic kinematics and high neutral hydrogen content, anchoring the faint end of the [[h-i-mass-function|H I mass function]] [McQuinn et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...918...23M/abstract). Conversely, gas-poor systems that lack young stars and exhibit minimal recent star formation are classified as [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]] (dSph and dE, respectively). Rather than being an entirely distinct class of objects, structural parameter correlations suggest a continuum where dSphs and dEs represent star-formation-quenched, early-type counterparts to star-forming dwarf irregulars, shaped by transformation processes that become increasingly dominant in shallow gravitational potential wells [Kormendy et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract). The 'Galaxy End Sequence' model supports this view, proposing a unitary population of galaxies undergoing gradual evolutionary changes rather than exhibiting a distinct break between star-forming and passive types [Eales et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.3125E/abstract). Furthermore, self-supervised machine learning applied to the MaNGA survey reveals that low-mass rotation-dominated quenched galaxies form a distinct data-driven cluster compared to massive rotators, indicating unique low-mass evolutionary pathways [Sarmiento et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...921..177S/abstract). Among these diverse morphologies, compact elliptical galaxies (cEs) constitute a 'mixed-bag' population, primarily formed through the tidal stripping of more massive progenitors [Ferré-Mateu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.5455F/abstract).

The [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]] in low-mass galaxies varies dramatically across this morphological sequence. Dwarf irregulars often possess highly extended H I envelopes that can be many times larger than their optical extent, and their star formation is typically highly stochastic and inefficient [McQuinn et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...802...66M/abstract). In contrast to these extended states, isolated dwarf galaxies often initially form as compact objects before undergoing secular size growth, a process largely driven by the accretion of high-angular-momentum gas from satellite mergers [Geda et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251026875G/abstract). Meanwhile, dwarf spheroidals possess negligible cold gas reservoirs, a state largely driven by rapid gas removal mechanisms such as ram pressure stripping or tidal shocks during interactions with massive host halos [Mayer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Natur.445..738M/abstract). 

A defining physical property of the low-mass regime is the transition to extreme [[dark-matter-fraction|Dark matter fraction]]. As the depth of the gravitational potential well decreases, the efficiency of baryonic retention drops, leading to significantly higher mass-to-light ratios than those found in massive galaxies [Kormendy et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract). Analyses of rotation curves and velocity dispersions indicate that low-mass galaxies are fundamentally dark-matter dominated at nearly all radii [Tortora et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.5483T/abstract). Interestingly, scaling relations across all stellar systems, from globular clusters to galaxy clusters, can be unified using a two-component virial theorem that explicitly accounts for these dark matter halo interactions [Dantas et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528L...5D/abstract). However, the exact central structure of these dark halos remains an active area of debate. Observations frequently indicate constant-density central cores, which conflicts with the strongly cusped profiles predicted by standard cold dark matter simulations [Kuzio de Naray et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741L..29K/abstract). However, recent deep H I observations probing further into the potential well suggest this discrepancy may narrow for some systems, with kinematics approaching the scaling expected in $\Lambda$CDM [Sardone et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..135S/abstract). 

Furthermore, the paradigm that all low-mass systems are heavily dark-matter dominated has been complicated by the recent discovery of [[dark-matter-free-galaxies|Dark matter-free galaxies]]. Both observations and cosmological simulations now suggest that extreme tidal stripping during close encounters with massive neighbors can completely strip a satellite galaxy's [[dark-matter-mass|Dark matter mass]] while leaving an intact stellar remnant, creating a highly unusual class of dark-matter-deficient dwarfs [Jing et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488.3298J/abstract), [Moreno et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6..496M/abstract).

## Historical Development of Low-Mass Galaxy Theory

The theoretical framework describing the formation and evolution of low-mass galaxies underwent a foundational shift in the late 1970s and 1980s. Foundational conference works during this era were crucial for contextualizing early theories on the origin, development, and chemical evolution of dwarf galaxies, paving the way for later cosmological models [Beckman et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989epg..conf.....B/abstract). This paradigm regarding dark matter dictating galaxy assembly was formalized by White & Rees (1978), who proposed a 'two-stage' model of galaxy formation. In this framework, dark matter halos assemble first via [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], governed by gravitational instability. Subsequently, the luminous components of galaxies form through the baryonic cooling and condensation of gas within these pre-existing potential wells.

Early analytical approaches utilized the Press-Schechter model to estimate the mass distribution of these collapsing dark matter halos. Within a [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] cosmology, this framework naturally predicts a steeply rising dark matter halo mass function at low masses. However, applying simple baryonic cooling to this halo distribution yielded a profound discrepancy: if cooling and star formation were perfectly efficient, the theoretical framework predicted vastly more [[faint-stellar-systems|Faint stellar systems]] than were actually observed.

To reconcile the hierarchical model with the observed [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]], theorists recognized that galaxy formation in low-mass halos must be an inherently inefficient process. It was proposed that energy injected by core-collapse supernovae heats and expels cold gas, severely regulating star formation in systems with shallow gravitational potential wells [Dekel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004cfa..collE..16D/abstract). This reliance on supernova feedback became a cornerstone of low-mass galaxy theory [Dekel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004cfa..collE..16D/abstract). Independent of feedback models, early observations independently recognized that blue dwarf galaxies exhibit remarkably high mass-to-light ratios, pointing toward their fundamental dark matter dominance or an initial mass function heavily weighted toward low-mass stars [Tinsley et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981MNRAS.194...63T/abstract). In [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], AGN feedback quenching cooling flows creates a natural break in the luminosity function, though hierarchical models still correctly predict the existence of massive galaxies out to $z=5$ [Bower et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.370..645B/abstract). 

As the standard $\Lambda$CDM model matured, it became clear that [[total-mass-astrophysics|Total mass (astrophysics)]] is the primary driver of galaxy evolution; however, for galaxies between $z=1$ and $3$, mergers and cold gas accretion play roughly equal roles in driving [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]]. Today, the theoretical understanding of low-mass galaxies has shifted from viewing them as simple, continuous star-forming systems to highly dynamic environments. Modern frameworks recognize that low-mass systems experience highly stochastic and bursty star formation histories. This evolution is driven by cold flow gas accretion along cosmic filaments, which serves as a primary driver of disk growth and star formation [Brooks et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...694..396B/abstract). The resulting cycles of rapid gas accretion, intense starbursts, and subsequent post-starburst gas outflows can temporarily halt star formation [McQuinn et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...802...66M/abstract), [Mintz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250616510M/abstract). These feedback cycles can even drive non-equilibrium fluctuations in the central gravitational potential, complicating dynamical mass estimates by violating the assumptions of standard Jeans modeling [El-Badry et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835..193E/abstract).

Despite the success of the hierarchical paradigm, theoretical tensions remain at the low-mass end of the galaxy sequence. Notably, the predicted abundance of low-mass halos in [[low-density-environments|Low-density environments]] (such as cosmic voids) significantly exceeds the observed population of dwarf galaxies [Roberts et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.352..478R/abstract). While early theories suggested that the cosmic ultraviolet background during the epoch of reionization could photo-heat the intergalactic medium and globally suppress baryonic accretion in halos with circular velocities $v_c \lesssim 40 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Babul et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...458..100B/abstract), [Hoeft et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AdAst2010E..87H/abstract), the literature remains conflicted. Resolved stellar population studies of local low-mass galaxies often show no clear signature of a universal reionization-driven quenching event, indicating instead that many low-mass halos sustain extended, fluctuating, or reignited star formation well after reionization concludes [Grebel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...610L..89G/abstract), [McQuinn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976...60M/abstract). These local studies help constrain the long-term behavior of various [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]].

## Dark Matter Dominance and the Cusp-Core Problem

Low-mass galaxies, particularly dwarf and [[giant-low-surface-brightness-galaxies|Giant low surface brightness galaxies]] (LSB), are traditionally observed to be heavily dark matter dominated at all radii [de Blok et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..117...39D/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract), [Tortora et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.5483T/abstract). In these systems, dynamical models applied to rotation curves reveal central mass-to-light ratios reaching up to $10 M_\odot/L_\odot$ in [[submaximal-discs|Submaximal discs]] [Starkman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.2292S/abstract). Across the galaxy mass spectrum, the [[dark-matter-fraction|Dark matter fraction]] exhibits a distinct U-shaped trend, with low-mass and very high-mass systems being significantly more dark matter dominated than intermediate-mass disk galaxies [Tortora et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.5483T/abstract). Studies of [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]] over the last 8 billion years show that their dark matter fraction evolution is strongly dependent on the central [[density-profiles|Density profiles]]. Indeed, the internal motions of stars in Local Group dwarfs remain the most direct way to determine the systematic properties of their dark matter halos [Wyse et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..244...44W/abstract).

Because of their shallow gravitational potential wells and high dark matter content, low-mass galaxies are ideal laboratories for testing [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] ($\Lambda$CDM) predictions on small scales. Collisionless $\Lambda$CDM simulations consistently predict that dark matter halos should follow a centrally concentrated "cuspy" density profile (such as the NFW profile). This profile is characterized by an inner logarithmic density slope $\alpha \equiv {\rm d}\ln\rho/{\rm d}\ln r \approx -1$. However, high-resolution rotation curves—particularly those derived from deep H I observations—consistently indicate that the halos of LSB and dwarf galaxies exhibit [[cored-dark-matter-halos|Cored dark matter halos]] with approximately constant central densities, where $\alpha \approx 0$ [Kuzio de Naray et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741L..29K/abstract), [Tortora et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.5483T/abstract). At the extreme end of the angular momentum spectrum, theoretical models even suggest the existence of [[dark-galaxies|Dark galaxies]] residing in halos with exceptionally high spin parameters, which prevents their gas disks from reaching the critical surface density threshold required for star formation altogether [Jimenez et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997MNRAS.292L...5J/abstract). Taking this to the theoretical extreme, models propose the existence of 'completely dark galaxies'—systems composed entirely of dark matter with no stellar or gaseous baryonic content. While intrinsically invisible, strategies for finding these purely dark halos focus on their potential to act as gravitational lenses, which could perturb the images of background quasars or galaxies [Trentham et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.322..658T/abstract).

This discrepancy between predicted cusps and observed cores, known as the cusp-core problem, is a subject of ongoing and vigorous debate. A leading proposed resolution relies on baryonic feedback to dynamically alter the [[central-dark-matter-density|Central dark matter density]]. In this scenario, repeated episodes of bursty star formation drive non-equilibrium gas outflows via supernova feedback; this rapidly fluctuates the central gravitational potential, kinematically heating the dark matter and flattening the central cusp into a core [El-Badry et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835..193E/abstract), [Tortora et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.5483T/abstract). However, the literature remains strongly divided on the efficacy of this mechanism. Opposing studies argue that the star formation histories and total baryonic masses of LSB galaxies are simply too low to provide the necessary energy to unbind the dark matter cusp, leaving the theoretical tension unresolved [Kuzio de Naray et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741L..29K/abstract). Alternate theories of gravity are sometimes invoked to circumvent the small-scale dark matter crisis entirely, but these models struggle to explain the structural stability of bulgeless disks without invoking abnormally high, unobserved levels of random stellar motions [Sánchez-Salcedo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.3918S/abstract).

Adding further complexity to the low-mass regime is the recent discovery of [[dark-matter-free-galaxies|Dark matter-free galaxies]] and [[deficient-dark-matter-galaxies|Deficient dark matter galaxies]], which fundamentally challenge the assumption that all low-mass galaxies are heavily dark matter dominated. Observations and state-of-the-art cosmological simulations have identified a sub-population of extreme low-mass galaxies exhibiting severely depressed dark matter fractions [Jing et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488.3298J/abstract), [Moreno et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6..496M/abstract). These systems are thought to be the remnants of [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] that have undergone extreme tidal stripping during close encounters with massive host halos. Because dark matter has a higher binding energy but is less centrally concentrated than the central stars, such tidal interactions preferentially strip the extended dark matter halo while leaving the deeply embedded stellar component relatively intact [Jing et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488.3298J/abstract), [Moreno et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6..496M/abstract).

## Stochastic and Bursty Star Formation Histories

Unlike massive systems that typically experience continuous star formation followed by gradual quenching, low-mass galaxies are characterized by non-deterministic, highly stochastic [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] (SFH) profiles. Because of their shallow gravitational potential wells, these systems are highly sensitive to stellar feedback. Supernova-driven winds and radiation pressure routinely heat or eject the interstellar medium, temporarily halting star formation. This produces a "bursty" star formation cycle governed by rapid ignition followed by extended dormant phases [McQuinn et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...802...66M/abstract). Observations of isolated [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|Star-forming dwarf galaxies]] reveal a significant broadening in the scatter of their specific star formation rate and gas fractions, reflecting an inefficient, fluctuating process comprised of multiple star-forming episodes rather than a single exponential decay [McQuinn et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...802...66M/abstract), [Muñoz López et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250901710M/abstract). Low-mass disk galaxies exhibit diverse star formation modes, ranging from quiescent 'superthin' disks to active nearby starbursts [Gallagher et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..285..303G/abstract).

The bursty star formation of low-mass galaxies causes them to oscillate above and below the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]]. The main sequence itself exhibits a distinct bend around a characteristic stellar mass of $M_* \approx 2 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$, below which the star formation rate follows a consistent power law [Stephenson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.4217S/abstract). At cosmic noon ($1 < z < 3$), spectroscopic surveys of galaxies show large deviations from this main sequence, frequently exceeding $0.8$ dex [Mintz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250616510M/abstract). The physical mechanism governing the dormant phases between these bursts remains a subject of active debate. It is currently unclear whether these fluctuations are driven primarily by molecular-cloud-scale stochasticity or by larger, galaxy-scale gas cycles. However, recent rest-frame optical spectroscopy suggests that variations are governed by long burst cycles. In these populations, strong Balmer breaks are found to be negatively correlated with the H$\alpha$ equivalent width, pointing to extended dormant phases on timescales of $\gtrsim 100$ Myr rather than rapid, small-scale flickering [Mintz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250616510M/abstract). 

A major point of tension in the literature concerns the impact of cosmic reionization on the SFH of low-mass halos. Theoretical models have historically predicted that the ultraviolet background during reionization heats the intergalactic medium, starving shallow potential wells of cold gas, delaying their collapse and star formation until much later epochs ($z \lesssim 1$) [Babul et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...458..100B/abstract). Conversely, resolved stellar population studies of nearby [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] often lack a ubiquitous reionization signature, with many systems exhibiting continuous or reignited star formation [Grebel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...610L..89G/abstract). High-resolution observations of extremely low-mass isolated galaxies reveal a "post-reionization pause"—a distinct cessation of star formation from $z \sim 5$ to $z \sim 1$, followed by a late-time reignition [McQuinn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976...60M/abstract). This implies that while reionization strongly suppresses star formation, it does not necessarily result in permanent quenching. Additionally, mass return from dusty evolved stars may provide sufficient gas reservoirs to rejuvenate star formation even in isolated dwarf galaxies [Mahani et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IAUS..373..264M/abstract).

The extended, episodic nature of star formation in low-mass galaxies is a core observational pillar of "downsizing." While massive galaxies assembled the bulk of their stellar mass and peaked in star formation activity at high redshifts ($z > 1.5$), active star formation shifted to lower-mass halos at later cosmic epochs [Crampton et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..199C/abstract). In support of this, the peak magnitudes of [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] are observed to shift continuously with redshift, providing clear evidence for the downsizing trend in low-mass systems [Pattarakijwanich et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833...19P/abstract). Consequently, whereas high-mass systems evolved into [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] relatively early, low-mass galaxies continue to undergo dynamic, bursty cycles of gas accretion and [[first-star-formation|First star formation]] well into the late Universe [Jeon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941....5J/abstract), [Muñoz López et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250901710M/abstract).

## Impact of Cosmic Reionization

The Epoch of Reionization fundamentally altered the thermodynamic state of the intergalactic medium, with profound implications for the evolution of low-mass galaxies. As the universe reionized, the establishment of a metagalactic UV background drove widespread photo-heating, substantially raising the kinetic temperature of intergalactic gas. For low-mass galaxies residing in shallow dark matter potential wells, this injected thermal energy can readily exceed the local gravitational binding energy. Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations predict that this photo-heating suppresses the accretion of fresh gas from the cosmic web and can even trigger the photo-evaporation of existing cold interstellar material [Babul et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...458..100B/abstract). This suppression mechanism becomes highly effective for halos with circular velocities $v_{\rm circ} \lesssim 40 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, establishing a characteristic mass threshold below which halos become severely baryon-deficient [Hoeft et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AdAst2010E..87H/abstract).

In the standard [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] paradigm, systems below the atomic cooling mass limit are exceptionally vulnerable to environmental heating. The permeating UV background interacts strongly with these low-mass systems. Early theoretical models consequently posited that the UV background prevents early collapse, which delays the formation epoch of these dwarf galaxies entirely until late cosmic epochs ($z \lesssim 1$), at which point they undergo a short starburst and fade [Babul et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...458..100B/abstract).

Despite these robust theoretical predictions, the observational literature regarding the impact of reionization on low-mass galaxies remains highly ambiguous and explicitly conflicting. Detailed reconstructions of the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] in [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]]—particularly [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]]—show no universal or widespread signature of reionization-driven quenching. Instead of a sharp truncation at high redshift, many local dwarf galaxies exhibit ancient star formation that proceeded continuously over several gigayears, and some experienced active star formation until relatively recent epochs [Grebel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...610L..89G/abstract). The stellar populations in these systems suggest that their evolutionary pathways may be governed more by localized feedback and environmental processes than by a deterministic, universe-wide cessation imposed by reionization [Grebel et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005nfcd.conf....1G/abstract).

Recent high-resolution observations and updated theoretical frameworks have attempted to reconcile this tension by framing reionization as a regulator rather than a strict quenching mechanism. Deep observations utilizing the James Webb Space Telescope have revealed that some extremely low-mass, isolated systems exhibit a distinct "post-reionization pause" in their SFH. In these cases, an initial epoch of [[first-star-formation|First star formation]] is followed by a prolonged dormant phase corresponding to the post-reionization era, but star formation eventually reignites at later times [McQuinn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976...60M/abstract). 

## Scaling Relations and Chemical Evolution

Low-mass galaxies trace the fundamental connections between stellar mass, [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]], and chemical enrichment across cosmic time. They generally follow an extrapolation of the mass-metallicity relation (MZR) observed in [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250104943L/abstract). However, the physical drivers of the significant scatter around this relation in the low-mass regime remain a subject of active and conflicting debate. Large statistical surveys suggest that this scatter is strongly coupled to the specific star formation rate (sSFR) and neutral gas mass: low-mass galaxies with large gas reservoirs typically exhibit high sSFRs and low metallicities, whereas gas-depleted dwarfs display lower sSFRs and correspondingly higher metal abundances [Lara-Lopez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.433L..35L/abstract). Conversely, detailed integral-field studies of dwarf galaxies report that invoking a dependence on the star formation rate does not meaningfully decrease the dispersion in the low-mass MZR, indicating that the fundamental plane of metallicity may break down or change character at low masses [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250104943L/abstract).

A persistent exception to the standard MZR is the population of tidal dwarf galaxies. Formed from the pre-enriched debris of [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]], these objects are distinctly metal-rich for their stellar mass. Regardless of their formation epoch, they lie well above the observed relation for local dwarf galaxies, making them distinct outliers driven by major accretion events [Dumont et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.2866D/abstract).

The internal chemical properties of these galaxies serve as fossil records of their assembly. While high-mass galaxies often show smooth, negative radial metallicity profiles, the gas-phase metallicity gradients in low-mass galaxies—especially those with high sSFRs—tend to be remarkably flat. Simulations and observations suggest that this flattening is caused by strong stellar feedback and bursty [[star-formation-history|star formation histories]], which drive gas outflows that aggressively redistribute metals and destroy pre-existing gradients [Ma et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.4780M/abstract). In the ultra-faint regime, chemical evolution diverges even from that of classical [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]]. Ultra-faint dwarfs reach solar $[\alpha/\text{Fe}]$ ratios at significantly lower metallicities than larger dwarfs, and individual systems can exhibit extreme neutron-capture element signatures.

Furthermore, the universality of the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] has been heavily scrutinized in low-mass stellar systems. Recent analyses indicate that the low-mass slope of the IMF in dwarf galaxies is not static, but exhibits a strong dependence on environmental metallicity. An IMF model featuring a metallicity-dependent slope for low-mass stars aligns more accurately with observational constraints than a universal profile, fundamentally impacting how stellar masses and star formation rates are derived in these environments [Yan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969...95Y/abstract).

Kinematically, low-mass galaxies provide critical boundary tests for the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] (BTFR). Historically, the kinematics of dwarf galaxies have shown significant scatter and apparent deviations from the BTFR defined by more massive [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]. However, deep high-resolution H I observations reveal that as kinematic measurements probe deeper into the potential well, the relationship between observed rotation velocities and the predicted maximum halo velocity tightly approaches the 1:1 scaling expected in a $\Lambda$CDM cosmology [Sardone et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..135S/abstract). These improved measurements confirm that isolated, gas-rich dwarfs robustly populate the low-mass extension of the BTFR.

## Environmental Quenching and Interaction

The evolutionary trajectory of low-mass galaxies is fundamentally linked to their local environment. While isolated systems often sustain prolonged, bursty star formation, low-mass galaxies residing in high-density regions, such as [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] or massive groups, are predominantly passive and gas-poor. This transformation is driven by environmental quenching mechanisms that are particularly effective against the shallow gravitational potential wells of low-mass dark matter halos [Conselice et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Ap&SS.284..631C/abstract).

When a gas-rich dwarf galaxy falls into a massive host halo, it becomes subjected to ram-pressure stripping by the hot intracluster medium and tidal stripping from the host's global gravitational field. Simulations demonstrate that the combination of tidal shocks and ram pressure can rapidly sweep away the entire cold gas reservoir of [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] [Mayer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Natur.445..738M/abstract). This abrupt gas removal, often aided by the cosmic ultraviolet background, halts star formation and transforms [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|Star-forming dwarf galaxies]] into gas-poor [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]]. Strangulation—the stripping of the satellite’s extended hot gas halo—further ensures that the galaxy is starved of any future fuel for star formation [Conselice et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005nfcd.conf..277C/abstract).

The efficiency and timing of this "environmental quenching" depend heavily on the cluster halo-to-stellar mass ratio. Cosmological hydrodynamic simulations indicate that low-mass galaxies within massive clusters crossed their "transition epoch"—the point at which they became systematically less star-forming than field galaxies—roughly $7.6$ Gyr ago [Jeon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941....5J/abstract). Interestingly, during the peak epoch of galaxy formation at $z \sim 2$, dense environments can temporarily have the opposite effect: gas accretion and frequent interactions in protoclusters have been shown to induce elevated star formation rates in low-mass galaxies before they are ultimately quenched [Daikuhara et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.2335D/abstract).

The statistical dichotomy between satellite and [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]] is stark. Approximately $50\%$ of low-mass satellite galaxies exhibit red colors indicative of [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]], whereas only about $25\%$ of central dwarfs do [Wang et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697..247W/abstract). Many of these seemingly isolated red dwarfs are located within three virial radii of a massive neighbor, suggesting they are "backsplash" galaxies that passed through a massive halo, lost their gas, and emerged quenched on the other side [Wang et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697..247W/abstract). Furthermore, environmental effects extend to the central regions of halos through galaxy assembly bias; observations reveal that the clustering of low-mass central galaxies is not uniform, but depends explicitly on their specific star formation rate [Wang et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.433..515W/abstract). The overall abundance of dwarf galaxies in [[low-density-environments|Low-density environments]] is significantly lower than predicted by standard cold dark matter models, highlighting broader tensions [Roberts et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.352..478R/abstract).

## Stellar and AGN Feedback Mechanisms

In galaxies with shallow gravitational potential wells, energy injection is a critical regulator of gas accretion and star formation. Historically, stellar feedback has been considered the sole dominant mechanism in these systems, though recent multi-wavelength surveys have revealed an increasingly complex interplay between supernova outflows and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback.

**Stellar Feedback and Potential Fluctuations**
Stellar feedback, primarily driven by core-collapse supernovae, regulates the gas reservoirs of low-mass galaxies by ejecting mass and injecting energy into the interstellar medium. Because star formation in these systems is often bursty, supernova outflows drive rapid, non-equilibrium fluctuations in the galactic gravitational potential [El-Badry et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835..193E/abstract). Over cosmological timescales, these repeated potential fluctuations dynamically heat collisionless components, which provides a physical mechanism for flattening the [[central-dark-matter-density|Central dark matter density]] and transforming theoretically predicted steep cusps into [[cored-dark-matter-halos|Cored dark matter halos]] [Tortora et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.5483T/abstract). 

**The Emergence of Low-Mass AGN Feedback**
The paradigm that AGN feedback is entirely negligible in the low-mass regime has been challenged by the discovery of [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] in low-mass and bulgeless disk galaxies via mid-infrared color selection [Satyapal et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...784..113S/abstract). Unlike higher-mass systems, where black hole spin and growth are largely driven by [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]], low-mass black holes appear to be spun up by secular gas accretion in disk-like structures [Wang et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833L...2W/abstract).

Spectroscopic data confirm that these low-mass AGNs are capable of driving powerful ionized gas outflows. Across the general galaxy population, AGN-driven outflows achieve velocities that are significantly faster and more blueshifted than outflows driven by pure star formation. Crucially, this dynamic trend persists in low-mass galaxies, where low-mass AGNs drive outflows that are faster and more blueshifted compared to star-forming counterparts [Salehirad et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...979...26S/abstract). The identification of low-mass AGNs in isolated environments further indicates that central black holes can operate as internal quenching mechanisms, driving galaxies to become [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] without requiring environmental stripping effects [Witherspoon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..192W/abstract).

## Low-Mass Galaxies at High Redshift

Under the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], low-mass galaxies at high redshift are the foundational building blocks of the universe's large-scale structure. They assemble first within small dark matter halos, driving early cosmic chemical enrichment and contributing substantially to the ultraviolet photon budget during the Epoch of Reionization. 

**Early Star Formation Efficiency**
In the local universe, dwarf galaxies exhibit star formation efficiencies (SFEs) more than a full dex lower than those of Milky Way-mass halos. However, models and recent data suggest this trend may be reversed during [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...790L..17M/abstract). The assembly of these primordial building blocks likely occurred in two phases: an early, rapid accretion phase where a quickly deepening potential well enabled highly efficient star formation, followed by a slower late-time accretion phase. 

The launch of JWST has revolutionized the empirical study of this epoch. Spectroscopic confirmations of early systems reveal extraordinary properties just $\sim 300$ Myr after the Big Bang, presenting a tension with standard models and suggesting that gas conversion in early low-mass halos was extremely efficient [Carniani et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240518485C/abstract). High-redshift Lyman-alpha galaxies act as the low-mass analogs of massive starbursts; they fit standard galaxy scaling relations but display exceptionally high star formation rates [Rhoads et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...780...20R/abstract). These early epochs were characterized by rapid assembly, whereas the overall decline in [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]] since $z=1$ is largely associated with the fading of late-type, low-mass galaxies [Madau et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997AIPC..393..481M/abstract).

**Burstiness and the Cosmic Noon**
By the time the universe reached 'cosmic noon' ($1 < z < 3$), low-mass galaxies exhibited highly stochastic, bursty star formation. Deep JWST PRISM spectroscopy has revealed that their [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] is dominated by long-timescale burst cycles ($\gtrsim 100$ Myr) punctuated by dormant phases where the specific star formation rate drops $\gtrsim 0.8$ dex below the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] [Mintz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250616510M/abstract). 

**Reionization and Quenching**
While models predict that photoionization heating should suppress accretion in shallow halos, early archaeological studies found no universal signature of reionization-driven quenching in the [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] of the Local Group [Grebel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...610L..89G/abstract). However, imaging of isolated dwarfs has uncovered distinct "post-reionization pauses." These systems underwent an initial burst of [[first-star-formation|First star formation]], followed by a cessation of activity between $z \sim 5$ and $z \sim 1$, before star formation reignited at lower redshifts [McQuinn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976...60M/abstract). 

## Anomalous Populations: Dark Matter Deficits and Ultra-Diffuse Galaxies

Historically, low-mass galaxies have been recognized as the most highly dark-matter-dominated objects in the Universe [Tinsley et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981MNRAS.194...63T/abstract). This characteristic is particularly evident in [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]], whose shallow gravitational potential wells and inefficient star formation leave them exceptionally rich in dark matter relative to their baryonic content [Bothun et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PASP..109..745B/abstract). 

Recently, surveys have identified extreme populations such as Ultra-Diffuse Galaxies (UDGs). These systems possess the stellar mass of typical dwarfs but exhibit spatial extents comparable to the Milky Way. Many UDGs behave as canonical, heavily dark-matter-dominated systems, though some isolated examples show diversity driven by their assembly history [Afanasiev et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.6312A/abstract). Further diversity in [[giant-low-surface-brightness-galaxies|Giant low surface brightness galaxies]] is driven by major mergers with co-rotating satellites, resulting in high-spin systems characterized by highly inefficient star formation [Wright et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250721231W/abstract).

Despite this general rule of dark matter dominance, the internal kinematics of these systems present significant theoretical challenges. One long-standing anomaly is the "cusp-core" problem. High-resolution rotation curves frequently reveal that their [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]] features constant-density central cores. This sharply contradicts the steep density cusps predicted by pure [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] ($\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}$) N-body simulations [Kuzio de Naray et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741L..29K/abstract). While some models argue that baryonic feedback can flatten the density profile, the literature remains conflicting on whether feedback alone is sufficient [El-Badry et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835..193E/abstract).

A more profound challenge has been the recent discovery of [[deficient-dark-matter-galaxies|Deficient dark matter galaxies]]. Observations have revealed a sub-population of low-mass galaxies that appear to lack dark matter almost entirely [Moreno et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6..496M/abstract). Hydrodynamical simulations suggest that these galaxies are not formed intrinsically without dark matter, but are instead extreme products of tidal disruption. During encounters with massive [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]], a [[satellite-galaxies|satellite galaxy]] can be subject to intense tidal forces that selectively remove the extended dark matter halo while leaving the compact stellar core relatively intact [Jing et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488.3298J/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The standard [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] ($\Lambda$CDM) paradigm faces significant challenges at the low-mass end, collectively known as the [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]] crisis. Foremost among these are two distinct abundance discrepancies: the 'missing satellites' problem and the field faint-end problem [Roberts et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.352..478R/abstract). Additionally, the 'cusp-core' problem remains unresolved. Dissipationless simulations predict steeply rising central [[density-profiles|Density profiles]] for dark matter halos, yet rotation curves frequently exhibit constant-density cores [Kuzio de Naray et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741L..29K/abstract). The recent discovery of low-mass galaxies completely lacking dark matter further complicates this picture, demonstrating that extreme tidal stripping can fundamentally decouple the stellar and dark matter components in [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] [Moreno et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6..496M/abstract).

The mechanisms driving the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and quenching of low-mass systems also remain poorly understood. A major open question is the extent to which cosmic reionization globally suppresses star formation. While some models suggest that UV background heating should halt gas accretion, resolved stellar populations in Local Group dwarfs often show no clear truncation signature [Grebel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...610L..89G/abstract). Instead, isolated systems exhibit complex SFHs that are not easily explained by a single reionization event [McQuinn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976...60M/abstract). Furthermore, the paradigm that supernova feedback exclusively regulates dwarf systems is shifting, with discoveries of [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] driving high-velocity gas outflows in the [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] [Witherspoon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..192W/abstract).

Resolving these tensions relies heavily on upcoming multi-wavelength surveys. Deep radio observations are poised to trace H I kinematics in extremely low-mass field systems to definitively test cosmological predictions in the [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|Local Volume and Nearby Galaxy Studies]] [Adams et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170910369A/abstract). Concurrently, the study of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] will be transformed by the next generation of optical telescopes and integral-field surveys. These facilities will provide spatially resolved spectroscopy of ultra-faint dwarfs, breaking degeneracies between dark matter microphysics and baryonic feedback. Finally, at high redshift, deep imaging continues to reveal unexpectedly luminous systems, demanding a recalibration of the earliest stages of assembly and star formation cycles [Mintz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250616510M/abstract).
