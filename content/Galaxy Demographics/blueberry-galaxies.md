## Article

**Blueberry galaxies** are a class of [[compact-galaxies|extremely compact]], metal-poor dwarf starburst galaxies situated in the [[low-redshift-star-forming-galaxies|local universe]]. Characterized by exceptionally high specific star formation rates and strong nebular emission that yields enormous ionization ratios [Rong et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180610149R/abstract), these systems possess very low stellar masses ($10^5 - 10^7 M_{\odot}$) [Paswan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210208240P/abstract) and gas-phase metallicities typically around 10% of the solar value [Brennan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A.259B/abstract), [Brennan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231213280B/abstract). They are widely recognized as the lower-redshift, lower-mass counterparts to [[green-pea-galaxies|Green Pea galaxies]] and represent an extreme, transient phase within the broader evolutionary sequence of [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] [Paswan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929...50P/abstract). Because of their pristine nature and uniquely blue optical continua, blueberry galaxies serve as critical local [[galaxy-analogues|Galaxy analogues]] for studying the extreme interstellar medium conditions prevalent during the early phases of cosmic assembly.

Despite their diminutive stellar masses, blueberry galaxies host substantial neutral hydrogen reservoirs. However, they consume this gas at an accelerated rate, exhibiting remarkably short depletion timescales. While initially identified as unresolved point sources, spatially resolved spectroscopy has revealed a more complex structural reality. Many blueberry galaxies are embedded within older, extended low-surface-brightness (LSB) stellar disks and show kinematic misalignments or spatial offsets between their gas and stellar components [Paswan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210208240P/abstract), [Paswan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929...50P/abstract), [Dutta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.5140D/abstract). These morphological disturbances strongly suggest that their intense, rejuvenating starbursts are frequently triggered by tidal interactions or advanced mergers.

A primary motivation for studying blueberry galaxies is their utility as templates for the primeval, Lyman-$\alpha$ emitting systems that drove cosmic reionization. Unlike typical local [[extreme-starbursts|Extreme starbursts]], which are often heavily dust-obscured, blueberries are [[ultraviolet-galaxies|UV-bright]], dust-poor, and highly efficient at leaking Lyman continuum photons. Recent multi-wavelength observations have confirmed that local blueberry galaxies share the shallow low-mass slope of the mass-metallicity relation observed in primeval galaxies at $3 < z < 10$ [Curti et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...684A..75C/abstract). Despite their foundational utility, they present several open questions in contemporary astrophysics, particularly regarding their systemically underluminous X-ray output [Adamcová et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A..27A/abstract) and whether their post-starburst remnants ultimately fade into passive [[low-luminosity-galaxies|Low-luminosity galaxies]] through internal gas exhaustion or external environmental quenching.

## Discovery and Classification

The historical development of dwarf starburst studies traces its lineage to early observations of [[zwicky-galaxies|Zwicky galaxies]] and [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|Blue compact dwarf galaxies]] (BCDs), which were recognized for their exceptionally blue optical continua indicating massive young stellar populations. Early redshift surveys identified a population of small blue galaxies at $z \approx 0.25$ that dominated galaxy counts and potentially contained significant baryonic matter, providing foundational historical context for modern compact dwarf studies [Cowie et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991Natur.354..460C/abstract). Early angular size surveys suggested that the population of faint blue galaxies contained a significant number of starburst dwarf galaxies at $z < 0.5$ with high apparent surface brightnesses [Roche et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997MNRAS.288..200R/abstract). It was also historically proposed that the excess population of "faint blue galaxies" observed at intermediate redshifts could correspond to low-surface-brightness (LSB) galaxies that are simply difficult to detect in local surveys [McGaugh et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.367..538M/abstract). Visual morphological surveys in the local universe have additionally identified a related population of "blue-cored dwarf ellipticals" and "[[blue-elliptical-galaxies|blue dwarf ellipticals]]" that exhibit recent central star formation [Ann et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..217...27A/abstract). Decades prior to the identification of blueberries, researchers heavily relied on local H II galaxies and Luminous Compact Blue Galaxies (LCBGs) as accessible structural and morphological analogs for probing high-redshift Lyman-break galaxies at $z \sim 3$ [Lowenthal et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASSL..329...17L/abstract). The persistent search for closer local analogs to the primeval galaxies of the early universe later led to the discovery of [[green-pea-galaxies|Green Pea galaxies]] at intermediate redshifts. The need to study even more extreme environments at the lowest possible mass scales motivated a search for an even more proximate population. 

This effort culminated in the formal identification of the "blueberry" galaxy class by Yang et al. (2017) [Rong et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180610149R/abstract). Extracted from large photometric and spectroscopic surveys like the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS), blueberries represent a lower-redshift and lower-mass extension of the Green Pea population. Early unresolved photometric observations suggested that blueberries might be isolated, purely primeval objects undergoing their first major episode of star formation. However, the advent of spatially resolved integral field unit (IFU) spectroscopy has shifted this paradigm. Blueberry galaxies are now increasingly classified not as peculiar isolated phenomena, but as an extreme, interaction-driven transient evolutionary stage within the broader family of BCDs [Paswan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929...50P/abstract).

## Physical Properties and Spectral Signatures

Blueberry galaxies are defined by their low stellar masses ($10^5-10^7 M_{\odot}$), compact dimensions ($1-2$ kpc cores), and exceptionally high specific star formation rates. For context, typical local [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|blue compact dwarfs]] exhibit a median star formation rate of roughly $\sim 0.3 M_{\odot} \text{ yr}^{-1}$ [Hopkins et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002AJ....124..862H/abstract); blueberries, as a specialized subclass, possess extreme rates that can significantly exceed this baseline and rapidly deplete their gas. The optical spectra of these systems are dominated by powerful nebular emission, which is directly responsible for their distinctive broadband colors. They are characterized by enormous equivalent widths of collisionally excited lines, reflecting extreme ionization parameters and yielding enormous ionization ratios. Diagnostic spectroscopy places these systems near the theoretical maximum-starburst limit on standard BPT diagrams, driven by relatively soft ionizing radiation fields [Rong et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180610149R/abstract). Earlier spectroscopic studies and atlases of luminous blue compact galaxies established important benchmarks for comparing such local emission-line galaxies to high-redshift analogs [Kong et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...389..845K/abstract), highlighting the extreme nature of blueberries even among broader BCD classifications. In broader dynamical contexts, dynamical mass studies of nearby Luminous Compact Blue Galaxies (LCBGs) indicate they share mass properties with Magellanic-type spirals and the more massive irregular galaxies [Garland et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...615..689G/abstract). Spectroscopic studies also reveal intriguing chemical signatures, including localized nitrogen overabundances, despite their overall pristine nature. 

A defining physical trait of Blueberry galaxies is their low gas-phase metallicity, which frequently hovers around $10\%$ of the solar value ($Z \approx 0.1 Z_{\odot}$). For instance, the host environment of the luminous, interacting Type IIn supernova SN 2021adxl—a confirmed Blueberry system—exhibited this extreme metal deficiency, marking it as the lowest reported metallicity for a Type IIn supernova environment [Brennan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A.259B/abstract), [Brennan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231213280B/abstract).

## Morphology and Structural Environments

While early SDSS observations classified blueberries as isolated, point-like [[extreme-starbursts|Extreme starbursts]], high-resolution imaging and spatially resolved IFU spectroscopy (such as SDSS-IV MaNGA) have revealed a significantly more complex structural reality. Many of these active, compact cores are embedded within or located at the outskirts of older, extended low-surface-brightness (LSB) stellar disks [Paswan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929...50P/abstract). The presence of these older LSB disks indicates that blueberries often represent a violent, rejuvenating burst within a pre-existing dwarf galaxy. Detailed star formation histories of dwarf galaxies in groups like M81 suggest that tidal interactions are a primary driver for episodic, intense starbursts in low-mass systems [Miller et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994PhDT........64M/abstract).

The morphological and kinematic features of blueberries strongly point toward interaction-driven starbursts. High-resolution *Hubble Space Telescope* imaging of blueberry candidates has revealed tidal features, such as stellar shells in their outskirts, which are powerful indicators of recent galaxy merging [Rong et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180610149R/abstract). Furthermore, spatially resolved spectroscopy frequently uncovers significant kinematic misalignments between the stellar and gaseous components of the host galaxy [Paswan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929...50P/abstract). 

The location of some blueberries at the extreme periphery of larger LSB galaxies prompted investigations into whether they might be tidal dwarf galaxies formed from collisional debris. However, metallicity diagnostics show a significant $\sim 0.5$ dex discrepancy and differing radial metallicity gradients between blueberries and their LSB companions, implying distinct chemical enrichment histories. Kinematic analyses also suggest that while blueberries are stable against internal motions, they are highly susceptible to tidal disruption by their hosts, indicating they are likely independent dwarf systems in an advanced stage of merging rather than long-lived tidal dwarfs [Paswan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210208240P/abstract). 

## Multi-wavelength Perspectives: Radio and X-ray

Recent multi-wavelength observations have provided critical insights into both the cold gas reservoirs fueling these starbursts and their high-energy emission mechanisms. 

**H I 21 cm Emission and Gas Kinematics**
Interferometric radio observations have successfully mapped the massive neutral gas reservoirs fueling Blueberry galaxies. Observations of the local Blueberry J1509+3731 ($z=0.03259$) reveal a substantial neutral hydrogen mass of $M_{\rm HI} \approx 3 \times 10^8 M_{\odot}$, yielding remarkably high gas-to-stellar mass ratios of $M_{\rm HI}/M_* \approx 2.4$. Despite these massive reservoirs, their extreme star formation rates drive remarkably short gas depletion timescales of $\sim 0.2$ Gyr, reflecting an exceptionally high star formation efficiency [Dutta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.5140D/abstract). Radio imaging also unveiled a spatial offset of $\approx 2$ kpc between the peak of the H I emission and the optical center of the galaxy. This structural decoupling provides compelling kinematic evidence supporting the merger-trigger hypothesis [Dutta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.5140D/abstract).

**X-ray Luminosity and Scaling Relation Tensions**
In the high-energy regime, Blueberry galaxies present a puzzling departure from standard empirical scaling relations. There is a long-standing theoretical expectation that star-forming galaxies, powered by high-mass X-ray binaries (MXRBs) in low-metallicity regions, should contribute significantly to the cosmic X-ray background [Griffiths et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ApJ...360..483G/abstract). Typically, a star-forming galaxy's X-ray luminosity correlates predictably with its star formation rate and metallicity, driven by these high-mass X-ray binaries. However, targeted observations utilizing the XMM-Newton observatory reveal that Blueberry galaxies are, as a whole, distinctly X-ray underluminous [Adamcová et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A..27A/abstract). The leading hypothesis to resolve this discrepancy posits a strict evolutionary delay: these [[compact-galaxies|Compact Galaxies]] may be [[very-young-galaxies|so young]] that they have not yet had sufficient time to form a significant population of evolved, X-ray-emitting binaries. Despite this general trend, rare individual outliers exhibit X-ray emission that significantly exceeds scaling predictions. Their elevated luminosities could be driven by the presence of hidden active galactic nuclei (AGN) or highly obscured ultraluminous X-ray sources (ULXs), though consensus has yet to be reached [Adamcová et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240813572A/abstract).

## Blueberries as High-Redshift Analogs

Because spatially resolved studies of the epoch of reionization remain observationally challenging, Blueberry galaxies provide an accessible window into the extreme interstellar medium conditions of the early Universe. Unlike classic local [[extreme-starbursts|Extreme starbursts]] (such as ultraluminous infrared galaxies) which are heavily dust-obscured, blueberries are UV-bright and generally dust-poor. Their extreme emission-line properties, highly ionized gas, and sub-solar metallicities closely mirror the rest-frame ultraviolet spectra of primeval galaxies at $2.4 < z < 3.5$, which feature weak absorption and bright nebular emission driven by hard radiation fields [Amorín et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017NatAs...1E..52A/abstract). This unique combination of a compact morphology and a dust-poor environment allows them to efficiently leak Lyman continuum photons.

The necessity of studying these local analogs is underscored by morphological and kinematic parallels across cosmic time. For instance, observations of the rest-frame far-ultraviolet morphologies of high-redshift Lyman-break galaxies at $z \approx 4$ often show major-merger fractions of 10-25% and bulge-like structures that are strikingly similar to those found in local compact starburst samples [Lotz et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...636..592L/abstract). 

Recent JWST/NIRSpec observations have further solidified the quantitative link between blueberries and high-redshift populations. Low-mass ($10^5 - 10^9 M_\odot$) galaxies at $3 < z < 10$ reveal a shallow slope at the low-mass end of the mass-metallicity relation (MZR), described by:
$$12+\log(\text{O/H}) = (7.72 \pm 0.02) + (0.17 \pm 0.03) \log(M_\star/10^8 M_\odot)$$
This shallow slope is in excellent agreement with the local MZR probed by Blueberry and [[green-pea-galaxies|Green Pea]] samples [Curti et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...684A..75C/abstract). The matching scaling relations suggest that the star formation feedback mechanisms operating in local blueberries—such as momentum-driven supernova winds—are broadly representative of early Universe galaxy assembly. This connection is further reinforced by structural studies: JWST imaging of galaxies at $z=7-12$ detects clear signatures of mergers and interactions in a significant fraction (e.g., 4 out of 19) of the observed sample, matching the merger-driven activity analogous to those seen in local blueberries [Treu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942L..28T/abstract).

Despite their utility, the analogy is not perfect. While blueberries generally conform to the local 3D Fundamental Metallicity Relation between stellar mass, metallicity, and star formation rate, JWST observations indicate that galaxies at $z > 6$ exhibit a median offset of $\sim 0.5$ dex in $\log(\text{O/H})$, appearing systematically less metal-enriched than local analogs predict [Curti et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...684A..75C/abstract). This challenges the assumption of a universal scaling relation across cosmic time, likely reflecting enhanced stochasticity in gas accretion during the earliest phases of galaxy assembly.

## Evolutionary Origins and Fate

While consensus is building that galaxy-galaxy interactions and advanced mergers are primary triggers for the blueberry phase, the literature notes some ambiguity. It remains actively debated whether all blueberries strictly require a merger origin, or if internal processes—such as massive supernova-driven outflows—might be sufficient to trigger in-situ starburst activity in some isolated systems [Rong et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180610149R/abstract). Adding complexity to this debate, observations of star-forming dwarf galaxies situated in highly underdense "void" environments reveal that they can also exhibit [[extreme-starbursts|extremely high specific star formation rates]] and disk-like morphologies, indicating that extreme starbursts do not exclusively rely on dense, interaction-rich environments to ignite [Hoyle et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AAS...206.1002H/abstract). Supporting this, studies of galaxies in the Bootes Void have shown that they often exhibit unusual or disturbed morphologies and significantly bluer colors compared to typical field populations [Cruzen et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997AJ....113.1983C/abstract).

Regardless of the trigger, the ultimate evolutionary fate of these systems is heavily dependent on their local environment. Because their extreme starbursts process their H I reservoirs with depletion timescales of only $\sim 0.2$ Gyr, the blueberry phase is highly transient. However, field dwarf galaxies below a stellar mass threshold of $10^9 M_{\odot}$ rarely undergo passive quenching purely through internal gas exhaustion. This is reinforced by observations showing that the fraction of passively evolving dwarf galaxies at low luminosities ($M_r > -18$) drops to essentially zero in low-density field environments [Haines et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.381....7H/abstract). Permanently terminating their star formation typically requires external environmental mechanisms, such as ram-pressure stripping or tidal truncation by a more massive neighbor [Geha et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757...85G/abstract). Consequently, after exhausting their rapidly depleting gas reservoirs or undergoing transformation within group settings, post-starburst blueberries are expected to eventually fade into passive [[low-luminosity-galaxies|Low-luminosity galaxies]] or dwarf ellipticals.
