[[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] and [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] are [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] characterized by a fundamental [[negligible-star-formation|cessation of significant star formation]], leading them to occupy the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] in color-magnitude space. Quantitatively, they are defined by a specific star formation rate (sSFR)—the ratio of current star formation to total [[stellar-component|stellar mass]]—that falls significantly below the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] for their respective redshift, in stark contrast to actively forming gas disks or [[violent-starbursts|Violent starbursts]] [Kennicutt et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ARA&A..36..189K/abstract). Observationally, the [[rest-frame-galaxy-properties|rest-frame optical light]] of these galaxies is dominated by evolved [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]], exhibiting prominent [[4000 Å break]] features and heavy alpha-element enhancements that indicate short, intense [[star-formation-history|star formation histories]] followed by rapid quenching. While traditional textbook definitions equate "passive" with a complete absence of nebular emission and interstellar gas, modern spectroscopic and submillimeter surveys reveal a more complex reality. Many quiescent systems maintain substantial dust and [[gas-fraction|molecular gas reservoirs]] and frequently display weak emission lines powered by [[low-luminosity-active-galactic-nuclei|Low-Luminosity Active Galactic Nuclei]] (AGN), shocks, or [[evolved-stars|post-AGB stars]], rather than by young [[ob-stars|OB stars]].

The transition of a galaxy from active star formation to quiescence—a process known as [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]]—is driven by a combination of internal and environmental mechanisms. In massive galaxies ($M_* > 10^{11} M_\odot$), internal mass quenching, likely driven by [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|AGN feedback]], rapidly heats or ejects gas to shut down star formation. Conversely, in lower-mass systems and [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]], environmental processes such as strangulation halt the accretion of cold gas, leading to a more gradual consumption of fuel and longer quenching timescales [Peng et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.521..192P/abstract). Following this cessation of star formation, quiescent galaxies undergo profound structural and kinematic evolution. At cosmic noon ($z \sim 2$), they frequently appear as extremely [[compact-galaxies|compact massive systems]] [Toft et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782...68T/abstract), many of which are observed to be rapidly rotating [Slob et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250604310S/abstract). Over cosmic time, these systems migrate onto the local Mass Fundamental Plane [Bezanson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799..148B/abstract) through a combination of "inside-out" size growth via dry minor mergers and progenitor bias [Damjanov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...91D/abstract), gradually transforming into the dispersion-supported [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] that dominate dense local environments. Transitional populations, such as recently quenched [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] and those undergoing brief [[rejuvenation-episodes|Rejuvenation episodes]], highlight that the pathway to quiescence can be episodic rather than strictly unidirectional.

Understanding the assembly of the quiescent population is a central challenge in modeling the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]] [Madau et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997AIPC..393..481M/abstract). While the comoving mass density of quiescent galaxies has grown rapidly since $z \sim 2$ following a [[morphological-downsizing|Morphological downsizing]] paradigm, recent observations by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have disrupted traditional hierarchical models by uncovering a substantial population of ultramassive quiescent galaxies observationally at $z > 3$ out to $z \sim 5$, while theoretical models predict their existence potentially out to $z \sim 8$. The existence of these fully quenched systems within the first billion years of the universe presents major open questions regarding the physical mechanisms capable of driving such rapid, early quenching. Furthermore, outstanding tensions remain concerning how intermediate-redshift quiescent galaxies retain dust reservoirs without ongoing star formation, the mass-dependent role of progenitor bias versus individual secular growth, and whether variations in high-redshift dynamical mass-to-light ratios reflect evolving structural properties or a fundamentally non-universal [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] in high-dispersion environments.

## Definitions and Observational Characterization

Passive and quiescent galaxies are fundamentally characterized by a dearth of ongoing star formation. Quantitatively, this is expressed through the specific star formation rate (sSFR), defined as the ratio of the [[instantaneous-star-formation-rate|Instantaneous star formation rate]] ($\dot{M}_*$) to the total [[stellar-component|stellar mass]] ($M_*$):

$$ \text{sSFR} = \frac{\dot{M}_*}{M_*} $$

While typical [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] in the local universe exhibit sSFRs of $\sim 0.1 \text{ Gyr}^{-1}$, quiescent galaxies are conventionally defined by an empirical threshold, frequently adopted as:

$$ \log(\text{sSFR} \text{ [yr}^{-1}]) \leq -10.5 \quad \text{or} \quad -11.0 $$

as measured via spectral energy distribution (SED) modeling [Girelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...632A..80G/abstract), [Cecchi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...880L..14C/abstract). Furthermore, near-infrared mass-to-light ratios (e.g., at 3.4 µm) show reduced sensitivity to age and [[chemical-abundances|metallicity]] compared to optical bands, providing more robust stellar mass estimates for quiescent systems [Norris et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832..198N/abstract). The adoption of high-dimensional [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] priors in SED modeling suggests that high-redshift galaxies may have systematically older [[galaxy-stellar-age|stellar populations]] and lower star formation rates than previously estimated [Leja et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877..140L/abstract).

Observationally, this cessation of star formation places them on the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] in color-magnitude space. However, broad-band optical colors alone suffer from a well-known age-dust degeneracy. To robustly separate genuinely quiescent systems from heavily dust-obscured starbursts, astronomers widely employ the [[rest-frame-galaxy-properties|rest-frame]] $U-V$ versus $V-J$ (UVJ) color-color diagram out to high redshifts ($z \sim 3-4$) [Patel et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748L..27P/abstract), [Long et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230504662L/abstract). In UVJ space, quiescent galaxies occupy a distinct sequence characterized by red $U-V$ colors—driven by evolved stellar populations—and relatively blue $V-J$ colors, indicating a lack of significant dust reddening. 

Spectroscopically, the rest-frame optical light of a quiescent galaxy is dominated by low-mass, long-lived stars. A primary diagnostic feature is the [[4000 Å break]] (parameterized as $D_n(4000)$), which arises from the accumulation of metal absorption lines in the atmospheres of older stars. The strength of the 4000 Å break serves as a reliable indicator of the mean stellar age. Additionally, the strength of [[strong-metal-and-balmer-absorption-features|Balmer absorption lines]] (e.g., $\text{H}\delta$) provides tight constraints on the time elapsed since the last major star formation episode. Strong Balmer absorption combined with an absence of emission lines is the defining spectroscopic signature of [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] (often historically referred to as [[e-a-galaxies|E+A galaxies]] or k+a galaxies), marking a transitional phase $\sim 0.1-1.5$ Gyr after a rapid [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] event [Paccagnella et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838..148P/abstract), [Vergani et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...509A..42V/abstract).

While textbook definitions of purely "passive" galaxies traditionally demand a complete absence of [[emission-line-galaxies|nebular emission lines]] (such as [O II] $\lambda 3727$ or H$\alpha$), the modern literature presents a more nuanced and sometimes conflicting picture. For instance, direct ALMA observations of massive quiescent galaxies at $z \sim 0.7$ reveal significant detections of CO, indicating molecular [[gas-fraction|gas fractions]] typically less than 0.1, which is consistent with their expected evolution into local massive early-type galaxies [Spilker et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...860..103S/abstract). Deep spectroscopic surveys routinely detect weak emission lines in structurally and photometrically quiescent galaxies. In these systems, elevated line ratios, such as high [N II]/H$\alpha$, indicate that the ionized gas is not excited by young [[ob-stars|OB stars]], but rather by shocks, post-AGB stars, or [[low-luminosity-active-galactic-nuclei|Low-Luminosity Active Galactic Nuclei]] (AGN) displaying LINER-like spectra [Kriek et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...700..221K/abstract), [Cheung et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.533..504C/abstract), [Newman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862..125N/abstract), [Kriek et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966...36K/abstract). Consequently, the term "quiescent" is increasingly preferred in the literature over "passive" or "[[dead-galaxies|Dead galaxies]]", as it accurately reflects the halting of star formation while acknowledging that these systems often remain nuclearly and dynamically active.

## Selection Techniques and the Color-Color Diagnostic

Observational surveys consistently reveal a bimodal distribution in [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]], distinguishing an actively star-forming [[blue-star-populations|blue cloud]] from a passive [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] [Blanton et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...648..268B/abstract). While a single optical color cut (such as $U-V$) is often used to identify quiescent galaxies at low redshift, this approach suffers from a fundamental age-dust degeneracy: heavily dust-obscured [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] can exhibit reddened colors that mimic the aged stellar populations of truly passive systems. To accurately trace the buildup of quiescent galaxies across cosmic time, multi-wavelength color-color diagnostics are required to break this degeneracy. 

The most widely adopted photometric diagnostic for isolating quiescent systems is the rest-frame $U-V$ versus $V-J$ (UVJ) color-color diagram [Williams et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691.1879W/abstract). By measuring [[rest-frame-galaxy-properties|Rest-frame galaxy properties]], the UVJ diagram leverages two distinct spectral features. The $U-V$ color brackets the [[4000 Å break]] and the Balmer break, making it highly sensitive to the mean age and specific star formation rate of the stellar population. Conversely, the $V-J$ color probes the near-infrared continuum, which is strongly affected by dust attenuation. In the UVJ plane, quiescent galaxies occupy a tight, distinct clump characterized by red $U-V$ colors and relatively blue $V-J$ colors. Meanwhile, star-forming galaxies populate a broad, diagonal sequence spanning from blue to highly reddened $U-V$ and $V-J$ colors, with their position largely dictated by galaxy mass, specific star formation rate, and inclination-dependent dust attenuation [Patel et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748L..27P/abstract). 

The efficacy of the UVJ selection is supported by structural and spectroscopic follow-up. Galaxies falling within the UVJ quiescent wedge typically exhibit compact, bulge-dominated [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] with high Sérsic indices and lack the strong emission lines characteristic of ongoing star formation [Patel et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748L..27P/abstract). This distinct separation allows for the robust calculation of passive galaxy [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|mass functions]] out to $z \sim 2.5$, provided that accurate photometric redshifts are available to calculate the rest-frame colors [Brammer et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...739...24B/abstract).

While the UVJ diagram is highly reliable at low to intermediate redshifts, its efficiency decreases at $z > 3$ where the rest-frame $J$-band shifts into the mid-infrared. Furthermore, the population of young, rapidly quenching systems—such as high-redshift [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]]—can exhibit different spectral energy distributions that fall outside standard UVJ selection boxes. At $z > 3$, traditional UVJ selection may miss up to 70% of massive quiescent systems [Long et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...68L/abstract). The discovery of quiescent systems at $z \sim 3.7-4.7$ with 1-2 Gyr old stellar populations challenges the view that all high-redshift quiescent galaxies are recently quenched post-starburst systems [Antwi-Danso et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230709590A/abstract). 

To address this, new color-color criteria utilizing observed-frame James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) NIRCam filters have been developed to efficiently capture early quiescent galaxies [Long et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...68L/abstract). Advanced manifold learning techniques are also being applied to JWST photometry to pre-select $z > 3$ quiescent candidates with high purity, bypassing the need to pre-compute rest-frame colors [de la Vega et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250109066D/abstract). Recent advancements include the development of automated Random Forest algorithms utilizing combined mid-infrared and optical data to classify galaxies into star-forming, AGN, and passive categories with exceptionally high completeness [Kyritsis et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230311691K/abstract). Additionally, targeted color selections sensitive to the $\sim 1600$ Å break have proven successful in identifying massive, fast-quenching galaxies at $z \sim 2$ shortly after their star formation has ceased, tracking populations transitioning onto the red sequence [Kubo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527..403K/abstract).

## Structural Evolution and Scaling Relations

Quiescent galaxies exhibit a distinct mass-size relation compared to star-forming systems. At all studied epochs out to $z \sim 4$, they are systematically more compact and bulge-dominated, typically characterized by steep surface brightness profiles with a high Sérsic index [Patel et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748L..27P/abstract), [Genin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A.343G/abstract). Additionally, observations from treasury surveys identify a flattening of the quiescent size-mass relation at low masses, where younger, disky [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] diverge from older, spheroidal massive galaxies [Cutler et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967L..23C/abstract). The discovery of massive, highly [[compact-galaxies|compact quiescent galaxies]] at $z \sim 2$ revealed stellar mass densities at least an order of magnitude higher than local ellipticals of comparable mass. These exceptionally dense systems are thought to form via gas-rich major mergers or violent disk instabilities that drive intense central starbursts—such as those observed in [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|High-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies]]—followed by rapid [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] [Toft et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782...68T/abstract).

The average effective radius of the quiescent population grows significantly from $z \sim 2$ to $z=0$. This structural evolution is driven by two primary mechanisms: "inside-out" growth via dry minor mergers, which add stellar mass to the outer envelope without substantially altering the dense core, and "progenitor bias," where newly quenched, physically larger galaxies continuously join the quiescent population at later cosmic times [Tacchella et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844L...1T/abstract). The relative contribution of these mechanisms is mass-dependent. For lower-mass quiescent systems ($M_* \sim 10^{10} M_\odot$), size growth is predominantly driven by progenitor bias. For intermediate-mass quiescent galaxies ($M_* \sim 5 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$), minor mergers and progenitor bias contribute comparably to the size evolution, while the [[high-mass-galaxies|High-mass galaxies]] ($M_* > 10^{11} M_\odot$) experience the least rapid increase in size, as they have already assembled most of their mass [Damjanov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...91D/abstract).

A robust feature of this structural evolution is that the central stellar mass surface density remains remarkably stable and independent of age at a fixed stellar mass [Suess et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899L..26S/abstract). However, there is ongoing debate regarding the apparent compactness of recently quenched [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]]. Some studies suggest that this apparent compactness may be an observational artifact: older quiescent galaxies exhibit strong negative color gradients, while young post-starburst systems have flatter gradients, meaning half-light radii measured in rest-frame optical bands can make young galaxies appear spuriously compact [Suess et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899L..26S/abstract). In stark contrast, conflicting literature explicitly confirms that young quiescent galaxies *are* physically smaller than their older counterparts, demonstrating that this size difference is real and driven primarily by the most massive subpopulation [Clausen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971...99C/abstract), [Yano et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...817L..21Y/abstract).

The dynamical properties of quiescent galaxies tightly correlate with their structure, scaling along the [[stellar-and-galactic-core-structure|Fundamental Plane]]. When formulated in terms of stellar mass surface density rather than luminosity, the "Mass Fundamental Plane" linking stellar mass, effective radius, and velocity dispersion is found to be exceptionally stable since at least $z \sim 1$ [Bezanson et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799..148B/abstract), [de Graaff et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903L..30D/abstract). Indeed, studies of massive compact quiescent (MCQ) galaxies at intermediate redshift reveal that they lie precisely on the local Fundamental Plane zero-point through [[passive-evolution-models|Passive evolution models]] alone, suggesting they represent the structural tail of the normal population distribution rather than a wholly distinct phenomenon [Zahid et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806..122Z/abstract). Remarkably, massive star-forming and quiescent galaxies lie on the exact same mass plane with a low intrinsic scatter [Bezanson et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799..148B/abstract), [de Graaff et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913..103D/abstract). This tight morpho-kinematic coupling implies that the observed redshift evolution of the traditional luminosity-based Fundamental Plane is almost entirely driven by the passive aging of [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]], consistent with standard [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]], rather than by profound shifts in the underlying dark matter fractions.

## Chemical Abundances and Stellar Population Synthesis

Extracting the ages and metallicities from the integrated light of galaxies relies heavily on [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models. Traditionally, this is achieved by measuring specific absorption-line indices—such as the [[4000 Å break]], $H\delta$, and metal-sensitive lines like Mg $b$ and Fe—to break the age-metallicity degeneracy that severely limits broadband photometric analyses. In recent years, high signal-to-noise spectroscopy has enabled full-spectrum modeling of rest-frame optical and near-infrared light, directly constraining the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and individual [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] of quiescent populations across cosmic time [Choi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...792...95C/abstract). Furthermore, spatially resolved kinematics and indices complicate this picture; LEGA-C observations indicate significant gradients in $D_n(4000)$ and H$\delta$ equivalent widths that depend strongly on both the total stellar mass and the local environment for quiescent populations [Sobral et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926..117S/abstract).

The relative abundance of $\alpha$-elements to iron, typically denoted as $[\mathrm{Mg}/\mathrm{Fe}]$, serves as a vital cosmic clock for a galaxy's star formation timescale. Because $\alpha$-elements are rapidly released by [[core-collapse-supernova-rate|core-collapse supernovae]], whereas iron is produced on longer timescales ($\sim 1$ Gyr) by [[red-sequence-sn-ia-rate|Type Ia supernovae]], highly $\alpha$-enhanced stellar populations indicate a short, intense period of star formation followed by rapid quenching. Deep spectroscopic surveys consistently show that massive quiescent galaxies are highly $\alpha$-enhanced, with typical values of $[\mathrm{Mg}/\mathrm{Fe}] \approx 0.3-0.5$ at $z \sim 1.4-2.1$ [Beverage et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966..234B/abstract), [Jafariyazani et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...897L..42J/abstract). This requires extremely short star formation timescales, implying that the progenitors of these systems formed the bulk of their stars in violent bursts at $z_{\mathrm{form}} \gtrsim 3-4$ [Beverage et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966..234B/abstract). 

In both the local Universe and at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 0.7$), quiescent galaxies exhibit strong scaling relations between their mass, age, and [[stellar-populations-by-metallicity|metallicity]]. Observations from the LEGA-C survey demonstrate that average stellar age and total metallicity correlate positively with stellar velocity dispersion, $\sigma_v$ [Beverage et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...948..140B/abstract). This behavior manifests as "downsizing": the most massive galaxies formed their stars earlier and over shorter timescales, chemically enriching more rapidly than their lower-mass counterparts [Damjanov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982..178D/abstract), [Pacifici et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...79P/abstract). Recent reassessments of stellar population scaling relations in the local Universe using [[sdss-luminous-red-galaxy|SDSS data]] reveal a revised bimodal mass-age distribution, alongside a [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Mass-Metallicity Relation]] shifted approximately 0.2 dex higher than previous estimates [Mattolini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250904570M/abstract). At lower masses ($M_* < 10^{11} M_\odot$), analyses of stellar metallicity indicate that environmental strangulation—the halting of the cold gas supply—may be the primary quenching mechanism, operating on timescales of roughly 4 Gyr [Peng et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.521..192P/abstract).

Comparing the chemical abundances of high-redshift quiescent galaxies to their local descendants reveals significant evolutionary tensions. While the mass-$[\mathrm{Mg}/\mathrm{H}]$ relation appears relatively stable over time, the mass-$[\mathrm{Fe}/\mathrm{H}]$ relation exhibits a notably lower normalization at higher redshifts [Leethochawalit et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...885..100L/abstract). Specifically, massive quiescent galaxies at $z \sim 1.4-2.1$ have $[\mathrm{Fe}/\mathrm{H}]$ values that are approximately $0.2$ dex lower than local massive ellipticals, alongside extreme $\alpha$-enhancements not consistently observed in $z \sim 0$ descendants [Beverage et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966..234B/abstract). Because the size growth of these galaxies is thought to be driven predominantly by dry minor mergers—which cannot increase the central $[\mathrm{Fe}/\mathrm{H}]$—the chemical properties of individual quiescent galaxies must continue to evolve post-quenching. The literature currently suggests that reconciling the chemical abundance patterns of $z \sim 2$ systems with today's massive early-type galaxies requires additional mechanisms, such as major mergers or localized central star formation fueled by recycled gas [Beverage et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966..234B/abstract), [Jafariyazani et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...897L..42J/abstract). Furthermore, discrepancies in derived $\alpha$-abundances can sometimes arise from the choice of underlying stellar library, highlighting that independent variation of $\alpha$-elements is critical when fitting high-redshift spectra [Jafariyazani et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986..148J/abstract).

## Quenching Mechanisms and Pathways to Quiescence

The transition of galaxies from the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] to the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] requires physical mechanisms that halt the supply or consumption of cold gas. These processes are broadly categorized into internal mass quenching and external environmental quenching, with the dominant mechanism depending strongly on the galaxy's stellar mass and local environment.

For massive galaxies ($M_* > 10^{11} M_\odot$), internal mechanisms are the primary drivers of quiescence. The most widely invoked process is [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]]. In this scenario, low-luminosity AGN (such as LINERs or radio-mode accreters) drive centrally located winds that inject mechanical energy into the interstellar and circumgalactic medium, heating the gas and preventing further cooling and star formation [Cheung et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.533..504C/abstract). Despite its efficiency, AGN feedback may not act instantaneously. Observational evidence shows that star formation and AGN activity can coexist during gas-rich phases, implying that feedback-driven quenching operates on timescales of $\gtrsim 0.5$ Gyr rather than abruptly shutting down star formation [Yesuf et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...901...42Y/abstract). Furthermore, external feedback, such as an AGN jet from a neighboring galaxy heating the surrounding circumgalactic medium, has been observed to suppress star formation in massive galaxies even within gas-rich cosmic web nodes [Wang et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260120473W/abstract). Galaxy assembly and quenching exhibit mass-dependent behaviors: massive galaxies assemble their stellar mass quickly at early cosmic epochs but have a slower quenching phase, whereas lower-mass systems assemble more slowly but experience a rapid declining phase [Pacifici et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...79P/abstract). Intriguingly, the stellar-to-halo mass ratio (SHMR) is observed to be greater for passive populations at $z \sim 2-3$ than for star-forming ones, implying an unusually high baryon-to-star conversion efficiency at high redshifts prior to quenching [Cowley et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874..114C/abstract).

In lower-mass systems ($M_* < 10^{11} M_\odot$) and [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]], environmental processes become the principal drivers of quiescence. For the local universe, the dominant environmental mechanism is strangulation (or starvation). Strangulation occurs when a galaxy's extended halo of hot gas is stripped or cut off upon entering a denser environment, halting the accretion of new cold gas. The galaxy slowly consumes its remaining gas reservoir, quenching over a typical timescale of $\sim 4$ Gyr [Peng et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.521..192P/abstract). This environmental [[galaxy-quenching-mechanisms|downsizing]] of chemical enrichment is clearly observed in large-scale structures where massive group galaxies reach higher metallicities earlier than their isolated field counterparts. Moreover, [[gas-depletion-and-consumption|molecular gas depletion]] alone may be insufficient to explain galaxy quenching; evidence from nearby galaxies suggests a simultaneous and significant decline in star formation efficiency is required to reach quiescence [Colombo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A.367C/abstract).

In denser group and cluster environments, or during major galaxy interactions, more violent mechanisms such as ram-pressure stripping and [[dwarf-galaxy-physical-processes|tidal disruption]] accelerate the quenching process. These interactions can be highly efficient; for example, tidal gas removal following recent mergers in $z \sim 0.7$ [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] has been observed to strip up to $50\%$ of a galaxy's molecular gas reservoir into extended tidal tails [D'Onofrio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250721249D/abstract). Such rapid gas removal is frequently associated with the short-timescale quenching that produces strong Balmer absorption features typical of [[e-a-galaxies|E+A galaxies]] spectra [Paccagnella et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838..148P/abstract).

The pathway to quiescence is often linked to structural transformation—sometimes referred to as morphological quenching—where the growth of a dominant central bulge stabilizes the gas disk against fragmentation. However, the evolution into a passive state is not strictly unidirectional. Observations of [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] at intermediate redshifts reveal that quenching can be episodic. Approximately $15-16\%$ of the quiescent population at $z \sim 0.8$ has undergone [[rejuvenation-episodes|Rejuvenation episodes]], temporarily returning to the star-forming sequence before quenching again. While these "on-off" cycles complicate simple [[passive-evolution-models|Passive evolution models]], rejuvenation events typically contribute only $\sim 2\%$ to the total stellar mass of the quiescent population, indicating they are a secondary evolutionary channel rather than the dominant mode of red sequence growth [Chauke et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877...48C/abstract).

## Kinematics and Dynamical Maturity

Historically, [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] and [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] were broadly categorized as non-rotating spheroidal systems supported entirely by random stellar motions. However, recent kinematic surveys reveal a far more complex dynamical maturity process. At cosmic noon ($z \sim 1.5-2.5$), high-resolution spectroscopy demonstrates that a substantial fraction of [[high-mass-quiescent-galaxies|High-mass quiescent galaxies]] exhibit strong rotational support, with rotational velocities frequently exceeding $100$ to $300 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Slob et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250604310S/abstract). Correspondingly, morphological studies indicate that up to 30-50% of the quiescent population at $z \sim 1-2$ presents disk-like morphologies, identifying them as passive disks [Bundy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...719.1969B/abstract). 

As these galaxies age, their angular momentum evolves in a mass-dependent fashion. Spatially integrated kinematics from surveys such as LEGA-C indicate that while lower-mass systems largely retain their rotation long after [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]], ultra-massive galaxies ($M_* > 10^{11.3} M_\odot$) gradually lose angular momentum as their [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] age. This transforms them into dispersion-supported slow rotators, a transition likely driven by incoherent minor merging and accretion events [Ji et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241000099J/abstract). Even in the local universe, passive disks still represent $\sim 30\%$ of the quiescent population by number and mass, challenging the assumption that quenching universally destroys disk structures [Fraser-McKelvie et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..117F/abstract).

Despite significant morphological and size evolution over cosmic time, the internal velocity dispersions of quiescent galaxies are remarkably stable. Observations indicate that the velocity dispersion function for massive quiescent systems has remained virtually constant from $z \sim 1$ to the present day [Taylor et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...939...90T/abstract), [Damjanov et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929...61D/abstract). Furthermore, both star-forming and quiescent galaxies populate a tightly coupled Mass Fundamental Plane—linking stellar mass surface density, effective radius, and velocity dispersion—that has maintained a stable intrinsic scatter since at least $z \sim 1$ [Bezanson et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799..148B/abstract), [de Graaff et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903L..30D/abstract). The stability of this relation implies that structural growth, such as size expansion via minor mergers, is strictly constrained to occur within this dynamical parameter space.

The relationship between dynamical mass ($M_{\rm dyn}$) and stellar mass ($M_*$) offers critical insights into the internal makeup of these systems. The dynamical-to-stellar mass ratio increases significantly as the universe ages. For high-redshift progenitors at $z \approx 1.8$, the dark matter fraction within the effective radius is consistent with an inside-out growth model driven by minor mergers depositing dark matter and ex-situ stars at large radii [Mendel et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...87M/abstract). 

However, translating kinematics into stellar mass profiles is complicated by ambiguities in the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. Strong correlations between $M_{\rm dyn}/M_*$ and velocity dispersion at $z \sim 2$ suggest that the IMF may not be universal, with high-dispersion galaxies exhibiting a more bottom-heavy IMF similar to local massive ellipticals [Mendel et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...87M/abstract), [Kriek et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966...36K/abstract). Conversely, conflicting literature argues that a universal IMF is sufficient, asserting that observed variations in the dynamical mass-to-light ratio are predominantly driven by differences in stellar age, metallicity, and dark matter fractions, rather than a shifting IMF [de Graaff et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913..103D/abstract). This tension regarding whether $M_{\rm dyn}/M_*$ discrepancies originate from IMF variations or evolving [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] remains an active area of debate.

## Historical Development and Redshift Evolution

The emergence and assembly of quiescent galaxies represent a critical phase in the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]]. Historically, hierarchical merging models predicted that massive early-type galaxies would form late and slowly. However, recent deep ground-based surveys and JWST observations have pushed the detection of fully quenched systems well into the early universe, identifying massive quiescent galaxies observationally at $z > 3$ out to $z \sim 5$, while cosmological hydrodynamical simulations predict their existence potentially out to $z \sim 8$ [Lovell et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.5520L/abstract). The existence of these heavily $\alpha$-enhanced, extremely dense systems necessitates highly efficient [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] mechanisms operating in the first billion years after the Big Bang. While some semi-analytical models underpredict the number density of these early massive systems by factors of up to 10 at $z \sim 4$ [Girelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...632A..80G/abstract), other cosmological simulations naturally produce passive galaxies at high redshift with number densities in good agreement with HST and early JWST observations [Lovell et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.5520L/abstract).

An emerging evolutionary link suggests that the most massive quiescent galaxies at $z > 2$ were likely sub-millimeter-bright dusty star-forming galaxies in their past, with early major mergers driving rapid black hole growth and subsequent quenching [Araya-Araya et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250926646A/abstract). In addition to massive systems, JWST spectroscopy has enabled the first spectroscopic confirmation of low-mass quiescent galaxies at cosmic noon [Marchesini et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942L..25M/abstract). Even earlier, the discovery of low-mass quiescent systems at $z=7.3$ suggests that rapid quenching in the first billion years may be driven by radiation-driven winds from [[early-type-stars|massive stars]] or early AGN activity [Gelli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954L..11G/abstract).

Following their initial appearance, the quiescent population undergoes dramatic [[number-density-evolution|Number density evolution]] between "cosmic noon" ($z \sim 2$) and the local universe. Measurements of stellar mass functions indicate that the comoving mass density of quiescent galaxies grows rapidly over this period, driving the bulk of the overall mass growth in the universe from $z = 3.5$ to $z=0$ [Muzzin et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777...18M/abstract). This mass assembly is strongly characterized by [[morphological-downsizing|Morphological downsizing]]. The most massive systems form the bulk of their stars rapidly and quench at early epochs, whereas lower-mass systems experience more extended star formation histories and quench later [Pacifici et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...79P/abstract). By $z \sim 0.75$, quiescent systems make up 40-60% of all galaxies at high masses, but only $\sim 15\%$ at low masses [Kajisawa et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PASJ...63S.403K/abstract). In dense early environments, while the [[star-forming-galaxies|star-forming mass function]] in $z \sim 2$ protoclusters matches that of the field, the quiescent mass function displays a notably flatter slope, hinting at accelerated early environmental quenching .

As the population grows, its average structural properties undergo significant transformation. High-redshift quiescent galaxies are systematically more compact than their local counterparts. The size evolution of the quiescent sequence is driven by a combination of dry minor mergers (which deposit material on the outskirts, promoting inside-out growth) and progenitor bias [Tacchella et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844L...1T/abstract). Progenitor bias refers to the continuous addition of newly quenched, structurally larger star-forming galaxies into the quiescent pool at later cosmic epochs, which systematically shifts the median size of the population upward. 

Finally, the traditional view of quiescent systems governed entirely by [[passive-evolution-models|Passive evolution models]] has been challenged by deep spectroscopy. At $z \sim 0.8$, approximately 15-16% of the quiescent population shows evidence of [[rejuvenation-episodes|Rejuvenation episodes]], temporarily returning to the star-forming sequence, though these events contribute only $\sim 2\%$ of their total stellar mass [Chauke et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877...48C/abstract). Additionally, while local elliptical galaxies are typically gas- and dust-poor, many quiescent galaxies at $z \sim 2.5$ possess significant dust reservoirs [Siegel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240911457S/abstract), demonstrating that quenching does not instantaneously strip a galaxy's interstellar medium [Donevski et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...678A..35D/abstract).

## Transitional Populations: Post-starbursts and Passive Spirals

The transition of galaxies from the star-forming sequence to the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] is rarely a simple, instantaneous event. Rather, morphological and spectral transformations often occur on different timescales, producing intermediate subpopulations such as [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] and passive spirals. These transitional systems, frequently found residing among [[green-valley-galaxies|Green Valley galaxies]], provide critical laboratories for isolating the physical mechanisms responsible for [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]].

**Post-Starburst Galaxies**
[[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] (historically termed [[e-a-galaxies|E+A galaxies]]) are identified spectroscopically by strong Balmer absorption lines coupled with a lack of nebular emission lines. This spectral signature indicates a recent, abrupt halt in star formation following a major burst [Paccagnella et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838..148P/abstract), [Vergani et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...509A..42V/abstract). They represent an extremely rapid quenching channel, with quenching timescales significantly shorter than the more gradual decline seen in the broader population of quiescent galaxies [Nersesian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251210383N/abstract). 

Structurally, young post-starbursts at high redshift ($z > 1.5$) are among the most compact quiescent systems, exhibiting flat color gradients and highly concentrated mass profiles [Yano et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...817L..21Y/abstract), [Suess et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899L..26S/abstract). In local clusters, they make up $\sim 7\%$ of galaxies and are strongly associated with recent accretion and environmental quenching [Paccagnella et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838..148P/abstract). High-resolution radio observations of molecular gas in intermediate-redshift post-starbursts reveal that up to $50\%$ of their molecular gas can be stripped into extended tidal tails following a merger, physically starving the central remnant of star-forming material [D'Onofrio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250721249D/abstract). 

**Passive Spiral Galaxies**
The classic Hubble paradigm linking quiescence strictly to [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] is challenged by the existence of passive spiral galaxies. These systems exhibit distinct spiral arm morphologies but possess red optical colors and negligible star formation. In the local universe, they are predominantly found in intermediate-density environments where environmental mechanisms like strangulation and ram-pressure stripping are highly efficient [Goto et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003PASJ...55..757G/abstract), [Shimakawa et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASJ...74..612S/abstract). Passive disks represent a substantial transitional phase; observations suggest that up to $60\%$ of galaxies migrating to the red sequence pass through a passive disk stage before subsequent minor mergers and secular fading transform them into dispersion-supported spheroids [Bundy et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...719.1969B/abstract), [Fraser-McKelvie et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..117F/abstract). In dense local environments, low-mass quiescent galaxies follow a size-mass relation that is systematically larger and flatter than those in lower-density environments, a structural difference likely driven by cluster-specific interactions [Cutler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250410572C/abstract).

**Green Valley Compaction and Rejuvenation**
As galaxies traverse the green valley, their total stellar mass remains largely constant, but their internal structure evolves significantly. This structural compaction indicates that morphological transformation is tightly coupled to the quenching process [Estrada-Carpenter et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951..115E/abstract).

However, quenching is not always a terminal state. While classic [[passive-evolution-models|Passive evolution models]] assume a single, unidirectional shutdown of star formation, recent spectroscopic surveys demonstrate that a fraction of quiescent galaxies experience [[rejuvenation-episodes|Rejuvenation episodes]]. At $z \sim 0.8$, approximately $15-16\%$ of quiescent galaxies show evidence of secondary star formation episodes that temporarily return them to the star-forming main sequence [Chauke et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877...48C/abstract). Such "on-off" cycles are also prevalent in cosmological hydrodynamical simulations, which suggest that a significant portion of local star-forming galaxies have undergone past phases of quenching and rejuvenation [Fortun\'e et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320858F/abstract). Despite their relatively high frequency, these rejuvenation events typically add only small fractions to an individual galaxy's stellar mass [Rutkowski et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250405511R/abstract). 

## Open Questions and Future Directions with JWST

The unexpected abundance of massive [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-redshift quiescent galaxies]] constitutes one of the most pressing challenges in contemporary extragalactic astronomy. Recent observations utilizing JWST spectroscopy and deep ground-based surveys have robustly confirmed the existence of massive, fully quenched systems at epochs as early as $z \sim 5$ [de Graaff et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240405683D/abstract), [Glazebrook et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024IAUS..377....3G/abstract). While some current semi-analytical models systematically underpredict the number density of these early massive systems and struggle to reproduce the extreme rapidity of their required quenching timescales [Girelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...632A..80G/abstract), [Cecchi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...880L..14C/abstract), JWST/NIRSpec surveys of massive quiescent candidates reveal a high diversity of formation and quenching timescales, emphasizing the need for models that can replicate rapid mass assembly in the very early universe [Nanayakkara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...981...78N/abstract). 

The use of 2D grism spectroscopy on lensed galaxies has allowed astronomers to measure spatially resolved star formation histories, notably finding flat age gradients in the inner cores of $z \sim 2$ galaxies, which challenges simple inside-out quenching models [Akhshik et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...900..184A/abstract). Further complicating structural evolution is the identification of [[massive-relic-galaxies|Massive relic galaxies]], which serve as early Universe analogs for the unevolved cores of modern massive ellipticals [Hartmann et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250106050H/abstract).

Another major open question is the role and retention of interstellar dust and gas within these seemingly dead systems. Identifying true quiescence at high redshift has historically been plagued by the dust-age degeneracy, where deeply obscured [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] mimic the red optical colors of mature stellar populations. Deep multi-band JWST imaging and spectroscopy have been critical in suppressing this degeneracy, revealing that many genuine quiescent galaxies at cosmic noon still host significant dust reservoirs [Siegel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240911457S/abstract), [Siegel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985..125S/abstract). Conversely, submillimeter observations of ultramassive quiescent galaxies at $3 < z < 4$ reveal an extreme dust deficiency [Chang et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260122844C/abstract). It remains unknown how intermediate-redshift quiescent galaxies maintain substantial dust masses over long timescales without ongoing star formation. 

The precise physical mechanisms responsible for quenching these galaxies—and maintaining that quiescence—remain highly debated. While [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] is widely invoked to drive massive outflows, some studies indicate that star formation and AGN activity can coexist in gas-rich phases, suggesting that feedback may not act instantaneously [Yesuf et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...901...42Y/abstract). The environmental preference of these early dead galaxies is also intensely debated. While some reside in dense protoclusters [Tanaka et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...59T/abstract), observations reveal that $z > 3$ massive quiescent galaxies often reside in environments no denser than their star-forming equivalents [Binh et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251214881B/abstract).

Furthermore, the structural evolution of these galaxies presents a complex interplay between individual inside-out size growth via minor mergers and "progenitor bias." In the latter scenario, newly quenched, physically larger galaxies continually enter the quiescent population, artificially inflating the average size of the population over time [Damjanov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...91D/abstract), [Tacchella et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844L...1T/abstract). Future observations must determine which effect dominates across different mass regimes and how the cosmic [[early-type-fraction|Early-type fraction]] is built up in both extreme high-density and low-density early environments. 

Finally, spatially resolved JWST spectroscopy is poised to address lingering uncertainties regarding the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] and detailed kinematics of distant passive systems. Observations of massive quiescent galaxies at $1.3 < z < 2.3$ indicate strong correlations between dynamical-to-stellar mass ratios and velocity dispersion, hinting that the IMF might vary systematically even at high redshift [Mendel et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...87M/abstract), [Kriek et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966...36K/abstract). Resolving these tensions requires linking the fossil records of elemental abundances with precise dynamical masses, a frontier where JWST will rigorously test and guide the next generation of galaxy formation models.
