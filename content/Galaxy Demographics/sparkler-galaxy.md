The **Sparkler galaxy** is a highly magnified, high-redshift galaxy at $z = 1.378$, renowned for hosting a system of compact, gravitationally bound stellar clumps. Discovered in the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) Early Release Observations of the [[smacs-j0723|SMACS J0723]] deep field, the galaxy is uniquely accessible due to the profound magnification provided by the massive foreground cluster, which acts as a gravitational telescope [Mowla et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937L..35M/abstract). Through [[galaxy-cluster-lensing|Galaxy Cluster Lensing]], researchers can resolve the Sparkler's internal structures down to parsec scales, revealing a network of numerous unresolved "sparkles" surrounding the main galactic body [Claeyssens et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.2180C/abstract). These features make the Sparkler a premier observational laboratory for understanding the assembly of galaxies and their star cluster systems during cosmic noon.

Photometric and spectroscopic analyses indicate that the Sparkler’s compact sources consist of a diverse mix of active star-forming regions and evolved, quenched [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]]. The globular cluster population features an old group with estimated formation redshifts between $z_{\rm form} \sim 7$ and $11$, alongside younger subpopulations. Because these evolved clusters formed a mere hundreds of millions of years after the Big Bang, their precise dating allows them to serve as independent "cosmic clocks" that trace the expansion history of the early Universe. Furthermore, the metallicity of these clusters provides clues to the early chemical enrichment of the galaxy. Despite having an estimated stellar mass of only $\sim 10^9 M_\odot$, its chemical profile has been argued by some to mimic the assembly history of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]], providing direct empirical evidence for early galactic mass accumulation. However, the ultimate fate of the Sparkler remains actively debated, with conflicting predictions over whether it will evolve into a massive spiral or a much lower-mass dwarf galaxy.

While the Sparkler offers an unprecedented view of early galactic building blocks, the physical nature of its clumps also remains the subject of active investigation. Many of the observed structures are significantly larger and more massive than standard local globular clusters, raising open questions about their dynamical evolution. Theoretical models suggest that the most massive clumps may succumb to dynamical friction and sink to the galactic center to build a nuclear star cluster or bulge, while the surviving outer clumps must undergo severe tidal stripping to approach the sizes of present-day analogs. Furthermore, the Sparkler's unexpectedly high metallicity for its low stellar mass poses an ongoing challenge to standard hydrodynamical simulations of galaxy assembly. Situated alongside other lensed high-redshift objects—such as [[earendel|Earendel]] and early-universe [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]]—the Sparkler galaxy exemplifies the extreme densities and [[accelerated-galaxy-evolution|rapid evolutionary timescales]] that characterize the formation of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] in the first billion years of cosmic history.

## Discovery and Observational Context

The Sparkler galaxy was discovered in the JWST Early Release Observations of the [[smacs-j0723|SMACS J0723]] field (specifically the cluster SMACS J0723.3-7327). Exploiting the massive foreground cluster as a gravitational telescope, observers identified the Sparkler as a highly magnified, spatially resolved system at a redshift of $z = 1.378$ [Mowla et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937L..35M/abstract). The SMACS J0723 field itself is a profound window into the early universe; alongside the Sparkler, the same Early Release Observations unveiled even higher-redshift galaxy candidates extending out to $z \sim 12$ and $z \sim 16$ [Atek et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.1201A/abstract). 

The Sparkler's defining morphological feature is a surrounding network of "sparkles"—compact, unresolved point-like sources that are multiply imaged and gravitationally bound to the host galaxy [Mowla et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937L..35M/abstract), [Claeyssens et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.2180C/abstract). JWST NIRCam photometry and gravitational lens modeling enabled the extraction of precise physical sizes for these clumps, revealing optical rest-frame effective radii ranging from $<10\text{ pc}$ to several hundreds of parsecs, and masses spanning $\sim 10^5\ M_\odot$ to $10^9\ M_\odot$ [Claeyssens et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.2180C/abstract). Complementary to this, advanced deconvolution techniques like the STARRED code were applied to mitigate point-spread function artifacts and specifically extract the spectral energy distributions (SEDs) of the compact sources [Millon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168...55M/abstract). JWST NIRISS spectroscopy independently confirmed the host galaxy's redshift via strong [O III] $\lambda5007$ emission in the main galactic body [Mowla et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937L..35M/abstract). Crucially, these spectroscopic data revealed a stark contrast: while the main body exhibits active star formation, several of the compact red "sparkles" lack emission lines entirely, indicating that they are evolved, quenched stellar systems [Mowla et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937L..35M/abstract). 

## Strong Gravitational Lensing and Magnification

The observation of the Sparkler galaxy at a spectroscopic redshift of $z_{\rm spec} = 1.378$ is made possible through the profound magnification provided by the massive foreground galaxy cluster [[smacs-j0723|SMACS J0723]], located at $z = 0.39$ [Mowla et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937L..35M/abstract). Acting as a gravitational telescope, the cluster's immense dark matter and baryonic mass warp the surrounding spacetime, producing a strong gravitational lensing effect that drastically amplifies the flux and stretches the spatial extent of background sources. This approach builds upon foundational work in similar lensed environments, such as the Hubble Frontier Field MACS J0416, where deep observations have been used to resolve star-forming clumps and complexes down to parsec scales ($<20\text{ pc}$), providing a foundation for the study of high-redshift cluster systems [Vanzella et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646A..57V/abstract). Furthermore, the strategy of systematically exploiting massive foreground clusters to magnify and identify exceptionally bright, high-redshift candidates heavily builds upon the precedents set by programs like the [[relics-survey|RELICS survey]] [Salmon et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889..189S/abstract).

A defining feature of the Sparkler system is the presence of numerous compact sources surrounding the main body of the galaxy. Several of these compact sources have been successfully cross-identified across multiple lensed images, providing definitive proof that they are physically associated with the host galaxy rather than being foreground or background interlopers [Mowla et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937L..35M/abstract). Identifying these multiple images is paramount for robust lens modeling. Parametric lens models of the SMACS J0723 field are constrained by 16 sets of multiply imaged galaxies, leveraging the discovery of 14 new sets (totaling 42 images) revealed by JWST, to reconstruct the foreground mass distribution with a root-mean-square positional accuracy of better than $0\farcs5$ [Pascale et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L...6P/abstract). This precision is essential for deriving accurate local magnification factors, which must be inverted to compute the intrinsic, delensed properties of the Sparkler's stellar clumps.

The extraction of precise photometry and spatial information from these highly magnified images is further enhanced by advanced image deconvolution and point-spread function (PSF) reconstruction algorithms. For instance, wavelet-based two-channel methods such as STARRED have been optimized to decouple point sources from the extended host galaxy emission, enabling accurate measurement of the spectral energy distributions of individual globular cluster candidates within the Sparkler while minimizing deconvolution artifacts [Millon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168...55M/abstract). 

The combination of intrinsic resolving power and the high magnification factor provided by [[galaxy-cluster-lensing|Galaxy Cluster Lensing]] has allowed researchers to probe the Sparkler's internal structure down to parsec scales. This extraordinary spatial resolution is unprecedented for $z \approx 1.38$ systems and demonstrates the indispensability of strong gravitational lensing in transitioning from bulk galaxy studies to the direct observation of [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]] during their early assembly phases.

## Ages and Metallicities of Globular Cluster Candidates

The compact "sparkles" surrounding the Sparkler galaxy represent some of the earliest known quenched stellar systems in the universe [Mowla et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937L..35M/abstract). High-resolution JWST NIRCam photometry and NIRISS spectroscopy have allowed researchers to confidently break the traditional age-metallicity degeneracy that typically complicates the analysis of unresolved [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|extragalactic star clusters]]. 

By applying detailed spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting techniques—including the use of MCMAME, a code tailored specifically for extracting the physical properties of star clusters—researchers have robustly constrained the ages and chemical compositions of these sources [Adamo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525L...6A/abstract). Analyses of the Sparkler's cluster population reveal a mixed distribution, providing a direct glimpse into the early chemical evolution of a galactic halo. One distinct group represents a definitively old population with ages strictly $> 1$ Gyr, potentially extending to the age of the Universe at $z = 1.38$ [Adamo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525L...6A/abstract). Initial SED modeling inferred extreme formation redshifts of $z_{\rm form} \sim 7-11$ for these oldest clusters, meaning these stellar populations began forming a mere $\sim 0.5$ Gyr after the Big Bang, making them some of the earliest observed objects to have subsequently quenched their star formation [Mowla et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937L..35M/abstract). A second subset of clusters is significantly younger and features a broader metallicity spread [Adamo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525L...6A/abstract).

## Galaxy Assembly and Milky Way Progenitors

The Sparkler galaxy serves as a direct observational laboratory for the [[early-galaxy-formation|early assembly phases of a galaxy]], though its ultimate $z=0$ mass is a subject of active scientific disagreement. With an estimated stellar mass of $\sim 10^9 M_\odot$, some analyses propose that the system's mass and chemical properties align with the expected evolutionary track of a [[milky-way|Milky Way]]-like progenitor or similar massive Local Group galaxy at a look-back time of $\sim 9.1$ Gyr [Forbes et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520L..58F/abstract). However, other authors explicitly contest this interpretation.

The debate stems from the exact chemical evolution of the Sparkler. Initial analyses found the oldest clusters to be metal-rich, implying exceptionally rapid chemical enrichment within the first few hundred million years following its initial formation [Forbes et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520L..58F/abstract). In this view, the metallicities of the host's active star-forming regions are more typical of local [[high-mass-galaxies|High-mass galaxies]] ($M_\star > 10^{10.5}\ M_\odot$), an enrichment level unexpected for the Sparkler's current low stellar mass, thereby closely mimicking the hierarchical formation pathways inferred for the Milky Way and its bimodal [[galactic-globular-cluster-system|globular cluster system]] [Forbes et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520L..58F/abstract). 

However, subsequent reanalyses suggest the oldest clusters are actually metal-poor, with younger clusters exhibiting a metallicity spread [Adamo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525L...6A/abstract). The evolutionary trajectory of the Sparkler faces scrutiny when compared against E-MOSAICS hydrodynamical simulations of star cluster formation. Forbes et al. report mild tension between the early formation ages of the Sparkler's clusters and E-MOSAICS predictions for Milky Way-mass progenitors, suggesting early galactic chemical evolution may proceed more efficiently than previously simulated [Forbes et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520L..58F/abstract). Conversely, independent reanalyses of the photometric data argue that the cluster age and metallicity distributions are entirely consistent with E-MOSAICS predictions, concluding that the clusters trace a standard self-enrichment history for a halo destined to become a low-mass dwarf galaxy—a system with a mass of only a few times $10^9 M_\odot$ at $z=0$, significantly smaller than the Milky Way [Adamo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525L...6A/abstract). 

Beyond *in situ* enrichment, the structural properties of the Sparkler strongly support a hierarchical galaxy assembly scenario. The distinct colour distributions, physical sizes, and spatial locations of several compact sources suggest they may be the remnant nuclei or globular clusters of a recently accreted satellite system [Claeyssens et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.2180C/abstract), [Forbes et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520L..58F/abstract). This ongoing merger activity highlights the complex assembly pathways of early galactic disks.

## Dynamical Evolution and Clump Survival

Although the compact sources surrounding the Sparkler galaxy possess stellar ages and metallicities consistent with typical [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|globular cluster populations]], their structural properties reveal significant differences from local analogues. Many of these "sparkles" have estimated masses exceeding $10^6\ M_\odot$ and half-light radii larger than $30\ \mathrm{pc}$, making them about ten times more massive and significantly more extended than typical present-day globular clusters [Giunchi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250713904G/abstract), [Giunchi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.129G/abstract). This discrepancy has prompted extensive theoretical investigation into their long-term dynamical evolution to determine whether they can survive intact to the present epoch.

Analyses comparing the crossing times to the ages of the clumps indicate that approximately $45\%$ to $60\%$ of the detected structures are gravitationally bound [Claeyssens et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.2180C/abstract). Nevertheless, observations of similar high-redshift lensed environments, such as the wider [[smacs-j0723|SMACS J0723]] field, show a general dearth of clumps older than $1\ \mathrm{Gyr}$. This suggests that the typical dissolution timescale for many of these objects is relatively short, and that a large fraction will not survive to the present day [Claeyssens et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.2180C/abstract).

For the gravitationally bound clumps that do survive early dissolution, their subsequent $\sim 9.2\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ of evolution from $z \approx 1.38$ to $z=0$ is governed primarily by dynamical friction and tidal stripping. $N$-body simulations modeling the Sparkler within a dark matter halo of mass $M_{200} \simeq 5 \times 10^{11}\ M_\odot$ demonstrate that dynamical friction strongly dictates the spatial distribution of the system [Giunchi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.129G/abstract). The most massive clumps—those with masses $> 10^7\ M_\odot$—rapidly lose orbital energy and sink toward the center of the galaxy. Rather than surviving as isolated Extragalactic globular clusters, these massive objects are expected to merge and contribute significantly to bulge growth or the assembly of a nuclear star cluster [Giunchi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250713904G/abstract), [Giunchi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.129G/abstract).

The clumps that avoid migrating to the galactic center are subject to severe tidal interactions, particularly tidal shocks induced by passages through the host galaxy's stellar disk. If tidal stripping is ignored, simulations predict that about $40\%$ of the initial clumps would survive to $z=0$, but their final mass distribution would peak at an anomalously high $5 \times 10^6\ M_\odot$, producing a population of overly massive clusters rarely seen in the local Universe [Giunchi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.129G/abstract). When the effects of disk-induced tidal shocks are incorporated, considerable mass is stripped from the outer envelopes of the clumps. This stripping shifts the peak of the surviving mass distribution down to approximately $2 \times 10^6\ M_\odot$, bringing them closer to the mass range of the most massive local globular clusters [Giunchi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250713904G/abstract), [Giunchi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.129G/abstract). 

Despite this mass reduction, tensions remain regarding their exact classification. Simulations indicate that even after nearly $10\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ of tidal stripping, the physical sizes of the surviving outer clumps remain systematically larger than those of standard globular clusters [Giunchi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.129G/abstract). Consequently, the literature is currently ambiguous and actively debates whether these massive clumps are the true progenitors of typical present-day globular clusters, or if they represent a distinct class of oversized starburst complexes whose primary role is to act as building blocks for the central regions of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]].

## Cosmological Implications and Cosmic Clocks

The precise dating of the oldest stellar populations provides a powerful, model-independent constraint on the expansion history of the Universe. Traditionally, the use of [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|globular clusters]] as "cosmic clocks" has been restricted to the $z=0$ Universe, placing lower limits on the present-day cosmic age. The discovery of evolved, gravitationally bound compact sources in the Sparkler galaxy extends this methodology to the distant Universe, leveraging a look-back time of roughly $9.1$ Gyr [Forbes et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520L..58F/abstract). 

By combining the extreme magnification provided by the foreground lens [[smacs-j0723|SMACS J0723]] with high-precision 6-band JWST/NIRCam photometry, astrophysicists can employ sophisticated stellar population models within a Bayesian inference framework to simultaneously constrain the clusters' ages, star formation histories, metallicities, and dust attenuation [Tomasetti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241206903T/abstract).

Age estimates for the Sparkler's clusters point to rapid, early formation. While initial SED fitting established that these clusters had already aged to $\sim 3.9-4.1$ Gyr by the epoch of observation [Mowla et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937L..35M/abstract), a subsequent Bayesian re-analysis of five prominently red compact sources derived an average age of $1.9 \pm 0.4$ Gyr at $z=1.378$ [Tomasetti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241206903T/abstract). Crucially, despite methodological variations in exact age determinations, the inferred ages of the Sparkler's globular clusters are fully consistent with the age-redshift relation predicted by the standard $\Lambda$CDM model using Planck18 parameters [Tomasetti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241206903T/abstract). 

The existence of such mature stellar systems at $z \sim 1.4$ demonstrates that early structure formation proceeded rapidly enough to assemble massive star clusters within the first billion years of cosmic time, without violating the bounds of $\Lambda$CDM [Mowla et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937L..35M/abstract). By serving as cosmic clocks at high redshift, these objects prove that the cosmic expansion rate and the absolute age of the Universe can be constrained across a wide range of epochs. Future high-resolution observations of similar lensed systems are expected to yield larger statistical samples, further refining cosmological parameters through the independent age-dating of high-redshift Extragalactic globular clusters [Tomasetti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241206903T/abstract).

The use of extremely old stellar populations as constraints on early universe formation timescales is not unique to the Sparkler. For example, similarly mature stellar populations have been observed in the member galaxies of XLSSC 122, an [[high-redshift-galaxy-clusters|evolved galaxy cluster]] at $z=1.98$. The derived stellar ages in XLSSC 122 indicate that star formation in these environments commenced as early as $z \sim 12$, further confirming that rapid, early stellar assembly can be successfully traced using evolved systems at the cosmic noon [Willis et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Natur.577...39W/abstract).

## Comparison with Other High-Redshift Systems

The Sparkler galaxy is emblematic of an emerging class of strongly lensed high-redshift systems that reveal the assembly of massive star clusters in the early universe [Mowla et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937L..35M/abstract). While the Sparkler’s compact sources are interpreted as mature, quenched [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|globular clusters]] formed at $z_{\rm form} \sim 7-11$ [Tomasetti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241206903T/abstract), recent observations have identified analogous systems at $z > 6$ where such extreme clusters are either actively forming or in their infancy. 

A prominent example is the Firefly Sparkle at $z \approx 8.3$, a highly magnified galaxy cocooned in a diffuse arc that represents a [[milky-way|Milky Way]] progenitor just $600$ Myr after the Big Bang [Mowla et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Natur.636..332M/abstract). Like the Sparkler, the Firefly Sparkle's mass is highly concentrated into distinct clumps. It contains at least ten unresolved star clusters with individual masses ranging from $10^5 M_\odot$ to $10^6 M_\odot$ and exceptional stellar mass surface densities of $\Sigma_* > 10^3 M_\odot\,\mathrm{pc}^{-2}$ [Mowla et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240208696M/abstract), [Mowla et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Natur.636..332M/abstract). These nascent clusters exhibit nebular-dominated spectra and top-heavy initial mass functions characteristic of extremely metal-poor, gas-rich environments, capturing the immediate aftermath of the rapid chemical enrichment inferred for the Sparkler's own ancient cluster population. In contrast to interpretations of the Sparkler as a single Milky Way progenitor, other massive systems like HFLS3 at $z=6.3$ have been reinterpreted through high-resolution integral field spectroscopy as dense merging groups of multiple galaxies rather than a single massive starburst [Jones et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...682A.122J/abstract).

At a slightly later cosmic epoch ($z \sim 6$), the Cosmic Spear (MACS 0308-zD1) and the Sunrise Arc (WHL 0137-zD1) offer further comparative insights. The Cosmic Spear, currently the brightest known lensed galaxy at $z > 6$, features a tail of multiple [[luminous-compact-star-clusters|extremely compact star clusters]]. These clusters boast effective radii of $R_{\rm eff} \sim 5$ pc and stellar masses of $M_* \sim 10^6-10^7 M_\odot$, yielding unprecedented surface densities of $\Sigma_* > 10^4 \, M_\odot \, \mathrm{pc}^{-2}$ [Abdurro'uf et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251208054A/abstract). Similarly, the Sunrise Arc contains star-forming clumps with delensed radii as small as $3$ pc [Welch et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...943....2W/abstract). In addition to entire star clusters, JWST's time-domain capabilities have enabled the resolution of even smaller structures within highly magnified high-redshift galaxies. For example, observations of the "Dragon arc" behind the Abell 370 cluster at $z=0.725$ have uncovered over 40 individual microlensed stars, primarily red giants and supergiants, within a single strongly lensed galaxy [Fudamoto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240408045F/abstract). Similarly, within the Sunrise Arc lies [[earendel|Earendel]], initially identified as a candidate single star. However, recent models suggest that its rest-ultraviolet to optical continuum is equally well-described by an intermediate-age ($t_{\rm age} \sim 30-150$ Myr), metal-poor ($Z_\star \lesssim 0.1 Z_\odot$) star cluster [Pascale et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988L..76P/abstract). 

Further pushing the boundaries of spatial resolution, strongly lensed systems like the arc SPT0615-JD1 at $z \sim 10$ provide critical insights into galaxy mergers and accretion in the very early universe [Salmon et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...864L..22S/abstract). Observations from the UNCOVER survey have extended these studies to spectroscopically confirmed $z > 12$ galaxies, which exhibit a significant range of effective radii (300-400 pc), adding to the complex picture of [[early-galaxy-formation|early galaxy assembly]] and highlighting a significant scatter in the size-mass relation at early times [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957L..34W/abstract).

The extreme physical conditions within these lensed starbursts share phenomenological links with the newly discovered population of [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]] (LRDs). LRDs are compact, highly reddened $z \gtrsim 7$ sources characterized by "V"-shaped spectral energy distributions and broad emission lines, often hosting overmassive black holes [Loeb et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RNAAS...8..182L/abstract), [Mérida et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251006408M/abstract). Like the massive clumps in the Sparkler and Firefly Sparkle, LRDs undergo an early, intense episode of star formation and may represent the low-spin, ultra-compact tail of early galaxy assembly where environmental interactions boost stellar and black hole mass growth simultaneously [Loeb et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RNAAS...8..182L/abstract), [Mérida et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251006408M/abstract). Together, these populations illustrate that the extreme densities and [[accelerated-galaxy-evolution|rapid evolutionary timescales]] observed in the Sparkler's globular cluster system are a widespread feature of galaxy formation during the first billion years of cosmic history.
