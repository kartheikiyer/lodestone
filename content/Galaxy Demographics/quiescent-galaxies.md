In extragalactic astrophysics, **quiescent galaxies** are defined as systems that have largely or entirely ceased actively forming stars, exhibiting specific star formation rates (sSFR) typically orders of magnitude below those of typical [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]]. Dominated by evolved, low-mass [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]], these galaxies lack the prominent nebular emission lines and blue continuum associated with young massive stars. Instead, they populate the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] on color-magnitude diagrams and occupy a distinct locus in color-color spaces (such as the UVJ diagram) that separates them from dust-reddened star-forming systems. The transition of a galaxy from the active blue cloud to the quiescent red sequence is known as [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]], a fundamental evolutionary milestone that transforms both the photometric and physical properties of the system.

The mechanisms driving this transition are generally divided into two regimes. For [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] and [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]], internal "mass quenching" dominates, primarily driven by feedback from [[low-luminosity-active-galactic-nuclei|Low-Luminosity Active Galactic Nuclei]] (AGN) that heats or expels the interstellar medium [Cheung et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.533..504C/abstract). Conversely, lower-mass [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] are largely subjected to environmental quenching processes, such as strangulation and ram-pressure stripping, which deplete their gas reservoirs as they orbit within dense group or cluster halos [Peng et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.521..192P/abstract). Following the cessation of star formation, quiescent galaxies undergo profound structural and kinematic evolution. High-redshift quiescent systems often appear as compact, rotationally supported disks (sometimes termed "[[red-nuggets|Red nuggets]]"), which gradually lose their angular momentum and grow in size through dissipationless minor mergers to become the extended, pressure-supported [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] observed in the local Universe [Bezanson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697.1290B/abstract). Throughout this structural transformation, the population remains anchored to tightly correlated structural constraints, most notably the [[stellar-and-galactic-core-structure|Stellar and Galactic Core Structure]] (Mass Fundamental Plane).

While classical textbook models describe quiescent galaxies as monolithic, gas-poor spheroids, modern multi-wavelength observations reveal a far more complex picture. For instance, many massive quiescent disk galaxies retain substantial reservoirs of cold atomic hydrogen, indicating that quiescence is often driven by a deficiency in molecular gas or heavily suppressed star formation efficiency rather than a complete absence of fuel. Furthermore, intermediate populations such as [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] and [[green-valley-galaxies|Green Valley galaxies]] highlight the diverse timescales of quenching, with some systems even experiencing subsequent gas accretion and rejuvenation episodes. Currently, the field faces significant open questions regarding the rapid assembly and abrupt quenching of massive galaxies in the early Universe. The recent confirmation of mature, fully quenched systems at redshifts $z > 4$ directly challenges hierarchical assembly models and underscores our incomplete understanding of the early buildup of the quiescent mass density [de Graaff et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240405683D/abstract), [Nanayakkara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...981...78N/abstract). Active research continues to investigate the exact duty cycles of AGN feedback, the role of progenitor bias in size evolution, and anomalous gas-to-dust ratios in early quiescent systems.

## Definition and Spectral Classification

Quiescent galaxies are fundamentally defined by a dearth of active star formation relative to their stellar mass. This is generally quantified by the specific star formation rate, $\text{sSFR} \equiv \dot{M}_\star / M_\star$. While [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] generally lie along the tightly correlated [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] with typical values of $\text{sSFR} \sim 0.1 \text{ Gyr}^{-1}$, quiescent systems fall significantly below this relation. Observational measurements in the local universe often find residual specific star formation rates as low as $\text{sSFR} \sim 10^{-12} \text{ yr}^{-1}$, indicative of near-total suppression of star-forming activity, though the exact fraction of truly "dead" galaxies with zero star formation remains an active question in both numerical simulations and observations [Corcho-Caballero et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.5108C/abstract). Some frameworks argue for a "Galaxy End Sequence," suggesting a continuous distribution of galaxies rather than a strict bimodality, where the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] naturally emerges from minimal color changes once a galaxy falls below a critical sSFR threshold [Eales et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.3125E/abstract).

Spectroscopically, quiescent galaxies are characterized by the absence or extreme weakness of nebular emission lines typically powered by massive, short-lived [[ob-stars|OB stars]], such as H$\alpha$ and [O II] $\lambda 3727$. Instead, their optical spectra are dominated by the photospheres of older, lower-mass stars. This produces strong [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]] (metallic absorption lines), a pronounced 4000 $\mathring{\text{A}}$ break ($D_n(4000)$), and generally red continuum emission [Zahid et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...841...32Z/abstract). However, despite their "red and dead" optical appearances, multi-wavelength indicators reveal nuances; mid-infrared and near-ultraviolet excess emissions are found in 26%-55% of quiescent red sequence galaxies, indicating that minor recent star formation is common even in [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|High-mass early-type galaxies]]. Transitional objects, such as [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] (often designated [[e-a-galaxies|E+A galaxies]] or k+a galaxies), exhibit strong Balmer absorption (e.g., H$\delta$) indicating a recently quenched starburst, but similarly lack active emission lines [Zahid et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..146Z/abstract), [Vergani et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...509A..42V/abstract). It is worth noting that while star formation is suppressed, many quiescent galaxies display weak emission lines characteristic of Low-Ionization Nuclear Emission-line Regions (LINERs) or [[low-luminosity-active-galactic-nuclei|Low-Luminosity Active Galactic Nuclei]] (AGN) [Dickey et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884..180D/abstract), [Kriek et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...700..221K/abstract). At higher redshifts, lensed quiescent galaxies often exhibit notably high [N II]/H$\alpha$ ratios (ranging from 2 to 6) and kinematics indicative of shocks, which may be directly linked to AGN jet-driven suppression of star formation [Newman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862..125N/abstract).

Photometrically, these galaxies populate the red sequence on a color-magnitude diagram. However, selecting quiescent galaxies purely based on a single optical color (e.g., $U-V$) is prone to severe contamination from dust-reddened star-forming galaxies. To break the degeneracy between age-reddening and dust-reddening, two-color diagrams are widely employed. The rest-frame $U-V$ versus $V-J$ (UVJ) color-color diagram has become the standard tool for this classification out to high redshift [Williams et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691.1879W/abstract), [Patel et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748L..27P/abstract). In the UVJ plane, quiescent galaxies occupy a distinct, tightly clustered region characterized by red $U-V$ colors (indicative of an older stellar population and a strong 4000 $\mathring{\text{A}}$ break) but relatively blue $V-J$ colors. This locus cleanly separates them from the broad sequence of dusty star-forming galaxies, which exhibit red colors in both $U-V$ and $V-J$ [Patel et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748L..27P/abstract). 

Once a galaxy arrives in the quiescent region of the UVJ diagram, its subsequent photometric and spectral development is generally described by [[passive-evolution-models|Passive evolution models]]. Without the addition of newly formed stars, the galaxy's stellar population naturally reddens and fades over cosmic time as the most massive remaining stars evolve off the main sequence, maintaining its position within the quiescent sequence.

## Internal Quenching and AGN Feedback

The cessation of star formation in galaxies is governed by a combination of environmental and internal processes. While external mechanisms typically dictate the evolution of satellite systems, internal processes—collectively referred to as mass quenching—dominate the suppression of star formation in [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] and [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]]. Observations of spatially resolved color and [[star-formation-rate-indicators-and-metrics|Star Formation Rate Indicators and Metrics]] (specific star formation rate) gradients indicate that galaxies with stellar masses $M_* > 10^{10.5} M_\odot$ typically quench from the "inside-out," a morphological signature characteristic of internally driven feedback [Liu et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...860...60L/abstract). Following this evolutionary track, the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] exhibits a clear evolutionary progression migrating from the blue cloud, through composite and Seyfert-like AGN host phases, before definitively arriving at the fully quenched population [Leslie et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455L..82L/abstract).

At the high-mass end, achieving and maintaining quiescence poses a theoretical challenge: the continuous cooling of hot gas within massive dark matter halos (typically $> 10^{12} - 10^{13} M_\odot$) must be offset to prevent the replenishment of the cold gas reservoir. Energy injection from [[low-luminosity-active-galactic-nuclei|Low-Luminosity Active Galactic Nuclei]] (AGN) is widely accepted as the primary feedback mechanism responsible for this regulation. The connection between central black holes and [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] is strongly supported by local demographic studies, which demonstrate that the probability of a galaxy being quiescent correlates more tightly with its directly measured supermassive black hole mass than with its total stellar mass [Terrazas et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830L..12T/abstract). 

Despite broad consensus on the necessity of AGN feedback, the specific physical modes by which the energy couples to the interstellar medium remain a subject of active modeling and debate. Theoretical frameworks generally invoke two regimes: a high-accretion "quasar mode" that can rapidly expel cold gas via radiation-driven winds, and a low-accretion Radio mode (or kinetic mode) that provides continuous mechanical heating to the circumgalactic medium, preventing gas from condensing. Empirical evidence for mechanical heating has been identified in a class of local quiescent galaxies dubbed [[red-geysers|Red geysers]]. These systems host low-luminosity AGN that drive bisymmetric, large-scale winds; kinematic calculations indicate these winds contain sufficient mechanical energy to heat ambient cooler gas and suppress star formation without requiring the complete ejection of the gas reservoir [Cheung et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.533..504C/abstract). Furthermore, the existence of a distinct population of "Optically Quiescent Quasars"—galaxies harboring IR-luminous AGN but displaying entirely passive optical spectra—suggests that some of these systems feature high covering factors of obscuring matter or host AGN that have only recently "switched on" to maintain quiescence [Greenwell et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.52712065G/abstract).

The importance of AGN feedback extends to higher redshifts, during the epochs when the bulk of massive galaxy quenching occurred. Deep X-ray observations reveal widespread low-luminosity AGN activity among massive quiescent galaxies at $z \sim 2$, suggesting that nuclear activity plays a critical role in impeding star formation during the earliest phases of galaxy assembly [Olsen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764....4O/abstract). Furthermore, statistical modeling of the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] of AGN host galaxies indicates that a subset of these systems experience rapid drops in their star formation rates over short timescales ($\lesssim 2 \text{ Gyr}$), pointing to the energetic efficiency of black hole-driven quenching [Smethurst et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.2986S/abstract).

While mass quenching is classically associated with giant ellipticals, recent spectroscopic surveys have complicated this picture by revealing AGN-like emission line ratios in the lowest-mass isolated quiescent galaxies ($M_* = 10^{9.0} - 10^{9.5} M_\odot$). Because these dwarf systems reside at distances $\gtrsim 1.5 \text{ Mpc}$ from any massive neighbors, environmental quenching is negligible, suggesting that internal AGN feedback may be capable of driving quiescence even in completely isolated [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] [Dickey et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884..180D/abstract), [Witherspoon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..192W/abstract). Complementing this, the recent discovery of isolated quiescent dwarf galaxies in the nearby Universe—such as the [[luminous-passive-dwarf-galaxies|Luminous passive dwarf galaxies]] finding at a tip of the [[luminous-red-giant-stars|Luminous red giant stars]] distance of 30 Mpc—further suggests that star formation can be shut down by internal feedback or high-velocity interactions even far from massive cluster halos [Carleton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961L..37C/abstract).

## Environmental Quenching and Satellite Evolution

The observed dichotomy in galaxy properties is strongly correlated with local environment, a phenomenon formalized as the environment-density relation. Galaxies residing in high-density environments, such as massive groups and clusters, exhibit a significantly higher [[early-type-fraction|Early-type fraction]] and lower specific star formation rates compared to their field counterparts [Tanaka et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....128.2677T/abstract). Consequently, [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] orbiting within massive dark matter halos are subjected to external physical processes that precipitate [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]]. In contrast to [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]], whose star formation is primarily suppressed by internal mechanisms such as [[low-luminosity-active-galactic-nuclei|Low-Luminosity Active Galactic Nuclei]] (AGN) feedback ("mass quenching"), satellites are predominantly affected by environmental quenching.

As a galaxy falls into a larger halo, several external mechanisms act to deplete its gas reservoir. The complete or partial removal of the hot [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] (CGM) halts the accretion of fresh cosmological gas, a process known as strangulation (or starvation). Spectroscopic analyses of [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]] in the local universe indicate that strangulation is the primary quenching mechanism for galaxies with stellar masses $M_* < 10^{11} M_\odot$ [Peng et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.521..192P/abstract). Because strangulation relies on the gradual consumption of the remaining cold gas via star formation, it operates on extended timescales of $\tau \approx 4$ Gyr [Peng et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.521..192P/abstract). This contrasts with ram-pressure stripping, wherein the hydrodynamic interaction with the intracluster medium rapidly and directly removes the cold interstellar gas. While ram-pressure stripping can be highly efficient in the dense cores of massive clusters, the persistence of blue satellites and the relatively long duration of the quenching phase suggest that strangulation dominates the global [[satellite-quenching|Satellite quenching]] budget.

The external nature of these quenching processes leaves distinct signatures on the structural and chemical evolution of satellite galaxies. Observations of specific star formation rate gradients reveal that low-mass galaxies ($M_* < 10^{10} M_\odot$) typically quench from the "outside-in," as external gas reservoirs are stripped or starved while central star formation persists [Liu et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...860...60L/abstract). Interestingly, the structural impact of these dense environments is profound: low-mass quiescent galaxies residing within dense groups and clusters are typically $\sim 70\%$ larger and more disk-like than their isolated field counterparts. This structural distinction suggests that for low-mass satellites, quiescence is often driven by the direct environmental quenching of intact star-forming disks rather than by violent, merger-driven morphological transformations [Yoon et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957...59Y/abstract).

The efficiency of satellite quenching evolves strongly with cosmic time. At $z \sim 1$, satellites dominate the low-mass red sequence, but the subsequent growth of the quiescent population over the next several billion years is primarily driven by the quenching of low-mass central galaxies [Tinker et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...778...93T/abstract). In the contemporary universe ($z \lesssim 1$), environmental quenching becomes the overwhelmingly dominant driver for low-mass galaxies, with its overall efficiency increasing toward the present day [Darvish et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...825..113D/abstract). The role of the local environment at higher redshifts ($z > 1.5$) was historically ambiguous in the literature. However, recent observations have pushed these limits further; JWST/NIRISS has provided the first spectroscopic confirmation of low-mass ($M_* \approx 10^{10} M_\odot$) quiescent galaxies at [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] (cosmic noon) [Marchesini et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942L..25M/abstract). Detailed statistical studies show that the quenching probability in dense environments remains significantly higher than in the field out to $z \sim 3$, with massive galaxies being nearly twice as likely to quench in overdensities compared to isolated regions [Taylor et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.2297T/abstract).

## Structural Scaling Relations and the Fundamental Plane

The structural and kinematic properties of quiescent galaxies are tightly correlated, traditionally characterized by the Fundamental Plane (FP). Historically used to study [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], the FP links a galaxy's [[stellar-and-galactic-core-structure|Stellar and Galactic Core Structure]] (effective radius, $R_e$, and velocity dispersion, $\sigma$) and surface brightness. While the FP is well-defined in the local universe, the classical luminosity-based FP exhibits significant zero-point evolution with redshift, scaling as $\Delta\log_{10} M/L_g \propto (-0.49 \pm 0.03)z$ [van de Sande et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793L..31V/abstract). This evolution is primarily driven by the aging of [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]], which causes the dynamical [[galaxy-mass-fractions|Galaxy mass fractions]] (mass-to-light ratio, $M_{\rm dyn}/L$) to increase over cosmic time [de Graaff et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903L..30D/abstract). At intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 0.8$), the predictions of [[passive-evolution-models|Passive evolution models]] alone are sufficient to account for these luminosity differences and bring massive quiescent galaxies onto the local FP [Zahid et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806..122Z/abstract).

To mitigate the transient effects of stellar population evolution, the relation is often recast as the Mass Fundamental Plane (MFP), which replaces surface brightness with [[galaxy-central-density|Galaxy central density]] (stellar mass surface density, $\Sigma_*$). Unlike the luminosity-based FP, the MFP is remarkably stable across cosmic time. Observations demonstrate that [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] ($M_* > 10^{10.5} M_\odot$) lie on a stable MFP from $z=0$ out to at least $z \sim 1$ [de Graaff et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903L..30D/abstract), with the plane's orientation remaining independent of redshift [Zahid et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...821..101Z/abstract). The intrinsic scatter about the mass FP is extremely tight, measured at just $0.055$ dex at $z \sim 0$ and $0.08$ dex at $z \sim 0.7$ [Bezanson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799..148B/abstract). Surprisingly, both massive [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] and quiescent galaxies fall on nearly the identical mass FP, suggesting a coupled structural and kinematic evolution that is largely insensitive to a galaxy's immediate star formation activity [Bezanson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799..148B/abstract), [de Graaff et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903L..30D/abstract).

The position of a galaxy on the MFP is deeply connected to its internal structure and the assumption of homology. However, quiescent galaxies are not strictly homologous; structural parameters such as the [[core-s-rsic-galaxy-models|Core-Sérsic Galaxy Models]] (Sérsic index) scale significantly with both velocity dispersion and proxies for stellar age, such as the $D_n(4000)$ index [Zahid et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...841...32Z/abstract). When non-homology is properly accounted for in the virial theorem galaxies appear to be in approximate virial equilibrium, yielding a remarkably linear scaling between stellar and dynamical mass.

The stability of the MFP provides a stringent constraint on [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. While the standard size-mass relation exhibits strong redshift evolution due to the inside-out growth of galaxies, the underlying MFP remains invariant. Because both high-redshift [[compact-galaxies|Compact Galaxies]] and extended local quiescent galaxies populate the same structural relation [Zahid et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...821..101Z/abstract), the structural transformation of galaxies—such as size growth via dry minor mergers—is constrained to occur *within* the MFP [de Graaff et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903L..30D/abstract).

## Morphological Evolution and Compactness

High-redshift ($z > 2$) quiescent galaxies exhibit profound structural differences from their local counterparts, typically presenting as highly [[compact-galaxies|Compact Galaxies]]. Often termed "[[red-nuggets|Red nuggets]]," these early passive systems have effective radii that are roughly 5 to 6 times smaller than local early-type galaxies—averaging $r_e \sim 0.6$ kpc for masses of $M_* \sim 10^{11} M_\odot$ [Straatman et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...808L..29S/abstract). The formation of these extreme objects is strongly linked to high-redshift [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|High-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies]] (SMGs); the matched formation redshifts and space densities suggest that SMGs act as the direct, gas-rich progenitors of compact quiescent galaxies at $z=2$ [Toft et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782...68T/abstract).

Contrary to the classical view that [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] directly produces dispersion-supported spheroids, structural and kinematic analyses reveal that a significant fraction of these early [[high-mass-galaxies|High-mass galaxies]] are disk-dominated "fast rotators." Observations of gravitationally lensed high-redshift systems show high intrinsic ellipticities ($0.75-0.85$) and substantial rotational support, with $V/\sigma$ ratios of $1.7-2.3$ [Newman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862..126N/abstract). This indicates that the initial cessation of star formation preserves the progenitor's underlying disk structure, meaning morphological transformation typically follows, rather than coincides with, quenching.

Over cosmic time, these compact systems undergo significant structural evolution, transforming into the extended, slow-rotating [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] seen today. This size growth is broadly consistent with an "[[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]]" scenario, wherein compact high-redshift galaxies form the dense cores of local ellipticals, while their extended envelopes are built gradually through dissipationless minor mergers [Bezanson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697.1290B/abstract). Supporting this picture, spatially resolved studies show that older quiescent galaxies exhibit [[steep-age-and-metallicity-gradients|Steep age and metallicity gradients]] (negative color gradients), consistent with the accretion of younger or more metal-poor material in their outskirts [Suess et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899L..26S/abstract). As these galaxies grow in mass and size through minor mergers, they systematically lose angular momentum, transitioning into dynamically hot, dispersion-supported structures [Newman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862..126N/abstract), [Ji et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241000099J/abstract). 

However, the literature remains ambiguous regarding the exact relative contributions of individual galaxy growth (via mergers) versus [[accelerated-galaxy-formation|Accelerated galaxy formation]] (progenitor bias) in driving the observed size evolution of the overall quiescent population. Progenitor bias occurs because galaxies that quench at later cosmic epochs are formed from larger star-forming progenitors, naturally raising the average size of the passive population without requiring individual galaxies to expand. While minor mergers appear to dominate the structural evolution of the most massive systems ($M_* > 10^{11} M_\odot$), progenitor bias is increasingly recognized as a comparable or dominant factor for lower-mass quiescent galaxies ($M_* \sim 10^{10} M_\odot$) [Damjanov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...91D/abstract).

## Transition Populations: Post-starburst and Green Valley

The migration of galaxies from the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] (the blue cloud) to the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] involves transitional phases characterized by intermediate colors and recent starburst or rapid quenching signatures. The study of these transition populations, notably [[green-valley-galaxies|Green Valley galaxies]] and [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]], places critical constraints on the quenching timescale and the physical mechanisms driving [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]].

Galaxies exhibiting colors intermediate between active star formation and passive quiescence are typically classified as [[green-valley-galaxies|Green Valley galaxies]]. Analyses of their [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and trajectories in color-mass space indicate that the transition through the green valley is relatively rapid, generally lasting $<2\text{ Gyr}$ [Trayford et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.3925T/abstract). Their intermediate morphologies and compactness suggest that morphological transformation and star formation cessation are concurrent or closely linked processes during this phase [Gu et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...855...10G/abstract), [Estrada-Carpenter et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951..115E/abstract). 

A more extreme transitional phase is represented by post-starburst galaxies (often historically termed [[e-a-galaxies|E+A galaxies]]). These systems are spectroscopically defined by strong Balmer absorption lines (e.g., $\text{H}\delta$) coupled with a lack of prominent nebular emission lines, indicating that a major starburst was abruptly truncated within the preceding $1\text{--}2\text{ Gyr}$. Observationally, massive [[compact-galaxies|Compact Galaxies]] at $0.2 < z < 0.8$ display physical properties and stellar population ages ($<1\text{ Gyr}$) that strongly link them as direct progenitors to the population of massive compact galaxies on the quiescent sequence [Zahid et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..146Z/abstract). Furthermore, observations of their [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] reveal enhanced $\text{Mg II}$ absorption and high-velocity cool gas outflows, implying that violent feedback during the starburst phase rapidly ejects or heats the gas reservoir [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250622287H/abstract).

While the dominant evolutionary pathway proceeds from blue to red, the process is not strictly unidirectional. A fraction of the quiescent population undergoes [[rejuvenation-episodes|Rejuvenation episodes]], where gas accretion or minor mergers trigger secondary star formation. Investigating the mechanisms that trigger such secondary bursts, recent studies reveal that roughly 55% of [[massive-blue-star-forming-galaxies|Massive blue star-forming galaxies]] with dense cores show clear evidence of recent central rejuvenation, often catalyzed by internal secular processes like bars or galaxy interactions. At $z \sim 0.8$, approximately $16\%$ of previously quiescent galaxies are found to have experienced rejuvenation events, which typically last ${\sim}0.7\text{ Gyr}$ and contribute ${\sim}2\%$ to the total stellar mass of the galaxy [Chauke et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877...48C/abstract). Additionally, the literature notes the existence of "Red Misfits"—galaxies that display red optical colors but remain actively [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], constituting ${\sim}11\%$ of galaxies across stellar masses in the local Universe.

## Kinematics and the Evolution of Rotational Support

Historically, [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] was thought to coincide directly with morphological transformation into dispersion-supported spheroids. However, spatially resolved kinematics of high-redshift quiescent systems reveal that quenching typically precedes structural transformation. At $z \gtrsim 2$, massive quiescent galaxies frequently possess prominent stellar disks and are classified as fast rotators. Lensed observations of these early [[compact-galaxies|Compact Galaxies]] show highly flattened morphologies and a high degree of rotational support [Newman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862..126N/abstract). Although their rotational support is slightly lower than that of coeval [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] of similar mass, they retain a substantial fraction of their initial angular momentum.

As the universe evolves, the quiescent population undergoes significant post-quenching kinematic evolution, gradually losing rotational support to become the pressure-supported slow rotators typical of local [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. This transition is heavily mass-dependent. Spatially resolved spectroscopy from the LEGA-C survey at $0.6 < z < 1$ demonstrates that the most [[high-mass-galaxies|High-mass galaxies]] ($M_* > 2 \times 10^{11} M_\odot$) have largely lost their dynamical support from rotation by cosmic noon. In contrast, intermediate-mass quiescent galaxies retain significant rotation long after quenching [Ji et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241000099J/abstract). 

The gradual loss of angular momentum and the corresponding shift from fast rotators to slow rotators is primarily attributed to [[ex-situ-galaxy-growth|Ex-situ galaxy growth]]. Incoherent, dissipationless minor mergers continuously erode the coherent rotation of the stellar disk while simultaneously building up an extended, dispersion-dominated envelope [Newman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862..126N/abstract), [Ji et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241000099J/abstract). This picture is further supported by chemical abundance studies; observations reveal that substantial stellar mixing through mergers likely follows quenching in order to match the properties of present-day ellipticals [Jafariyazani et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986..148J/abstract). Crucially, the gradual loss of angular momentum occurs without disrupting the overall virial equilibrium of the galaxy.

## Gas Reservoirs and Dust Content

Standard textbook models typically characterize quiescent galaxies as gas-poor systems devoid of the raw materials necessary for star formation. However, recent multi-wavelength surveys have challenged this monolithic picture. Consistent with the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] framework, a substantial fraction of massive quiescent central disk galaxies retain remarkably large atomic gas reservoirs. These [[h-i-rich-galaxies|H I-rich galaxies]] frequently exhibit symmetric double-horn structures, indicating massive, regularly rotating [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] (atomic gas (H I) disks) comparable to those found in [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] [Zhang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884L..52Z/abstract). 

The suppressed star formation in these systems is therefore not driven by an absolute absence of cold gas, but rather by a critical deficiency in molecular gas ($H_2$) and a significantly reduced star formation efficiency. Observations indicate that the initial departure from the star-forming main sequence is primarily driven by the exhaustion of the molecular gas reservoir. Once a galaxy is fully quenched, any further suppression of star formation is dictated by a sharp drop in the efficiency with which any remaining $H_2$ is converted into stars [Colombo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...644A..97C/abstract). The exact physical mechanisms that prevent the abundant H I from condensing into star-forming molecular clouds remain an active area of inquiry in models of [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]].

The dust content of quiescent galaxies also undergoes significant evolution post-quenching. In the local Universe, quiescent galaxies typically exhibit low gas and dust fractions, consistent with the steady depletion of their interstellar medium on a [[gas-depletion-and-consumption|Gas Depletion and Consumption]] (depletion timescale) of $\sim 2$ Gyr [Gobat et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...644L...7G/abstract). At cosmic noon ($z \sim 2$), detailed multi-band imaging similarly reveals significant reddening, indicative of non-negligible dust reservoirs that subsequently decline as these galaxies evolve toward $z=0$ [Siegel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985..125S/abstract).

At higher redshifts, estimating the remaining gas reservoirs of quiescent galaxies is complicated by an evolving gas-to-dust ratio. Astronomers frequently use the long-wavelength dust continuum as a proxy for cold gas mass. However, ALMA observations reveal severe dust deficiencies, complicating efforts to measure residual cold gas reservoirs using standard [[star-formation-rate-tracers|Star formation rate tracers]]. Cosmological simulations suggest that dust is preferentially destroyed in quiescent environments, rendering the dust continuum an unreliable tracer of $H_2$ in high-redshift quiescent systems [Spilker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250716914S/abstract). 

## Historical Development and the Downsizing Paradigm

Historically, the theoretical understanding of early-type quiescent galaxies was anchored in the monolithic collapse model, which posited that these systems formed the bulk of their stellar mass in a single, rapid burst of star formation at high redshift. Following the establishment of the $\Lambda$CDM cosmological model, this view was largely supplanted by the framework of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] (hierarchical assembly). Tracking these systems over time by analyzing numerical simulations reveals three distinct evolutionary regimes: gas accretion dominates structural growth at high redshift, *in situ* star formation peaks at intermediate redshifts, and dissipationless mergers drive mass assembly at low redshift [van de Voort et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..778V/abstract).

However, as observational constraints on the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]] improved, it became evident that the baryonic growth of galaxies does not strictly mirror the bottom-up assembly of dark matter halos. Instead, the galaxy population exhibits a phenomenon known as "downsizing." In the downsizing paradigm, [[high-mass-galaxies|High-mass galaxies]] assemble their stellar mass much more rapidly than [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], though they undergo a comparatively slower [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] phase [Gu et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...855...10G/abstract). Spectral energy distribution modeling of historical star formation rates quantifies this effect: massive galaxies reach their peak star formation in $\lesssim 2$ Gyr but decrease their star formation slowly, whereas lower-mass galaxies grow much more slowly, taking $\gtrsim 4$ Gyr to reach their peak before rapidly quenching over shorter timescales [Pacifici et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...79P/abstract). 

The global build-up of the quiescent population is governed by this mass-dependent evolution. Tracking [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] out to $z = 4$ reveals that the mass density of the quiescent population increases dramatically over cosmic time, scaling since $z=3.5$ [Muzzin et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777...18M/abstract). For galaxies with $M_* \gtrsim 3 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$, the mass density grows by a factor of roughly 10 between $z \sim 2$ and the local universe [Brammer et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...739...24B/abstract). Despite the overarching success of the downsizing paradigm, high-resolution spectroscopy has confirmed the existence of fully quenched, massive galaxies at $z \sim 3-5$ [Nanayakkara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...981...78N/abstract). These systems indicate that highly efficient, monolithic-like collapse and rapid quenching mechanisms can and do operate in the very early Universe.

## High-Redshift Quiescence and Early Cosmic Dawn

The advent of JWST has fundamentally revised the observational census of the early Universe, revealing a robust population of quiescent [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] at redshifts $z > 3$ and extending into the [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] (Cosmic Dawn, $z > 5$). Prior to these observations, hierarchical assembly models predicted that such early epochs would be dominated almost exclusively by actively star-forming systems and [[extreme-starbursts|Extreme starbursts]]. However, recent spectroscopic confirmations have identified galaxies that completed their [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and underwent [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] within the first billion years of cosmic time [de Graaff et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240405683D/abstract).

A prototypical example is the massive quiescent galaxy RUBIES-EGS-QG-1 at $z = 4.896$. Spectroscopic analysis reveals a stellar mass of $M_* \approx 10^{10.9} M_\odot$ formed in an intense, short-lived burst, followed by a rapid and persistent cessation of star formation [de Graaff et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240405683D/abstract). The existence of these objects implies that rapid early quenching mechanisms operate across a wide range of halo masses during [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] [Gelli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954L..11G/abstract). Furthermore, early galaxies at $z > 7$ exhibit chemical abundances significantly lower than expected from local scaling relations, suggesting they are subject to continuous dilution by the infall of pristine gas during these formative epochs [Heintz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7.1517H/abstract).

The discovery of these galaxies introduces significant tension with theoretical models, which systematically underpredict the number density of massive quiescent galaxies at $z > 4$ [Cecchi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...880L..14C/abstract). Reconciling the density discrepancy requires physical models capable of highly efficient baryon conversion into stars, followed by abrupt quenching. Proposed mechanisms include super-Eddington accretion driving extreme [[low-luminosity-active-galactic-nuclei|Low-Luminosity Active Galactic Nuclei]] (AGN) feedback, which rapidly heats or expels the interstellar medium before rejuvenation can occur [Turner et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250916111T/abstract). 

Structurally, these early quenched systems push the limits of the compactness observed at cosmic noon, exhibiting exceptional central mass surface densities that indicate a highly dissipative formation phase [Estrada-Carpenter et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...898..171E/abstract). Furthermore, interstellar medium properties in high-redshift quiescent galaxies can diverge significantly from local scaling relations. For instance, massive quiescent galaxies at $z \sim 1$ reveal severe dust deficiencies, complicating efforts to measure residual cold gas reservoirs using standard [[star-formation-rate-tracers|Star formation rate tracers]] [Spilker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250716914S/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite significant observational and theoretical progress, the physical mechanisms that assemble and maintain the population of quiescent galaxies remain a subject of active debate. Current research is largely focused on reconciling theoretical predictions with extreme high-redshift observations, disentangling the structural evolution of the red sequence, and understanding the precise duty cycles of gas accretion and depletion.

A primary challenge to current cosmological models is the existence of massive, fully quenched galaxies in the early Universe. Recent observations have confirmed the presence of quiescent galaxies at $z > 4$ that assembled rapidly and quenched abruptly, challenging current frameworks of hierarchical structure formation [de Graaff et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240405683D/abstract). Conversely, in the local universe and within dense environments, simulations frequently suffer from the over-quenching problem. Semi-analytic models tend to over-predict the fraction of red satellite galaxies by assuming the instantaneous stripping of gas reservoirs upon accretion. Observations suggest instead that environmental quenching relies on delayed processes like strangulation, operating on extended timescales [Peng et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.521..192P/abstract).

The structural evolution of quiescent galaxies from compact "fast rotators" at cosmic noon to extended, dispersion-supported ellipticals locally is also highly complex. It is actively debated whether this size growth is driven by individual galaxy evolution (such as dissipationless minor mergers) or by progenitor bias—the continuous addition of newly quenched, physically larger galaxies to the quiescent population. For intermediate-mass quiescent galaxies, size growth is largely driven by progenitor bias [Damjanov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...91D/abstract). In contrast, the structural evolution of the most [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] is dominated by individual growth via minor mergers [Damjanov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...91D/abstract), [Bezanson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697.1290B/abstract).

Another major open question is how galaxies maintain [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] while retaining substantial gas. Observations reveal that a vast majority of massive, quiescent central disk galaxies harbor large reservoirs of cold atomic hydrogen. The dynamics of the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] (CGM) are critical to solving this puzzle. For example, massive [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] exhibit strong Mg II absorption out to $1$ Mpc, indicating that short-lived starbursts expel massive amounts of cool gas into the CGM [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250622287H/abstract). 

Finally, the physical nature and frequency of [[rejuvenation-episodes|Rejuvenation episodes]] remain poorly understood. While crossing the green valley is typically modelled as a unidirectional process, roughly $15\%$ to $16\%$ of massive quiescent galaxies at $0.5 < z < 1.5$ show evidence of minor secondary star formation [Chauke et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877...48C/abstract), [Rutkowski et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983L..32R/abstract). Whether these "red misfits" represent a distinct evolutionary channel or merely stochastic fluctuations in gas accretion remains a subject of ongoing investigation.
