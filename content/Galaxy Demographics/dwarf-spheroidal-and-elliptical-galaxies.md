## Article

**Dwarf spheroidal (dSph) and dwarf elliptical (dE) galaxies** are the most numerous yet least luminous dynamically hot stellar systems in the Universe. Classified broadly as [[low-luminosity-galaxies|Low-luminosity galaxies]], these pressure-supported systems are characterized by a highly deficient interstellar medium and predominantly evolved, ancient to [[intermediate-age-stellar-populations|Intermediate-age stellar populations]]. Unlike massive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], which often reside at the centers of deep gravitational potential wells, dSphs and dEs are primarily found in high-density regions such as [[dwarf-galaxy-clusters|Dwarf galaxy clusters]] or acting as [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] around massive hosts like the Milky Way and the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] [Hodge et al. (1966)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1966ApJ...144..869H/abstract). The recognition of these faint, gas-poor systems as a ubiquitous galactic population [Kormendy et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987nngp.proc..163K/abstract) has established them as fundamental building blocks in hierarchical assembly models and crucial tracers of environmental quenching.

Structurally, dwarf early-type galaxies deviate significantly from their massive counterparts. Instead of the steep central cusps typical of giant ellipticals, dSphs and dEs generally feature diffuse, nearly exponential surface brightness profiles. More importantly, their internal kinematics reveal them to be among the most heavily dark matter-dominated objects known. With dynamic mass-to-light ratios sometimes exceeding hundreds of times that of the Sun, they serve as critical astrophysical laboratories for investigating the nature of dark matter [Mateo et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ARA&A..36..435M/abstract). Their flat velocity dispersion profiles and surprisingly low central densities pose severe challenges to the standard $\Lambda$ Cold Dark Matter ($\Lambda$CDM) paradigm, fueling ongoing debates surrounding the "cusp-core" and "too big to fail" problems within the study of [[subhalo-physics-and-galactic-substructure|Subhalo Physics and Galactic Substructure]].

The evolutionary pathways of dSphs and dEs are shaped by a complex interplay of internal feedback and environmental quenching. Detailed studies of their chemical abundances reveal surprisingly diverse, often episodic star formation histories that feature [[steep-age-and-metallicity-gradients|Steep age and metallicity gradients]]. Transforming gas-rich, star-forming [[late-type-galaxies-and-stellar-populations|late-type progenitors]] into quiescent [[spheroidal-galaxies|Spheroidal Galaxies]] requires powerful external drivers; processes such as ram-pressure stripping, harassment, and tidal stirring efficiently ablate gas and dynamically heat the stellar disks as they plunge into cluster or group environments [Mayer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Natur.445..738M/abstract), [De Rijcke et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724L.171D/abstract). Consequently, these galaxies provide essential constraints on the efficiency of [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]]. 

This article explores the physical, chemical, and structural properties of these [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] across a broad parameter space, extending to extreme classes like [[giant-low-surface-brightness-galaxies|ultra-diffuse galaxies]] and ultra-faint dwarfs, which may represent pristine fossils of [[primeval-formation-stages|cosmic reionization]]. It examines the complex nuclear architectures of these systems, evaluating the coexistence of [[compact-galactic-nuclei|massive nuclear star clusters]] and potential [[dwarf-black-holes|Dwarf black holes]]. Finally, the article outlines major open questions—such as the presence of undetected "dark gas" in progenitor systems, the universality of their dark matter halos, and the viability of alternative gravity theories—highlighting how next-generation surveys will soon map the local cosmic web with unprecedented spectroscopic and astrometric clarity.

## Classification and Structural Scaling Relations

The structural classification of dynamically hot, gas-poor galaxies at the faint end of the luminosity function ($M_B \gtrsim -18$) primarily divides them into [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|dwarf ellipticals (dEs)]] and [[spheroidal-galaxies|dwarf spheroidals (dSphs)]]. While they share a lack of ongoing star formation with giant elliptical galaxies, their structural parameters reveal significant deviations from classical elliptical scaling relations. 

A primary differentiator between giant ellipticals and dwarf early-type galaxies is their surface brightness profile, typically parameterized by the Sérsic law:
$$ I(R) = I_e \exp \left\{ -b_n \left[ \left(\frac{R}{R_e}\right)^{1/n} - 1 \right] \right\} $$
where $R_e$ is the effective half-light radius and $n$ is the Sérsic index. Massive elliptical galaxies generally exhibit steep central cusps well-described by the de Vaucouleurs profile ($n \approx 4$). In contrast, dEs and dSphs are far more diffuse, featuring nearly exponential surface brightness profiles ($n \lesssim 2$) that lack a dense central concentration [Kormendy et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract).

These structural differences manifest clearly in global scaling relations, such as the Kormendy relation (correlating mean surface brightness $\langle \mu \rangle_e$ with $R_e$) and the Fundamental Plane (FP). In the traditional parameter space of the FP, dEs and dSphs occupy a distinct locus from massive ellipticals and [[galactic-bulges|classical bulges]]. Instead of following the tight plane of giants, the dwarf population deviates toward regions indicative of highly dark-matter-dominated systems [Toloba et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1210.7816T/abstract). Furthermore, gas-rich dwarfs and [[disk-galaxies|disk galaxies]] form two unique, separate sequences in the mass-scale length plane. This structural dichotomy indicates that dwarfs are systematically more diffuse than larger disk galaxies, potentially due to inefficient star formation or differing primordial distributions [Schombert et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..296S/abstract). This kinematic and structural dichotomy has led some researchers to classify dEs and dSphs not as true ellipticals, but rather as "red and dead" analogs to late-type dwarf irregulars, forming a parallel sequence of galaxies quenched by environmental or internal feedback [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract). Moreover, across the broader mass spectrum, early-type galaxies are fundamentally divided into a bimodal distribution of fast and slow rotators, highlighting distinct formation pathways inherently linked to their stellar specific angular momentum [Cappellari et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250302746C/abstract).

However, this dichotomy remains a subject of active debate. An alternative theoretical framework argues that dwarf and giant ellipticals form a single, unified continuous sequence. In this view, the apparent discontinuities and curved relations in the Kormendy relation and FP arise mathematically from forcing linear fits onto non-linear scaling relations. When properly scaled, structural parameters like the Sérsic index vary continuously with stellar mass and luminosity across all early-type galaxies, unifying dEs and giants [Graham et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....48..231G/abstract).

At the extremes of the structural parameter space lie two notable subclasses: [[compact-galaxies|compact ellipticals (cEs)]] and [[giant-low-surface-brightness-galaxies|ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs)]]. Compact ellipticals, such as M32, are exceedingly rare systems characterized by unusually high surface brightnesses and small effective radii ($R_e \lesssim 2$ kpc). While they appear to form a smooth continuation of the high-mass elliptical [[intrinsic-galaxy-and-stellar-properties|size-luminosity relation]], recent simulations suggest cEs can form via unique pathways, such as the ram-pressure confinement of starbursts within gas-rich progenitors falling into massive host halos [Du et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...875...58D/abstract). Additionally, Tidal Dwarf Galaxies (TDGs) represent a distinct origin class formed from the debris expelled during galactic collisions. Unlike primordial dwarfs, TDGs lack significant dark matter and exhibit unique metallicities that are uncharacteristically high for their mass [Duc et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASSP...28..305D/abstract).

Conversely, UDGs exhibit spatial extents typical of giant galaxies but possess the low surface brightnesses and stellar masses of dwarfs. Recent spatially resolved spectroscopy demonstrates that UDGs bridge the gap between cluster dEs and fainter dSphs on both the Faber-Jackson and mass-metallicity relations [Chilingarian et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884...79C/abstract). This structural continuity suggests that UDGs are not a distinct species, but rather a transitional population of dwarf early-type galaxies heavily shaped by tidal heating and supernova feedback [Afanasiev et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.6312A/abstract).

## Internal Dynamics and the Dark Matter Content

[[spheroidal-galaxies|Dwarf spheroidal galaxies (dSphs)]] exhibit internal kinematics that mark them as some of the most dark-matter-dominated systems in the Universe [Mateo et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ARA&A..36..435M/abstract). Spectroscopic measurements of their line-of-sight velocity dispersion profiles often reveal flat, sustained distributions, typically remaining around $\sigma \sim 10\ \mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}$ out to large radii [McConnachie et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.380L..75M/abstract). Application of the Jeans equations to these systems in the context of [[stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|Stellar and Galactic Dynamics]] yields dynamic mass-to-light ratio estimates ranging from tens to several hundreds of times solar ($M/L_\odot$), far exceeding the baryonic mass of their stellar populations [Mateo et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ARA&A..36..435M/abstract), [Mashchenko et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...624..726M/abstract). Remarkably, kinematic modeling suggests that many dSphs share a common dark matter mass scale, containing a roughly constant total dark mass of $M_d \approx 2.6 \times 10^7\ M_\odot$ within the central $400\ \mathrm{pc}$ [Burkert et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...808..158B/abstract).

The detailed dark matter distribution within these galaxies, however, presents a significant challenge to standard $\Lambda$ Cold Dark Matter ($\Lambda$CDM) cosmology. Collisionless $N$-body simulations of [[subhalo-physics-and-galactic-substructure|Subhalo Physics and Galactic Substructure]] predict that dark matter subhaloes should assemble with steep, centrally divergent density "cusps" ($\rho \propto r^{-1}$). In stark contrast, the velocity dispersion profiles of observed dSphs strongly favor shallow, constant-density "cores" ($\rho \propto r^0$) [Burkert et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...808..158B/abstract). This discrepancy, known as the cusp-core problem, remains an active area of debate. A leading resolution posits that impulsive baryonic feedback—specifically, repeated mass-loss events driven by supernovae—can gravitationally unbind the central dark matter, transforming a primordial cusp into an extended core [Read et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356..107R/abstract). Additionally, the sudden removal of baryons via ram-pressure stripping during environmental accretion can further reduce the central dark matter density by a factor of roughly $(1 - f_b)^4$, where $f_b$ is the cosmic baryon fraction [Arraki et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.1466A/abstract). Another probe of the inner dark matter profile lies in the survival of [[globular-clusters|globular clusters]]. For instance, the resolution of the Fornax globular cluster paradox—explaining why its clusters have not spiraled into the galactic center via dynamical friction—can be achieved through a self-consistent two-component model of stars and dark matter that accurately reflects the galaxy's internal dynamics [Cowsik et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...699.1389C/abstract).

Closely related to the cusp-core tension is the too big to fail problem. Kinematic data from dSph satellites of the Milky Way and the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] indicate that these galaxies are significantly less dense in their centers than the most massive subhaloes predicted by $\Lambda$CDM simulations [Tollerud et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440.3511T/abstract), [Kravtsov et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AdAst2010E...8K/abstract). If such dense subhaloes exist, they are seemingly devoid of stars, failing to host the luminous satellites expected of their mass. 

While some authors argue that dSphs reside within a universal dark matter density profile [Burkert et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...808..158B/abstract), observations of a broader sample of [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]] complicate this picture. Certain outliers in the Andromeda system, such as And XIX, XXI, and XXV, exhibit unusually large half-light radii ($r_e \gtrsim 600\ \mathrm{pc}$) alongside exceptionally low velocity dispersions ($\sigma_v < 5\ \mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}$). These systems deviate significantly from any proposed universal profile. The literature suggests this discrepancy is likely driven by severe tidal interactions with their host galaxy rather than underlying dark matter physics alone, highlighting the difficulty in disentangling primordial dark matter distributions from subsequent environmental evolution [Collins et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783....7C/abstract).

## Star Formation Histories and Population Gradients

The star formation histories (SFHs) of [[spheroidal-galaxies|dwarf spheroidal (dSph)]] and [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|dwarf elliptical (dE)]] galaxies display remarkable diversity. Contrary to early models that posited them as simple, coeval primordial systems, modern deep photometric studies utilizing the color-magnitude diagram reveal extended and highly complex evolutionary paths [Tolstoy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ARA&A..47..371T/abstract). A defining feature of many classical dSphs is episodic star formation, where starbirth occurs in distinct bursts separated by gigayears of quiescence. The [[carina-dwarf-spheroidal-galaxy|Carina Dwarf Spheroidal Galaxy]] serves as a canonical example, exhibiting at least four major star-forming episodes over its lifetime. These include an old population ($\sim 11$ Gyr), an [[intermediate-age-stellar-populations|intermediate-age population]] ($\sim 5$ Gyr), and a relatively young trace population ($\le 1$ Gyr) [Smecker-Hane et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997AIPC..393..571S/abstract), [Monelli et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MmSAI..75..114M/abstract). Similar multi-episodic or prolonged SFHs are observed in the Fornax and [[sextans-dwarf-spheroidal-galaxy|Sextans Dwarf Spheroidal Galaxy]] systems [Pancino et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..274..421P/abstract), [Smecker-Hane et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..192..150S/abstract). Such intermediate-age stellar populations can also be robustly traced through the presence and identification of significant Carbon star populations, as prominently observed in galaxies like Leo I [Demers et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002AJ....123..238D/abstract).

By contrast, in massive ellipticals, models occasionally invoke bursts of [[massive-star-formation|Massive Star Formation]] governed by a top-heavy initial mass function (IMF) to help explain their observed mass-to-light ratios and rapid metal enrichment [Zepf et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...466..114Z/abstract). Dwarf systems, however, generally show much more protracted and inefficient enrichment histories. Despite the prevalence of intermediate-age stellar populations, virtually all thoroughly studied Local Group dSphs host an ancient stellar backbone indistinguishable in age from the oldest Galactic [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] [Grebel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...610L..89G/abstract). The presence of these ancient ($>10$ Gyr) populations is unambiguously confirmed by RR Lyrae stars. As robust tracers of early star formation, RR Lyrae stars have been extensively mapped in dSphs such as Draco and Sagittarius [Kinemuchi et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..259..130K/abstract), [Clementini et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010vsgh.conf..107C/abstract). The properties of these variables, particularly regarding the Oosterhoff dichotomy, provide critical constraints on hierarchical assembly. Observations reveal that the specific chemical and kinematic signatures of dSph RR Lyrae populations differ from those in the bulk of the Milky Way's [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]], complicating the traditional view that classical dSphs are identical to the primary building blocks of the Galactic halo [Clementini et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010vsgh.conf..107C/abstract).

Spatially, these varied generations of stars are rarely well-mixed, and population gradients are a ubiquitous feature in local dwarf galaxies [Grebel et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApSSS.277..231G/abstract). Systematic surveys demonstrate that metal-poor, older stars generally exhibit a more extended spatial distribution and higher velocity dispersion, while more metal-rich, younger stars are centrally concentrated [Harbeck et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....122.3092H/abstract), [Kawata et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...641..785K/abstract). For example, the Sculptor dSph displays a pronounced bimodal metallicity distribution where the lower-metallicity component is significantly more extended than the kinematically colder, high-metallicity central component [Kawata et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...641..785K/abstract). Similar radial gradients on the horizontal branch—where red horizontal-branch stars are more centrally localized than their blue counterparts—are evident in Sextans, Tucana, and several Andromeda companions [Harbeck et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....122.3092H/abstract). Deep surface density profiles confirm that red (metal-rich) stellar populations possess steeper radial density gradients than blue (metal-poor) populations, even in isolated systems [Sharina et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Ap.....61..435S/abstract). Radial variations in the [[extended-horizontal-branch|Extended horizontal branch]] further confirm these localized star-forming episodes.

The mechanisms driving these episodic histories and spatial gradients remain a subject of active debate. While [[primeval-formation-stages|cosmic reionization]] undoubtedly impacted the earliest gas reservoirs, the sheer variety of SFHs among dSphs of similar masses suggests that local processes—such as stellar feedback, mass loss, and environmental interactions—play a dominant role in shaping their evolution, rather than a universal reionization cutoff [Grebel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...610L..89G/abstract). Theoretical models propose that the episodic nature of star formation in satellite dSphs may be regulated by orbital eccentricity; variations in the gas cooling time occur as the dwarf navigates the host galaxy's gravitational and ionizing radiation fields, intermittently triggering and quenching starbursts [Nichols et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748..149N/abstract). Meanwhile, steep metallicity gradients within single systems naturally arise from the dissipative collapse of enriched gas into the central core following initial, more extended episodes of star formation [Kawata et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...641..785K/abstract).

## Chemical Evolution and Nucleosynthesis

The chemical properties of dwarf spheroidal (dSph) and dwarf elliptical (dE) galaxies serve as fossil records of their formation, gas retention, and star formation histories. By studying the chemical signatures of individual stars and integrated populations, astrophysicists can trace the balance between star formation efficiency and the feedback mechanisms that drive [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]].

A fundamental scaling law for these systems is the mass-metallicity relation, wherein the mean metallicity of a galaxy correlates strongly with its total mass and luminosity. In the Local Group, dSphs tend to be systematically more metal-rich at a given luminosity than gas-rich [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|dwarf irregulars]], indicating distinct evolutionary conditions . Reproducing the observed mass-metallicity relation in cosmological simulations requires a careful treatment of feedback. It is generally understood that a combination of mechanisms is necessary: supernova-driven outflows must eject metals to maintain subsolar metallicities, while preventative feedback (such as pre-heating) is required to prevent baryons from collapsing into the shallow gravitational potentials of low-mass halos in the first place [Lu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...66L/abstract).

The metallicity distribution function (MDF) provides deeper insight into the [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|chemical enrichment]] history of these galaxies. Far from being simple, coeval populations, the MDFs of nearby dSphs are often broad or bimodal, pointing to extended or episodic star formation histories [Tolstoy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ARA&A..47..371T/abstract), [Shetrone et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251104365S/abstract). Furthermore, these galaxies frequently exhibit pronounced radial metallicity and age gradients. In systems such as the Sculptor and the [[sextans-dwarf-spheroidal-galaxy|Sextans Dwarf Spheroidal Galaxy]], observations reveal a dynamically hotter, spatially extended metal-poor stellar population surrounding a kinematically colder, centrally concentrated metal-rich population [Kawata et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...641..785K/abstract), [Pancino et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..274..421P/abstract). This structure is commonly attributed to the dissipative collapse of enriched gas toward the center following initial starbursts.

Detailed elemental abundances, particularly the alpha-element abundance ratios like [O/Fe] relative to [Fe/H], act as a cosmic clock for [[dsph-chemical-evolution|dSph chemical evolution]]. By studying these abundance patterns, researchers can trace the relative contributions and temporal evolution of Type Ia and Type II supernovae. Because $\alpha$-elements (such as oxygen) are dispersed rapidly by [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] via Type II supernovae, while iron-peak elements are contributed over gigayear timescales by Type Ia supernovae, models successfully reproduce the observed [O/Fe]-[Fe/H] diagrams of dSphs by invoking inefficient or episodic star formation. Enough time elapsed for Type Ia supernovae to heavily enrich the interstellar medium with iron before the galaxy could reach a high overall metallicity [Smecker-Hane et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..192..150S/abstract), [Marcolini et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007EAS....24...27M/abstract).

The underlying driver of this inefficient enrichment is supernova feedback. Three-dimensional hydrodynamic simulations show that supernova-driven winds are highly effective at driving metal-enriched gas out of the shallow potential wells characteristic of dwarf galaxies [Fragile et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...590..778F/abstract). The fraction of metals lost directly impacts the chemical evolution of subsequent stellar generations, explaining both the poor self-enrichment and the overall subsolar metallicities that define the dSph and dE classes.

## Environmental Quenching and Morphological Transformation

The spatial distribution of low-mass galaxies exhibits a strong morphology-density relation: [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|gas-rich, rotationally supported dwarf irregulars]] dominate the field, while gas-poor, pressure-supported [[spheroidal-galaxies|dwarf spheroidals (dSphs)]] and [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|dwarf ellipticals (dEs)]] are predominantly found in the proximity of massive hosts. While older textbook models sometimes restricted the unambiguous identification of dSphs to the Local Group, deep photometric surveys have since confirmed large populations of these extremely low-luminosity systems in the Virgo, Fornax, Hydra I, and Centaurus clusters [Phillipps et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...493L..59P/abstract), [Misgeld et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....48..207M/abstract). This dichotomy strongly suggests that [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]] are driven by **environmental quenching** and morphological transformation, wherein late-type progenitors are processed as they accrete into massive dark matter halos [Kormendy et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..323K/abstract), [Mayer et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AdAst2010E..25M/abstract). Interestingly, the environmental impact on dwarfs extends beyond confirmed satellites; those situated near massive halos tend to have higher stellar masses for their given halo mass and form stars earlier, even before being formally accreted [Christensen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..236C/abstract). Furthermore, recent modeling results for the satellite system of Centaurus A using the Galacticus framework verify that universal star formation and quenching processes likely govern dwarf evolution across diverse massive host environments [Weerasooriya et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968...78W/abstract).

The quenching of star formation in these satellites is primarily governed by **ram-pressure stripping**. As a gas-rich dwarf plunges into a cluster or group environment, it traverses the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]] or intracluster medium. The resulting hydrodynamic drag efficiently ablates the dwarf's interstellar medium once the ram pressure exceeds the satellite's gravitational restoring force. Observational surveys of the Milky Way and the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] reveal that over $90\%$ of dwarf galaxies within $270 \text{ kpc}$ of their hosts are [[h-i-deficient-galaxies|H I deficient]] [Nichols et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732...17N/abstract). In denser environments like the Fornax cluster, the radially increasing ratio of early-to-late-type dwarfs confirms that late-type systems are actively converted into quiescent dEs via ram pressure during plunging orbits [De Rijcke et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724L.171D/abstract). The efficiency of this gas removal is frequently amplified by internal feedback; supernovae and heating from the cosmic ultraviolet background during [[primeval-formation-stages|reionization]] pre-expand the gas, allowing even the tenuous coronal gas of a host halo to efficiently strip the dwarf's baryons [Mayer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Natur.445..738M/abstract), [Nichols et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732...17N/abstract).

While ram-pressure stripping explains the cessation of star formation, the structural transition from a disky progenitor to a spheroidal remnant requires dynamical heating. This morphological transformation is widely attributed to **tidal stirring**. During repeated pericentric passages, the tidal field of the host galaxy induces strong non-axisymmetric instabilities (such as bars) within the dwarf's stellar disk. Over successive orbits, these structures buckle and dynamically heat the stellar component, transforming a rotationally supported disk into a pressure-supported prolate or triaxial spheroid with a density distribution approximated by a modified Plummer law [Łokas et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...708.1032L/abstract), [Łokas et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...751L..15L/abstract). 

Contrary to traditional views that early-type galaxies are universally gas-poor, observations have led to the discovery of HI gas disks in low-luminosity E/S0 galaxies that support ongoing star formation [Sadler et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..230..285S/abstract). In fact, field ellipticals can contain $1\%$ to $10\%$ of the cold interstellar matter found in spirals, supporting long-lived, low-level star formation cycles in less dense environments [Knapp et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..163..119K/abstract). However, in massive clusters, morphological change is complemented by **harassment**—high-speed, impulsive encounters with other cluster members and the global cluster potential. Harassment efficiently strips weakly bound outer stars and heats the surviving stellar population, contributing to the formation of both dEs and [[giant-low-surface-brightness-galaxies|ultra-diffuse galaxies]]. For more isolated environments or the outskirts of groups, an alternative mechanism known as resonant stripping has been proposed. Here, gravitational resonances driven by encounters with other dwarf galaxies strip material and induce spheroidal morphologies without requiring a single, giant host [D'Onghia et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.460..605D/abstract). While environmental interactions primarily drive the formation of dEs and dSphs, it is worth noting that relatively isolated dwarf galaxies can experience secular size growth through the formation of rotation-supported stellar disks, often triggered by minor mergers with satellites possessing high orbital angular momentum [Geda et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251026875G/abstract). Additionally, minor mergers with dark matter-dominated satellites can induce strong, episodic starbursts and trigger morphological transformations toward spheroidal shapes even in gas-rich dwarfs [Starkenburg et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...587A..24S/abstract).

The rapid environmental removal of baryons also has profound implications for the internal dark matter distributions of these galaxies, directly linking environmental quenching to [[subhalo-physics-and-galactic-substructure|Subhalo Physics and Galactic Substructure]]. The impulsive blow-away of gas via ram pressure causes the central gravitational potential to rapidly shallow. Cosmological simulations indicate that this process can reduce the central dark matter density within the inner $200\text{--}500 \text{ pc}$ by a factor of approximately $(1-f_b)^4 \approx 0.5$, where $f_b$ is the cosmological baryon fraction. This feedback-assisted expansion provides a viable mechanism to transform primordial cold dark matter cusps into the shallow, constant-density cores frequently observed in local dSphs [Read et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356..107R/abstract), [Arraki et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.1466A/abstract). Furthermore, high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations suggest that many Galactic dSphs, such as Sculptor and [[antlia-dwarf|Antlia II]], may actually be out-of-equilibrium systems caught at their first infall into the Milky Way's halo. In these cases, their observed large velocity dispersions are driven by recent tidal shocks and aggressive ram-pressure stripping rather than steady-state kinematics in an equilibrium dark matter halo [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.7144W/abstract).

## Nuclear Structures and Central Black Holes

The central regions of dwarf early-type galaxies—comprising [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|dwarf ellipticals (dEs)]] and [[spheroidal-galaxies|dwarf spheroidals (dSphs)]]—exhibit a complex interplay between massive black holes and dense nuclear star clusters (NSCs). While the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]] in massive ellipticals are universally dominated by [[supermassive-black-holes-smbhs|Supermassive black holes (SMBHs)]] that scale tightly with the host's velocity dispersion, the nuclei of dwarf galaxies are predominantly characterized by the presence of massive, compact star clusters. 

NSCs in dwarf early-type galaxies are among the most extreme [[dense-stellar-systems|Dense stellar systems]] known, with typical stellar masses in the range of $10^5 - 10^7 M_\odot$ and half-light radii of $\sim 5$ pc [Graham et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.397.2148G/abstract). Their physical properties bear a strong resemblance to [[compact-galaxies|ultra-compact dwarfs]], suggesting possible evolutionary links. Spectroscopic evidence further shows that the [[compact-galactic-nuclei|stellar nuclei]] of dEs often exhibit younger ages than their host galaxies while sharing similar metallicities, pointing to subsequent central star formation events from enriched gas [Paudel et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AN....330..969P/abstract). The presence of these nuclei also correlates with the galaxy's broader origin. Recent studies leveraging deep learning suggest that nucleated dEs are likely formed in-situ alongside massive host galaxies, whereas non-nucleated dEs exhibit a more ex-situ distribution, aligning primarily with a cluster's global gravitational potential [Paudel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251121010P/abstract). A local example of complex dwarf nuclear structure is found in the Sagittarius dwarf spheroidal galaxy, where the central stellar overdensity is spatially coincident with the [[globular-clusters|globular cluster]] M54. It remains an open debate whether M54 formed in situ within the potential well of the dwarf's nucleus or whether it spiraled into the center via dynamical friction [Monaco et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..327..190M/abstract). Furthermore, the structural and kinematic evolution of these central star clusters—which tend to remain isotropic in cuspy halos but become radially anisotropic in cored halos—can be utilized as a tool to measure the underlying dark matter distribution in the host dwarf galaxy [Webb et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.3708W/abstract).

In intermediate-mass spheroids ($10^8 - 10^{11} M_\odot$), NSCs and SMBHs can coexist. Observations of these "hermaphrodite nuclei" reveal that the scaling relation between the central massive objects and the host galaxy is not linear across all masses. Instead, the total nucleus-to-spheroid baryon mass ratio decreases with increasing host mass. This relationship is parameterized quantitatively as:
$$ \log \left[ \frac{M_{\rm BH} + M_{\rm NC}}{M_{\rm sph}} \right] = -(0.39 \pm 0.07) \log \left[ \frac{M_{\rm sph}}{10^{10} M_\odot} \right] - (2.18 \pm 0.07) $$
where $M_{\rm NC}$ is the mass of the nuclear cluster and $M_{\rm sph}$ is the mass of the host spheroid [Graham et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.397.2148G/abstract). Once galaxies grow massive enough to undergo dry merging, NSCs are generally destroyed, and the ratio is expected to flatten to a constant value. The exact formation sequence—whether the NSC or the black hole forms first, or if one triggers the growth of the other—remains a major unresolved question in the field.

Detailed structural analyses show that dwarf nuclei can also evolve via secular processes. For instance, the dwarf elliptical NGC 4486A hosts both a nuclear gas disk and a distinct nuclear stellar disk [Kormendy et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph..7218K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AJ....129.2636K/abstract). This architecture indicates that accreted gas can dissipate, settle into the core, and trigger star formation. Such secular growth can drive the central density distributions toward cuspier profiles, leading to chaotic orbital mixing in phase space that subsequently alters the global morphology of the galaxy [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AJ....129.2636K/abstract).

Furthermore, the presence of intermediate-mass or [[dwarf-black-holes|Dwarf black holes]] in early-type dwarfs has profound implications for galaxy formation models. It has been hypothesized that early active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback from black holes could operate in the gas-rich progenitors of today's dSphs. This feedback potentially provides a unifying mechanism to explain dwarf galaxy structural anomalies—such as the core-cusp problem and unusually low baryon fractions—without requiring modifications to cold dark matter paradigms [Silk et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839L..13S/abstract). Future high-resolution spectroscopic missions aim to dynamically detect this quiescent black hole population by resolving stellar velocity dispersions down to $\sim 30$ km s$^{-1}$ in nearby dwarfs [Pacucci et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250701523P/abstract). Such measurements will be critical for testing heavy versus light seed formation scenarios and tracing the co-evolution of black holes and nuclear clusters across cosmic time.

## The Ultra-Faint and Ultra-Diffuse Regimes

Recent deep photometric surveys have vastly expanded the known structural parameter space of [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]], revealing two extreme regimes: ultra-faint dwarf galaxies (UFDs) and ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs). 

**Ultra-Faint Dwarfs (UFDs)**
UFDs define the extreme faint tail of [[low-luminosity-galaxies|Low-luminosity galaxies]], featuring some of the lowest stellar masses ever measured. Kinematic studies show they are the most dark matter-dominated systems known, possessing extreme mass-to-light ratios that imply they reside in relatively massive subhalos where star formation was highly inefficient [Mayer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Natur.445..738M/abstract), [D'Onghia et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.460..605D/abstract). 

A prevailing theoretical paradigm suggests that many UFDs are "reionization fossils." In this scenario, star formation was globally suppressed prior to or during the epoch of [[primeval-formation-stages|cosmic reionization]] ($z > 1$) by the cosmic ultraviolet background [Mayer et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AdAst2010E..25M/abstract). The background UV flux heated the primordial gas, preventing it from cooling and condensing. Subsequent ram-pressure and tidal stripping upon accretion into a massive host—such as the Milky Way or the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]]—swept away the remaining unbound baryons [Mayer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Natur.445..738M/abstract). The profound impact of these tidal forces is directly observable in systems like the Sagittarius dwarf spheroidal, where tidal extensions have been discovered spanning up to 10 degrees from the galaxy's main body, beautifully traced by RR Lyrae stars [Mateo et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...458L..13M/abstract). 

However, the literature remains divided on whether cosmic reionization acts as a universal formation filter for all UFDs. Alternate studies argue that local environmental processes and internal feedback might be sufficient to quench these galaxies without requiring a dominant reionization signature. For example, some models hypothesize that dwarf progenitors continued forming the bulk of their stars within partially rotationally supported [[disk-galaxies|H I discs]] in a reionized universe, until they were eventually quenched by ram-pressure stripping upon accretion [Milosavljević et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440...50M/abstract). Additionally, high-resolution simulations demonstrate that the tidal stirring of gas-rich, disky progenitors with shallow inner dark matter density profiles ($\rho \propto r^{-0.6}$) can successfully reproduce the kinematic and structural properties of UFDs upon their infall into a host halo [Łokas et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...751L..15L/abstract). The extreme faintness of these galaxies also makes them elusive targets in isolated field environments. However, recent transient studies suggest that apparently "hostless" supernovae observed in the field likely originate from undetected ultra-faint dwarf galaxies, providing a novel method to constrain the slope of the low-mass end of the galaxy luminosity function [Strolger et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250405117S/abstract).

**Ultra-Diffuse Galaxies (UDGs)**
At the opposite end of the structural spectrum are [[giant-low-surface-brightness-galaxies|ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs)]]. While UDGs share the low surface brightness and total stellar masses of typical [[spheroidal-galaxies|dwarf spheroidals]], they are extremely spatially extended, often boasting effective radii comparable to that of the Milky Way ($R_e \gtrsim 1.5$ kpc). Found in a wide variety of environments ranging from isolated groups to dense clusters like Coma, UDGs exhibit regular, elliptical-like morphologies and form a continuous structural sequence linking faint dwarf spheroidals to brighter elliptical galaxies [Chilingarian et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884...79C/abstract), [Afanasiev et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.6312A/abstract).

Dynamical modeling of stellar kinematics in UDGs indicates that they are highly dark matter-dominated, with dark matter fractions between $50\%$ and $90\%$ within their half-light radii [Chilingarian et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884...79C/abstract). Studies of transitional objects, such as the large dwarf spheroidal KDG 64 in the M81 group, show old, metal-poor stellar populations completely lacking recent star formation [Afanasiev et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.6312A/abstract). 

The origin of UDGs is generally attributed to a combination of internal and external physical drivers. Strong [[galaxy-and-gaseous-outflows|Galaxy and gaseous outflows]] driven by intense, episodic supernova feedback can perturb the central dark matter potential, causing the stellar distribution to dynamically expand. Subsequent environmental processing, including ram-pressure stripping and tidal heating, then quenches star formation and preserves their diffuse, quiescent nature [Chilingarian et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884...79C/abstract), [Afanasiev et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.6312A/abstract).

## Dwarf Galaxies in the Context of Non-Newtonian Gravity

When their internal kinematics are interpreted within the standard cold dark matter ($\Lambda$CDM) paradigm, many [[spheroidal-galaxies|Spheroidal Galaxies]] in the dwarf regime are among the most dark-matter-dominated systems known. As such, they serve as crucial astrophysical laboratories for testing non-Newtonian gravity theories, most notably Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND). 

To test MOND using dSph internal kinematics, one must adapt standard dynamical tools. By deriving an analog of the virial theorem and the [[king-profile|King profile]] formula in the MOND framework, it is possible to estimate the stellar mass-to-light ratios ($M/L$) necessary to reproduce the observed velocity dispersions without invoking dark matter [Gerhard et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...397...38G/abstract). In a purely baryonic MOND scenario, the $M/L$ values derived from the kinematics should be consistent with those expected from the old, metal-poor stellar populations typical of dSphs.

Applying these non-Newtonian virial relations yields highly conflicting results across different [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]]. For the Fornax dSph, the relatively low observed velocity dispersion implies a MOND mass-to-light ratio in the range of:
$$ 0.3 \lesssim M/L \lesssim 1 $$
The core value of $M/L \approx 0.3$ is anomalously low, falling significantly below the expectations for a normal, old stellar population [Gerhard et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...397...38G/abstract). Conversely, the highly elevated velocity dispersions observed in the Draco and Ursa Minor dwarf spheroidals cannot be fully explained by MOND; these systems would still require a substantial component of unseen dark matter even under modified gravity laws [Gerhard et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...397...38G/abstract). 

Because of these discrepancies, the inferred $M/L$ values must vary by a factor of at least 20 among the Galactic dSphs to satisfy MOND [Gerhard et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...397...38G/abstract). This vast spread in required baryonic mass-to-light ratios strongly conflicts with the relatively uniform stellar populations observed across these systems.

More recent computational approaches have explicitly modeled the motion of stars in spherically symmetric potentials to calculate bulk velocity dispersions and radial dispersion profiles for dSphs surrounding the Milky Way and the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] [Alexander et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835..233A/abstract). While some MOND calculations of bulk dispersions can be tuned to agree well with specific observations [Alexander et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835..233A/abstract), the theory struggles to provide a universal solution. The coexistence of heavily dark-matter-dominated systems alongside [[low-dark-matter-galaxies|Low-dark matter galaxies]] creates a tension that modified gravity theories have difficulty accommodating without invoking unseen mass. Furthermore, kinematic studies of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] present additional scaling challenges: observations showing strong dark matter signatures in bright, boxy ellipticals are problematic for MOND, whereas the lack of such signatures in faint, disky systems challenges the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm [Romanowsky et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006EAS....20..119R/abstract).

In summary, the literature reflects significant tension regarding the viability of MOND as an alternative to dark matter in dwarf galaxies. While MOND remains a fringe alternative for explaining velocity dispersions in certain contexts, the broad diversity of dSph internal kinematics—particularly the extreme cases of Fornax, Draco, and Ursa Minor—severely challenges its predictive power as a universal replacement for dark matter.

## Open Questions and Future Observational Frontiers

While environmental transformation and feedback models successfully explain many properties of dwarf spheroidal (dSph) and dwarf elliptical (dE) galaxies, fundamental mysteries remain regarding their baryonic physics, dark matter distributions, and formation pathways. 

A major outstanding issue in the chemical and structural evolution of these galaxies concerns the phases of the interstellar medium in their progenitors. Observations of [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|Star-forming dwarf galaxies]] and dwarf irregulars—which are thought to transform into quiescent dSphs via environmental stripping—reveal significant quantities of "dark gas." This pervasive component, which remains undetected as either molecular or cold hydrogen, complicates measurements of the total baryon budget, star formation efficiency, and gas depletion timescales [Hunter et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ARA&A..62..113H/abstract). Furthermore, the role of circumgalactic medium (CGM) accretion remains critical, as recent studies indicate that [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|CGM accretion]] can provide 20%-50% of the star-forming gas for dwarf galaxies across cosmic time, continually fueling their evolution prior to quenching [Tung et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988..127T/abstract). Understanding the evolution of metals is similarly critical; findings on metal retention in the circumgalactic medium indicate that simulated isolated dwarfs retain a significant fraction of their expelled metals in a warm gas phase ($10^{4.5}-10^{5.5}$ K) [Piacitelli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250508861P/abstract).

The co-evolution of central compact objects in the low-mass regime is also highly debated. While massive ellipticals host [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]], dwarf galaxies are frequently nucleated by [[dense-stellar-systems|Dense stellar systems]] rather than black holes [Graham et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.397.2148G/abstract). However, intermediate-mass black holes may still exist in old dwarfs, potentially providing early AGN feedback capable of quenching star formation and altering central dark matter profiles. Such feedback could simultaneously address the core-cusp and "too-big-to-fail" problems by impulsively heating the dark matter halo [Silk et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839L..13S/abstract). Detecting these elusive black hole seeds remains an observational challenge. The upcoming Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO) aims to dynamically detect quiescent [[dwarf-black-holes|Dwarf black holes]] with masses as low as $10^{4.5} M_\odot$ in dwarf galaxies out to $10-30 \text{ Mpc}$ by resolving stellar velocity dispersions down to $30 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Pacucci et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250701523P/abstract). These measurements will clarify whether dwarf galaxies retain the imprints of Population III remnants or direct-collapse heavy seeds.

In the dark sector, there is ongoing tension regarding whether all dSphs reside within subhalos that share a universal dark matter mass profile. While some studies suggest a common enclosed mass scale of $M_d \approx 2.6 \times 10^7 M_\odot$ within the central $400 \text{ pc}$ [Burkert et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...808..158B/abstract), kinematic data from the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] reveal significant outliers. Satellites such as And XIX, XXI, and XXV exhibit unusually large half-light radii ($> 600 \text{ pc}$) and low velocity dispersions ($\sigma_v < 5 \text{ km s}^{-1}$), indicating that host-galaxy tidal interactions may destroy any universal scaling in [[subhalo-physics-and-galactic-substructure|Subhalo Physics and Galactic Substructure]] [Collins et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783....7C/abstract).

To disentangle these competing evolutionary processes, next-generation surveys are poised to map the local cosmic web with unprecedented spectroscopic detail. To resolve these issues, next-generation multi-wavelength surveys will reveal the 3D internal stellar motions and gas accretion histories needed to rigorously test small-scale structure formation models [Haynes et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019IAUS..344....3H/abstract). The 4DWARFS survey, utilizing the 4MOST instrument, will provide radial velocities, chemical abundances, and stellar ages for $140,000$ stars in [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] and stellar streams [Skúladóttir et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Msngr.190...19S/abstract). Combined with precision astrometry from Gaia, this will robustly identify accreted populations and trace hierarchical assembly down to the smallest scales [Fritz et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...623A.129F/abstract). Furthermore, future $30-40\text{m}$ class telescopes will extend spatially resolved spectroscopy to ultra-low-mass dwarfs ($10^5 - 10^7 M_\odot$) across diverse environments, testing models of environmental quenching and reionization fossils at the extreme faint end of the galaxy luminosity function [Aguerri et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251214783A/abstract). Another unresolved area that will benefit from future facilities is the relationship between dwarfs and their globular cluster (GC) populations in dense environments. Recent observations in clusters like Perseus highlight a striking anti-correlation between GC specific mass and stellar mass, where lower surface brightness dwarf galaxies appear disproportionately more GC-rich [Tang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251211070T/abstract). The origins of this trend, and whether it reflects distinct formation efficiencies or preferential tidal stripping of field stars over dense clusters, remain vital open questions.
