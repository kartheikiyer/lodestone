The **galaxy mass-to-light ratio** (denoted $\Upsilon$ or $M/L$) is a fundamental astrophysical parameter that relates a galaxy's integrated radiant flux to its physical mass, canonically expressed in solar units ($M_\odot/L_\odot$). It serves as a primary [[mass-proxy|Mass proxy]] in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], bridging observational [[integrated-photometry|Integrated photometry]] with the underlying mass distribution of both luminous baryons and dark matter halos. Astrophysicists generally distinguish between the *stellar* mass-to-light ratio ($\Upsilon_*$), which quantifies the mass locked purely in [[stellar-component|Stellar component]]s, and the *dynamical* (or total) mass-to-light ratio, which incorporates all matter components, including gas and the system's [[dark-matter-fraction|Dark matter fraction]]. The foundational derivations of these ratios—ranging from early virial analyses of elliptical galaxies [Fish et al. (1964)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1964ApJ...139..284F/abstract) to the kinematic mapping of binary galaxy dark halos [Turner et al. (1976)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1976ApJ...208..304T/abstract)—established the modern paradigm that mass does not strictly trace light across the universe.

The stellar mass-to-light ratio is highly sensitive to a galaxy's [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]], [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] (metallicity), and the assumed initial mass function (IMF). It is traditionally derived using [[traditional-sed-fitting-techniques|Traditional SED fitting techniques]] or empirical [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]] that correlate $\Upsilon_*$ with broadband [[rest-frame-colors|Rest-frame colors]]. Because optical bands are heavily influenced by young, massive stars and dust extinction, near-infrared wavelengths (such as the K-band) are broadly preferred for yielding a more stable $\Upsilon_*$ that traces the older stellar backbone. Furthermore, systematic variations in the mass-to-light ratio are the primary physical drivers behind the behavior of [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]]. For example, a systematically increasing $\Upsilon$ with galaxy mass explains the "tilt" of the Fundamental Plane in [[early-type-fraction|early-type galaxies]] [Bernardi et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10344B/abstract), while wavelength-dependent $\Upsilon$ variations govern the slope of the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] in rotationally supported disks.

On macroscopic scales, comparing a system's [[lensing-derived-total-mass|dynamical mass]]—inferred from [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|Stellar and Galactic Kinematics]], extended gas [[universal-rotation-curves|Universal Rotation Curves]], or [[strong-gravitational-lensing|Strong gravitational lensing]]—to its total luminosity provides a direct measure of its dark matter content. Observations reveal that the total mass-to-light ratio exhibits a non-monotonic, "U-shaped" dependence on the system's mass. The ratio reaches a minimum of approximately $100\ h_{75}^{-1}\ M_\odot/L_\odot$ (in the B-band) near the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|characteristic luminosity]] ($L^*$) of typical galaxies, where the conversion of baryons into stars is most efficient [Marinoni et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..232..132M/abstract), [Marinoni et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...569..101M/abstract). Conversely, the $M/L$ ratio increases sharply toward both the faintest [[low-luminosity-galaxies|dwarf spheroidal galaxies]] and the most [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] and rich clusters, reflecting environments dominated by dark matter where [[integrated-star-formation|Integrated star formation]] has been heavily suppressed or quenched.

Tracing the mass-to-light ratio across the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]] remains a vibrant area of active research, particularly with the advent of deep infrared surveys. At [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|high redshifts]] ($z > 7$), galaxies exhibit extremely low $\Upsilon_*$ values indicative of young, bursty stellar populations. This has sparked intense debate over whether recently discovered early luminous systems are genuinely [[overly-massive-galaxies|Overly massive galaxies]] challenging standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology, or if their stellar masses have simply been overestimated by miscalibrated mass-to-light ratios. In the local universe, theoretical frontiers continue to grapple with the contested presence of dark-matter-free dwarf galaxies, potential environmental variations of the IMF, and systematic uncertainties in resolving the [[age-extinction-degeneracy|Age-extinction degeneracy]], all of which critically dictate the accuracy of extragalactic mass scales.

## Fundamental Concepts and Physical Basis

Modern [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] are the result of physical processes occurring over 14 billion years, from the recombination of primordial gas to the hierarchical assembly of stars, gas, and dark matter [Cimatti et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191206216C/abstract). Comprehensive frameworks link these physical processes of galaxy formation to the multi-wavelength imaging and [[integrated-light-spectroscopy|Integrated light spectroscopy]] used to extract their evolutionary properties [Cimatti et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020igfe.book.....C/abstract). Within these frameworks, the mass-to-light ratio, denoted $\Upsilon$ or $M/L$, is a fundamental parameter that links a galaxy's integrated radiant flux to its physical mass. It is canonically expressed in solar units ($M_\odot/L_\odot$), which anchors extragalactic mass measurements to the well-defined mass and luminosity of the Sun. At a microscopic level, this integrated metric is fundamentally anchored by the [[stellar-luminosity-function|stellar mass-luminosity relation]] for individual stars—spanning from $0.07$ to $33\ M_\odot$—which serves as the physical baseline for integrated galaxy $M/L$ ratios [Henry et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..318..159H/abstract). Because galaxies are composed of both luminous baryons and dark matter halos, astrophysical analyses must distinguish between the stellar mass-to-light ratio ($\Upsilon_\ast$) and the total (or dynamical) mass-to-light ratio. The derivation of either quantity relies heavily on [[integrated-photometry|Integrated photometry]] measured across specific wavebands.

The stellar mass-to-light ratio $\Upsilon_\ast$ serves as a direct [[mass-proxy|Mass proxy]] that connects a galaxy's observed emission to the total mass of its stellar populations. Its value is driven by star formation history, [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]], and the initial mass function (IMF). Because massive, highly luminous stars dominate a galaxy's radiant output but are short-lived, $\Upsilon_\ast$ varies significantly with the age of the stellar population: young, star-forming galaxies exhibit much lower $\Upsilon_\ast$ values than older, [[galaxy-populations|quiescent galaxies]]. Empirically, [[rest-frame-colors|rest-frame optical colors]] such as $(B-V)$ or $(g-r)$ serve as robust predictors of $\Upsilon_\ast$ across diverse galaxy types, including [[giant-low-surface-brightness-galaxies|low-surface-brightness systems]], allowing $\Upsilon_\ast$ to be estimated to a precision of $\sigma_{\log \Upsilon} \simeq 0.06$ dex under ideal assumptions [Wilkins et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431..430W/abstract), [Du et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019RAA....19..171D/abstract), [Du et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....159..138D/abstract). However, near-infrared wavelengths (such as the K-band or $3.6\ \mu\mathrm{m}$) are generally preferred over optical bands; they are significantly less sensitive to recent star formation and dust extinction, yielding a more stable $\Upsilon_\ast$ that traces the dominant, older stellar backbone [Lin et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591..749L/abstract), [Forbes et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4611F/abstract). Despite these tight photometric correlations, systematic uncertainties in [[traditional-sed-fitting-techniques|stellar population synthesis models]]—particularly assumptions regarding the universality of the IMF—can lead to mass-scale discrepancies of up to a factor of 3 when comparing distinct galaxy populations [Kannappan et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...657L...5K/abstract).

In contrast to $\Upsilon_\ast$, the total mass-to-light ratio compares integrated luminosity with [[lensing-derived-total-mass|dynamical masses]] inferred from stellar kinematics, gas rotation, or [[strong-gravitational-lensing|Strong gravitational lensing]]. This metric directly quantifies the overall [[dark-matter-fraction|Dark matter fraction]] and traces [[galaxy-mass-fractions|Galaxy mass fractions]] across different environments. Observations demonstrate that the total $M/L$ is not constant; rather, it exhibits a non-monotonic, "U-shaped" dependence on the system's mass scale. The ratio reaches a minimum of roughly $\Upsilon \approx 100\ h_{75}^{-1}\ M_\odot/L_\odot$ (in the B-band) near the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|characteristic luminosity]] ($L^\ast$) of typical galaxies, where star formation has historically been most efficient [Marinoni et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..232..132M/abstract), [Marinoni et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...569..101M/abstract), [Eke et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.355..769E/abstract). At both lower masses ([[low-luminosity-galaxies|dwarf galaxies]]) and higher masses, the total $M/L$ increases significantly—reaching $278 \pm 42\ h\ M_\odot/L_\odot$ in rich galaxy groups (approaching values seen in massive clusters) [Parker et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...634..806P/abstract)—reflecting a lower baryonic fraction locked in stars compared to dark matter.

On cosmological scales, $\Upsilon$ is an essential conversion factor used to map the observed [[galaxy-luminosity-function|luminosity density]] of the universe to its underlying stellar and total mass density. The evolution of $M/L$ over cosmic time provides critical constraints on mass assembly. For instance, recent observations of extremely luminous galaxies at $z > 7$ have sparked intense debate; tensions exist regarding whether these galaxies are genuinely [[overly-massive-galaxies|Overly massive galaxies]] or if their stellar masses have been overestimated by applying local $\Upsilon_\ast$ scaling relations to young, bursty stellar populations with exceptionally low mass-to-light ratios [Rojas-Ruiz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240800843R/abstract), [Donnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250103217D/abstract). Similarly, ambiguity remains in the local universe regarding the precise $M/L$ limits of purportedly dark-matter-free dwarf galaxies, where low [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|velocity dispersions]] challenge the limits of dynamical $M/L$ estimation [Martin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...859L...5M/abstract).

## Historical Development of Dynamical Estimates

Historically, the translation of integrated luminosity into a reliable mass estimate relied heavily on the application of the [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|virial theorem]] to self-gravitating systems. Foundational reviews established the standard classification of optical and radio properties of galaxies, including early constraints on integrated energy distributions and masses [Sandage et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975gaun.book.....S/abstract). Early dynamical estimates of the mass-to-light ratio ($M/L$) frequently assumed that mass perfectly traced light, implying a spatially constant $M/L$ profile. Studies of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] and cluster environments established the first high-baseline $M/L$ values. For instance, early photometric and dynamical analyses by Fish found that the potential energy ($PE$) of [[galaxy-populations|elliptical galaxies]] scaled with mass as:
$$PE \propto M^{3/2}$$
yielding mean visual $M/L$ values of roughly $31 \pm 3$ $M_\odot/L_\odot$ [Fish et al. (1964)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1964ApJ...139..284F/abstract). Similar values were recovered for the [[mean-bcg-luminosity|brightest cluster galaxies]], cementing the idea that these massive systems possessed substantial mass components relative to their luminous output [Sandage et al. (1973)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1973ApJ...183..731S/abstract). Large-scale surveys and compilations, notably the foundational Third Reference Catalogue of Bright Galaxies ([[rc3-catalog|RC3 catalog]]), provided essential multi-wavelength data—including [[photometric-systems-and-color-indices|optical and infrared magnitudes]], as well as morphological classifications—that enabled systematic studies of mass-to-light variations across the [[galaxy-structure|Hubble sequence]] [de Vaucouleurs et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991rc3..book.....D/abstract).

The extension of dynamical measurements to binary galaxies provided some of the first robust statistical evidence for extended [[total-galactic-halo-luminosity|dark halos]]. Early studies of these pairs systematically underestimated their dynamical masses by an order of magnitude due to severe selection effects, particularly an observational bias toward pairs with small projected spatial separations. By rigorously correcting for these biases, Turner demonstrated that spiral galaxies must possess dark halos containing approximately ten times the mass of the luminous disk [Turner et al. (1976)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1976ApJ...208..304T/abstract). Further investigations into such systems added nuance; for example, [[integrated-photometry|surface photometry]] of 26 binary spirals indicated that while some systems show increasing M/L with radius, others maintain a constant ratio, with a mean individual $M/L_B$ of 8.5 [Blackman et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984MNRAS.208...91B/abstract). Assuming a Hubble constant of $H_0 = 50$ km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$, these corrected binary galaxy dynamics yielded total $M/L$ ratios of approximately 65. Furthermore, this analysis quantified the morphological dependence of the mass-to-light ratio, establishing that early-type systems exhibit an $M/L$ roughly $2.0 \pm 0.5$ times greater than that of late-type systems [Turner et al. (1976)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1976ApJ...208..304T/abstract). Additionally, early dynamical measurements of [[low-luminosity-galaxies|dwarf spheroidals]] like Sculptor yielded visual $M/L$ ratios of approximately $6.0$, over twice that typically observed in globular clusters, hinting early on at the presence of dark matter in these faint systems [Armandroff et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986AJ.....92..777A/abstract).

As observational techniques improved, the simplistic assumption of a singular isothermal sphere or a globally constant $M/L$ came under theoretical scrutiny. The construction of self-consistent spherical galaxy models revealed significant degeneracies when $M/L$ was assumed to be independent of radius. Dynamical models relying on specific [[sersic-profile|surface brightness profiles]], such as the [[de-vaucouleurs-law|De Vaucouleurs law]], demonstrated that a central [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|velocity dispersion]] measurement at a single radius is insufficient to uniquely determine the global $M/L$ without invoking ad hoc assumptions about the kinematic profile [Richstone et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApJ...286...27R/abstract). Accurate derivations required either measurements of the root-mean-square velocity dispersion within an aperture enclosing a large fraction of the total luminosity, or spatially resolved dispersion profiles spanning across the galaxy [Richstone et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApJ...286...27R/abstract).

The accumulated dynamical evidence ultimately forced a paradigm shift in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]]. The assumption that a single, constant $M/L$ could characterize a galaxy or galaxy cluster was replaced by the understanding that the parameter is highly dependent on scale and environment. Large-scale surveys and [[galaxy-galaxy-gravitational-lensing|weak lensing]] measurements of galaxy groups later confirmed this transition, demonstrating a steep increase in the mass-to-light ratio from "poor" groups ($M/L \approx 134$ $h M_\odot/L_{\odot, B}$) to "rich" groups ($M/L \approx 278$ $h M_\odot/L_{\odot, B}$), effectively mapping the transition from star-forming field environments to systems with a much higher [[dark-matter-fraction|Dark matter fraction]] [Parker et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...634..806P/abstract).

## Stellar Population Synthesis and MLCRs

The stellar mass-to-light ratio, denoted $\Upsilon_*$, is principally derived by comparing observed photometry or spectroscopy with stellar population synthesis (SPS) models. In this context, a galaxy's spectral energy distribution functions as a fundamental "fingerprint" for reconstructing its evolutionary history and stellar populations [Iyer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250217680I/abstract). By employing [[traditional-sed-fitting-techniques|Traditional SED fitting techniques]] or [[multi-component-sed-fitting|Multi-component SED fitting]] approaches, astrophysicists extract fundamental physical parameters such as the [[galaxy-stellar-age|Galaxy stellar age]], metallicity, and star formation history (SFH). The resulting mass-to-light ratio is highly sensitive to the assumed [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] and the treatment of stellar evolutionary phases.

The derivation of $\Upsilon_*$ via spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting is persistently complicated by the [[age-extinction-degeneracy|Age-extinction degeneracy]] and the age-metallicity degeneracy, where old, metal-rich, or heavily dust-obscured stellar populations produce similarly red spectra. Resolving these degeneracies frequently requires broadband coverage spanning from the ultraviolet to the mid-infrared. For example, incorporating WISE mid-infrared photometry helps to effectively constrain active galactic nucleus (AGN) contributions; however, studies indicate that lacking these mid-infrared bands does not actually significantly bias the resulting stellar mass estimates for star-forming galaxies [Siudek et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A.308S/abstract). Furthermore, the literature remains explicitly conflicted on the absolute scaling of $\Upsilon_*$. Systematic differences in SPS model ingredients (such as the choice between Bruzual-Charlot and Maraston models) can yield relative stellar mass scale discrepancies of up to a factor of 3 between distinct galaxy classes [Kannappan et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...657L...5K/abstract).

To bypass the computationally intensive nature of full SED fitting, researchers frequently utilize empirical Mass-to-Light Color Relations (MLCRs). For example, the comprehensive [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|SDSS]] Data Release 2 physical property catalogs provided a transformative dataset, supplying derived stellar masses, mass-to-light ratios, [[star-formation-rate-indicators-and-metrics|star formation rates]], metallicities, and dust attenuation estimates for over 200,000 star-forming and AGN host galaxies [Brinchmann et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph..6220B/abstract). These scaling relations typically model the mass-to-light ratio as a function of [[photometric-systems-and-color-indices|optical color indices]]. Empirical relations exhibit a linear dependence for quiescent galaxies and a quadratic dependence for star-forming galaxies [López-Sanjuan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A..51L/abstract). Theoretical models demonstrate that a single optical color can predict the rest-frame $V$-band $\Upsilon_*$ with a precision of $\sim 0.06$ dex, provided the IMF and redshift are defined [Wilkins et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431..430W/abstract). Observational surveys have confirmed that [[rest-frame-colors|Rest-frame colors]] such as $(g-r)$ or $(g-i)$ are highly effective predictors of the optical mass-to-light ratio up to $z \approx 1.5$ [López-Sanjuan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A..51L/abstract). To capture dynamic changes in stellar populations alongside simple colors, researchers have introduced parameters like "Star Formation Acceleration" (SFA)—a derivative of the star formation rate—which uses [[normalized-star-formation-rate|linear regression of observed colors]] to distinguish between rapidly quenching and bursting galaxy populations [Martin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...842...20M/abstract). Furthermore, these optical indices are sufficiently robust to produce spatially resolved $\Upsilon_*$ maps across local galaxy disks [Du et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019RAA....19..171D/abstract). Moreover, color indices can improve photometric [[chemical-abundances|metallicity]] estimates by up to 50% compared to using luminosity alone. This improvement stems from the tight link between color, star formation rate residuals, and the stellar mass-to-light ratio [Sanders et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...775..125S/abstract).

Despite the utility of MLCRs, individual empirical calibrations exhibit internal discrepancies of $0.1$ to $0.3$ dex when applied across different photometric bands for the same galaxy [Du et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....160..122D/abstract). Broader offsets between distinct literature calibrations, however, are primarily driven by differing underlying SPS assumptions—such as the adopted SFH or IMF—rather than intrinsic physical differences like galaxy [[luminous-high-surface-brightness-galaxies|surface brightness]] [Du et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....160..122D/abstract), [Du et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....159..138D/abstract). 

To mitigate these optical uncertainties, researchers increasingly rely on near-infrared (NIR) [[photometric-bands-and-systems|passbands]]. In the NIR (e.g., $K$-band), the mass-to-light ratio varies by less than a factor of two across diverse star formation histories, compared to a factor of ten in the blue optical bands. Consequently, $3.6 \mu\text{m}$ imaging has emerged as a superior tracer of total stellar mass, as it is largely insensitive to recent star formation bursts, dust attenuation, and metallicity variations in older stellar populations [Forbes et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4611F/abstract), [Schombert et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.1496S/abstract).

## Kinematic and Lensing Probes of Dark Matter

By comparing a system's total dynamical mass to its integrated luminosity, astronomers can constrain the [[dark-matter-fraction|Dark matter fraction]] across vastly different astrophysical scales. Unlike the stellar mass-to-light ratio ($\Upsilon_*$), which measures only the baryonic component locked in stars, the dynamical mass-to-light ratio ($M/L$) incorporates all matter, providing a direct probe of dark matter halos.

For star-forming disk galaxies, extended H I gas dynamics and [[universal-rotation-curves|Universal Rotation Curves]] map the gravitational potential far beyond the optical disk. Recent spatially resolved studies of the Radial Acceleration Relation (RAR) have demonstrated a tight empirical coupling between the observed dynamical acceleration and the [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|baryonic mass]]. By applying locally varying mass-to-light ratios, the RAR exhibits a remarkably low intrinsic scatter of just $0.045$ dex, reinforcing the strict [[galaxy-scaling-relations|scaling]] between dark and baryonic matter in these systems [Vărăşteanu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.541.2366V/abstract), [Varasteanu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250420857V/abstract). In more complex disk structures, the [[galaxy-structure|morphology of outer rings]] in barred galaxies (such as NGC 6782) can be used to dynamically constrain both the mass-to-light ratio of the stellar bar and the central dark matter fraction [Quillen et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...487..603Q/abstract). For face-on spiral galaxies, where rotation curves are difficult to measure due to [[galaxy-inclination|inclination effects]], novel methods determine the dynamical $M/L$ by analyzing the thickness of the disk and vertical velocity dispersion profiles, directly probing the local [[galaxy-central-density|disk surface density]] [Hu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...446L...5H/abstract). However, mapping the $M/L$ profile from gas kinematics is not entirely free of systematic uncertainties; in the outskirts of some galaxies, large-scale magnetic fields may provide non-gravitational support that can subtly bias the derived dynamical mass [Jałocha et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421.2155J/abstract). Within our own [[besan-on-galaxy-model|Milky Way]], studies have incorporated COBE/DIRBE near-infrared data and terminal velocity plots to determine the mass-to-light ratio of the [[baade-s-window|Galactic bulge]] and disk. These derivations are essential for constraining the microlensing optical depths toward the Galactic center [Bissantz et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997MNRAS.289..651B/abstract).

In [[early-type-fraction|early-type galaxies]] and [[low-luminosity-galaxies|dwarf spheroidals]], which generally lack extended cold gas, the mass distribution must be inferred from stellar kinematics. The [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|velocity dispersion]] of stars, globular clusters, or planetary nebulae is often modeled using the [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|Jeans equations]] or Schwarzschild orbit-superposition techniques. These methods can accurately recover the intrinsic $M/L$ even when the physical inclination is only marginally constrained [Krajnović et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.357.1113K/abstract). Kinematic tracers reveal that dynamical $M/L$ ratios reach extreme values at the low-mass end of the galaxy sequence; the Draco dwarf spheroidal, for example, requires $M/L_V \approx 130-140$ in standard cold dark matter cosmologies [Łokas et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006EAS....20..113L/abstract). Furthermore, observations of globular clusters in Andromeda (M31) show velocity dispersions as high as $40$ km/s, implying mass-to-light ratios roughly twice those of Galactic globular clusters [Peterson et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ddse.work..161P/abstract). Moreover, very early infrared photometry of the outer halo of M87 revealed a high mass-to-light ratio ($M/L > 100$), providing some of the first compelling evidence for massive dark matter halos extending around [[galaxy-populations|giant elliptical galaxies]] [Boughn et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...265L..55B/abstract). Conversely, reports of dwarf galaxies completely lacking dark matter (such as NGC 1052-DF2) remain highly debated. Reassessments of the sparse globular cluster kinematics in such systems suggest that small sample sizes yield poorly constrained upper limits ($M/L_V < 6.7-8.1$), which are insufficient to definitively rule out the presence of a dark matter halo [Martin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...859L...5M/abstract). Furthermore, discrepancies can arise at large radii in [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], where kinematic models occasionally conflict with X-ray derived mass profiles [Samurovic et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006SerAJ.173...35S/abstract).

Gravitational lensing provides a complementary, purely geometry-dependent probe of the total mass that is free from the assumption of dynamical equilibrium. [[strong-gravitational-lensing|Strong gravitational lensing]] by [[luminous-red-galaxies|Luminous red galaxies]] at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 0.2-0.5$) yields typical inner-halo mass-to-light ratios of $M/L_B \approx 14\ h\ M_\odot/L_\odot$ [Anguita et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748..129A/abstract). On larger scales, [[galaxy-galaxy-gravitational-lensing|weak lensing]] is instrumental in extending $M/L$ measurements to [[luminosity-functions-and-scaling-relations|galaxy groups]] and clusters. Weak lensing surveys of "normal" [[galaxy-property-distributions|galaxy clusters]] at $z \sim 0.2$ demonstrate that mass closely follows light on scales of $0.2$ to $2$ Mpc, although significant cluster-to-cluster scatter in the $M/L$ ratio remains [Wittman et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000cucg.confE..60W/abstract). More broadly, weak lensing analyses demonstrate a steep increase in $M/L$ with system mass: poor groups exhibit $M/L_B \approx 134\ h$, whereas rich groups reach $M/L_B \approx 278\ h$, closely approaching the extreme values seen in massive clusters [Parker et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...634..806P/abstract). Similarly, statistical studies of binary galaxies yield total $M/L \approx 65$, implying the ubiquitous presence of massive dark halos extending far beyond the visible galaxies [Turner et al. (1976)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1976ApJ...208..304T/abstract). Furthermore, the gas-to-light ratio in galaxy clusters has been proposed as a nearly universal constant, which helps in estimating the overall [[galaxy-mass-fractions|baryon density]] ($\Omega_b$) via Oort's method [Carlberg et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998JRASC..92R..23C/abstract).

Collectively, these kinematic and lensing measurements demonstrate that the relationship between dynamical mass and light is not uniform. Instead, the global $M/L$ ratio exhibits a distinctive "U-shaped" dependence on mass. The lowest dark matter fractions—and hence the minimum total $M/L$ ratios—occur near the characteristic luminosity $L^*$ (corresponding to $M/L_B \approx 100\ h_{75}^{-1}$ on halo scales) [Marinoni et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..232..132M/abstract), [Marinoni et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...569..101M/abstract), [Eke et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.355..769E/abstract). Star formation efficiency peaks in these $L^*$ halos, while the $M/L$ ratio systematically increases toward both the most massive galaxy clusters and the faintest dwarf galaxies.

## Scaling Relations and the Fundamental Plane

The [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] of galaxies provide critical insights into the structural and compositional regularities of both [[early-type-fraction|early- and late-type systems]]. Across these empirical laws, the mass-to-light ratio ($\Upsilon$) acts as the primary variable that links the observable luminosity of a galaxy to its underlying dynamical mass.

For early-type galaxies, the defining scaling law is the Fundamental Plane (FP), which relates the [[half-light-radius|effective radius]] $R_e$, central [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|velocity dispersion]] $\sigma_0$, and mean [[luminous-high-surface-brightness-galaxies|surface brightness]] $\langle I \rangle_e$ via the relation $R_e \propto \sigma_0^a \langle I \rangle_e^b$. Observations consistently yield specific coefficients; for example, in the SDSS $r$-band, the plane is best fit by $a \approx 1.49$ and $b \approx -0.75$ [Bernardi et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10344B/abstract). This systematic deviation from simpler dimensional expectations is known as the "tilt" of the Fundamental Plane, indicating that $\Upsilon$ is not constant but increases systematically with galaxy mass. At fixed luminosity, optical studies find that the mass-to-light ratio scales as $\Upsilon \propto L^{0.14}$ [Bernardi et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10344B/abstract). 

The physical origin of the FP tilt is a subject of ongoing debate. It is partially driven by stellar population variations, as more massive galaxies tend to be older and more metal-rich. However, detailed spectroscopic analyses show that stellar population effects alone cannot fully account for the tilt; even after correcting for the [[chemical-abundances|mass-metallicity relation]] (yielding a residual scaling of $\Upsilon \propto M^{0.15}$), a significant dynamically driven tilt remains [Proctor et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.386.1781P/abstract). This residual variation is attributed to structural [[galaxy-structure|non-homology]] or systematically changing dark matter fractions. Furthermore, variations in the mass-to-light ratio across scales from globular clusters to galaxy clusters suggest that dark matter halos in groups may be lost when they are incorporated into larger clusters [Ibarra-Medel et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011RMxAC..40...64I/abstract). The mass-to-light ratio is also deeply intertwined with galaxy geometry, exhibiting a tight correlation with the [[sersic-profile|Sersic profile]] index $n$ and stellar mass in a 3D parameter space [D'Onofrio et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...727L...6D/abstract). Intriguingly, this behavior extends downward to star clusters, which populate a continuous "Fundamental Manifold," suggesting that the physics governing baryonic settling and the resulting $\Upsilon$ are universal, even in systems lacking dark matter halos [Zaritsky et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...727..116Z/abstract).

In rotationally supported disk galaxies, the analogous kinematic scaling is the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]], which connects total luminosity to the maximum [[universal-rotation-curves|rotational velocity]] $V$. The logarithmic slope of this relation, $\partial\ln L/\partial\ln V$, exhibits a pronounced wavelength dependence: it is relatively shallow in the optical $B$-band ($\sim 2.5$) and steepens to $\sim 4.0$ in the near-infrared $K$-band. This shift is a direct consequence of systematic variations in the stellar mass-to-light ratio [Gavazzi et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...419..469G/abstract). More massive disk galaxies possess older, more metal-rich stellar populations and are therefore intrinsically [[rest-frame-colors|redder]]. Consequently, their fraction of blue light decreases with mass, systematically driving up the optical $\Upsilon$. In contrast, near-infrared emission is less sensitive to recent star formation and dust extinction, yielding an $\Upsilon$ that is much closer to constant across the galaxy sequence. As a result, infrared luminosity serves as a much more faithful tracer of dynamical mass [Gavazzi et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...419..469G/abstract). When the cold gas mass is added to the stellar mass derived from these near-infrared $\Upsilon$ values, the even tighter [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] emerges, demonstrating that accurate constraints on the mass-to-light ratio are essential for deciphering galactic scaling laws.

## Wavelength Dependence and Dust Effects

The stellar mass-to-light ratio, denoted $\Upsilon_*$, exhibits a profound dependence on the observed photometric band. This wavelength dependence arises primarily because a galaxy's spectral energy distribution is a composite of diverse stellar populations, compounded by the wavelength-dependent attenuation of interstellar dust. In the [[ultraviolet-galaxies|ultraviolet]] and blue optical bands (e.g., the [[photometric-bands-and-systems|U and B bands]]), the integrated light is disproportionately dominated by young, massive, and highly luminous stars. Consequently, optical mass-to-light ratios are extremely sensitive to recent star formation histories and can vary by up to a factor of $10$ across different galaxy types. Furthermore, dust extinction is most severe at these shorter wavelengths, introducing significant systematic uncertainties into optical mass estimations.

To circumvent these degeneracies, astronomers rely on empirical [[color-mass-relation|Color-Mass-to-Light Relations (CMLRs)]], which are closely tied to the broader [[color-magnitude-relation|Color-magnitude relation]]. These scaling laws tightly correlate broadband [[rest-frame-colors|Rest-frame colors]] with $\Upsilon_*$, allowing for mass estimates without intensive spectroscopy. Studies have demonstrated that single-color indices, most notably the $(g-r)$ and $(g-i)$ colors, are highly robust predictors of the optical $\Upsilon_*$ for both high and low surface brightness galaxies [Du et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....159..138D/abstract). These relations remain remarkably stable over cosmic time, successfully predicting $\Upsilon_*$ for both quiescent and star-forming populations up to $z \approx 1.5$ [López-Sanjuan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A..51L/abstract). Spatially resolved studies further confirm that $(g-r)$ color maps accurately trace radial $\Upsilon_*$ [[color-gradients|gradients]] within local galaxies [Du et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019RAA....19..171D/abstract). Although dust can systematically affect these estimations, the reddening vector is often naturally aligned with the CMLR slope in certain color spaces, partially mitigating the error [Wilkins et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431..430W/abstract). Despite their utility, individual empirical relations can exhibit internal discrepancies of $0.1$ to $0.3$ dex in predicted stellar mass when applied across different photometric bands for the same galaxy, while broader differences between distinct literature calibrations typically stem from variations in the assumed [[age-extinction-degeneracy|stellar population synthesis models]] (e.g., initial mass functions) rather than intrinsic galaxy properties [Du et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....160..122D/abstract).

Because of the high variance in the optical, [[integrated-photometry|near-infrared (NIR) photometry]]—specifically the [[k-z-relation|K-band]] ($2.2 \, \mu\mathrm{m}$) and the $3.6 \, \mu\mathrm{m}$ band—is widely considered superior for tracing total stellar mass [Jarrett et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....125..525J/abstract). In the NIR regime, luminosity is dominated by the older, lower-mass stars (such as K and M dwarfs or [[stellar-component|red giants]]) that comprise the bulk of a galaxy's baryonic mass. Because the NIR is significantly less sensitive to both recent starbursts and dust extinction, the variation in $\Upsilon_*$ is reduced to a factor of roughly two across a wide range of star formation histories. For instance, [[panchromatic-surveys|Spitzer/IRAC]] mid-infrared imaging has been successfully employed to trace old stellar populations and characterize disk-to-halo transitions in the extended outskirts of local galaxies like [[notable-ngc-galaxies-n-o-sector|M31]] [Barmby et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..321...19B/abstract). At $3.6 \, \mu\mathrm{m}$, $\Upsilon_*$ is also exceptionally stable against variations in the metallicity of old stellar populations [Forbes et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4611F/abstract).

The stability of the near-infrared mass-to-light ratio has direct implications for [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]]. For instance, the slope of the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] varies substantially with wavelength. In the optical bands, luminosity scales with a lower power of the rotational velocity due to the systematic reddening and increased $\Upsilon_*$ of more massive galaxies. In the infrared, however, the slope closely approaches the theoretical value expected for a constant mass-to-light ratio, confirming that infrared luminosity acts as a much cleaner proxy for dynamical mass [Gavazzi et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...419..469G/abstract). This advantage extends to larger scales; even in galaxy clusters, the $K$-band luminosity correlates tightly with cluster mass, yielding robust binding mass estimates where optical bands would be obscured or biased by variations in star formation efficiency [Lin et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591..749L/abstract).

## Environmental and Structural Variations

The mass-to-light ratio ($\Upsilon \equiv M/L$) of a galaxy is not a universal constant; rather, it varies systematically with galaxy [[galaxy-structure|morphology]], structural parameters, and local environment. These variations reflect underlying differences in stellar populations, star formation histories, and the relative dominance of dark matter.

**Morphology and Internal Structure**
The stellar mass-to-light ratio correlates strongly with galaxy morphology. [[early-type-fraction|Early-type galaxies]] typically possess older, redder stellar populations and consequently exhibit higher $\Upsilon$ values than late-type spirals. In early-type systems, the mass-to-light ratio is a fundamental parameter of [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]], such as the Fundamental Plane, where observations indicate a scaling of approximately $\Upsilon \propto L^{0.14}$ at fixed luminosity [Bernardi et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10344B/abstract). Early observations of H I in [[tight-s0-5-stellar-mass-relation|S0 galaxies]] demonstrated that even in these seemingly quiescent early-type systems, there is a clear correlation between the gas mass-to-light ratio and the galaxy's optical color, linking residual gas content to subtle variations in stellar populations [Balick et al. (1976)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1976ApJ...209..710B/abstract).

Furthermore, $\Upsilon$ is not spatially uniform within individual galaxies. Spatially resolved studies reveal that late-type galaxies typically display negative [[color-gradients|radial M/L gradients]] that steepen with increasing galaxy mass, consistent with inside-out star formation models. Conversely, early-type galaxies exhibit shallower M/L gradients that vary depending on a characteristic transition mass [Tortora et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.1557T/abstract). At the faintest light levels, wide-area [[stellar-counts|star counts]] are critical for resolving the structural properties of galaxy halos; observations targeting the outskirts of M31 and M33 have successfully used stellar density maps to trace large-scale structure, substructure, and stellar content far beyond the visible disk [Ferguson et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASSP....3..239F/abstract).

[[luminous-high-surface-brightness-galaxies|Surface brightness]] also plays a structural role, though its impact on the stellar M/L ratio is often less drastic than historically presumed. Studies of [[giant-low-surface-brightness-galaxies|low surface brightness galaxies (LSBGs)]] demonstrate that they broadly obey the same empirical color-mass-to-light relations as their high surface brightness counterparts. Discrepancies in the derived $\Upsilon$ for these systems typically stem from differences in the assumed stellar population synthesis models—such as the Initial Mass Function (IMF) or star formation history—rather than intrinsic deviations in the galaxies' surface brightness [Du et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....159..138D/abstract). Additionally, the structural parameter space occupied by galaxies in the optical window reveals a universal luminosity-surface brightness relation, highlighting the prevalence of low-luminosity, low-surface-brightness dwarfs in the local universe [Driver et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..218..309D/abstract).

**Environmental and Scale Variations**
The total dynamical mass-to-light ratio shows a pronounced dependence on the environment and the total mass of virialized halos. Observational evidence demonstrates that the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]]—and thereby the distribution of mass-to-light ratios—depends heavily on local density, providing foundational support for [[backward-evolution-models|theories of biased galaxy formation]] where massive halos preferentially collapse in overdense environments [Giovanelli et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ASSL..141..133G/abstract). Results from the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS]] confirm the dependence of the galaxy luminosity function on surface brightness, spectral type, and local density [Colless et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASA...21..352C/abstract). Across a wide dynamical range, the mass-to-light function exhibits a distinct "U-shape." The minimum of this distribution occurs near the characteristic luminosity of an $L^*$ galaxy (as parameterized by the [[schechter-function|Schechter function]]), where the B-band mass-to-light ratio reaches roughly $100\, h_{75}^{-1} M_\odot/L_{\odot, B}$ [Marinoni et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..232..132M/abstract), [Marinoni et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...569..101M/abstract), [Eke et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.355..769E/abstract). This minimum implies that $L^*$ galaxies represent the scale at which the conversion of baryons into stars is most efficient.

Towards larger scales, the total $\Upsilon$ rises significantly, reflecting a higher dark matter fraction. [[galaxy-galaxy-gravitational-lensing|Weak gravitational lensing]] measurements demonstrate that the mass-to-light ratio increases from $134 \pm 26\, h M_\odot/L_{\odot, B}$ in "poor" galaxy groups to $278 \pm 42\, h M_\odot/L_{\odot, B}$ in "rich" groups [Parker et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...634..806P/abstract). This steep increase highlights the transition from actively star-forming field environments to passively evolving [[galaxy-property-distributions|cluster regimes]]. This structural transition is also captured by the [[halo-mass-luminosity-relation|halo occupation number]], which scales non-linearly with total system mass (e.g., $N_{\rm gal} \propto M^{0.55}$), indicating that massive clusters host proportionally less stellar light per unit dark matter mass than smaller halos [Marinoni et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...569..101M/abstract). 

Finally, kinematic analyses of cluster infall regions provide a bridge between the deep cluster potential and the surrounding field. The global mass-to-light ratios measured in these infall zones have been used to trace the universal matter density, though some early estimates yielded surprisingly low values of $\Omega_m \sim 0.1$ compared to the standard cosmological consensus [Rines et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ogci.conf..193R/abstract).

## Evolution Across Cosmic Time

The stellar mass-to-light ratio ($\Upsilon_*$) of galaxies is inextricably linked to the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]]. Because younger stellar populations are significantly more luminous per unit mass than older populations, $\Upsilon_*$ serves as a critical tracer of a galaxy's star formation history. Early [[backward-evolution-models|evolutionary models]] utilized detailed studies of the [[besan-on-galaxy-model|solar neighborhood]] to predict how the integrated light of distant galaxies evolves over time [Kron et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982VA.....26...37K/abstract). Observations indicate that the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] peaked around $z \sim 2$ and has declined by roughly an order of magnitude to the present day. Correspondingly, as the universe ages and the fraction of old stars increases, the average $\Upsilon_*$ of the galaxy population systematically rises. [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Redshift surveys]] at $z \sim 0.5$ reveal a marked steepening of the faint-end slope of the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]], driven by a 5-10 fold increase in the population of star-forming galaxies [Colless et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995wfsd.conf..263C/abstract). Note that studies of galaxy evolution at the start of the millennium shifted toward qualitatively different datasets requiring more detailed scientific analyses [Ellis et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001PASP..113..515E/abstract). For instance, observations of $K$-band selected field galaxies up to $z \sim 1.2$ show that near-infrared mass-to-light ratios systematically decline with increasing redshift for galaxies above $10^{10} h^{-2} M_\odot$, reflecting an epoch where the [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|cosmic stellar mass density]] was approximately $50\%$ of its local value [Drory et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...608..742D/abstract). Expanding on this, studies of the galaxy cosmological mass function (GCMF) from $z=0.5$ to $5.0$ map the assembly of stellar mass over time, interpreting its evolution as a signature of continuous mass assembly driven by hierarchical galaxy mergers and shifting global star formation histories [Lopes et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...572A..27L/abstract). Despite this overall temporal evolution in $\Upsilon_*$, empirical scaling relations remain remarkably stable; the tight correlation between $\Upsilon_*$ and [[rest-frame-colors|Rest-frame colors]] (such as $g-i$) shows no significant redshift evolution up to $z = 1.5$ [López-Sanjuan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A..51L/abstract).

At much higher redshifts ($z > 6$), which probe the [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|epoch of reionization]], the determination of $\Upsilon_*$ becomes both more challenging and a source of active debate in the literature. This early epoch is heavily obscured; however, discoveries of exceptionally bright [[total-ir-luminosity|submillimetre galaxies]] residing in nascent clusters just one billion years after the Big Bang demonstrate that intense bursts of star formation and rapid mass assembly were already underway in the early universe [Bolatto et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.486..199B/abstract). Early observations with the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) identified populations of highly luminous [[lyman-break-selection-and-galaxies|Lyman-break galaxies]] at $z > 7$. This led to initial reports of [[overly-massive-galaxies|Overly massive galaxies]] that seemingly challenged the halo mass assembly limits of standard $\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}$ cosmology. However, subsequent theoretical analyses argue that these high inferred stellar masses are likely artifacts of overestimating the mass-to-light ratio. Models of early galaxy formation demonstrate that standard cosmology can produce high ultraviolet luminosities driven by extremely low $\Upsilon_*$ values, which are characteristic of very young stellar populations and highly bursty star formation histories [Prada et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230411911P/abstract). When analyzing such populations over cosmic time, it is necessary to differentiate between luminosity functions of the "mass function type" (which are well-fit by standard Schechter functions) and those of the "star formation rate function type" driven by young stars; the latter require a Saunders function due to their high intrinsic scatter in star formation rates and mass-to-light ratios [Salim et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...758..134S/abstract).

Recent spectroscopic confirmations from wide-area, independent sight-line [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]] have helped constrain these early scaling relations while minimizing the significant effects of cosmic variance [Rojas-Ruiz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240800843R/abstract). At $z = 7 - 9$, stellar mass is found to scale almost linearly with [[nuv-luminosity-function|ultraviolet luminosity]], following the relation $M_* \propto L_{\mathrm{UV}}^{0.85 \pm 0.12}$ [Rojas-Ruiz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240800843R/abstract), [Rojas-Ruiz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985...80R/abstract). Crucially, this relation exhibits a large intrinsic scatter of approximately $0.5$ dex, which is entirely consistent with the stochastic nature of star formation in early, low-mass halos. Reconciling the observed ultraviolet luminosity functions with the evolving galaxy stellar mass function requires that the typical mass-to-light ratio of galaxies increases systematically beyond $z \simeq 6$. This evolution implies the presence of ever-younger stellar populations as one looks further back in time, successfully matching observational constraints without necessarily requiring modifications to the initial mass function or new cosmological physics [Donnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250103217D/abstract). Future progress in understanding the epoch of reionization relies on synergies between galaxy surveys of bright sources and direct reionization measurements of faint populations [Furlanetto et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190306197F/abstract).

## Current Frontiers and Theoretical Challenges

The precise derivation of the stellar mass-to-light ratio ($\Upsilon_*$) remains subject to foundational theoretical and methodological challenges, particularly regarding the universality of the [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] and the treatment of dust and star formation histories. A major open question is whether the IMF varies across different galactic environments. Systematic uncertainties inherent in [[traditional-sed-fitting-techniques|Traditional SED fitting techniques]]—such as the choice between Bruzual-Charlot and Maraston population synthesis models—can yield overall mass scales that disagree by factors of up to 2 in the local universe. More critically, these assumptions can cause relative mass scales to disagree by factors greater than 3 between distinct morphological classes [Kannappan et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...657L...5K/abstract). Furthermore, dynamical studies of spiral disks often require low baryonic mass-to-light ratios, which may necessitate a non-universal IMF or specific alterations in the star formation history to reconcile with photometric estimates [Portinari et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Ap&SS.284..723P/abstract).

Tensions also persist in interpreting dynamical mass-to-light ratios ($\Upsilon_{\rm dyn}$), particularly when attempting to quantify the [[dark-matter-fraction|Dark matter fraction]] in extreme environments. Kinematic modeling of dwarf spheroidal galaxies often yields exceptionally high $\Upsilon_{\rm dyn}$ under cold dark matter (CDM) assumptions. For instance, advanced made-to-measure models applied to the Draco dwarf estimate a V-band ratio of $\Upsilon_V = 539 \pm 136 \, M_\odot/L_\odot$ [Long et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.405..301L/abstract). Evaluating these systems under Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) yields drastically lower ratios (e.g., $\Upsilon_V \approx 21$ for Draco), though this remains too high to be explained by stellar content alone, highlighting a persistent mass deficit [Łokas et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006EAS....20..113L/abstract). Conversely, in [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] like the early-type NGC 4649, both MOND and Newtonian dynamics can successfully reproduce observed [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|velocity dispersions]] using a constant $\Upsilon_B \approx 7$, without requiring significant dark matter in the outer halo [Samurović et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...488..873S/abstract). In the local universe, standard assumptions regarding dark matter are continually tested. Recent mass distribution models for the Milky Way, derived from precise Gaia [[stellar-counts|billion-star density maps]], even challenge the strict necessity of massive dark halos, suggesting that observed [[universal-rotation-curves|flat rotation curves]] might be explained through alternative mass models or modified dynamics [Li et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161207781L/abstract). The literature regarding extremely low-$\Upsilon$ objects is highly conflicting; recent claims of [[galaxy-populations|galaxies]] entirely lacking dark matter, such as NGC 1052-DF2, have been contested on the grounds that statistical uncertainties in tracer velocity dispersions provide only weak upper limits ($\Upsilon_V < 6.7$ to $8.1$) that do not strictly exclude typical dark matter halos [Martin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...859L...5M/abstract).

At the high-redshift frontier, JWST observations of luminous galaxies at $z > 7$ have prompted intense theoretical debate. Initial reports of [[overly-massive-galaxies|Overly massive galaxies]] emerging $\sim 600$ Myr after the Big Bang seemingly challenged the standard cosmological model, due to inferred stellar masses exceeding $10^{10} \, M_\odot$ [Prada et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230411911P/abstract). However, recent theoretical work suggests these tensions arise from overestimating $\Upsilon_*$ by improperly assuming older, dust-obscured stellar populations. When the degeneracy between the mass-to-light ratio and star formation efficiency is broken, the data remain entirely consistent with a non-evolving, halo-mass-dependent efficiency [Donnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250103217D/abstract). In this paradigm, the typical mass-to-light ratio of the galaxy population is modeled to systematically increase with redshift beyond $z \simeq 6$, while current data can still be reproduced by assuming ever-younger stellar populations [Donnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250103217D/abstract). Spectroscopic follow-ups confirm that the stellar mass at $z = 7-9$ scales with ultraviolet luminosity according to:
$$ M_* \propto L_{\rm UV}^{0.85 \pm 0.12} $$
However, this relation exhibits a large intrinsic scatter of $\sim 0.5$ dex, reflecting stochastic, bursty star formation histories rather than a breakdown of $\Lambda$CDM [Rojas-Ruiz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240800843R/abstract). Consequently, accurately modeling the bursty nature of the early universe is critical to deriving realistic mass-to-light ratios and reconciling these discoveries with the standard cosmological model [Prada et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230411911P/abstract).
