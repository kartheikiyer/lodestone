**CIGALE** (Code Investigating GALaxy Emission) is a state-of-the-art, open-source software package designed for [[multi-component-sed-fitting|Multi-component SED fitting]] of galaxies. At its core, CIGALE derives fundamental physical properties—such as stellar mass, star formation rate (SFR), and star formation history—by modeling the panchromatic spectral energy distribution (SED) of a galaxy across the electromagnetic spectrum. The tool is predicated on the physical principle of energy balance: it assumes that the ultraviolet and optical radiation emitted by stars and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|active galactic nuclei]] that is absorbed by interstellar dust must be strictly conserved and thermally re-emitted in the mid- to far-infrared [Noll et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...507.1793N/abstract). By explicitly coupling dust attenuation to dust emission, CIGALE breaks traditional degeneracies between intrinsic stellar age and reddening, providing robust constraints on a galaxy's overall energy budget in a manner similar to other established energy-balance tools like [[magphys|MAGPHYS]].

Originally developed in Fortran, the framework was completely rewritten into a highly modular, parallelized Python architecture (`pcigale`) to accommodate the increasing complexity of modern astrophysical observations [Roehlly et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ASPC..485..347R/abstract), [Boquien et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A.103B/abstract). This structural modularity allows users to easily interchange distinct physical recipes, generating models that combine varied [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] libraries, flexible star formation histories, nebular emission models, and advanced dust templates. A major milestone in the code's evolution is the [[x-cigale|X-CIGALE]] extension, which introduces X-ray modules alongside [[clumpy-torus-models|Clumpy torus models]] to rigorously account for Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics. By fitting high-energy fluxes simultaneously with infrared photometry, CIGALE effectively disentangles non-thermal central engine activity from host galaxy star formation, mitigating the systematic overestimation of stellar masses and SFRs in active systems. 

To determine the physical properties of a galaxy, CIGALE employs a Bayesian statistical framework. By utilizing Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques, the code rapidly explores high-dimensional parameter spaces to derive rigorous probability density functions and uncertainty estimates for each target variable. Optimized for modern multi-core computing, CIGALE's inference speeds now rival those of machine learning algorithms, enabling the simultaneous fitting of photometric and spectroscopic data for massive survey catalogs and high-redshift observations from JWST. 

The following article outlines the theoretical framework and software architecture of CIGALE, detailing its specific modules for stellar populations, dust physics, and AGN emission. It also previews current applications in large-scale survey processing and highlights several ongoing open questions in the field. Notably, these include persistent systematic discrepancies in mass and SFR predictions between energy-balance frameworks and full 3D radiative transfer codes (such as [[skirt|SKIRT]] or the [[cygnus-models|CYGNUS models]]), the ongoing difficulty of accurately constraining low-luminosity AGN fractions without ultra-deep mid-infrared coverage, and the challenge of interpreting disparate SFR timescales when integrating low-frequency radio emission into panchromatic fits.

## Introduction to CIGALE and the Energy Balance Principle

CIGALE is an open-source, highly parallelized software package used to model the panchromatic SEDs of galaxies. Capable of fitting multi-wavelength photometric and spectroscopic data from the X-ray and UV-to-radio domains, the code is widely utilized to derive fundamental physical parameters such as stellar mass ($M_*$), star formation rate (SFR), and dust luminosity [Boquien et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A.103B/abstract), [Burgarella et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024SPIE13098E..0NB/abstract).

The fundamental physical assumption underpinning CIGALE is the principle of energy balance, an approach it shares with other widespread SED fitting tools such as [[magphys|MAGPHYS]] [da Cunha et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ascl.soft06010D/abstract). In this framework, the energy emitted by stellar populations and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|active galactic nuclei]] that is absorbed by interstellar dust in the ultraviolet (UV) and optical regimes must equal the energy reprocessed and thermally re-emitted by that dust in the mid- and far-infrared [Noll et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...507.1793N/abstract), [Vika et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...597A..51V/abstract). By explicitly coupling the attenuated stellar emission to dust emission models, the energy balance requirement breaks traditional degeneracies between dust attenuation and intrinsic stellar age, yielding significantly more robust constraints on the galaxy's overall energy budget [Noll et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...507.1793N/abstract). Comparative studies on low-luminosity FIR sources indicate that both MAGPHYS and CIGALE reliably identify low-mass galaxies residing on the local star-forming main sequence [Pappalardo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...589A..11P/abstract).

To construct a model SED, CIGALE sequentially combines modular physical components: it generates a star formation history, applies simple stellar populations (e.g., [[pegase-code|PEGASE (code)]] or Maraston models), incorporates nebular emission, and attenuates the resulting spectrum using flexible dust laws (such as a modified Calzetti curve) [Noll et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...507.1793N/abstract), [Boquien et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A.103B/abstract). The absorbed energy is then re-distributed using dust emission templates (e.g., Dale & Helou), alongside modules for active galactic nuclei and non-thermal radio emission. Recent iterations of the code have also integrated dedicated high-energy modules, such as [[x-cigale|X-CIGALE]], to securely fit AGN intrinsic power spanning ten orders of magnitude in luminosity [Yang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491..740Y/abstract), [Yang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927..192Y/abstract), [López et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...692A.209L/abstract).

Originally developed in Fortran, the tool was rewritten in Python 3 (`pcigale`) to maximize modularity and performance [Roehlly et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ASPC..485..347R/abstract). CIGALE relies on Bayesian analysis to estimate parameter expectation values and uncertainties. Because grid-based SED fitting can suffer from exponential increases in computational time as the number of free parameters grows, a Markov Chain Monte Carlo implementation (CIGALEMC) was developed to efficiently sample high-dimensional parameter spaces [Serra et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...740...22S/abstract). Today, CIGALE is optimized for processing multi-million object survey catalogs; its generation and inference speeds for large galaxy samples are competitive even with modern machine learning architectures [Burgarella et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024SPIE13098E..0NB/abstract).

While highly efficient, the energy balance approximation relies on specific geometric and structural assumptions. Comparative studies between energy balance codes and complete 3D radiative transfer algorithms (such as SMART or the [[cygnus-models|CYGNUS models]]) indicate that these assumptions can lead to systematic discrepancies. For instance, CIGALE has been found to predict systematically lower SFRs and higher stellar masses than pure radiative transfer models for the same observational data [Gao et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...654A.117G/abstract), [Papadopoulos et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.536.2433P/abstract).

## Evolution and Software Architecture

CIGALE was originally implemented in Fortran as a tool to fit SEDs from the far-ultraviolet to the far-infrared using a strict energy balance principle [Noll et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...507.1793N/abstract). Early descriptions of CIGALE established its core capabilities as a versatile code designed to comprehensively fit such multi-wavelength observational data [Roehlly et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASPC..461..569R/abstract). The historical context of modeling galaxy SEDs by coupling dust models with larger frameworks traces back to earlier foundational efforts, such as combining the GRASIL dust model with the GALFORM semi-analytical model for statistical studies [Silva et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001Ap&SS.276.1073S/abstract). Similarly, the expansion of multi-wavelength models like GRASIL to simultaneously encompass the radio and X-ray domains [Silva et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003RMxAC..17...93S/abstract) paralleled the conceptual evolution that CIGALE would later undergo. While the original compiled code was effective for fundamental photometric modeling, the growing complexity of galaxy observations and the demand for increased flexibility motivated a major software overhaul. This effort began with the development of **pcigale**, a port to Python designed to enhance code versatility [Roehlly et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ASPC..485..347R/abstract), which ultimately culminated in a complete rewrite as a highly optimized Python 3 framework [Boquien et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A.103B/abstract).

At the core of the modern Python 3 implementation is a strict modular architecture. This design paradigm isolates distinct physical processes into interchangeable blocks, allowing users to effortlessly swap or update models for star formation histories, stellar population synthesis (e.g., utilizing [[pegase-code|PEGASE (code)]] or Maraston models), dust attenuation, and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]]. This modularity has been the primary driver of the code's continuous evolution, enabling seamless integration of major community extensions such as [[x-cigale|X-CIGALE]], which expanded the framework's fitting capabilities into the X-ray regime [Yang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491..740Y/abstract).

Historically, CIGALE utilized a purely grid-based statistical approach, constructing an exhaustive multidimensional library of theoretical SEDs and evaluating them against observations via a Bayesian-like $\chi^2$ analysis. However, as the inclusion of complex sub-grid physics increased the number of free parameters, this grid-based approach suffered from exponential increases in computation time. To address this limitation in high-dimensional parameter spaces, researchers developed CIGALEMC, an implementation utilizing Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques. The MCMC approach scales approximately linearly with the number of parameters, allowing for a faster and more efficient exploration of the posterior distributions without sacrificing accuracy [Serra et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...740...22S/abstract). 

Despite the execution overhead typically associated with interpreted languages, the modern CIGALE framework achieves exceptional performance through aggressive parallelization designed for multi-core computing architectures. The code is engineered to efficiently handle the generation of millions of models and the simultaneous fitting of massive survey datasets [Boquien et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A.103B/abstract). Consequently, the code has been used to generate large value-added catalogs of galaxy physical properties in deep fields like the Hawaii-HDF-N [Gao et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019RAA....19...39G/abstract). CIGALE has also been successfully applied to scale up to 2.8 million sources in the Vera C. Rubin Observatory LSST Deep-Drilling Fields (such as W-CDF-S, ELAIS-S1, and XMM-LSS) to derive comprehensive stellar masses and SFRs [Zou et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..262...15Z/abstract).

Due to its statistical and physical robustness, CIGALE is routinely used as a benchmark for comparison against newer Bayesian codes like Lightning [Doore et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..266...39D/abstract). Furthermore, CIGALE is often evaluated alongside other generative SED packages such as ProSpect, which specializes in modeling complex star formation and metallicity histories [Robotham et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.495..905R/abstract). To probe the limits of the energy balance assumption, comparative studies frequently contrast CIGALE against full dust radiative transfer simulations produced by tools like POWDERDAY [Narayanan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..252...12N/abstract). While 3D radiative transfer traditionally incurs massive computational costs, CIGALE's high speeds are now finding peers in newer MCMC-based SED codes like SMART, which exclusively use radiative transfer models while achieving comparable computation times [Varnava et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.2304V/abstract). Recent structural updates have further optimized CIGALE to concurrently fit both broadband photometry and spectroscopic data, maintaining its position as one of the most efficient SED fitting codes available, actively competing with deep-learning methods [Burgarella et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024SPIE13098E..0NB/abstract). Beyond standalone fitting, modern SED generation tools are frequently integrated directly with semi-analytic models to study multi-wavelength emission across cosmic time. For instance, SED generation has been coupled with the SHARK semi-analytic model to predict panchromatic galaxy emission from the far-ultraviolet to the far-infrared at $0 \le z \le 10$ [Lagos et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.4196L/abstract). Similarly, specialized pipelines such as `mentari` have been developed to generate robust panchromatic SEDs directly from the Dusty SAGE semi-analytic model [Triani et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.2500T/abstract).

## Stellar Populations and Star Formation History Modeling

To derive global physical properties such as stellar mass ($M_*$), star formation rate (SFR), and mass-weighted age, CIGALE relies on coupling [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] (SPS) libraries with highly flexible Star Formation History (SFH) prescriptions. The code constructs composite stellar populations by integrating simple stellar populations over a parameterized or non-parametric SFH. To generate the intrinsic stellar continuum from the far-ultraviolet to the near-infrared, CIGALE incorporates libraries such as the Maraston models and the [[pegase-code|PEGASE (code)]] [Noll et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...507.1793N/abstract). 

The choice of SFH parametrization significantly influences the derived stellar parameters. CIGALE incorporates several analytical SFH models, including single-exponentially decreasing ($1\tau$-dec), double-exponentially decreasing ($2\tau$-dec), and delayed SFH models [Ciesla et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...576A..10C/abstract). Recent versions of the code also support non-parametric SFHs, which are particularly useful for capturing recent starbursts or complex quenching events [Arango-Toro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...675A.126A/abstract). Extensive mock catalog testing reveals a structural tension in optimizing both mass and age estimates with analytical models: while the $2\tau$-dec model provides robust estimates of $M_*$ for normal star-forming galaxies, it frequently yields unrealistic stellar population ages. In contrast, the delayed SFH model achieves a more balanced performance, recovering both $M_*$ and SFR to within a maximum offset of 10% while returning more physically realistic age estimates [Ciesla et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...576A..10C/abstract). CIGALE has also been successfully applied to derive ages and masses for individual star clusters in nearby galaxies, such as those in the [[phangs-survey|PHANGS-HST survey]] [Turner et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502.1366T/abstract). In the nearby universe, CIGALE was notably used to fit the 258 galaxies of the Local Volume Legacy survey, confirming that SED-derived SFRs recover approximately 88% of the value derived from standard hybrid empirical indicators [Dale et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....165..260D/abstract).

A critical consideration in CIGALE's stellar population modeling is the inclusion of nebular continuum and emission lines. Ignoring nebular emission leads to severe systematic errors, particularly at high redshifts. Strong emission lines, such as [OIII] and H$\alpha$, often redshift into near-infrared photometric filters (e.g., the $K_s$-band at $z \sim 3.5$), artificially inflating the broad-band flux and mimicking the presence of a massive, older stellar population. Failing to model this nebular contribution causes the SED fitting process to systematically overestimate stellar masses, with discrepancies exceeding 1 dex in extreme cases [Yuan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...631A.123Y/abstract). 

The intrinsic stellar emission is subsequently coupled to the interstellar medium through CIGALE's strict energy balance principle, where UV and optical light absorbed by dust is re-emitted in the infrared. The exact $M_*$ derived from the SPS fitting remains highly degenerate with the assumed dust physics; literature studies indicate that altering the choice of the dust attenuation law can shift stellar mass estimates by a factor of two [Małek et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...620A..50M/abstract). Furthermore, cross-code comparisons highlight an ongoing tension in the literature regarding SED modeling methodologies: energy balance codes like CIGALE generally predict systematically lower SFRs and higher stellar masses compared to pure radiative transfer models that solve for complex, non-isotropic host geometries [Papadopoulos et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.536.2433P/abstract).

## Dust Attenuation and Infrared Emission

CIGALE's treatment of the interstellar medium relies on the principle of energy balance, enforcing the constraint that the total ultraviolet and optical luminosity absorbed by dust must be exactly re-emitted in the mid- to far-infrared [Noll et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...507.1793N/abstract), [Boquien et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A.103B/abstract). Mathematically, this enforces the constraint:

$$ \int_{0}^{\infty} L_{\lambda, \mathrm{absorbed}} \, d\lambda = \int_{0}^{\infty} L_{\lambda, \mathrm{dust}} \, d\lambda $$

To model the absorbed radiation, CIGALE allows users to implement highly flexible **dust attenuation** curves, including modified Calzetti laws and other varied parameterizations. The choice of attenuation law is a critical systematic in spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting; studies have shown that varying the assumed attenuation law can alter stellar mass estimates by an average factor of two [Małek et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...620A..50M/abstract). 

The absorbed energy is subsequently distributed across the infrared spectrum using comprehensive libraries of dust emission templates. Users can select from established frameworks, such as the empirical **Dale & Helou models** [Noll et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...507.1793N/abstract). These template libraries account for both the thermal continuum produced by large, cold dust grains in thermal equilibrium and the stochastic heating of very small dust grains.

A defining characteristic of the mid-infrared emission spectrum is the presence of prominent **PAH features** (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons). CIGALE models these complex emission bands (e.g., at $6.2\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, $7.7\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, and $11.3\ \mu\mathrm{m}$), which serve as sensitive diagnostics of the interstellar radiation field and the dominant heating sources. For example, the equivalent width of the $6.2\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ feature and the luminosity ratio $\log(L_{7.7 \mu\mathrm{m}}/L_{11.3 \mu\mathrm{m}})$ are routinely used to distinguish starburst-dominated galaxies from active systems. In the context of [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]], the hard radiation field of an AGN can preferentially destroy smaller aromatic molecules, thereby lowering the $7.7\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ to $11.3\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ ratio compared to normal star-forming galaxies [Wu et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...723..895W/abstract). Consequently, while the $8\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ band provides only a rough SFR estimate due to its heavy dependence on PAH properties, the inclusion of $24\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ photometric data provides a highly reliable SFR tracer up to $z \sim 0.9$ [Figueira et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...667A..29F/abstract). To bridge further wavelength regimes, integration with Atacama Cosmology Telescope (ACT) millimeter data allows CIGALE to decompose emissions into radio synchrotron components, identifying the relative contributions of star formation and AGNs in the millimeter regime [Kilerci et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...45K/abstract). Additionally, recent multi-wavelength modeling suggests that turnovers in the low-frequency [[radiosed|radio SEDs]] of local LIRGs are primarily driven by free-free absorption [Dey et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938..152D/abstract).

Recent developments have expanded CIGALE's dust modeling to include two-sector models that couple HII regions with photon-dominated regions (PDRs) evaluated using [[cloudy-code|CLOUDY (code)]] grids. This advancement accounts for the partial shielding of starburst emission by dense clouds, significantly improving the interpretation of far-infrared line ratios by constraining parameters such as gas density, metallicity, and the PDR covering fraction [Theulé et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...682A.119T/abstract). 

Despite these sophisticated implementations, structural tensions remain when comparing energy balance codes to full 3D radiative transfer models, such as GRASIL-3D [Domínguez-Tenreiro et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.439.3868D/abstract) and those generated by [[skirt|SKIRT]]. Comparative studies indicate that energy balance approaches may systematically predict lower SFRs and higher stellar masses than pure radiative transfer methods, likely due to fundamental differences in assumed host galaxy geometries [Gao et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...654A.117G/abstract), [Papadopoulos et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.536.2433P/abstract). In this vein, CIGALE's energy balance methods are increasingly contextualized against complex, multiphase forward-modeling pipelines such as SÍGAME v3. SÍGAME applies gas fragmentation in post-processing to cosmological simulations, utilizing full radiative transfer and photoionization models like [[cloudy-code|CLOUDY (code)]] to achieve highly accurate, sub-grid ($\lesssim 200\ \mathrm{pc}$) modeling of infrared line emission [Olsen et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922...88O/abstract).

## AGN Modeling and the X-CIGALE Extension

To prevent biased estimates of host galaxy properties in active systems, the spectral contribution of an [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|active galactic nucleus (AGN)]] must be explicitly modeled. The presence of an AGN can severely impact parameter estimation; for example, the SFR is systematically under-estimated as the AGN fractional contribution increases in Type-1 systems [Ciesla et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...576A..10C/abstract).

To address this, the [[x-cigale|X-CIGALE]] extension was developed to fit panchromatic spectral energy distributions (SEDs) stretching from the X-ray to the radio [Yang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491..740Y/abstract), [Yang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927..192Y/abstract). A central feature of X-CIGALE is the integration of the SKIRTOR module, a sophisticated two-phase [[clumpy-torus-models|clumpy torus model]] that computes the UV-to-infrared AGN emission, alongside new prescriptions for polar-dust extinction in moderately obscured Type-1 AGNs [Yang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491..740Y/abstract).

Because X-ray data provide a relatively unobscured tracer of AGN intrinsic power, incorporating an X-ray module allows the code to break deep degeneracies between AGN emission and host galaxy dust heating. X-CIGALE fits the AGN accretion disk and X-ray binary emission simultaneously, accounting for intrinsic X-ray anisotropy by parameterizing the X-ray luminosity as a function of the viewing angle $\theta$, such that $L_X(\theta) \propto 1+\cos\theta$ [Yang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927..192Y/abstract). By fitting X-ray fluxes or upper limits alongside infrared photometry, the code efficiently isolates the non-thermal central engine.

Accurately modeling low-luminosity AGNs (LLAGNs) requires specialized treatments due to the spectral dominance of the host galaxy and fundamental shifts in accretion physics. Recent updates have introduced tailored modules for LLAGNs (such as LINERs and Seyferts) that utilize advection-dominated accretion flows (ADAFs) and truncated accretion disks, coupled with empirical $L_X-L_{12\mu\mathrm{m}}$ relations [López et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240416938L/abstract), [López et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...692A.209L/abstract). These models reveal that unlike high-luminosity quasars, LLAGNs exhibit an $\alpha_{ox}$ index (the spectral slope between the UV and X-ray regimes) that correlates only weakly with the Eddington ratio, indicating a transition in photon production mechanisms [López et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...692A.209L/abstract).

Despite these advancements, robustly constraining the fractional AGN contribution to the total infrared luminosity, $frac_{\mathrm{AGN}}$, remains highly challenging when the AGN contributes less than 20% of the total infrared budget [Ciesla et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...576A..10C/abstract). Furthermore, the literature highlights a critical observational dependency: without sufficient mid-infrared coverage (e.g., from the WISE satellite), up to 68% of purely star-forming galaxies can be erroneously misclassified as AGNs by SED fitting codes [Siudek et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A.308S/abstract). When adequate multi-wavelength data are available, however, CIGALE's AGN modules are highly effective. They are currently being deployed to characterize heavily reddened supermassive black hole hosts, successfully identifying the AGN fractions of the ultra-compact "Little Red Dots" discovered by JWST at $z \sim 4-10$ [Feeney et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240913441F/abstract). Beyond the high-redshift universe, CIGALE has also been used to model the SEDs of isolated galaxies with active nuclei to study SMBH accretion in the absence of recent galaxy mergers [Kompaniiets et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231112000K/abstract). Furthermore, analysis with the tool indicates that in interacting systems, infrared AGN luminosity and stellar mass are more tightly correlated than star formation rate and stellar mass [Ramos Padilla et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.4325R/abstract).

## Statistical Framework and Parameter Estimation

The history and methodology of SED fitting techniques matured significantly alongside CIGALE's development, as detailed in foundational reviews of the field [Walcher et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Ap&SS.331....1W/abstract). Today, CIGALE relies on a robust Bayesian analysis framework to estimate physical properties from multi-wavelength SEDs. Rather than simply identifying the single best-fitting model, the code evaluates the likelihood distribution across a multi-dimensional parameter space. This approach allows the algorithm to determine the probability density function for each physical quantity, such as stellar mass, star formation rate, and dust luminosity [Noll et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...507.1793N/abstract). 

In its standard implementation, CIGALE generates a massive grid of theoretical SED models based on user-defined parameter ranges. For a given set of observational fluxes $f_{\rm obs, i}$ with uncertainties $\sigma_i$ and corresponding model fluxes $f_{\rm mod, i}$, the code evaluates the goodness-of-fit using the standard $\chi^2$ statistic:
$$ \chi^2 = \sum_{i} \left( \frac{f_{\rm obs, i} - f_{\rm mod, i}}{\sigma_i} \right)^2 $$
The statistical likelihood of each model is proportional to $\exp(-\chi^2/2)$. By marginalizing over all nuisance parameters, the code computes the expectation value and standard deviation for each target property, providing a robust measure of parameter uncertainty [Noll et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...507.1793N/abstract), [Boquien et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A.103B/abstract). This methodology is heavily utilized in [[multi-component-sed-fitting|Multi-component SED fitting]] and is conceptually similar to the energy balance techniques employed by [[magphys|MAGPHYS]].

A fundamental limitation of grid-based fitting is the "curse of dimensionality": computation time scales exponentially with the number of free parameters. To efficiently explore high-dimensional spaces—which are common when utilizing complex dust attenuation laws or diverse star formation histories—a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) module, CIGALEMC, was developed. Unlike full-grid evaluations, the MCMC approach scales approximately linearly with the number of parameters. This allows for a faster and more accurate sampling of the parameter space using efficiently chosen random walks, drastically optimizing CPU time without sacrificing the accuracy of the posterior distributions [Serra et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...740...22S/abstract).

To validate the reliability of these statistical inferences, CIGALE incorporates a built-in routine for generating mock catalogs to test parameter constrainability [Noll et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...507.1793N/abstract), [Małek et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...562A..15M/abstract). By comparing the known input parameters of the mock data against the output expectation values, users can quantitatively assess the robustness of the fit for their specific filter set. When expanding filter sets, researchers note that while narrow-band data provides higher spectral resolution, it primarily improves the diversity of recovered metallicities and dust optical depths in Bayesian fits [Csizi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A..37C/abstract).

The modern Python architecture of CIGALE is highly parallelized, allowing it to process grids of millions of models at speeds that can compete with deep-learning inference times [Boquien et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A.103B/abstract), [Burgarella et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024SPIE13098E..0NB/abstract). Furthermore, the robust likelihood distributions and mock catalogs generated by CIGALE are increasingly utilized as foundational training sets for machine learning algorithms (e.g., MLP-GaP), which emulate the code's Bayesian predictions to rapidly process massive catalogs from next-generation sky surveys [Guo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24l5019G/abstract).

## Spectroscopic Integration and High-Redshift Applications

Historically, energy balance models like CIGALE and [[magphys|MAGPHYS]] relied exclusively on broadband photometry to derive global galaxy properties. However, modern iterations of CIGALE now perform joint photometric and spectroscopic [[multi-component-sed-fitting|Multi-component SED fitting]]. This upgrade allows the code to simultaneously fit broadband fluxes alongside nebular emission line fluxes and equivalent widths [Burgarella et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024SPIE13098E..0NB/abstract). 

Incorporating nebular emission is physically critical for accurate parameter estimation, particularly at high redshifts. Strong emission lines, such as [O III] and H$\alpha$, contribute heavily to broadband fluxes when they are redshifted into near-infrared filters. Neglecting this nebular contribution leads to severe systematic overestimations of stellar masses—sometimes by more than 1 dex in catastrophic cases [Yuan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...631A.123Y/abstract). To accurately reproduce these observables and line ratios, recent [[multi-component-models|Multi-component models]] within the code couple HII starburst emission regions with photon-dominated regions (PDRs) [Theulé et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...682A.119T/abstract). Furthermore, leveraging higher spectral resolution has proven beneficial for survey-level analyses; for instance, using narrow-band photometry from surveys like PAUS with CIGALE can significantly improve the reconstruction and measurement of the D4000 spectral break index compared to broad-band data alone [Renard et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515..146R/abstract). Similarly, the JOLINES catalog utilizes CIGALE to reconstruct the continuum for more accurate emission-line flux measurements in J-PAS narrow-band spectrophotometry [Fernández-Ontiveros et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251121822F/abstract).

To meet the demands of the epoch of reionization, the code has been explicitly updated for compatibility with JWST/NIRSpec, MIRI, and NIRISS. Highly optimized for multi-core architectures, CIGALE can process massive spectroscopic samples at speeds competitive with machine learning inference, successfully performing a simple fitting analysis for about 1200 JWST/NIRSpec spectra in approximately three hours [Burgarella et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024SPIE13098E..0NB/abstract). This efficiency has established it as a standard tool for characterizing distant interacting systems, such as the lensed dusty star-forming galaxy SPT0418-47 at $z = 4.225$, where it constrained the stellar mass to $M_* = 1.5 \pm 0.3 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$ [Cathey et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967...11C/abstract). Observationally, massive spectroscopic campaigns are vital for characterizing the early universe. The JWST-PRIMAL archival survey provides a critical JWST/NIRSpec reference sample encompassing nearly 600 galaxies at $z=5.0 - 13.4$, offering a comprehensive foundation for deriving redshifts, physical properties, metallicities, and analyzing Lyman-$\alpha$ damping wings [Heintz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240402211H/abstract), [Heintz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A..60H/abstract). To robustly interpret such high-redshift JWST observations, spectral fitting tools are often validated against detailed cosmological radiation hydrodynamics simulations. For example, the SPHINX public data release provides a vast statistical sample of forward-modeled mock images and spectra that serve as crucial benchmarks for understanding these early galaxies [Katz et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..44K/abstract). Additionally, the SERRA simulations offer a valuable theoretical framework for comparing multi-wavelength properties—from the UV to the FIR continuum—of high-redshift ($z > 6$) galaxies [Pallottini et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513.5621P/abstract). Furthermore, the intricate modeling of complex resonant emission lines like Lyman-$\alpha$ in cosmological simulations has spurred the development of advanced tools such as THOR, a highly optimized, GPU-accelerated and MPI-parallelized radiative transfer code [Byrohl et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250711603B/abstract).

In this high-redshift context, CIGALE shares conceptual space with contemporary Bayesian tools like BEAGLE, which similarly integrates stellar and photoionized gas modeling for distant galaxy interpretation [Chevallard et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.1415C/abstract). BEAGLE has notably been used to forward-model deep observations, simulating JWST/NIRSpec low-resolution prism spectra to predict the detectability of key optical emission lines at high redshifts [Chevallard et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.2621C/abstract). It also provides a complementary approach to tools like GLEAM, which focus specifically on batch-fitting Gaussian models to emission and absorption lines in 1D spectra rather than full panchromatic SED modeling [Stroe et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....161..158S/abstract). On the theoretical front, post-processing pipelines for cosmological simulations (like FIRE) are actively validated against the precise line measurements that CIGALE models, such as [O III] and H$\beta$ at $z > 6$ [Yang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.5989Y/abstract).

Despite these advances, the literature acknowledges significant ambiguities when modeling the ionizing properties of high-redshift sources. Estimates of the Lyman continuum escape fraction ($f_{\rm esc}$) at $z \sim 6$ remain highly sensitive to the chosen SED fitting framework and the treatment of dust attenuation. Comparisons between CIGALE and other fitting codes demonstrate that $f_{\rm esc}$ estimates for the same objects can range anywhere from $0$ to $0.8$, reflecting the ongoing tension and uncertainty inherent in interpreting UV-slopes from current photometric and spectroscopic data [Jeon et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201105918J/abstract).

## Machine Learning and Computational Speed

The advent of large-scale surveys requires processing multi-wavelength photometric and spectroscopic data for millions—and soon billions—of galaxies. Analyzing such vast datasets using [[multi-component-sed-fitting|Multi-component SED fitting]] can be computationally prohibitive if not heavily optimized. Early versions of CIGALE mitigated the exponential scaling of computation time with the number of free parameters by utilizing Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling methods to navigate the parameter space efficiently [Serra et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...740...22S/abstract). The modern Python implementation further maximizes efficiency by parallelizing the model grid computation and likelihood evaluations across multi-core architectures [Boquien et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A.103B/abstract). 

Consequently, CIGALE is highly competitive with modern computational alternatives. While it is commonly assumed that physical SED fitting is inherently much slower than machine learning (ML), CIGALE operates with an inference time on the same order as convolutional or deep-learning methods, without requiring a prolonged training phase [Burgarella et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024SPIE13098E..0NB/abstract). In benchmarking tests, the code successfully performs a simple fitting analysis for roughly $1,200$ high-resolution JWST/NIRSpec spectra in approximately three hours [Burgarella et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024SPIE13098E..0NB/abstract).

Despite CIGALE's native speed, the sheer data volume of next-generation surveys has motivated the development of ultra-fast machine learning frameworks. In this ecosystem, CIGALE serves as a robust physics engine utilized to forward-model immense, high-fidelity mock catalogs that train ML algorithms. One prominent example is the Multi-Layer Perceptron for Predicting Galaxy Parameters (MLP-GaP) [Guo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24l5019G/abstract). MLP-GaP leverages mock datasets generated by CIGALE to learn the non-linear mappings between broad-band photometry and fundamental galaxy properties. Once trained, MLP-GaP achieves inference times suitable for billions of objects, rapidly predicting stellar masses ($M_*$) and star formation rates (SFR) that demonstrate excellent consistency with full Bayesian SED derivations [Guo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24l5019G/abstract).

Similarly, CIGALE’s sophisticated treatment of [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] provides the ground truth for AGNBoost, a machine learning framework based on the XGBoostLSS algorithm [Hamblin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603130H/abstract). Trained on a library of $10^6$ simulated CIGALE galaxies, AGNBoost simultaneously predicts photometric redshifts and the fractional contribution of the AGN to the $3-30\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ mid-infrared emission ($frac_{\mathrm{AGN}}$). By relying on CIGALE's rigorous enforcement of energy balance and [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models for training, these machine learning tools bridge the gap between detailed radiative physics and the sub-second inference times demanded by modern cosmological surveys.
