## Article

**Spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting** is a foundational computational technique in extragalactic astrophysics used to extract the physical properties of astronomical objects—most notably galaxies—from their observed multi-wavelength light. At its core, SED fitting is an inverse problem: it compares observed broad-band photometry or spectroscopy to a vast grid of synthetic [[template-fitting|templates]] to identify the most probable physical state of the target. By bridging observational data with theoretical [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models, SED fitting allows astronomers to constrain macroscopic [[integrated-galaxy-properties|Integrated galaxy properties]] such as stellar mass, star formation history (SFH), dust attenuation, and gas-phase metallicity. While early implementations relied on simple [[least-squares-method|Least squares method]] comparisons to empirical templates to find a single best-fit solution [Walcher et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Ap&SS.331....1W/abstract), modern [[model-fitting|Model fitting]] relies heavily on Bayesian inference frameworks. These modern algorithms generate robust posterior probability distributions for target parameters, allowing researchers to rigorously quantify [[physical-parameter-estimation|uncertainties]] and marginalize over complex physical degeneracies. 

The accuracy of [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] via SED fitting is deeply tied to its assumed theoretical ingredients, particularly the initial mass function (IMF), stellar evolution isochrones, and the choice of synthetic or empirical stellar spectral libraries (such as the [[miles-stellar-library|MILES (stellar library)]]). Historically, models utilized [[traditional-sed-fitting-techniques|Traditional SED fitting techniques]] characterized by rigid, parametric star formation histories (like the exponentially declining $\tau$-model) and simplified foreground dust screen assumptions [Mitchell et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435...87M/abstract). However, the methodology has since evolved into panchromatic [[multi-component-sed-fitting|Multi-component SED fitting]] that self-consistently balances ultraviolet light absorbed by dust with its thermal re-emission in the infrared (e.g., utilizing codes like [[magphys|MAGPHYS]], CIGALE, or Prospector) [Chang et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..219....8C/abstract), [Boquien et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A.103B/abstract), [Johnson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ascl.soft05025J/abstract). Furthermore, advanced codes now routinely incorporate flexible [[non-parametric-methods-in-astrophysics|Non-parametric methods in astrophysics]] to model stochastic star formation alongside non-stellar flux contributions, such as ionizing nebular emission and active galactic nuclei (AGN) tori. Correctly modeling these components is critical to avoiding severe systematic biases that can artificially inflate derived stellar ages, masses, and star formation rates.

Despite its ubiquity, SED fitting is subject to profound systematic tensions and open questions. A prominent challenge is the age-metallicity-dust degeneracy, which is compounded by the "outshining" effect: because highly luminous young stars easily mask older, more massive stellar populations, the traditional [[integrated-light-method|Integrated light method]] can systematically underestimate total stellar mass by up to a factor of five. This discrepancy has driven a modern shift toward computationally intensive, spatially resolved pixel-by-pixel analyses and structural decomposition using tools like [[galfit|GALFIT]]. Looking forward, the era of *JWST* and upcoming wide-field surveys (such as LSST and Euclid) is reshaping the field. Researchers are currently working to untangle the extreme emission lines and bursty SFHs of $z > 4$ galaxies, interrogate the physical reality of local dust energy balance violations, and deploy advanced machine learning and simulation-based inference to accelerate posterior estimation for billions of galaxies without sacrificing statistical rigor.

## Foundations of Stellar Population Synthesis

To translate a modeled star formation and chemical enrichment history into an observable spectrum, fitting routines rely fundamentally on [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] (SPS) models. The construction of an SPS model rests on three primary theoretical ingredients: an initial mass function (IMF), stellar evolution tracks (isochrones), and a stellar spectral library [Ge et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.1675G/abstract). 

The IMF governs the mass distribution of a newly formed stellar generation, dictating the ratio of luminous, short-lived massive stars to dim, long-lived low-mass stars. Variations in the assumed IMF introduce systematic offsets in the overall mass-to-light ratio ($M/L$). Stellar mass is widely considered the most robustly recovered property in [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]]. Comparisons between full SED fitting and empirical mass-to-light versus color relations (MLCRs) demonstrate that both approaches yield similar results, with random scatter and inter-code modeling uncertainty between different fitting frameworks typically bounded within $0.1-0.15$ dex [Mobasher et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...808..101M/abstract), [Pacifici et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944..141P/abstract). Dynamical mass estimators serve as an independent check on SPS-derived stellar masses; such comparisons in local galaxies reveal unmodelled scatters of only $\sim 0.05-0.12$ dex, reaffirming the baseline precision of SPS frameworks [Dogruel et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953...45D/abstract). However, altering fundamental physical priors, such as the assumed SFH or IMF, can introduce much larger systematic biases—ranging from $0.6$ dex up to $\sim 1$ dex [Roediger et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.3209R/abstract), [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963...74W/abstract). Variations in IMF can also systematically shift derived ages and metallicities, particularly for older populations [Ge et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.1675G/abstract). An alternative to traditional SPS forward-modeling is to [[integrated-light-spectroscopy|fit integrated spectra]] as a linear combination of empirical stellar [[template-fitting|templates]] without assuming a priori stellar evolution models; this approach provides independent constraints on the underlying IMF [Martín-Navarro et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691L..10M/abstract). Isochrones subsequently map the evolution of these stars, determining their effective temperature ($T_{\rm eff}$) and surface gravity ($\log g$) at a given age and metallicity ($Z$).

To map the physical parameters of the isochrones to emitted continuous spectra, SPS models require stellar libraries. The choice between empirical and synthetic (theoretical) libraries remains a major source of systematic uncertainty in [[spectral-modeling-and-sed-synthesis|Spectral Modeling and SED Synthesis]]. Empirical libraries, such as the [[miles-stellar-library|MILES (stellar library)]], are built from observed spectra of local [[benchmark-stars|Benchmark stars]]. While they provide highly realistic spectral features, they are fundamentally limited by the chemical abundance patterns of the solar neighborhood. This results in restricted parameter space coverage, often lacking sufficient representation for very metal-rich or $\alpha$-enhanced stellar populations [Ge et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.1675G/abstract), [Knowles et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3450K/abstract). 

Synthetic libraries circumvent this limitation by computing emergent spectra from theoretical stellar atmosphere models across a continuous grid of parameters. However, synthetic spectra suffer from modeling inaccuracies—such as incomplete opacity lists or simplified thermodynamic assumptions. These inaccuracies can lead to discrepancies in spectral line indices, with some synthetic models yielding equivalent width predictions that result in unphysical age and metallicity derivations [Byrne et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.4995B/abstract).

The literature exhibits tension regarding the optimal library choice, as neither approach is free of systematic biases. Using empirical libraries with limited parameter space coverage often results in underestimated galaxy ages, whereas utilizing synthetic libraries systematically underestimates stellar metallicities [Coelho et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.2025C/abstract). Crucially, the choice of spectral library and IMF does more than induce minor scatter in parameter space. It has been demonstrated that altering the underlying SPS ingredients—for instance, varying the library, initial mass function, and metallicity prescriptions within the BPASS framework—can shift derived star formation rates by $1.4 \pm 1.0$ dex. This level of systematic uncertainty is severe enough that the choice of stellar library alone can artificially transform a galaxy's inferred physical state from actively star-forming to quiescent [Jones et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543..167J/abstract). Furthermore, the choice of internal dust correction method during full-spectrum fitting, such as using reddening options versus multiplicative polynomials, significantly impacts the strength and accuracy of derived mass-metallicity and mass-age relations [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240613924L/abstract).

## Evolution of SED Fitting Methodologies

The methodology of modeling galaxy spectral energy distributions has undergone profound changes over the past few decades, evolving from simple analytic approximations to highly complex, data-driven statistical frameworks. 

Early implementations of SED modeling relied on [[traditional-sed-fitting-techniques|Traditional SED fitting techniques]] such as empirical [[template-fitting|Template fitting]] and the [[least-squares-method|Least squares method]] ($\chi^2$ minimization). These approaches compared observed broad-band fluxes against a discrete grid of template spectra derived from [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models to identify a single "best-fit" solution for [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]]. Historically, these models employed simple parametric star formation histories (SFHs)—such as exponentially declining $\tau$-models—and assumed uniform foreground dust screens [Mitchell et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435...87M/abstract). While computationally efficient, strict $\chi^2$ minimization struggles to navigate the complex degeneracies inherent in galaxy spectra, particularly the interplay between stellar age, metallicity, and dust attenuation, and provides a limited, often underestimated quantification of parameter uncertainties. Other approaches refine the $\chi^2$ methodology by entirely avoiding restrictive priors; for instance, the FIREFLY code employs an iterative $\chi^2$ minimization technique that fits combinations of single-burst stellar population models without relying on mathematical priors or additive polynomials to adjust the continuum [Wilkinson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.4297W/abstract). Other innovative methods, such as the SEW (Equivalent Widths) fitting approach, perform full-spectrum linear fitting using an equivalent widths spectrum, enabling the derivation of dust attenuation curves directly as model outputs without relying on rigid prior assumptions [Lu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540.1491L/abstract).

To robustly handle these degeneracies, the field transitioned toward Bayesian inference frameworks utilizing Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) and nested sampling algorithms [Acquaviva et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012IAUS..284...42A/abstract), [Pirzkal et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...775...11P/abstract). The Fisher Matrix formalism has been introduced as a computationally efficient tool for astronomical survey design, allowing researchers to predict parameter uncertainties and optimize wavelength coverage without the need for intensive MCMC sampling [Acquaviva et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...749...72A/abstract). When utilizing these probabilistic methods in [[full-spectrum-fitting|full-spectral fitting]] codes (such as STARLIGHT), correctly initializing Markov chains is of paramount importance to avoid severe biases, particularly when attempting to fit heavily reddened spectra [Cid Fernandes et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.4480C/abstract). Unlike $\chi^2$ methods that yield localized point estimates, Bayesian approaches construct full posterior probability distribution functions, enabling researchers to rigorously marginalize over nuisance parameters. Concurrently, models expanded from fitting narrow optical/near-infrared windows to utilizing panchromatic data spanning from the ultraviolet to the far-infrared and radio. This transition was facilitated by energy-balance codes like [[magphys|MAGPHYS]] and CIGALE, which physically couple the UV/optical light attenuated by dust with its thermal re-emission in the infrared [Chang et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..219....8C/abstract), [Boquien et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A.103B/abstract). Advanced codes like SMART now allow for MCMC inference using exclusively multicomponent radiative transfer models for UV-to-millimeter SEDs, achieving run times comparable to simpler energy-balance methods [Varnava et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.2304V/abstract). Leveraging expanded wavelength coverage is vital for mitigating severe [[photometry-and-redshift-estimation|photometric redshift]] (photo-$z$) "catastrophes." For instance, heavily dust-obscured intermediate-redshift sources (such as the dusty galaxy COSBO-7) can masquerade as high-redshift systems unless constrained by flexible dust prescriptions and the entire UV-to-radio baseline [Battisti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250701251B/abstract). Consequently, modern large-scale applications—such as the DESI value-added catalog, which derived physical properties for more than 1.3 million galaxies—highlight the critical importance of incorporating wide-baseline photometry (e.g., WISE mid-infrared data) to successfully avoid widespread misclassifications between star-forming galaxies and AGNs [Siudek et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A.308S/abstract).

As Bayesian techniques matured, the underlying physical models of galaxies grew in complexity. The rigid parametric SFHs of the past are increasingly being replaced by flexible, [[non-parametric-methods-in-astrophysics|non-parametric SFHs]], which have been shown to reduce systematic biases in stellar mass recovery from $\sim 0.4$ dex to $< 0.1$ dex [Lower et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904...33L/abstract). Furthermore, the inclusion of multi-wavelength physical components became standard in [[multi-component-sed-fitting|Multi-component SED fitting]]. Modern panchromatic fits incorporate nebular continuum and line emission, which is critical for avoiding systematic overestimations of stellar mass and age in high-redshift star-forming galaxies where strong emission lines contaminate broadband filters [Schaerer et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...515A..73S/abstract), [Yuan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...631A.123Y/abstract). Furthermore, properly modeling AGN torus emission has proven essential; neglecting mid-infrared AGN contributions can lead to errors in derived stellar ages and star formation rates by up to a factor of 10 [Leja et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...854...62L/abstract).

## Parametrizing Star Formation and Metallicity Histories

In [[spectral-modeling-and-sed-synthesis|Spectral Modeling and SED Synthesis]], reconstructing a galaxy's mass assembly requires specifying its star formation history (SFH) and metallicity evolution. These prescriptions govern the relative weights of the simple stellar populations used in [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models to synthesize the integrated light of the galaxy.

Historically, SFHs were modeled using simple analytic functions, most notably the exponentially declining $\tau$-model where the star formation rate is given by $\text{SFR}(t) \propto \exp(-t/\tau)$, or the delayed-$\tau$ model where $\text{SFR}(t) \propto t \exp(-t/\tau)$. While computationally inexpensive, these smooth functional forms fail to capture the true diversity and stochasticity of galaxy evolution, particularly the presence of recent starbursts [Iyer et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838..127I/abstract), [Lower et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904...33L/abstract). The inability of single-$\tau$ models to accommodate bursty star formation can severely bias derived physical parameters; for instance, parametric delayed-$\tau$ models exhibit an average systematic bias of up to $\sim 0.4$ dex in stellar mass estimation [Lower et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904...33L/abstract). More flexible parametric forms, such as the Double Power Law (DPL) or skewed Normal distributions, have been shown to outperform simpler models. The DPL model is particularly robust at capturing recent starbursts within the last $500\text{ Myr}$ and has proven highly effective in spatially resolved, pixel-by-pixel analyses [Mosleh et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983..181M/abstract).

To overcome the restrictive shapes of analytic functions, modern approaches frequently employ [[non-parametric-methods-in-astrophysics|non-parametric (or step-wise) SFHs]]. These models compute the SFR in discrete time bins or use a flexible basis of SFH components to reconstruct the assembly history [Iyer et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838..127I/abstract). By decoupling early- and late-time star formation, non-parametric models significantly improve the accuracy of [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]], reducing average stellar mass biases to $< 0.1$ dex [Lower et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904...33L/abstract). They also yield systematically higher stellar mass estimates than traditional parametric models, which helps resolve tensions in the observed cosmic star formation rate density [Leja et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...936..165L/abstract). However, the literature remains somewhat conflicting regarding the absolute superiority of non-parametric methods. While they reduce functional bias, non-parametric models are computationally intensive and their results—particularly for early-time star formation and [[age-distribution|mass-weighted ages]]—are highly sensitive to the choice of mathematical priors [Lower et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904...33L/abstract), [Mosleh et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983..181M/abstract), [Lapasia et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260108693L/abstract). Furthermore, highly stochastic or bursty SFHs, which are typical of high-redshift galaxies observed by JWST, can still introduce substantial systematic uncertainties of $\sim 0.8$ dex in the SFR and $\sim 0.3$ dex in the stellar age, even with flexible modeling frameworks [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963...74W/abstract).

Alongside the SFH, the chemical enrichment history plays a critical role due to the well-known age-metallicity degeneracy. Assuming a fixed or purely solar metallicity across all stellar populations in a galaxy is a common simplification, but it introduces significant mass-dependent systematic errors [Mitchell et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435...87M/abstract), [Bellstedt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540.2703B/abstract). Implementing an evolving gas-phase metallicity evolution, often coupled to the SFH via closed-box or simple regulatory chemical evolution models, provides a more physically motivated framework. Studies incorporating simultaneous SFH and metallicity evolution demonstrate that the assumption of fixed solar metallicity creates some of the most substantial biases in parameter estimation, fundamentally altering the inferred cosmic star formation history and the derived stellar masses [Bellstedt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5581B/abstract), [Bellstedt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540.2703B/abstract).

## Dust Attenuation and Energy Balance Modeling

In panchromatic [[sed-reconstruction|SED reconstruction]], treating the interstellar dust is crucial for accurate [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]]. Dust grains absorb and scatter rest-frame ultraviolet (UV) and optical starlight, re-emitting the absorbed energy as thermal radiation in the mid- and far-infrared (IR) [Casey et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.3094C/abstract), [Yuan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...613A..13Y/abstract). To self-consistently link these regimes, many modern [[multi-component-sed-fitting|Multi-component SED fitting]] codes (such as [[magphys|MAGPHYS]] and X-CIGALE) employ an energy balance principle. This assumption mandates that the total luminosity attenuated in the UV/optical must precisely equal the total luminosity integrated across the IR dust emission [Boquien et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A.103B/abstract). Mathematically, this is expressed as:
$$ L_{\rm dust} = \int_{0}^{\infty} L_{\lambda}^{\rm intrinsic} \left( 1 - e^{-\tau_\lambda} \right) d\lambda $$
where $L_{\lambda}^{\rm intrinsic}$ is the unattenuated stellar spectrum and $\tau_\lambda$ is the effective wavelength-dependent dust optical depth. Observationally, this relationship is often parameterized using the infrared excess (IRX)—the ratio of total IR to UV flux—which correlates with the UV continuum slope to provide constraints on the color excess $E(B-V)$ [Yuan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...613A..13Y/abstract). In heavily obscured starbursts, standard astronomical silicate models often fail to reproduce observed absorption features, requiring an empirical "starburst silicate" model that accounts for larger grain sizes [Dopita et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Ap&SS.333..225D/abstract). Additionally, the modeling of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) emission remains a sensitive area of SED reconstruction, where variations in modeling parameters can introduce a 15-20% variance in derived physical properties [Maragkoudakis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241201875M/abstract).

Dust attenuation laws are typically parameterized by a total optical depth (e.g., $A_V$ or $\tau_V$) and an effective attenuation curve slope (often denoted $n$ or $\delta$). Large statistical samples fit with Bayesian methods indicate that the attenuation slope tends to flatten as optical depth increases [Nagaraj et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932...54N/abstract). However, the physical reality of this $A_V$--$\delta$ correlation remains a subject of active debate in the literature. Some studies argue that the correlation is largely an artifact of strong fitting degeneracies between the intrinsic UV slope $\beta_0$, the dust slope $\delta$, and $A_V$, particularly when restrictive or overly simplistic star formation histories are assumed [Qin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511..765Q/abstract). Conversely, other analyses assert that the correlation is a real physical effect, demonstrating that these degeneracies can be robustly broken if high-quality infrared constraints (e.g., WISE photometry) are included in the fit [Meldorf et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.3242M/abstract).

While the energy balance assumption acts as a powerful prior for integrated galaxy properties, it fundamentally approximates complex, 3D star-dust geometries. While computationally convenient, assuming a uniform foreground dust screen rather than accounting for inhomogeneous 3D geometry can severely bias broad-band parameter recovery; for upcoming surveys like the Rubin LSST, this simplification has been shown to bias photometric redshifts by up to $0.02$ and underestimate stellar masses by $0.4$ dex [Hahn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240919054H/abstract). Radiative transfer simulations reveal that from any specific viewing angle, the strict energy balance between apparent UV attenuation and IR emission can be violated by up to a factor of 3 [Faucher et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957....7F/abstract). Optical depth also exhibits a strong viewing-angle dependence, with edge-on systems exhibiting significantly higher $\tau$ than face-on systems, primarily for galaxies with stellar masses $\log(M_*/M_\odot) \gtrsim 10$ [Nagaraj et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932...54N/abstract). 

Furthermore, the applicability of energy balance breaks down in high-resolution, spatially resolved SED fitting. When examining galaxy sub-regions, the strict local energy balance criterion becomes increasingly incorrect at physical scales $\lesssim 1\text{ kpc}$, as UV photons originating in one region may travel and heat dust in an entirely different pixel [Smith et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.1705S/abstract). Resolving these local dust variations is critical for mitigating age-dependent "outshining," where unattenuated young stars mask older populations. Pixel-by-pixel analyses often recover higher overall dust attenuations ($\Delta A_V \approx +0.08$ mag) and slightly grayer attenuation curves ($\Delta \delta_{\rm ISM} \approx +0.08$) than integrated fits, leading to systematically higher resolved stellar mass estimates [Hamed et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115963H/abstract).

## Panchromatic Integration of AGN and Nebular Emission

While traditional [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models successfully reproduce the optical and near-infrared continuum of quiescent galaxies, star-forming systems and active galactic nuclei (AGN) require the addition of non-stellar components. Modern panchromatic [[model-fitting|Model fitting]] must account for the reprocessing of ionizing radiation by gas and dust to maintain energy balance and avoid severe systematic biases in derived physical properties.

**Nebular Emission**
In star-forming galaxies, young, massive stars produce copious amounts of Lyman continuum photons that ionize the surrounding interstellar medium. The subsequent recombination produces both nebular continuum and strong emission lines. Neglecting these nebular contributions consistently leads to the overestimation of stellar masses and ages [Schaerer et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...515A..73S/abstract). This effect is particularly pronounced at high redshifts ($z > 3$), where high equivalent width lines—such as [O III] $\lambda 5007$ or H$\alpha$—redshift into standard near-infrared broadband filters (e.g., the $K_s$-band at $z \sim 3.5$), artificially inflating the observed continuum flux [Yuan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...631A.123Y/abstract). To address this, tools like the FADO code have pioneered self-consistent modeling of both nebular continuum and emission lines, demonstrating that purely stellar population synthesis codes (e.g., standard STARLIGHT runs) significantly overestimate stellar mass and age during high specific SFR phases [Cardoso et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A..56C/abstract).

Self-consistent modeling of stellar and nebular emission is therefore critical to accurately characterize star-forming populations. However, incorporating highly flexible nebular emission models (e.g., allowing for varying ionization parameters, gas densities, and ionizing photon budgets) can introduce its own systematic uncertainties, altering inferred stellar masses and star formation rates (SFRs) by $\sim 0.2$ dex [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963...74W/abstract). Recent high-redshift observations from JWST emphasize that accurately modeling the impact of strong emission lines on broadband photometry is essential to robustly recover the stellar mass function at $z > 5$, as uncorrected line contamination can lead to overestimating the masses of low-mass galaxies and underestimating those of high-mass galaxies [Cochrane et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241202622C/abstract).

**AGN and Dusty Tori**
In galaxies hosting an AGN, the central accretion disk heats a surrounding dusty structure (the AGN torus), which re-radiates the absorbed energy primarily in the mid-infrared. Failing to model this non-stellar heating can severely bias parameter estimation; misattributing the mid-infrared AGN emission to dust heated by star formation can cause errors in the derived SFR and stellar age by up to a factor of 10 [Leja et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...854...62L/abstract). Furthermore, broadband SED fitting can identify substantial populations of obscured AGNs that are otherwise missed by simple mid-infrared color selection criteria [Leja et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...854...62L/abstract). Hybrid templates that combine active galactic nuclei (AGN) and host galaxy spectra are essential for correctly classifying and fitting the spectral energy distributions of X-ray selected sources [Salvato et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...690.1250S/abstract). SED fitting using CIGALE on DESI data has proven effective at identifying $\sim 70\%$ of narrow/broad-line AGNs and 87% of WISE-selected AGNs, unifying multi-wavelength selection criteria [Siudek et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609143S/abstract). Modern energy-balance codes such as Prospector have also demonstrated the ability to classify up to 92% of the faint radio population into star-forming galaxies, radio galaxies, or radio-quiet AGNs using ultraviolet-to-far-infrared photometry [Das et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531..977D/abstract).

To disentangle the AGN from the host galaxy, SED models employ various parametrizations of the AGN torus. The literature reflects ongoing debate regarding the optimal torus geometry: while some studies of local ultraluminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs) find that a smooth, tapered AGN torus yields better fits than clumpy models [Varnava et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250214101V/abstract), [Varnava et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.538..426V/abstract), clumpy two-phase models remain widely implemented across fitting codes. 

To further break the degeneracy between obscured starbursts and AGN, advanced codes now extend the fitting baseline to higher energies. For instance, [[x-cigale|X-CIGALE]] integrates X-ray data with UV-to-IR photometry, utilizing polar-dust extinction and clumpy torus models (e.g., SKIRTOR) to provide unique constraints on the intrinsic AGN power [Yang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491..740Y/abstract). Similar multi-wavelength extensions are implemented in codes like Lightning [Doore et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..266...39D/abstract). Despite these advancements, extreme degeneracies persist at the high-redshift frontier. A prominent example is the population of "Little Red Dots" discovered by JWST at $z > 4$; their SEDs can be fit by either extreme, compact starbursts or heavily obscured AGNs, with best-fitting models often requiring an AGN luminosity fraction of 40% to 85% [Durodola et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240610329D/abstract).

## Spatially Resolved and Multi-component Decomposition

Traditional SED fitting has long relied on the [[integrated-light-method|Integrated light method]] to estimate the global properties of galaxies. However, treating a galaxy as a single unresolved point source averages over its internal structure, leading to significant systematic biases. Highly luminous, young stellar populations can dominate the integrated optical and ultraviolet light, effectively "outshining" older, more massive, and heavily dust-obscured populations. As a result, spatially unresolved SED fitting often systematically underestimates the total stellar mass of a galaxy—a phenomenon sometimes referred to as the "missing mass" problem. For galaxies with high specific star formation rates (e.g., $\text{sSFR} \sim 10^{-8}\text{ yr}^{-1}$), unresolved estimates can be lower by a factor of 2 to 5 compared to resolved measurements [Sorba et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.1532S/abstract).

To mitigate these biases, modern analyses increasingly employ spatially resolved, pixel-by-pixel SED fitting. By analyzing distinct spatial bins independently, models can account for local variations in dust attenuation and star formation histories across a galaxy's structure. Recent analyses of high-redshift ($3 \leq z < 9$) galaxies using JWST data demonstrate that resolved stellar mass estimates are systematically higher than integrated fits, with a median offset of $+0.24$ dex [Hamed et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115963H/abstract). This discrepancy is largely driven by the recovery of higher local dust optical depths ($\Delta A_V \approx +0.08$) and grayer attenuation curves within compact star-forming regions that are otherwise washed out in integrated light [Hamed et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115963H/abstract). Dedicated software packages, such as [[pixedfit|piXedfit]], facilitate this process by spatially matching multiband imaging with integral field spectroscopy to perform Bayesian parameter inference on a resolved grid [Abdurro'uf et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..254...15A/abstract). In this context, flexible parametric histories, such as the double-power law (DPL), have proven particularly robust for recovering recent localized starbursts [Mosleh et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983..181M/abstract). Looking to the future, observations from the Euclid space telescope will enable deep, spatially resolved stellar mass maps for local galaxies at $z < 0.1$, although accurate mass, age, and metallicity recovery from these synthetic images relies strictly on physically-motivated priors [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...702A..72E/abstract). However, pixel-by-pixel fitting is computationally intensive and subject to scale-dependent physical limitations; at spatial resolutions below $\sim 1$ kpc, the assumption of local energy balance between ultraviolet absorption and infrared re-emission begins to break down [Smith et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.1705S/abstract).

An alternative to a pure pixel grid is [[profile-decomposition|structural decomposition]], which uses macroscopic physical components (e.g., bulges and disks) as the basis for [[multi-component-sed-fitting|Multi-component SED fitting]]. Multi-wavelength image decomposition tools, such as GALFITM (an extension of [[galfit|GALFIT]]), fit wavelength-dependent Sérsic profiles to extract the distinct photometry of individual structural components [Häußler et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.430..330H/abstract), [Häußler et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...664A..92H/abstract). This structural separation avoids the biased assumption that an entire galaxy shares a single mass-to-light ratio or dust screen. Simultaneous spatial and spectral fitting pipelines combine structural extraction directly with [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] to derive component-specific evolutionary pathways. Such decomposition has revealed, for example, that present-day bulges are primarily composed of stars formed in the early universe ($\sim 12$ Gyr ago), whereas $70\%$ of present-day star formation occurs within disk populations [Bellstedt et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.5452B/abstract). This structural evolution is corroborated by resolved observations from the GASS survey, which demonstrate that H I-rich galaxies typically exhibit bluer, more actively star-forming outer discs, consistent with 'inside-out' galaxy formation paradigms [Wang et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412.1081W/abstract). While integrated SED modeling often struggles to accurately constrain geometry and viewing angles [Qiu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.2268Q/abstract), multi-component decomposition natively incorporates morphology, offering a more physically consistent reconstruction of galaxy assembly.

## Computational Advances and Machine Learning

State-of-the-art Bayesian [[model-fitting|Model fitting]] requires sampling complex, high-dimensional posteriors via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) or nested sampling. Evaluating [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models at each sampling step typically consumes 10 to 100 CPU hours per galaxy [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract). Upcoming wide-field surveys—such as those conducted by the Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST), Euclid, and the China Space Station Telescope (CSST)—will observe billions of galaxies, rendering traditional Bayesian sampling computationally intractable. This bottleneck has driven the rapid adoption of machine learning to accelerate [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] without sacrificing statistical rigor.

Techniques utilizing the interpolation of pre-computed template libraries, such as SpeedyMC, have achieved Bayesian inference speeds of approximately one second per galaxy, representing a 20,000-fold acceleration over standard methods [Acquaviva et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012IAUS..284...42A/abstract). A major breakthrough in computational efficiency is the application of simulation-based inference (SBI) and amortized neural posterior estimation (ANPE). In this framework, neural networks or normalizing flows are trained on millions of simulated galaxy SEDs to learn the mapping between the observables (e.g., photometric fluxes and redshift) and the posterior probability distributions of the underlying physical parameters. Once trained, ANPE algorithms do not require iterative model evaluations. The SEDFLOW code, for example, can evaluate the posteriors of 12 or more parameters in approximately one second per galaxy, achieving speedups of $10^4$ to $>10^5$ compared to traditional MCMC [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract). Similar SBI frameworks, such as Synference, extend this capability to 14-band photometry, achieving highly calibrated posteriors at a nearly fixed computational cost of $\sim 18$ galaxies per CPU second [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110640H/abstract).

Beyond posterior estimation, other architectures rely on supervised machine learning to directly regress physical parameters. The MIRKWOOD code utilizes ensemble tree-based models and conformal prediction to non-linearly map fluxes to properties while providing rigorous, distribution-free uncertainty intervals [Gilda et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916...43G/abstract), [Gilda et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Astro...3...14G/abstract). Taking a different approach, symbolic regression has been employed to derive explicit analytical expressions that estimate stellar mass from a linear combination of a few broadband colors and redshifts [Kumar et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251213389K/abstract). These mathematical expressions mimic the accuracy of traditional SED fitting algorithms but can be evaluated instantaneously, completely bypassing the need for complex numerical solvers. Similarly, comparative analyses of machine learning algorithms like Pt-SNE against traditional template-fitting codes (e.g., LePhare) demonstrate that ML approaches can yield significant speedups (approximately $3,000\times$) while simultaneously reducing biases in stellar mass estimation and increasing informational efficiency [Asadi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220544A/abstract).

Machine learning also provides a natural framework for incorporating data formats that are difficult to model with standard SED techniques. Traditional integrated SED fitting discards morphological information, but recent image-based conditional flow matching and convolutional neural networks can process pixel-level morphology simultaneously with photometry. By leveraging these structural features, such models have proven highly effective at breaking pervasive parameter degeneracies—such as the well-known dust-age degeneracy—which strictly photometry-based models struggle to resolve [Yunus et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251205078Y/abstract).

Despite these massive gains in computational efficiency, the literature broadly emphasizes that machine learning approaches are fundamentally limited by the quality of their training data. If the synthetic training set relies on oversimplified physics, or if it does not properly encapsulate the noise properties and parameter space of the target observations, the resulting neural networks can confidently predict biased parameters with artificially tight uncertainties. Thus, producing highly realistic, representative mock catalogs with accurately modeled dust geometries and star formation histories remains the primary challenge in deploying these techniques at cosmic scales.

## Degeneracies, Systematics, and Model Uncertainties

[[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] via spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting is fundamentally limited by the error budget of the underlying [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models. Across different codes and methodologies, stellar mass is generally the most robustly recovered parameter, with typical systematic uncertainties of $0.1-0.15$ dex [Mobasher et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...808..101M/abstract), [Pacifici et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944..141P/abstract). However, the choice of available photometric bands can still induce systematic effects. For example, findings from the SPIDER survey indicate that when estimating stellar masses for early-type galaxies, removing ultraviolet bands introduces substantially larger uncertainties ($\sim 0.15$ dex) compared to removing near-infrared bands ($\sim 0.06$ dex) [Swindle et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AJ....142..118S/abstract). In contrast to stellar mass, extracting age, metallicity, and star formation rate (SFR) is highly susceptible to profound parameter degeneracies and broader systematic biases. Uncertainties in derived physical properties like stellar mass and age can be validated and recalibrated by analyzing the distribution of [[photometry-and-redshift-estimation|photometric redshift]] outliers, as errors in redshift typically propagate into other SED parameters in predictable ways [Acquaviva et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...804....8A/abstract).

**The Age-Metallicity-Dust Degeneracy**
The classic SED fitting degeneracy dictates that old, metal-rich, or heavily dust-obscured stellar populations produce observationally similar red broadband colors. A modern manifestation of this challenge is the correlation between the dust attenuation curve slope ($\delta$) and optical depth ($A_V$). The literature is currently conflicted on whether this correlation is physical or primarily driven by fitting degeneracies between the intrinsic UV slope ($\beta_0$), $\delta$, and $A_V$ [Qin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511..765Q/abstract). While some studies suggest high-quality infrared data can break this degeneracy [Meldorf et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.3242M/abstract), underlying assumptions about dust energy balance—the presumption that UV/optical absorption strictly equals infrared re-emission—can be physically violated by up to a factor of $3$ depending on the galaxy's viewing angle [Faucher et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957....7F/abstract). Furthermore, the choice of photometric bands plays a pivotal role in constraining these degeneracies. For instance, in studies of star clusters, GALEX FUV and NUV bands provide much more critical constraints on ages and metallicities than WISE W1 and W2 infrared data [Fan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018AJ....156..191F/abstract). Additionally, caution is warranted when relying solely on broad-band data to break these degeneracies. Results from the LEGA-C spectroscopic survey indicate that photometry alone often fails to accurately predict observed [[lick-indices|spectral line indices]] (such as the Fe4383 index) in $z \sim 1$ galaxies. This implies that age and metallicity estimates derived from purely photometric SED fitting are heavily influenced by a priori modeling choices rather than the data itself [Nersesian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...681A..94N/abstract).

**SFH and IMF Systematics**
The choice of star formation history (SFH) prior strongly influences the derived properties. [[model-fitting|Model fitting]] with traditional parametric forms (e.g., exponentially declining $\tau$-models) can introduce an average systematic bias of $0.4$ dex in stellar mass [Lower et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904...33L/abstract). While non-parametric models reduce this specific bias to $< 0.1$ dex, highly stochastic or bursty SFHs—particularly in high-redshift galaxies—still introduce $\sim 0.8$ dex systematics in SFR and $\sim 0.3$ dex in age [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963...74W/abstract). Furthermore, the assumption of a universal Initial Mass Function (IMF) represents the single largest systematic uncertainty in high-redshift mass and SFR estimates, capable of shifting values by $\sim 1$ dex [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963...74W/abstract).

**Component Contamination**
Systematic errors compound when physically relevant emission sources are excluded. [[multi-component-sed-fitting|Multi-component SED fitting]] is frequently required to account for active galactic nuclei (AGN) and nebular emission. Neglecting AGN mid-infrared torus emission can mimic stellar dust heating, causing SFR and stellar age estimates to be artificially inflated by up to a factor of $10$ [Leja et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...854...62L/abstract). Additionally, fixed luminosity-to-SFR conversions using infrared data can introduce significant biases for quenched galaxies. In these systems, mid-infrared dust heating is often dominated by older stellar populations rather than active star formation, leading to overestimated SFRs if this contribution is not isolated [Salim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..227....2S/abstract). Similarly, failing to include nebular continuum and line emission leads to severe overestimations of stellar mass and age, as strong emission lines (e.g., [O III] redshifted into the $K_s$-band at $z \sim 3-4$) masquerade as elevated broadband stellar continuum flux [Schaerer et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...515A..73S/abstract), [Yuan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...631A.123Y/abstract).

**Outshining and Spatial Resolution**
Finally, spatially unresolved (integrated) SED fitting frequently suffers from the "outshining" effect, where the light from luminous, young stellar populations drowns out the flux from older, more massive underlying populations. This effect causes integrated fits to systematically underestimate total stellar masses. In high-sSFR galaxies, unresolved mass estimates can be underestimated by factors of up to $5$ [Sorba et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.1532S/abstract). Resolved pixel-by-pixel SED fitting mitigates this by recovering higher localized dust optical depths and grayer attenuation curves, leading to systematically higher total stellar masses (by a median offset of $+0.24$ dex) compared to integrated light estimates [Hamed et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115963H/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The advent of *JWST* has pushed the frontiers of [[model-fitting|Model fitting]] into uncharted territory, particularly for galaxies at $z > 4$. A major open question in the high-redshift regime is the robust separation of stellar continuum from extreme nebular emission lines. These emission features can significantly contaminate broadband photometry, leading to massive overestimations of stellar mass if not properly modeled [Schaerer et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...515A..73S/abstract), [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963...74W/abstract), [Cochrane et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241202622C/abstract). Furthermore, uncertainties in the stellar Initial Mass Function (IMF) currently represent the largest systematic error in high-redshift analyses, capable of introducing offsets of $\sim 1$ dex in stellar mass and star formation rate (SFR) estimates [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963...74W/abstract). The recent discovery of high-redshift "Little Red Dots" has further complicated interpretations; these sources possess spectral energy distributions that can masquerade as either heavily obscured compact starbursts or active galactic nuclei (AGN), with current models suggesting AGN may contribute 40-85% of their rest-frame optical luminosity [Durodola et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240610329D/abstract).

Observations suggest that early galaxy assembly is highly stochastic, necessitating flexible priors to model bursty star formation histories (SFHs). Traditional parametric models, such as exponentially declining $\tau$-models, often fail to capture this burstiness, systematically underestimating early star formation and introducing average biases of $\sim 0.4$ dex in stellar mass compared to flexible models [Lower et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904...33L/abstract). While the adoption of [[non-parametric-methods-in-astrophysics|Non-parametric methods in astrophysics]] mitigates this bias, it replaces it with a strong sensitivity to the choice of prior. Unconstrained burstiness can inject systematic uncertainties of $\sim 0.8$ dex in SFR and $\sim 0.3$ dex in age [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963...74W/abstract). Developing standardized, physically motivated priors from cosmological simulations to constrain early star formation remains a critical future direction [Gao et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...979...66G/abstract).

Spatially resolved pixel-by-pixel SED fitting is another active frontier, driven by the realization that unresolved [[integrated-galaxy-properties|Integrated galaxy properties]] often suffer from the "outshining" effect. Luminous, young stellar populations can mask older, more massive components, causing unresolved fits to underestimate total stellar mass by factors of up to 5 in galaxies with high specific SFRs [Sorba et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.1532S/abstract), [Hamed et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115963H/abstract). However, spatially resolved analyses highlight the limitations of global energy balance assumptions between UV attenuation and IR emission. Viewing angle effects and complex star-dust geometries can violate energy balance assumptions by up to a factor of 3 [Faucher et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957....7F/abstract). Furthermore, the literature remains conflicted on whether the observed correlation between the dust attenuation curve slope ($\delta$) and optical depth ($A_V$) reflects a genuine physical relationship or is merely an artifact of fitting degeneracies with the intrinsic UV slope [Qin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511..765Q/abstract), [Meldorf et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.3242M/abstract). Additionally, reconciling theoretical models with observational data remains an ongoing challenge; currently, there are notable discrepancies where leading hydrodynamical simulations (such as EAGLE) systematically overestimate mid-infrared fluxes and underestimate UV attenuation compared to local observational samples like DustPedia [Trčka et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494.2823T/abstract).

Finally, the sheer volume of data from upcoming massive surveys like the Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST), Euclid, and CSST necessitates a paradigm shift in [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]]. Traditional Bayesian sampling techniques are computationally prohibitive for billions of sources. Consequently, the field is rapidly adopting machine learning and Simulation-Based Inference (SBI). Techniques such as Amortized Neural Posterior Estimation (ANPE) can output 12-parameter posteriors in approximately one second per galaxy, accelerating inference by factors of $10^4$ to $>10^5$ compared to MCMC methods [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract), [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110640H/abstract). The pressing challenge for these methods is ensuring generalization; models trained on unrepresentative data risk injecting hidden biases and underestimating uncertainties [Gilda et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916...43G/abstract). Future pipelines will increasingly focus on incorporating image-level morphology alongside photometry to break age-dust degeneracies and refine posterior inference [Yunus et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251205078Y/abstract).
