**SKIRT** (Stellar Kinematics Including Radiative Transfer) is a state-of-the-art, open-source code designed for [[three-dimensional-dust-radiative-transfer|Three-dimensional dust radiative transfer]] in astrophysics. It operates as a fully three-dimensional [[monte-carlo-methods-in-astrophysics|Monte Carlo radiative transfer]] (MCRT) framework to simulate the complex, non-local, and non-linear interactions between the interstellar [[radiation-field|Radiation field]] and cosmic dust. By tracking millions of individual photon packages emitted from [[composite-astrophysical-models-and-populations|stellar populations]] or active galactic nuclei (AGN) as they propagate through arbitrarily complex spatial grids, SKIRT self-consistently computes dust absorption, anisotropic multiple scattering, and thermal re-emission. This rigorous 3D treatment overcomes the severe physical limitations of historical analytical approximations—such as the Kylafis & Bahcall method—which frequently produce unphysical results in optically thick or highly inclined systems. Originally developed to model the observed kinematics of dusty disc galaxies [Baes et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.343.1081B/abstract), SKIRT has evolved into a comprehensive panchromatic tool capable of generating synthetic multi-wavelength images, polarization maps [Peest et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...601A..92P/abstract), and spectral energy distributions (SEDs) from the X-ray [Fujiwara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220993F/abstract) to the submillimeter [Baes et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJS..196...22B/abstract).

Within the broader field of [[dust-modeling-and-radiative-transfer|Dust Modeling and Radiative Transfer]], SKIRT has been instrumental in addressing longstanding observational puzzles, most notably the "dust energy balance problem." By modeling realistic, clumpy interstellar environments, SKIRT simulations have demonstrated that highly obscured star-forming regions efficiently "hide" dust mass from optical sightlines. This geometric effect explains why models relying solely on optical extinction routinely underestimate observed far-infrared emission. Furthermore, the code has heavily shaped our understanding of galaxy-wide attenuation, proving that the observed attenuation law—unlike intrinsic dust extinction—is fundamentally driven by macroscopic star-dust geometry and can vary drastically with a galaxy's inclination and dust column density. Because of its high physical fidelity, SKIRT serves as a primary tool for "forward modeling" large-scale cosmological hydrodynamical simulations (such as IllustrisTNG and EAGLE). By post-processing these simulations into mock observations, researchers can perform direct comparisons with empirical survey data, offering a physically rigorous counterpart to empirical SED fitting codes like [[magphys|MAGPHYS]] or [[cigale|CIGALE]].

Despite algorithmic optimizations such as adaptive spatial grids, stellar foam source generation, and continuous absorption schemes, the computational expense of full 3D MCRT remains significant. To circumvent this, recent computational advances have integrated SKIRT with machine learning, yielding deep-learning emulators (such as the STARDUSTER framework) that replicate SKIRT's robust SEDs and spatial maps at a fraction of the computational cost. Looking forward, the code remains at the center of several critical open questions in extragalactic astrophysics. Chief among these is resolving the severe tension between simulated and observed abundances of highly dust-obscured, far-infrared luminous galaxies at high redshifts ($z \ge 4$). Addressing these discrepancies requires overcoming complex sub-grid modeling challenges, such as accurately capturing the porosity of the multiphase interstellar medium and evaluating the impact of dynamically evolving dust-to-metal ratios in early galaxies.

## Overview and Core Principles of SKIRT

SKIRT is a state-of-the-art framework for [[three-dimensional-dust-radiative-transfer|Three-dimensional dust radiative transfer]], widely utilized to simulate the complex **dust-starlight interaction** within galaxies and active galactic nuclei (AGN). Operating as a full 3D [[monte-carlo-methods-in-astrophysics|Monte Carlo radiative transfer]] (MCRT) code, SKIRT is designed to solve the non-local and non-linear problem of radiation propagating through an arbitrary, dusty interstellar medium.

At its core, SKIRT models the [[radiation-field|Radiation field]] by emitting individual **photon packages** from defined stellar or AGN sources. These packages are tracked probabilistically as they traverse a discretized 3D geometry, undergoing scattering, absorption, and re-emission by dust grains. The necessity of a full 3D MCRT approach is well-documented in the literature: simpler analytical treatments, such as the Kylafis & Bahcall (KB) approximation, are known to break down in optically thick or highly inclined systems where rigorous multiple-scattering tracking is strictly required [Lee et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.2912L/abstract). 

A defining feature of the code is its capability for **panchromatic modeling**, generating synthetic observables spanning from X-ray to submillimeter wavelengths. To achieve this, SKIRT strictly enforces energy balance: ultraviolet and optical photon packages absorbed by the medium are self-consistently re-emitted in the infrared. This dust emission model natively handles local thermal equilibrium (LTE) emission from large silicate and graphite grains, alongside non-local thermodynamic equilibrium (NLTE) emission from transiently heated small grains and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) [Baes et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJS..196...22B/abstract). SKIRT has also been extensively applied to our own galaxy, generating a self-consistent 3D model of the Milky Way's stellar and dust infrared emission. By adjusting spiral arm configurations and PAH abundances, SKIRT successfully fits multi-wavelength profiles from surveys such as GLIMPSE and MIPSGAL [Robitaille et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...545A..39R/abstract).

Over time, SKIRT has been expanded to capture a wide array of physical phenomena. The code incorporates kinematics by calculating precise Doppler shifts that account for the 3D velocity vectors of both the emitting stellar sources and the individual scattering dust grains [Baes et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.343.1081B/abstract). It also tracks polarization states caused by dust scattering, utilizing a "peel-off" mechanism to efficiently compute resolved polarization maps at optical and near-infrared wavelengths [Peest et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...601A..92P/abstract). Recently, the code has been extended to the high-energy regime to model X-ray reflection and fluorescence from clumpy AGN tori and polar dust [Fujiwara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220993F/abstract). While SKIRT focuses on producing observational predictions as a post-processing tool, it is important to note that other specialized on-the-fly radiative transfer algorithms (such as the 2D hydrodynamical implementations described by [Novak et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.427.2734N/abstract)) are required to dynamically calculate the radial forces on gas from dust absorption, providing critical insights into the radiative driving of outflows in active galactic nuclei and starbursts.

Because tracking millions of photon packages through complex, high-resolution grids is computationally expensive, SKIRT employs several optimization strategies. These include the use of "stellar foams" for efficient random source positioning and advanced tree-based spatial grids [Baes et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJS..196...22B/abstract). To further accelerate parameter-space exploration—such as automated spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting—recent efforts have successfully trained deep-learning emulators (e.g., STARDUSTER) and applied dimensionality reduction techniques (like PCA and NMF) to replicate SKIRT's panchromatic outputs with minimal computational overhead [Smole et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...669A.152S/abstract), [Qiu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930...66Q/abstract).

## Historical Development and Evolution

Prior to the widespread adoption of full 3D [[monte-carlo-methods-in-astrophysics|Monte Carlo Methods in Astrophysics]], modeling the [[dust-modeling-and-radiative-transfer|transfer of radiation in dusty galaxies]] heavily relied on simplified geometries and analytical techniques. A prominent early method was the KB approximation (proposed by Kylafis & Bahcall), which directly calculated the single-scattered intensity while approximating multiple scattering components. While computationally efficient and reasonably accurate for optically thin, face-on galaxies, the KB approximation demonstrated severe physical limitations in high-optical-depth and high-inclination regimes. For edge-on galaxies (inclinations $i \gtrsim 85^\circ$) or systems with a central face-on optical depth $\tau \gtrsim 1$, the KB approximation can produce errors exceeding $40\%$ and sometimes yields unphysical infinite intensities along highly obscured lines of sight [Lee et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.2912L/abstract). As noted in contemporary reviews, assuming oversimplified, uniform dust distributions for spiral galaxies rather than realistic exponential profiles leads to "catastrophic" misinterpretations of the radiative transfer [Kylafis et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AIPC..761....3K/abstract). These limitations necessitated the transition to fully self-consistent 3D Monte Carlo frameworks capable of handling complex, clumpy dust distributions and non-linear scattering. 

The SKIRT code was originally introduced as "Stellar Kinematics Including Radiative Transfer" to calculate the observed kinematics of dusty disc galaxies. Early versions uniquely incorporated the Doppler shifts introduced by both the velocities of the emitting stars and the individual scattering dust grains [Baes et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.343.1081B/abstract). Early applications of SKIRT also extended to Low Surface Brightness (LSB) galaxies, such as UGC 7321, revealing that a significant fraction of their dust is embedded in a highly clumped medium [Matthews et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph..9018M/abstract). Alongside SKIRT, other codes emerged to tackle these complexities; for instance, the TRADING code contributed significantly to self-consistent dust emission modeling in clumpy disks for systems like NGC 891 [Bianchi et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...490..461B/abstract). 

As the demand for modeling arbitrary 3D distributions of dust and starlight grew, SKIRT rapidly evolved into a general-purpose panchromatic radiative transfer tool. A major algorithmic leap occurred with the implementation of advanced optimization techniques, including the use of a stellar foam algorithm to efficiently generate random photon emission positions, combined with continuous absorption and eternal forced scattering [Baes et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJS..196...22B/abstract). These updates allowed the code to self-consistently calculate the non-local thermodynamic equilibrium (NLTE) emission of arbitrary dust mixtures across grids containing millions of cells [Baes et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJS..196...22B/abstract).

Historically, macroscopic galaxy dust properties were derived by fitting smooth, analytical 1D or 2D surface brightness profiles. Recognizing that the asymmetric, clumpy structure of real galaxies hides dust far more effectively than smooth models, the developers introduced FitSKIRT [De Geyter et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...550A..74D/abstract). FitSKIRT couples the core SKIRT radiative transfer engine with genetic algorithms to automatically fit fully 3D models to observed UV and optical images. By navigating the multi-dimensional parameter space of dusty galaxies, FitSKIRT minimizes human bias and allows for the automated recovery of complex geometric parameters [De Geyter et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...550A..74D/abstract).

Throughout its evolution, rigorous code benchmarking has been fundamental to validating SKIRT's complex 3D implementations. As new physical modules were added—such as the incorporation of dust scattering polarization in co-moving reference frames—they were strictly benchmarked against analytically calculated solutions and previously published numerical results to ensure accuracy in extreme or heavily obscured regimes [Peest et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...601A..92P/abstract). 

## Computational Architecture and Algorithms

Modeling the non-local, non-linear problem of [[three-dimensional-dust-radiative-transfer|Three-dimensional dust radiative transfer]] in complex interstellar environments carries a notoriously high computational cost. To address this, SKIRT is built upon a highly optimized software architecture that leverages advanced spatial discretization, variance-reduction algorithms, and parallelization within the framework of [[monte-carlo-methods-in-astrophysics|Monte Carlo Methods in Astrophysics]].

**Spatial Discretization and Adaptive Grids**
The simulation domain in SKIRT is discretized using a cellular approach [Baes et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.343.1081B/abstract). This allows the code to efficiently resolve the highly clumped, multiphase interstellar medium while accurately mapping arbitrary 3D structures of stars and dust [Baes et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJS..196...22B/abstract). Traversal of photon packets through these complex grid structures is optimized using an efficient trilinear interpolation technique, enabling smooth integration of the optical depth [Baes et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.343.1081B/abstract). Thanks to these adaptive grids, SKIRT can easily scale to accommodate highly inhomogeneous galaxy models containing millions of individual dust cells [Baes et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJS..196...22B/abstract).

**Variance Reduction and the Peel-off Mechanism**
Photon packets in SKIRT are launched from emission sources sampled using a "stellar foam" algorithm, which efficiently generates random source positions [Baes et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJS..196...22B/abstract). To drastically reduce Poisson noise and improve overall computational efficiency, SKIRT utilizes several variance-reduction schemes. A core algorithmic feature is the implementation of continuous absorption and "eternal forced scattering," which ensures that photon packets contribute to the energy density of the [[radiation-field|Radiation field]] continuously along their entire propagation path, rather than only at discrete interaction points [Baes et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJS..196...22B/abstract). 

Furthermore, to generate spatially resolved synthetic observations, SKIRT heavily relies on the peel-off mechanism. At every scattering event, this algorithm analytically computes the probability of a photon scattering directly toward the observer's line of sight, yielding high signal-to-noise images with fewer total photon packets. Notably, SKIRT fully supports the peel-off mechanism even in highly complex scattering regimes, such as when calculating spatial polarization signatures from [[mie-theory|light scattering off spherical dust grains]] and electrons in non-aligned reference frames [Peest et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...601A..92P/abstract).

**Optimization and Coupling**
To handle the vast number of photon packets required for convergence in optically thick regimes, SKIRT's architecture relies on documented optimizations such as eternal forced scattering and continuous absorption. This allows the code to scale efficiently and rapidly calculate the NLTE dust emission for arbitrary mixtures of transiently heated grains and PAHs [Baes et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJS..196...22B/abstract). Furthermore, to predict self-consistent interstellar radiation fields that include both gas emission and complex dust scattering, the MIXCLASK tool has been developed to iteratively couple the photoionization code [[cloudy-code|CLOUDY]] directly with SKIRT [Romero et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1727R/abstract).

Despite these algorithmic efficiencies, executing full Monte Carlo radiative transfer on massive cosmological simulations remains a severe computational bottleneck. To circumvent this, recent developments have integrated machine learning and statistical reconstruction techniques directly with SKIRT outputs. Deep-learning architectures and dimensionality reduction algorithms bypass the need to execute the full radiative transfer pipeline during parameter fitting, providing enormous speedups for multiwavelength SED prediction and spatial structure reconstruction [Smole et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...669A.152S/abstract), [Qiu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930...66Q/abstract).

## Dust Physics and Spectral Energy Distributions

SKIRT handles the complex physics of dust-starlight interactions through a full three-dimensional [[monte-carlo-methods-in-astrophysics|Monte Carlo treatment]] of photon propagation, explicitly tracking absorption and anisotropic multiple scattering. This rigorous approach is critical because, as previously noted, analytical approximations break down heavily for highly inclined or optically thick systems [Lee et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.2912L/abstract). The historical importance of such approaches dates back to early Monte Carlo attenuation curve atlases, which provided foundational insights into extinction across a wide set of Hubble types and inclinations [Ferrara et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJS..123..437F/abstract). Furthermore, modern SKIRT models demonstrate that because of complex star-dust geometries and the scattering of light into the line of sight, the [[dust-modeling-and-radiative-transfer|observed attenuation law]] of a galaxy diverges significantly from the intrinsic dust extinction curve. Even for a fixed intrinsic dust type, the effective attenuation slope $R_{V,\mathrm{eff}}$ can vary from $0.5$ to $7$ depending on galaxy morphology and inclination, systematically flattening ([[greybody-emission|'greying']]) at higher optical depths [Duarte et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250304906D/abstract). Recent SKIRT simulations further reveal that the temporal evolution of these attenuation curves is deeply driven by evolving grain size distributions and dynamic star-dust geometry, which together dictate the steepening and flattening of the curves over time [Matsumoto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250821157M/abstract).

A defining feature of SKIRT is its ability to self-consistently compute the NLTE thermal emission from dust grains. Very small grains and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are not in thermal equilibrium with the local [[radiation-field|Radiation field]]; rather, their temperatures fluctuate wildly following the absorption of individual ultraviolet or optical photons, a process known as stochastic heating. SKIRT solves this computationally demanding problem using a specialized library approach (interfacing with codes like DustEM) to calculate the non-lte emission of these transiently heated particles across millions of grid cells [Baes et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJS..196...22B/abstract). The framework supports arbitrary dust compositions, including the advanced THEMIS dust model, enabling the generation of panchromatic spectral energy distributions (SEDs) from the far-ultraviolet to the submillimeter [Mosenkov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...616A.120M/abstract). Because of the physical rigor of these models, synthetic photometry derived from comparable 3D radiative transfer simulations (such as those using the SUNRISE code) has been used to validate empirical SED fitting codes like [[magphys|MAGPHYS]]. These studies demonstrate that empirical codes generally recover fundamental physical parameters well, though their accuracy degrades significantly if the assumed analytical dust extinction curves are mismatched to the true complex geometry [Hayward et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.1512H/abstract).

By accurately tracking the 3D distribution of the radiation field, SKIRT has been used extensively to quantify the sources of dust heating in galaxies. While it is conventionally assumed that young stars ($< 100\ \mathrm{Myr}$) dominate far-infrared emission, SKIRT models show that evolved stellar populations provide a substantial and sometimes dominant heating contribution. The exact fraction is highly dependent on galaxy morphology: young stars generally provide $50\%{-}70\%$ of the heating in late-type spirals [De Looze et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...571A..69D/abstract), [Nersesian et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...637A..25N/abstract), but evolved stellar populations can account for up to $91\%$ of the absorbed luminosity in early-type spirals like M31 [Viaene et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...599A..64V/abstract). Across diverse samples, the young stellar heating fraction is found to correlate tightly with the specific star formation rate and the NUV$-r$ color. Additionally, detailed 3D radiative transfer modeling of the M51 interacting system has quantified how companion galaxies can provide up to $38\%$ of the local dust heating through external radiation fields [Nersesian et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A..90N/abstract). Building on these interacting system analyses, recent axisymmetric radiative transfer modeling of M51 has successfully reproduced its azimuthally averaged radial profiles from the UV all the way to submillimeter wavelengths [Inman et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526..118I/abstract). Moreover, analyzing these radiation fields extends beyond heating; although not performed with SKIRT, complementary high-resolution simulations using the TIGRESS-NCR code and its own adaptive ray-tracing scheme explicitly follow both far-ultraviolet (FUV) and Lyman continuum (LyC) radiation to show strong correlations between local UV fields and gas distributions. This enables the development of robust methods to calibrate gas surface density maps directly from observed thermal dust emission [Linzer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..173L/abstract).

Finally, the precise SEDs generated by SKIRT have illuminated the longstanding "dust energy balance problem." Simulations comparing smooth analytical geometries against the output of high-resolution hydrodynamical models demonstrate that complex, clumpy structures in the interstellar medium absorb UV/optical light less efficiently than smooth models of equal mass. This effectively hides a large fraction of the dust mass from optical view while still contributing heavily to the thermal infrared emission [Baes et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJS..196...22B/abstract), [Saftly et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...576A..31S/abstract). This clumpy treatment extends to high-redshift systems, where SKIRT models demonstrate that UV-to-FIR constraints for star-forming galaxies can be reconciled by invoking highly clumpy dust geometries and compact dust cores .

## The Dust Energy Balance Problem

The "dust energy balance problem" refers to a persistent discrepancy encountered in the study of spiral galaxies, wherein the amount of dust inferred from optical extinction is insufficient to account for the total thermal dust emission observed in the far-infrared (FIR). In a self-consistent physical framework, the energy removed from the stellar [[radiation-field|Radiation field]] via dust attenuation must exactly equal the energy re-radiated at longer wavelengths. However, when researchers use [[three-dimensional-dust-radiative-transfer|Three-dimensional dust radiative transfer]] codes to fit smooth, axisymmetric spatial models to optical and near-infrared images, the predicted FIR emission routinely falls short of observations. 

SKIRT has been instrumental in quantifying this discrepancy, particularly in highly inclined or edge-on spiral galaxies where projection effects and high optical depths severely complicate [[dust-modeling-and-radiative-transfer|Dust Modeling and Radiative Transfer]]. Detailed SKIRT models of edge-on galaxies consistently underestimate the observed FIR emission by factors of $1.5$ to $4$ [Baes et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518L..39B/abstract), [Mosenkov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...616A.120M/abstract). For example, a multi-wavelength SKIRT analysis of the edge-on spiral UGC 4754 found that the radiative transfer model underestimated the FIR emission by a factor of two to three. Due to the high-quality spectral coverage of the FIR data, the authors were able to definitively rule out systematic errors in the assumed FIR dust emissivity as the source of the deficit [Baes et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518L..39B/abstract). 

While the problem is ubiquitous, the literature indicates that the exact magnitude of the underestimation is ambiguous and varies significantly from galaxy to galaxy. In a study of the HEROES galaxy sample, the FIR deficit ranged from $1.5\times$ to $4\times$ for most targets, implying that energy balance heavily depends on galaxy-specific morphologies and the spatial resolution of the input models [Mosenkov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...616A.120M/abstract). 

The consensus resolution to the dust energy balance problem points to the inhomogeneous, clumpy structure of the interstellar medium. Highly obscured star-forming regions are optically thick; they contribute almost nothing to the global optical surface brightness profile (and thus do not influence the extinction fit) but still radiate prodigiously in the FIR [Baes et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518L..39B/abstract). This geometric effect was explicitly demonstrated using SKIRT to forward-model high-resolution hydrodynamic simulations of galaxies. When mock edge-on images of these complex, clumpy simulated galaxies were fitted with simple, smooth analytical components (such as an exponential disk), the recovered dust mass was approximately three times smaller than the true dust mass known to exist in the simulation [Saftly et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...576A..31S/abstract). This confirms that complex spatial asymmetries naturally produce the observed energy balance discrepancy when extrapolating from optical extinction to FIR emission.

## Active Galactic Nuclei and AGN Torus Modeling

SKIRT is extensively utilized to simulate the highly irradiated, dusty environments surrounding active galactic nuclei (AGN), offering critical insights into the structure of AGN dusty tori. Early analytical models often assumed smooth, single-phase dust distributions, but high-resolution infrared spectroscopy has consistently shown these models to be inadequate. Modern [[dust-modeling-and-radiative-transfer|radiative transfer studies]] emphasize the necessity of a [[ctorus-model|clumpy torus]] or a multi-phase medium. For instance, detailed SKIRT modeling of the archetypal Seyfert 2 galaxy NGC 1068 demonstrates that fitting N- and Q-band spectra requires complex, two-component or concentric geometries [Victoria-Ceballos et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926..192V/abstract). The best-fitting models for such systems favor specific grain constraints, such as a maximum grain size of $1\,\mu\text{m}$, and trace a dust distribution spanning from a dense central component (with an outer radius of $\sim 1.8\text{ pc}$) to a less dense, extended structure reaching out to $28\text{ pc}$ [Victoria-Ceballos et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926..192V/abstract).

This extended mid-infrared emission is increasingly attributed to a polar dust component, rather than being confined strictly to an equatorial flared disk [Victoria-Ceballos et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926..192V/abstract). SKIRT has been instrumental in modeling these coupled equatorial and polar structures. To this end, the code has recently been expanded to compute X-ray reflection spectra originating from both the clumpy torus and the polar dusty gas. The IMPACTX model relies on SKIRT to calculate generic X-ray spectral energy distributions from a [[cygnus-models|Nenkova-style clumpy torus]] paired with a paraboloid-shell polar dust geometry [Fujiwara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220993F/abstract). Simultaneous application of these models to the X-ray and infrared spectra of nearby AGN demonstrates that multi-wavelength constraints are essential to robustly determine the nuclear architecture [Fujiwara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220993F/abstract). Beyond standard spectral fitting, SKIRT simulations of lensed quasars reveal that despite their large physical extent, AGN dusty tori can be significantly affected by gravitational microlensing [Stalevski et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.1576S/abstract). This microlensing effect is particularly pronounced in the rest-frame near-infrared, potentially altering the observed flux ratios between lensed images over timescales ranging from decades to centuries [Stalevski et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.1576S/abstract). 

Finally, proper [[dust-modeling-and-radiative-transfer|Dust Modeling and Radiative Transfer]] is crucial for avoiding structural misclassification in non-active or weakly active galaxies. Forward-modeling with SKIRT has shown that circum-nuclear dust rings in early-type galaxies can mimic the surface brightness profiles of AGN cores or unresolved nuclear stellar clusters [Bonfini et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.1161B/abstract). Accounting for the extinction caused by these centralized dust features is therefore of paramount importance to derive accurate fluxes for low-luminosity AGNs.

## Mock Observations for Cosmological Simulations

To validate cosmological hydrodynamical simulations against survey data, researchers employ "forward modeling" or post-processing to generate mock observations. SKIRT is a primary tool for processing the outputs of particle- and grid-based simulations, translating physical properties such as gas density, stellar mass, and metallicity into observable synthetic images, spectra, and data cubes. By applying [[three-dimensional-dust-radiative-transfer|Three-dimensional dust radiative transfer]], SKIRT properly accounts for the absorption, scattering, and thermal re-emission of the interstellar [[radiation-field|Radiation field]], enabling direct apples-to-apples comparisons with empirical data.

### Large-Volume Cosmological Simulations
SKIRT has been extensively applied to large-volume cosmological runs, notably the IllustrisTNG and EAGLE suites. Through synthetic photometry and spectral energy distribution (SED) generation, SKIRT-processed simulations successfully reproduce many global scaling relations at $z=0$, such as the slope and scatter of the multi-wavelength Tully-Fisher relation [Baes et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...696A..52B/abstract). In the context of the EAGLE simulations, SKIRT mock observations have provided critical insights into galaxy-wide dust attenuation, linking derived attenuation slopes directly to the average dust surface density using a two-component screen model [Trayford et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.3937T/abstract). Extensive public databases, such as the TNG50-SKIRT Atlas, provide high-resolution ($100$ pc) synthetic broad-band images spanning from the far-ultraviolet (FUV) to the submillimeter [Baes et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.181B/abstract), [Trčka et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.516.3728T/abstract). Beyond broadband photometry, the detailed SKIRT-calculated UV radiation fields have also been used to produce spatially resolved synthetic atomic hydrogen (H I) maps for TNG50, revealing that these simulated galaxies often exhibit central H I deficits compared to empirical observations [Gebek et al. ### Zoom-in Simulations and Kinematics
For higher-resolution studies, SKIRT is frequently applied to zoom-in simulations of Milky Way-mass galaxies, such as the Auriga, ARTEMIS, and CIELO projects. For instance, the CIELO project uses SKIRT to generate synthetic SDSS-band images for galaxies across a wide variety of cosmic environments [Tissera et al. Applying SKIRT to the NEWHORIZON simulation has also proven vital for translating morphological and kinematic indicators between theoretical models and real-world observations [Jang et al. Similarly, within the FIRE-2 project, SKIRT modeling generates crucial predictions for the spatial distribution of dust continuum emission at high redshifts ($1 < z < 5$), demonstrating that it is often more compact than the cold gas distribution but more extended than the stellar component [Cochrane et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488.1779C/abstract).

At smaller scales, the code is used to model star cluster populations in simulated dwarf galaxy starbursts; these mock observations reveal that spatial crowding frequently leads to overestimates of star cluster masses and artificially shallower mass function slopes compared to the intrinsic simulation data [Lahén et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.4560L/abstract). More broadly, emulating integral-field unit (IFU) observations of Auriga galaxies demonstrates that dust attenuation and inclination effects can artificially decrease the measured kinematic ratio $V/\sigma$ by nearly a factor of two [Barrientos Acevedo et al. ### Dust Prescriptions and Machine Learning
The accuracy of SKIRT mock observations depends heavily on the underlying dust prescriptions. Traditionally, cosmological simulations assign dust mass using a fixed dust-to-metal (DTM) ratio. However, recent on-the-fly (OTF) dust evolution models reveal that dynamic DTM ratios fundamentally alter synthetic morphologies. In simulations tracking OTF dust, intense central starbursts can create "DTM cavities"—local depressions in the DTM ratio—resulting in brighter bulges and a lower fraction of late-type galaxies compared to fixed-DTM approximations [Byun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...992...92B/abstract). 

Finally, the high fidelity of SKIRT mock observations has found utility in modern machine learning applications. By supplementing empirical training sets with SKIRT-generated synthetic images from IllustrisTNG, deep learning models demonstrate significantly improved robustness against domain shifts, physical pixel size variations, and observational noise [Holzschuh et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515..652H/abstract).

## Recent Advances: Machine Learning and Emulation

While the SKIRT code represents the gold standard for [[three-dimensional-dust-radiative-transfer|Three-dimensional dust radiative transfer]], its non-local, non-linear treatment of photon scattering and absorption makes it computationally demanding. This computational cost traditionally prohibits its direct use in rapidly interpreting large-scale galaxy surveys or performing exhaustive Bayesian parameter estimation. To bypass this bottleneck, researchers have increasingly turned to deep learning and SED emulation. 

A prominent example of this synthesis is the STARDUSTER framework [Qiu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930...66Q/abstract). STARDUSTER utilizes a supervised deep-learning architecture comprising three specialized neural networks to map galaxy geometry parameters and star formation histories directly to multi-wavelength SEDs. Trained on a relatively modest dataset of $\sim 4000$--$5000$ SKIRT simulations, the emulator predicts dust attenuation and thermal dust emission with exceptional accuracy. It achieves error margins of only $\sim 0.005$ mag for attenuation and $\sim 0.1$--$0.2$ mag for emission when compared to direct SKIRT calculations [Qiu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930...66Q/abstract). 

By functioning as a rapid surrogate model, STARDUSTER enables simulation-backed SED fitting of observed galaxies. It provides a computationally tractable alternative to widely used empirical or semi-analytic fitting codes like [[magphys|MAGPHYS]] or [[cigale|CIGALE]], while retaining the rigorous 3D geometric properties computed by SKIRT. However, applying such deep-learning emulators to real data highlights persistent physical degeneracies between star-dust geometry and intrinsic dust properties. For example, breaking the degeneracy between a galaxy's inclination angle and its stellar mass in these models requires stringent mid-infrared constraints, optimally at rest-frame wavelengths near $\lambda \approx 40\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ [Qiu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930...66Q/abstract).

Beyond integrated SEDs, machine learning is also used to accelerate the production of spatially resolved SKIRT images. Because [[monte-carlo-methods-in-astrophysics|Monte Carlo Methods in Astrophysics]] inherently suffer from Poisson noise in low-photon-count regimes, generating high signal-to-noise mock observations of complex 3D environments is highly resource-intensive. Recent frameworks utilize dimensionality reduction techniques, such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF), combined with the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) to detect and reconstruct non-random spatial structures. When applied to SKIRT outputs of high-resolution cosmological zoom-in simulations, this Bayesian inference approach reproduces the structure of high-photon-number reference images approximately $5$ times faster, maintaining median residuals below $20\%$ [Smole et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...669A.152S/abstract).

Finally, generative modeling has leveraged SKIRT databases (such as those post-processed from the IllustrisTNG simulation) to train robust neural networks for observational astronomy. Generative models trained on SKIRT mock images can produce synthetic galaxies that are statistically indistinguishable from the source data, achieving a Wasserstein distance as low as $5.08 \times 10^{-2}$ [Holzschuh et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515..652H/abstract). Crucially, supplementing empirical training sets with these SKIRT-generated images significantly improves the robustness of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) against domain shifts, such as observational discrepancies in physical pixel sizes or varying background noise levels. For instance, incorporating SKIRT-based generative data into CNN denoising tasks yields up to a $45\%$ performance improvement when confronted with out-of-distribution noise levels [Holzschuh et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515..652H/abstract). To further lower the barrier to generating synthetic data, newer tools such as the GalaxyGenius Python package have been introduced. GalaxyGenius streamlines the complex SKIRT radiative transfer pipeline, enabling the automated and efficient generation of mock galaxy observations tailored to the specific instrumental profiles of various space and ground-based telescopes [Zhou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250615060Z/abstract). These advances establish SKIRT not just as a standalone simulation tool, but as a foundational data engine for AI-driven astrophysical analyses.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While SKIRT has significantly advanced the field of [[three-dimensional-dust-radiative-transfer|Three-dimensional dust radiative transfer]], several theoretical and methodological frontiers remain open. A primary challenge lies in bridging the gap between large-scale cosmological simulations and high-resolution multi-wavelength observations.

**High-redshift Galaxies and JWST Synergies**
Current cosmological simulations (such as IllustrisTNG and FLARES) post-processed with SKIRT consistently underpredict the abundance of the most dust-obscured, luminous far-infrared (FIR) galaxies at $z \ge 4$ [Shen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.5560S/abstract), [Vijayan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.4999V/abstract). Furthermore, these models tend to overestimate the peak dust temperatures of $z \ge 6$ galaxies by approximately $20\text{ K}$ [Shen et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.5560S/abstract). Addressing these discrepancies is a major priority, driven heavily by emerging JWST synergies. Comparisons between synthetic images and JWST observations highlight an active debate: it remains unresolved whether these tensions stem from missing physics in the underlying hydrodynamic simulations or from the radiative transfer post-processing recipes [Shen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.5560S/abstract), [Kapoor et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...692A..79K/abstract).

**ISM Porosity and Sub-grid Modeling**
A fundamental bottleneck in reconciling simulated UV and IR fluxes with observations is accurately capturing ISM porosity. Because large-volume cosmological simulations lack the mass resolution to explicitly resolve the multi-phase, clumpy structure of the interstellar medium, radiative transfer codes must rely on sub-grid modeling to represent star-forming regions and birth clouds [Kapoor et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...692A..79K/abstract). Developing physically motivated sub-grid models that properly capture the time-resolved disruption of birth clouds remains a critical frontier. Additionally, the standard practice of assuming a fixed dust-to-metal (DTM) ratio is increasingly challenged. Recent on-the-fly (OTF) dust evolution models suggest that intense nuclear starbursts create localized "DTM cavities", which significantly alter the predicted morphology of high-redshift galaxies compared to fixed-DTM approximations [Byun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...992...92B/abstract).

**The Dust Energy Balance Problem**
On local galactic scales, the long-standing "dust energy balance problem" remains an open question. Despite the use of highly sophisticated [[monte-carlo-methods-in-astrophysics|Monte Carlo Methods in Astrophysics]], models that successfully fit the optical extinction in edge-on spiral galaxies consistently underestimate the observed FIR emission [Baes et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518L..39B/abstract), [Saftly et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...576A..31S/abstract), [Mosenkov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...616A.120M/abstract). While there is strong consensus that complex, inhomogeneous dust structures hide mass more effectively than smooth analytical models [Saftly et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...576A..31S/abstract), it remains heavily debated whether clumpiness alone can fully resolve the discrepancy, or if a fundamental revision of intrinsic dust emissivity models is required.

**Attenuation Degeneracies and Future Directions**
Finally, breaking the degeneracy between star-dust geometry and intrinsic dust grain properties is a persistent observational challenge. Forward-modeling with SKIRT demonstrates that the effective attenuation slope, $R_{V,\text{eff}}$, can vary drastically—from $0.5$ to $7$—simply due to changes in inclination and dust column density, even when the intrinsic dust chemistry is held constant [Duarte et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...700A.169D/abstract). Determining whether the observed increase in $R_{V,\text{eff}}$ with stellar mass is a physical change in dust composition or a purely geometric effect of higher dust density [Duarte et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...700A.169D/abstract) requires expanded multi-wavelength constraints, such as rest-frame $40\,\mu\text{m}$ data [Qiu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930...66Q/abstract). To address these multi-scale challenges, future development of the SKIRT framework is increasingly focused on the integration of panchromatic X-ray spectral modeling [Fujiwara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220993F/abstract) and the continued deployment of deep learning emulators to accelerate high-dimensional parameter space exploration [Qiu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930...66Q/abstract).
