## Article

In strong gravitational lensing, **source plane reconstruction** is the numerical and statistical process of mapping highly distorted, multiply-imaged background galaxies back to their intrinsic, unlensed spatial configurations. Because gravitational deflection conserves the surface brightness of the source, astronomers can theoretically invert the lens equation to deduce both the original morphology of the background object and the mass distribution of the foreground deflector. While textbook scenarios involving simple point-mass lenses yield highly deterministic parity and magnification rules, realistic galaxy clusters feature complex, asymmetric mass profiles that produce intricate [[arc-geometry|Arc geometry]] and severe structural distortions. By exploiting these massive foreground deflectors as "cosmic telescopes," source plane reconstruction allows for the study of high-redshift ($z \sim 1-3$) galaxies at sub-kiloparsec resolutions that would otherwise remain far below the diffraction limits of modern observatories, revealing detailed kinematics and star-forming clumps in the early Universe [Johnson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...843...78J/abstract), [Sharma et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.1427S/abstract), [Sharma et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505L...1S/abstract).

Mathematically, recovering the unlensed source and the deflecting potential from noisy image-plane observables is a severely ill-posed inverse problem. Early historical attempts relied on direct matrix inversion or simple [[ray-shooting|Ray-shooting]] algorithms, occasionally utilizing exact [[finite-field-inversion-formulae|Finite-field inversion formulae]] to map local ellipticities directly into mass maps [Kaiser et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995wfsd.conf..246K/abstract), [Seitz et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996A&A...305..383S/abstract). However, direct inversion methods notoriously amplify observational noise and fail to appropriately deconvolve the instrumental point spread function (PSF), frequently generating false-positive, small-scale mass fluctuations. Consequently, the field has broadly transitioned toward Bayesian forward-modeling. Modern algorithms optimize adaptive pixel grids using sophisticated priors—such as maximum entropy or Tikhonov regularization—to enforce physically plausible smoothness while accommodating the flexibility of "free-form" (non-parametric) mass models [Seitz et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&A...337..325S/abstract). To fully constrain the deflector's mass profile from its dense core to its outskirts and break the limiting mass-sheet degeneracy, strong lensing core reconstructions are frequently combined with extended weak lensing constraints, including [[weak-lensing-magnification|Weak lensing magnification]] and higher-order shape distortions like [[gravitational-flexion|Gravitational flexion]] [Bradac et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph.10643B/abstract).

This article explores the theoretical foundations and numerical evolution of source plane reconstruction, detailing the ongoing methodological debate between rigid parametric mass models and flexible free-form density grids. It examines the critical technical challenges of PSF deconvolution and noise mitigation required for precision morphological analysis of high-redshift sources. Furthermore, it highlights recent computational breakthroughs utilizing deep learning, where continuous neural fields and score-based diffusion models are increasingly deployed to replace manually crafted regularization with expressive, data-driven priors. Finally, the article surveys active open questions in the field, particularly the persistent difficulty of disentangling smooth, large-scale cluster mass from individual galaxy-scale subhalos, and the critical challenge of ensuring that observed small-scale anomalies reflect true dark matter substructure rather than systematic artifacts introduced by the source reconstruction itself.

## Conceptual Foundations and the Inverse Problem

The foundational principle of gravitational lensing is the conservation of surface brightness. In the absence of emission or absorption, the surface brightness of an observed image $I(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ at angular coordinate $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is identical to the intrinsic surface brightness of the source $I^{(s)}(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ at its unlensed position $\boldsymbol{\beta}$. This relationship is written as:
$$ I(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = I^{(s)}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) $$

The mapping between the source and image planes is governed by the lens equation,
$$ \boldsymbol{\beta} = \boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) $$
where $\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the deflection angle determined by the foreground mass distribution. While simple textbook models of point-mass lenses yield highly deterministic parity rules and magnification patterns, the complex mass profiles of realistic galaxy clusters produce highly distorted [[arc-geometry|Arc geometry]] and intricate multiple-image configurations. In these complex environments, light does not always perfectly trace mass, and deterministic inversion of the lens equation is complicated by severe degeneracies between smooth dark matter and galaxy-scale halos [Limousin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...588A..99L/abstract), [Cha et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..186C/abstract). To navigate the complexities of multi-component systems, the singular perturbative method has proven effective at decoupling the inner potential of a central galaxy from larger-scale environmental perturbations [Alard et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...513A..39A/abstract). Furthermore, assessing the sensitivity of fold and cusp magnification relations to dark matter substructure serves as a foundational diagnostic for identifying local mass distribution anomalies independent of the global profile [Aazami et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.372.1692A/abstract).

The core mathematical challenge of source plane reconstruction is the inverse problem: deriving both the unlensed source morphology $I^{(s)}(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ and the deflection field $\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ from noisy image-plane observables. It is important to distinguish this specific pixel-level inversion from the related cosmological inverse problem of density and velocity field reconstruction (e.g., using algorithms like BORG), which maps large-scale cosmic structure evolution rather than individual lensed galaxy morphologies [Stiskalek et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250200121S/abstract). Similar Bayesian methodologies, incorporating Poissonian data and velocity distortions, have been adapted to reconstruct spatially distorted cosmic signals and large-scale cosmological density fields from galaxy counts [Weig et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.409.1393W/abstract). Historically, this lensing inversion was approached via direct matrix inversion, simple ray-tracing, or [[ray-shooting|Ray-shooting]], where locally averaged image ellipticities were converted directly into mass maps using [[finite-field-inversion-formulae|Finite-field inversion formulae]] [Seitz et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996A&A...305..383S/abstract). However, direct inversion is an ill-posed problem; it notoriously amplifies observational noise, leading to overfitting and the generation of spurious, false-positive small-scale mass fluctuations [Seitz et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&A...337..325S/abstract), [Cha et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931..127C/abstract). Alternative direct approaches like LensPerfect sought to mitigate these issues through the smooth interpolation of the deflection field to exactly reproduce all multiple-image positions [Coe et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...681..814C/abstract).

To further mitigate these instabilities, the field has undergone a paradigm shift toward Bayesian forward modeling and regularized inverse methods. Rather than directly mapping pixels backward, modern algorithms simultaneously model the lens potential and source intensity on an adaptive grid, enforcing regularization priors such as Maximum Entropy or Tikhonov regularization [Bridle et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.299..895B/abstract), [Koptelova et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.11508K/abstract), [Suyu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691..277S/abstract). By penalizing unphysical noise fluctuations while retaining the flexibility of "free-form" non-parametric models, entropy-regularized algorithms (e.g., MARS) can achieve sub-arcsecond convergence (down to $\sim 0.001''$) in source positions and successfully extract substructure information [Cha et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931..127C/abstract), [Cha et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951..140C/abstract).

Furthermore, pixel-level forward modeling effectively accounts for the asymmetric lensing shear and the instrumental point spread function (PSF). Because observations are always convolved with a PSF, decoupling it from the source reconstruction via forward modeling is paramount for accurately interpreting sub-kiloparsec features, such as star-forming clumps down to $\sim 100 \text{ pc}$ scales at high redshift [Johnson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...843...78J/abstract), [Sharma et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505L...1S/abstract).

Despite these methodological advancements, there remains an active tension in the literature regarding the necessary complexity of source priors. Inadequate source modeling—such as utilizing simplistic analytic shapelets instead of adaptive pixelated or Delaunay meshes—can leave residuals that are erroneously interpreted as dark matter substructure, producing convincing yet spurious subhalo detections [Ephremidze et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250218571E/abstract). Consequently, ensuring robust source reconstruction increasingly requires multi-band analysis and sophisticated adaptive-grid schemes to disentangle true lens perturbations from source-plane systematics [Ephremidze et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250218571E/abstract), [Ding et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250411445D/abstract). The integration of these strong lensing constraints with extended weak lensing data, such as [[weak-lensing-magnification|Weak lensing magnification]] and [[gravitational-flexion|Gravitational flexion]], further helps break the mass-sheet degeneracy, ensuring physical and self-consistent solutions across both the cluster core and its outskirts [Bradac et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph.10643B/abstract), [Cain et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.4287C/abstract).

## Historical Development of Numerical Inversion

The numerical inversion of gravitationally lensed images has evolved from simple geometric approximations to sophisticated statistical frameworks capable of resolving sub-kiloparsec source features. The earliest approaches aimed at reconstructing extended source morphologies relied on iterative techniques. A foundational milestone was the algorithm developed by Kochanek in 1989 [Kochanek et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989MNRAS.238...43K/abstract), often referred to as the "Ring Cycle." This method iteratively solved for both the unlensed source brightness distribution and a parametrized lens potential to reconstruct radio structures such as the Einstein ring MG1131+0456. By minimizing a figure of merit based on the scatter of surface brightness in the source plane, this work established the paradigm of forward modeling extended lensed emission. 

In the realm of cluster mass reconstruction, early work relied heavily on direct methods. The seminal Kaiser-Squires method directly inverted observed local ellipticities (shear) to map the dimensionless surface mass density, or convergence, $\kappa$ [Kaiser et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995wfsd.conf..246K/abstract). Another early milestone in this era was the unique source reconstruction of the highly magnified, beaded, ringlike morphology in the cluster 0024+1654, which successfully inverted multiple long arcs to deduce the unlensed structure of a background galaxy [Colley et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...461L..83C/abstract). While computationally efficient, direct reconstruction approaches suffer from several drawbacks: they are susceptible to the mass-sheet degeneracy, amplify low-frequency noise, and exhibit severe boundary artifacts when applied to finite observational fields [Squires et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...473...65S/abstract). Efforts to mitigate these edge effects led to the development of exact [[finite-field-inversion-formulae|Finite-field inversion formulae]] designed to minimize the reconstruction's sensitivity to observational noise [Seitz et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996A&A...305..383S/abstract). 

Because direct inversion frequently leads to overfitting and the generation of spurious mass peaks, the field transitioned toward "inverse" or forward-modeling techniques. Early cosmological applications of Bayesian inversion, such as Wiener reconstruction of all-sky galaxy surveys in spherical harmonics, provided the first optimal maps of underlying density fields across the sky and set an important conceptual foundation for adopting statistical priors [Lahav et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...423L..93L/abstract). These evolving methods treat reconstruction as an ill-posed inverse problem, requiring regularization to yield stable, physically plausible solutions. Entropy-regularized likelihood methods, or Maximum Entropy Methods (MEM), became a cornerstone of this shift in the late 1990s [Seitz et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&A...337..325S/abstract), [Bridle et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.299..895B/abstract). By enforcing an entropic prior on the positive additive mass distribution, these algorithms effectively suppressed spurious small-scale fluctuations while remaining flexible enough to resolve high-resolution substructure where the local lensing signal was sufficiently strong.

Modern reconstruction techniques have expanded on these regularized inverse methods to handle the complex, highly constrained environments of massive galaxy clusters. Today, algorithms frequently utilize adaptive pixel-grids to vary the source plane resolution based on image intensity [Enia et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.3467E/abstract). Historically, benchmarking these evolving tools has been essential. Comparative studies of weak and strong lensing field reconstructions have rigorously evaluated methods like FLens (which uses multi-scale entropy) against SVD linear inversions and Lenstool's Bayesian MCMC optimization, demonstrating the trade-offs in accuracy and resolution for cluster field reconstruction [Jullo et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.437.3969J/abstract). Recent free-form algorithms like MARS utilize maximum-entropy regularization to achieve sub-arcsecond convergence in source positions (down to $\sim 0.001''$) without assuming that light perfectly traces mass [Cha et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931..127C/abstract), [Cha et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951..140C/abstract). 

Despite these algorithmic advances, an active debate remains regarding the use of free-form versus parametric mass modeling. Unconstrained free-form models can inadvertently fit noise if poorly regularized, whereas parametric models may artificially force "peaky" or cored profiles that mask true substructure [Limousin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...588A..99L/abstract). To combat these degeneracies, contemporary methods routinely combine strong lensing constraints in the cluster core with larger-scale weak lensing shear and [[weak-lensing-magnification|Weak lensing magnification]], yielding robust mass profiles from the core to the outskirts [Bradač et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...437...39B/abstract), [Bradac et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph.10643B/abstract). Furthermore, advanced pipelines are actively exploring the inclusion of higher-order shape distortions, such as [[gravitational-flexion|Gravitational flexion]], to constrain subhalo populations, though the literature remains conflicted on whether flexion is practically robust against noise compared to shear alone [Cain et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.4287C/abstract), [Pires et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...723.1507P/abstract).

## Parametric vs. Free-form (Non-parametric) Modeling

In the context of source plane reconstruction, a fundamental methodological choice dictates how the deflector's mass distribution and the background source's surface brightness are represented. This choice lies along a spectrum between rigid parametric models and highly flexible, non-parametric architectures. There is an ongoing debate in the literature regarding the optimal balance between these two paradigms, as each carries distinct physical assumptions and systematic vulnerabilities.

Parametric models represent the deflecting mass using a finite superposition of analytic profiles, such as the Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) or pseudo-isothermal ellipsoids, and typically invoke the assumption that the dark matter traces the luminous cluster members. While physically motivated and generally robust against low signal-to-noise data, parametric models can artificially restrict the solution space. For complex clusters, rigid parameterizations often fail to uniquely disentangle smooth, large-scale dark matter halos from galaxy-scale perturbers. For example, exhaustive analyses of the Frontier Fields cluster MACS J0717.5+3745 reveal a severe degeneracy between "peaky" and "cored" mass components, demonstrating that parametric models can yield vastly different source-plane magnifications while fitting the image-plane [[arc-geometry|Arc geometry]] equally well [Limousin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...588A..99L/abstract). Recent analyses highlight the effectiveness of broken power-law models for determining precise Einstein radii and radial convergence profiles, demonstrating that they can capture mass-profile slope variations that traditional single power-law models miss [Du et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953..189D/abstract). 

Conversely, **free-form inversion** reconstructs the lensing potential or density field using a large number of independent cells, basis functions, or pixels, removing the strict requirement that mass must trace light [Sebesta et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488.3251S/abstract). An alternative multiscale approach is Particle Based Lensing (PBL), a reconstruction technique that treats lensed galaxies as discrete particles with variable smoothing scales, dynamically adapting to the local constraint density [Deb et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...687...39D/abstract). This flexibility allows for the discovery of unexpected substructures, but it inherently risks overfitting observational noise, which can manifest as spurious small-scale mass fluctuations and unphysical source morphologies. To mitigate this, free-form methods must rely heavily on Bayesian priors or regularization. Modern algorithms, such as the MAximum-entropy ReconStruction (MARS) method, enforce smoothness via entropy-based regularization. By optimizing these priors, MARS effectively suppresses spurious substructure while reaching exceptional convergence, often reaching precision limits of $\sim 0.001''$ in source-plane positions [Cha et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931..127C/abstract).

To efficiently sample the parameter space without sacrificing resolution, many non-parametric techniques employ an **adaptive grid** or a **pixelated reconstruction**. These methods dynamically allocate computational resources, providing high spatial resolution in regions with dense observational constraints—such as near critical curves or regions of high surface brightness—while maintaining a coarser grid in the unconstrained cluster outskirts [Cheng et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872..185C/abstract). New open-source packages like RELENSING utilize adaptive irregular grids to characterize cluster potential while employing smoothing functions to stabilize mass profile recovery [Torres-Ballesteros et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.4494T/abstract). Such pixel-level forward modeling is increasingly favored over direct ray-tracing, as it gracefully handles asymmetric shear and properly accounts for point spread function (PSF) convolution, allowing for the resolution of sub-kiloparsec star-forming clumps in the source plane [Johnson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...843...78J/abstract), [Sharma et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505L...1S/abstract).

Recognizing the complementary strengths of both paradigms, the field is increasingly moving toward hybrid models. These approaches anchor the global cluster potential with parametric halos while introducing flexible perturbations to capture complex core geometries. For instance, augmenting parametric models with a free-form surface of **B-splines** has been shown to improve the root-mean-square error on multiple-image positions by a factor of $3$ to $4$ compared to purely parametric approaches, while still preserving the robust global constraints required for accurate [[weak-lensing-magnification|Weak lensing magnification]] mapping [Beauchesne et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.2002B/abstract). Similarly, recent hybrid algorithms integrate analytic nodes for the brightest cluster galaxies with free-form grids to prevent the over-smoothing of dense central cores, achieving sub-arcsecond image-plane precision even in highly substructured systems [Cha et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250813262C/abstract).

## Regularization and Bayesian Inference

In strong gravitational lensing, mapping the observed image plane back to the source plane requires inverting the lens equation. As noted previously, unregularized inversions often suffer from severe overfitting, boundary artifacts, and spurious small-scale fluctuations [Cha et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931..127C/abstract). To yield physically plausible, stable solutions and overcome the limitations of early [[finite-field-inversion-formulae|Finite-field inversion formulae]] [Seitz et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996A&A...305..383S/abstract), modern source plane reconstruction relies heavily on regularized inverse methods and Bayesian inference.

The paradigm has largely shifted away from direct inversion toward Bayesian forward modeling [Brewer et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...637..608B/abstract), [Suyu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691..277S/abstract). In this framework, the lens potential and the pixelated source intensity are inferred simultaneously. By defining a posterior probability distribution, the problem is cast as the optimization of a likelihood function $\mathcal{L}$ penalized by a regularization term or prior $\mathcal{R}$. The objective function typically takes the form:
$$ \mathcal{J} = -2 \ln \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{d} | \mathbf{s}, \mathbf{p}) + \lambda \mathcal{R}(\mathbf{s}) $$
where $\mathbf{d}$ represents the image data, $\mathbf{s}$ is the source surface brightness distribution, $\mathbf{p}$ contains the lens mass parameters, and $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter controlling the relative weight of the regularization. 

The choice of $\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{s})$ dictates the physical behavior of the reconstruction. *Tikhonov regularization* is frequently employed to suppress high-frequency noise by penalizing the squared norm of the source gradients, thereby enforcing a generic smoothness constraint [Koptelova et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.11508K/abstract). However, the *Maximum Entropy Method (MEM)* has proven particularly effective for both source and free-form mass reconstructions [Seitz et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&A...337..325S/abstract), [Bridle et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.299..895B/abstract). MEM utilizes an entropic prior that naturally enforces non-negativity in the reconstructed flux or mass distributions. By regularizing the solution toward a smooth default state while retaining the flexibility to fit high-density constraints, MEM successfully captures localized substructures (such as $\sim 100$ pc star-forming clumps) and helps break the mass-sheet degeneracy when combined with [[weak-lensing-magnification|Weak lensing magnification]] or multiple-image data. Modern MEM implementations, such as the MAximum-entropy ReconStruction (MARS) algorithm, have demonstrated the ability to achieve sub-arcsecond convergence (down to $0.001''$ in source positions) without introducing the false-positive mass peaks that plague unregularized models [Cha et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931..127C/abstract), [Cha et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951..140C/abstract).

These regularization principles also extend to wide-field surveys; for example, the Dark Energy Survey Year 3 results rigorously evaluated diverse map reconstruction methods—including traditional Kaiser-Squires inversions alongside advanced techniques utilizing Null B-mode and sparsity priors—to robustly handle wide-field sky coverage and complex masking [Jeffrey et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4626J/abstract).

The efficacy of Bayesian inference combined with appropriate regularization was notably demonstrated in the unlensing of the HST observations of the Einstein ring 1RXS J1131-1231, successfully reconstructing source structures down to a resolution of $0.045^{\prime\prime}$ [Brewer et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.390...39B/abstract). Evaluating the high-dimensional posterior of a pixelated source and complex lens model often requires advanced sampling techniques, such as Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) or diffusive nested sampling [Brewer et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...637..608B/abstract), [Brewer et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412.2521B/abstract). Recently, the field has begun integrating deep learning to replace traditional analytic priors with data-driven models. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have been demonstrated to efficiently suppress edge artifacts better than traditional linear inversion methods in weak lensing mass reconstructions [Hong et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923..266H/abstract). In the realm of strong lensing, continuous neural fields are now being employed to enable the non-parametric inference of the source morphology while simultaneously inferring the foreground lens configuration without relying on hand-crafted regularization [Mishra-Sharma et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022mla..confE..34M/abstract). Furthermore, Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) and score-based models are increasingly utilized as highly expressive, non-parametric priors to facilitate the joint inference of both the source and lens mass profiles while smoothly suppressing artifacts [Karchev et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221104365K/abstract), [Barco et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251104792B/abstract).

## Synergistic Joint Reconstructions

Standalone weak lensing analyses are fundamentally limited by the mass-sheet degeneracy, an invariance transformation that leaves the reduced shear field unaltered but obscures the absolute mass normalization of the lens. Conversely, strong lensing provides exquisite, high-resolution constraints via the exact positions of multiple images and the extended morphologies of lensed arcs, but these constraints are strictly localized to the inner core of the cluster near the critical curves. A synergistic multi-probe analysis integrates these complementary datasets to produce self-consistent mass models spanning from the cluster core to its outskirts [Bradac et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph.10643B/abstract), [Bradač et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...437...39B/abstract). 

By extending weak shear formalisms into the inner cluster regions and imposing strong lensing constraints as rigid boundary conditions, the mass-sheet degeneracy can be effectively broken [AbdelSalam et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998NewAR..42..157A/abstract), [Cacciato et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...458..349C/abstract). Augmenting the shear field data with constraints from [[weak-lensing-magnification|Weak lensing magnification]] further assists in anchoring the overall radial density profile, removing one of the primary limitations in recovering absolute cluster masses [Umetsu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250504694U/abstract). Furthermore, minimal variance estimators have been proposed to isolate weak lensing magnification signals from galaxy clustering noise using their distinct flux dependencies [Yang et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.3485Y/abstract). In wide-field joint analyses, weak lensing convergence maps can also be synergistically reconstructed by linearly weighting galaxy overdensities in different magnitude bins, effectively "counting galaxies" to complement traditional shear measurements as demonstrated with DES Y3 data [Qin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241200829Q/abstract). Because real massive clusters exist in complex cosmic environments, deriving an accurate mass model often requires separating the line-of-sight weak lensing effects from the main strong lens deflector, which can be accomplished by estimating line-of-sight properties using a halo-rendering estimate based on the COSMOS field galaxies [Birrer et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JCAP...04..049B/abstract). Within the cluster itself, non-parametric maximum likelihood algorithms have been developed to treat the lensing effects of individual cluster galaxies and global cluster components simultaneously to improve mass profiles [Geiger et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.302..118G/abstract).

The inclusion of [[gravitational-flexion|Gravitational flexion]]—the second-order lensing distortion responsible for the asymmetric bending or "bowing" of background images—adds another powerful dimension to joint reconstructions. Studies have demonstrated that incorporating flexion constraints alongside strong lensing and weak shear significantly enhances the detection of small-scale substructures. To measure this delicate signal accurately, techniques such as the Higher Order Lensing Image's Characteristics (HOLICs) moment approach explicitly account for point spread function (PSF) anisotropy and the weight function of noisy shape moments [Okura et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...680....1O/abstract). The addition of robust flexion data allows for the detection of individual subhaloes down to masses of $\sim 3 \times 10^{12} M_\odot$ at angular resolutions finer than $10^{\prime\prime}$, yielding aperture mass estimates that match intrinsic distributions to within $\sim 13\%$ [Cain et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.4287C/abstract), [Leonard et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...666...51L/abstract), [Er et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1008.3088E/abstract). 

Despite its theoretical promise, the practical utility of flexion remains a subject of active debate in the literature. Because flexion signals drop off rapidly with distance and are highly sensitive to shape measurement noise, some comparative analyses conclude that flexion is intrinsically less sensitive than weak shear for reconstructing global convergence maps. Consequently, the literature explicitly cautions against relying on flexion alone for broad mass mapping, emphasizing that it must be carefully weighted in a joint inversion [Pires et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...723.1507P/abstract).

Beyond purely lensing-based observables, modern reconstructions increasingly incorporate multi-wavelength astrophysical data to build comprehensive cluster profiles. Joint analyses leveraging both free-form lensing inversions and X-ray surface brightness data (used to independently estimate the projected Newtonian potential) have been shown to improve the accuracy of central potential reconstructions by up to a factor of two [Huber et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...627A.143H/abstract). Another powerful synergy is the reconstruction of projected cluster mass distributions via the inversion of the thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect, which provides an independent thermodynamic probe of the intracluster medium when assuming hydrostatic equilibrium [Majer et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460..844M/abstract). Similarly, joint reconstruction methods also incorporate thermal gas information via X-ray and Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect data to achieve high precision in potential mapping [Konrad et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...553A.118K/abstract). To complement these static mass probes, the inclusion of cluster galaxy kinematics and projected escape velocities further refines the gravitational potential at large radii without relying strictly on hydrostatic equilibrium assumptions [Umetsu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250504694U/abstract). In these multi-probe contexts, the integration of deprojected galaxy velocity dispersions allows for the 2D projected gravitational potential to be derived from line-of-sight kinematics [Sarli et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...570A...9S/abstract). Ultimately, deriving a unique, degeneracy-free mass model through these synergistic approaches is a prerequisite for the accurate, pixel-level morphological reconstruction of high-redshift sources.

## Technical Challenges: PSF Deconvolution and Observational Noise

High-fidelity source plane reconstruction is fundamentally limited by the accuracy of point spread function (PSF) modeling and the mitigation of observational noise. Early PSF correction methods relied on directly fitting galaxy images as PSF-convolved sources to ensure systematic errors remained below the percent level [Kuijken et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999A&A...352..355K/abstract). Because the instrumental PSF smears the signal in the image plane *after* the gravitational deflection has occurred, direct inversion techniques—such as simple [[ray-shooting|Ray-shooting]] algorithms—struggle to properly execute PSF deconvolution on the highly distorted lensed images. 

To overcome this geometric mismatch, the consensus approach has shifted toward Bayesian forward-modelling [Suyu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691..277S/abstract), [Johnson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...843...78J/abstract). In this paradigm, a parametrized or pixelated source model is numerically ray-traced to the image plane, convolved with the PSF, and compared directly against the observed data. By accurately accounting for both the asymmetric lensing shear and the instrumental blurring simultaneously, adaptive pixel-grid forward-modelling techniques are able to reconstruct source morphologies with remarkable precision, successfully resolving star-forming clumps in high-redshift galaxies down to physical scales of $\sim 100 \text{ pc}$ [Sharma et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505L...1S/abstract). To further optimize fidelity in the most highly magnified regions, recent methods perform pixel-level modeling directly along "primary curves"—the directions of the eigenvectors of the Jacobian lensing matrix—allowing for the simultaneous reconstruction of the source and a highly precise local lens model near critical curves [Çağan Şengül et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241002859C/abstract).

Handling the complex, intrinsic morphologies of background galaxies alongside the anisotropy of the PSF requires sophisticated mathematical frameworks. Shapelets—a complete linear decomposition utilizing localized orthogonal basis functions—allow for the simultaneous reconstruction of arbitrary galaxy images and the correction of higher-order PSF anisotropies. This generalized framework improves the recovery of the shear field and yields higher-order shape moments that are particularly relevant to measurements of [[gravitational-flexion|Gravitational flexion]] [Massey et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IAUS..225...31M/abstract). Beyond the instrumental PSF, the foreground deflector's own luminous emission often overlaps with the lensed images. Introducing a Multi-Gaussian Expansion (MGE) approach serves as an efficient method for modeling complex lens light profiles and significantly reducing residual contamination in the subsequent source reconstructions [He et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.2441H/abstract). More recently, methods such as the Bayesian Fourier Domain (BFD) formalism bypass the assignment of explicit shapes entirely. Instead, BFD compresses uncrowded pixel data into a vector of moments to analytically estimate the probability of the lensing distortion, achieving a fractional shear recovery accuracy of $m = (2.1 \pm 0.4) \times 10^{-3}$ on background-limited images [Bernstein et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.4467B/abstract).

Observational noise dictates the overall signal-to-noise ratio of the reconstruction, posing severe challenges for unconstrained or free-form inversions. Low signal-to-noise ratio data frequently leads to overfitting, which manifests as spurious small-scale substructures in the resulting mass and source maps. To stabilize the inversion, maximum-entropy and Tikhonov regularization schemes are essential; these priors suppress noise-induced artifacts while remaining flexible enough to recover physically plausible, quasi-unique source distributions [Seitz et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&A...337..325S/abstract), [Cha et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931..127C/abstract). When comprehensive source deconvolution remains computationally prohibitive or excessively noisy, astronomers can extract robust metrics directly from the data. For instance, computing the Gini coefficient in the image plane serves as a valuable, lens-model-independent morphological diagnostic, since surface brightness is conserved during the lensing process [Florian et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832..168F/abstract).

Even with robust regularization, high-frequency PSF interpolation errors and spatially correlated residual PSF fluctuations propagate directly into systematic shear biases [Lu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...858..122L/abstract). While traditional single-exposure methods often fail to recover these high-frequency spatial features, recent hierarchical, multi-layer PSF reconstructions that stack model residuals across multiple exposures have proven successful at identifying and removing stable instrumental patterns [Alonso et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240415795A/abstract). Furthermore, next-generation surveys relying on dithered exposures face stringent astrometric requirements to prevent noise aliasing. To achieve accurate results when combining undersampled images, relying on highly pessimistic astrometric registration uncertainties of $\sim 10^{-3}$ pixels yields problematic artificial shear errors on the order of $\mathcal{O}(10^{-4})$ [Shapiro et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PASP..125.1496S/abstract).

## High-Resolution Morphological Analysis of High-z Galaxies

Strong gravitational lensing acts as a cosmic telescope, magnifying background galaxies and allowing morphological analyses at spatial resolutions far exceeding the diffraction limits of current observatories. Through careful source plane reconstruction, the intrinsic properties of high-redshift ($z \sim 1-3$) galaxies can be mapped on sub-kiloparsec scales, providing direct insight into the peak epoch of cosmic star formation.

Historically, source reconstruction relied on direct [[ray-shooting|Ray-shooting]], which simply maps observed image pixels back to the source plane using the lens equation. However, modern approaches strongly favor pixel-level Bayesian forward modeling. By defining an adaptive grid or Delaunay tessellation in the source plane and forward-lensing it through the deflector's potential, these methods explicitly account for the instrumental point spread function (PSF) and the highly asymmetric lensing shear [Johnson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...843...78J/abstract), [Sharma et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505L...1S/abstract). This mitigates the geometric distortions inherent to direct inversion and yields signal-to-noise ratio improvements of up to a factor of $10$ for small-scale structures [Sharma et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505L...1S/abstract). To further optimize these reconstructions for extreme magnifications, recent algorithms like SourceREACH employ K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) Regression to finely tune noise smoothing and resolution when modeling giant arcs behind galaxy cluster halos [Eid et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251111952E/abstract).

This enhanced resolution is transformative for studying star-forming clumps in the early Universe. Reconstructions routinely resolve structures down to $30-100 \text{ pc}$ at $z \sim 2$ [Sharma et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505L...1S/abstract), [Sharon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941..203S/abstract). For instance, forward modeling of the lensed galaxy SDSS J1110+6459 at $z=2.481$ successfully resolved over two dozen individual star-forming clumps [Johnson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...843...78J/abstract). In extreme cases such as the "Sunburst Arc" (PSZ1 G311.65-18.48 at $z=2.3703$), the unique [[arc-geometry|Arc geometry]] provides magnifications up to $\mu \sim 80$. Source plane analysis of this system has placed strict upper limits of $r \lesssim 32 \text{ pc}$ on the physical size of its Lyman continuum leaking clumps [Sharon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941..203S/abstract). Similarly, the extraordinarily bright lensed galaxy RCSGA 032727-132609 has served as a landmark case study, enabling astronomers to peer into 100-pc scale star-forming regions within a high-redshift giant arc and map its intense star formation history [Sharon et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...746..161S/abstract).

Beyond static galaxy morphology, spatially resolved reconstructions of integral field spectroscopy data enable detailed kinematic studies. By applying adaptive coadded source plane reconstruction to the lensed galaxy SDSS1958+5950 at $z=2.225$, researchers decoupled complex velocity gradients at a spatial resolution of $\sim 170 \text{ pc}$, identifying the system as an ongoing merger rather than a single rotating disk [Sharma et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.1427S/abstract).

Reconstructing the source plane is equally critical for determining global parameters such as intrinsic luminosity and size. Using adaptive pixel schemes to invert interferometric sub-millimeter observations, researchers have found magnification factors of $3-10$ for high-$z$ galaxies, which are systematically lower than those derived from simple single-Sérsic profile fits [Enia et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.3467E/abstract). Correcting for this magnification yields intrinsic star formation rates of $900-3500\ M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$ and median effective radii $r_{\text{eff}} \sim 1.77 \text{ kpc}$, placing these galaxies well below the Eddington limit for radiation pressure regulated starbursts [Enia et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.3467E/abstract).

Despite these successes, derived intrinsic properties remain sensitive to the specific mass model of the lens. While the median size-mass relation $R_{\text{eff}} \propto (1+z)^{-1.05 \pm 0.37}$ at $z=1-3$ is statistically robust, differences between public lens models can introduce a $\sim 25\%$ scatter in the size measurements of individual galaxies [Yang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.1028Y/abstract). Furthermore, there is an ongoing debate regarding the required complexity of the source model. If the adopted source parameterization lacks sufficient degrees of freedom (e.g., utilizing analytic shapelets instead of pixel-based Delaunay triangulations), unmodeled structural complexities in the source galaxy can be erroneously mapped into the lens potential. This source-lens degeneracy has been shown to produce convincing but spurious detections of dark matter subhaloes in the lens plane [Ephremidze et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250218571E/abstract), highlighting the necessity of highly flexible source grids in precision morphological analysis.

## Advances in Machine Learning and Neural Fields

The transition from direct matrix inversion and manually crafted regularization (such as maximum entropy) to Bayesian forward modeling has led to the development of sophisticated automated tools. Software frameworks like AutoLens have emerged as a key solution for the automated Bayesian modeling of lens mass and pixel-grid source reconstruction, significantly streamlining the inference pipeline [Nightingale et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.4738N/abstract). This transition has recently culminated in the integration of deep learning techniques. By leveraging data-driven priors, modern neural network architectures can circumvent many of the traditional challenges of source plane reconstruction, such as noise overfitting and edge artifacts.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have been successfully applied to map both weak and strong lensing observables. For instance, multilayered CNNs utilizing alternating convolution and trans-convolution filters significantly outperform traditional linear inversions by efficiently suppressing artifacts near field edges [Hong et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923..266H/abstract). Notably, CNN-based reconstructions have been shown to lift the mass-sheet degeneracy in a model-independent manner when applied to wide-field cluster mass estimation [Hong et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923..266H/abstract). Furthermore, deep learning optimization techniques are now utilized to accelerate convergence in highly parameterized free-form mass models, allowing algorithms to stabilize and solve for $\sim 300,000$ free parameters in merely a few hours [Cha et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..186C/abstract). In the context of complex multi-component halos, machine learning further facilitates targeted Likelihood-Free Inference (LFI) and variational inference, allowing researchers to accurately marginalize over stochastic lens components such as unresolved dark matter substructure [Coogan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201007032C/abstract).

In the domain of highly resolved strong lensing, continuous neural fields have emerged as a powerful framework. By replacing discrete pixel grids or shapelet-based representations of the source, neural fields facilitate non-parametric source reconstruction while simultaneously inferring a distribution over the foreground lens galaxy configurations [Mishra-Sharma et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022mla..confE..34M/abstract). This continuous, coordinate-based representation naturally accommodates the complex morphology and [[arc-geometry|Arc geometry]] of high-redshift sources without the geometric distortion limitations typical of traditional [[ray-shooting|Ray-shooting]] algorithms.

Perhaps the most profound recent shift is the deployment of generative models to regularize the highly ill-posed inverse problem. Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) and score-based models are now utilized to impose realistic, data-driven morphological priors on the unlensed source galaxies. Chains of conditional reconstruction steps, such as Denoising Diffusion Restoration Models (DDRMs), iteratively reconstruct the source, yielding samples that are consistent with both the noisy observation and the learned prior distribution of unlensed galaxy images [Karchev et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221104365K/abstract). Extending this concept, continuous-time score-based models enable the joint "blind" inference of both the source light distribution and the parametric lens mass profile without a strictly known forward operator, successfully recovering marginal posteriors free of systematic bias [Barco et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251104792B/abstract).

Despite these rapid advancements, there is an ongoing tension in the literature regarding the interpretability and safety of data-driven priors compared to physically motivated priors. While samples drawn from diffusion models demonstrate strong qualitative consistency with traditional posterior distributions in low-to-medium noise scenarios [Karchev et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221104365K/abstract), the extent to which the neural network's training set might artificially induce or suppress the appearance of physical substructures remains an active point of caution. Consequently, validating the outputs of these highly non-linear models against traditional methods is strictly necessary when assessing small-scale dark matter perturbations.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite significant advances in source plane reconstruction, several fundamental questions remain regarding the degeneracies inherent in gravitational lens inversion. A primary challenge is disentangling the smooth, large-scale cluster mass distribution from individual galaxy-scale perturbers. Parametric mass models frequently suffer from a "peaky" versus "cored" degeneracy, where substantially different mass profiles yield nearly identical image plane rms errors [Limousin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...588A..99L/abstract). Conversely, free-form reconstructions avoid rigid profile assumptions but are susceptible to overfitting, often generating spurious small-scale mass fluctuations if not properly regularized [Cha et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931..127C/abstract). 

A critical open debate is whether certain observed small-scale anomalies represent true dark matter substructure or are merely systematic artifacts of the source reconstruction process. The literature demonstrates that inadequate source complexity—such as relying on simple shapelet basis functions—can produce convincing but spurious detections of $\sim 10^{10} M_\odot$ subhalos. This tension is currently being addressed by shifting toward pixel-based or Delaunay triangulation source modeling, which significantly alleviates these systematics [Ephremidze et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250218571E/abstract). To enhance detection robustness, recent methodologies have demonstrated that simultaneously reconstructing extended lensed arcs alongside multiple-image flux ratios significantly "turbocharges" the model's sensitivity to low-mass dark matter haloes [Gilman et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.1687G/abstract). Furthermore, while incorporating [[gravitational-flexion|Gravitational flexion]] theoretically improves sensitivity to subhalos down to $3 \times 10^{12} M_\odot$ [Cain et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.4287C/abstract), its practical utility remains contested due to high sensitivity to shape-measurement noise [Pires et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...723.1507P/abstract).

To avoid global lens-model biases when studying high-redshift galaxy evolution, there is a growing movement toward model-independent characterization. These approaches seek to extract local lens properties (such as the magnification and shear matrices) directly from the observables of [[arc-geometry|Arc geometry]] [Wagner et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Univ....5..177W/abstract). Advances in automated feature detection allow for the matching of individual star-forming clumps across multiple images, constraining local derivatives of the potential empirically and independently of any assumed global mass profile [Lin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.1821L/abstract).

As the field prepares for the massive data volumes of next-generation surveys like Euclid and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST), scalability and the mitigation of image-level systematics are paramount. Reconstructing the point spread function (PSF) and correcting for its chromatic dependence on the source spectral energy distribution (SED) represent the dominant limiting factors for precision [[weak-lensing-magnification|Weak lensing magnification]] and shear mapping [Lu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...858..122L/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260110795E/abstract). To handle the computational bottleneck of forward-modeling thousands of lenses while breaking the mass-sheet degeneracy, researchers are increasingly turning to deep learning. Continuous neural fields and score-based denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) are being deployed to jointly infer source morphologies and lens mass profiles. These algorithms act as highly expressive, data-driven priors that replace traditional analytic regularization and allow for the joint inference of the source and the physical forward operator (the lens mass profile) [Karchev et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221104365K/abstract), [Mishra-Sharma et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022mla..confE..34M/abstract), [Barco et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251104792B/abstract).

Finally, the JWST era is transforming source plane reconstruction by providing unprecedented resolution and depth, increasing the number of multiply imaged constraints per cluster by up to an order of magnitude [Ghosh et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494.3998G/abstract), [Cha et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..186C/abstract). Maximizing the utility of this data requires algorithms capable of resolving the substructure debate through multi-band analysis [Ephremidze et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250218571E/abstract). Future efforts will likely rely on hybrid inversion frameworks, such as MrMARTIAN, which embed physically motivated analytic nodes within free-form adaptive grids. These methods promise to suppress spurious fluctuations while retaining the flexibility required to map the complex, unrelaxed cores of massive galaxy clusters [Cha et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951..140C/abstract), [Cha et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250813262C/abstract).
