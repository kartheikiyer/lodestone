In modern astrophysics, large-scale surveys produce data of unprecedented volume and complexity, encompassing multi-wavelength galaxy spectra, massive photometric catalogs, and high-resolution pixel-level imagery. Analyzing these datasets in their native observational spaces exposes algorithms to the "curse of dimensionality," where distance metrics become uninformative and traditional statistical modeling becomes intractable. **Low-dimensional representations** mitigate this challenge by compressing high-dimensional data into a reduced, compact set of latent variables—often referred to as a latent space. This process of [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]] and [[feature-extraction|Feature extraction]] is designed to discard observational noise and redundant information while preserving fundamental physical and morphological properties. By mapping complex observations into just a few latent dimensions, astronomers can efficiently track stellar population evolution, automate redshift estimation, and perform robust [[objective-classification|Objective classification]] on millions of sources.

Historically, the construction of these representations relied heavily on linear projections, most notably [[principal-component-analysis-in-astronomy|Principal Component Analysis in astronomy]] (PCA). Early applications of PCA demonstrated that the vast majority of spectral and morphological variance in galaxies could be captured by just a few orthogonal components, replacing ad hoc empirical templates with continuous, data-driven bases [Sodre et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..114..149S/abstract) [Glazebrook et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...492...98G/abstract) [Lahav et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001misk.conf...33L/abstract). These derived components routinely correlate directly with underlying physics; for example, the first principal component typically traces stellar mass and mean age, while mathematical basis functions like shapelets provide compact parameterizations of traditional Hubble types [Kelly et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127..625K/abstract) [Massey et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.363..197M/abstract). However, because astronomical data frequently exhibit complex, non-Euclidean topologies, modern [[multivariate-analysis-in-astronomy|Multivariate analysis in astronomy]] increasingly utilizes nonlinear manifold learning and deep self-supervised autoencoders. These advanced techniques reveal that the structural and spectral diversity of galaxies is fundamentally continuous, mapping onto a low-dimensional [[galaxy-manifold|Galaxy Manifold]] governed by a minimal set of evolutionary parameters like star formation history and gas accretion.

Today, mapping data into low-dimensional latent spaces is the cornerstone of automated discovery in massive surveys. It enables rapid similarity searches and unsupervised anomaly detection, allowing astronomers to isolate rare phenomena—such as strong gravitational lenses or unusual galaxy mergers—from datasets containing billions of normal objects. Furthermore, recent advances in self-supervised "foundation models" have facilitated cross-modal mapping, embedding disparate data types like imaging and spectroscopy into unified representational spaces. Despite these successes, critical open questions remain. The field actively debates the true intrinsic dimensionality of diverse astronomical datasets and grapples with the tension between the mechanistic interpretability of linear models and the opaque, yet highly accurate, nature of deep neural networks. Looking forward, researchers are exploring the "Platonic Representation Hypothesis": the tantalizing possibility that increasingly large, independently trained models across different observational domains are naturally converging upon a single, universal mathematical representation of astrophysical reality.

## Introduction to Astronomical Data Compression

Modern astronomical surveys produce datasets of unprecedented size and complexity, spanning high-resolution multi-band imaging, massive spectroscopic catalogs, and integral field data cubes. Analyzing these datasets directly in their native observation space—such as raw pixel grids or continuous spectral flux bins—exposes algorithms to the "curse of dimensionality." In high-dimensional spaces, data volume grows exponentially, causing distance metrics to become sparse and traditional statistical modeling to become intractable [Takeuchi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271...44T/abstract). Even basic categorization tasks can be challenging in these regimes; for example, separating stars and galaxies in high-dimensional [[multidimensional-color-space|multicolor infrared spaces]] typically requires robust supervised classifiers like [[vector-machine-methods|Vector machine methods]] (SVM) [Solarz et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...541A..50S/abstract). Fortunately, the macroscopic properties of galaxies and stellar populations are typically governed by a small number of underlying physical processes, such as mass assembly, star formation history, and metallicity evolution. 

The fundamental objective of astronomical data compression is to map an input observation $x \in \mathbb{R}^D$ (where $D$ may represent thousands of dimensions) into a compact vector of latent variables $z \in \mathbb{R}^d$, such that $d \ll D$. This process of [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]] discards observational noise while preserving the essential physical and morphological information required for subsequent analysis.

Historically, [[principal-component-analysis-in-astronomy|Principal Component Analysis in astronomy]] (PCA) has been the foundational linear technique for this reduction, particularly for spectroscopic data. By constructing an orthogonal basis that maximizes variance, PCA effectively condenses highly correlated spectra into just two or three dominant components. The first principal component (PC1) routinely accounts for 85% to over 99% of the total spectral or global parameter variance and acts as a strong proxy for stellar mass or age [Ferreras et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph.11456F/abstract) [Chang et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1009.0030C/abstract). The second component (PC2) typically captures signatures of recent star formation or gas fraction. These linear projections have proven highly effective for automated redshift determination via orthogonal templates [Glazebrook et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...492...98G/abstract) and large-scale spectral classification [Madgwick et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10220M/abstract) [Marchetti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.1424M/abstract).

Similar dimensionality reduction principles apply to the spatial domain. The morphological complexity of 2D galaxy images can be efficiently parameterized using a small set of linear basis functions, such as Fourier series or polar shapelets, which can capture up to 90% of a galaxy's structural variance using as few as three to nine components [Odewahn et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...568..539O/abstract) [Massey et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.363..197M/abstract). More recently, [[feature-extraction|Feature extraction]] has shifted toward deep generative models. For instance, modern diffusion autoencoders can compress multi-band galaxy images into a highly compact semantic representation of just five latent dimensions, yielding strong correlations with human-derived morphological labels and physical parameters [Gagliano et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251204145G/abstract).

While global linear projections like PCA excel in interpretability, their reliance on Euclidean distance metrics can obscure subtle non-linear physical trends [Lawlor et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833...26L/abstract). Consequently, modern analysis increasingly utilizes *manifold learning* algorithms such as Locally Linear Embedding (LLE) and t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) to uncover complex data topologies [Reis et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3400437R/abstract) [Semenov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241209358S/abstract). These techniques map objects onto a continuous [[galaxy-manifold|Galaxy Manifold]]; notably, a 2D manifold representation has been shown to capture 93.2% of the variance in the [[multidimensional-flux-space|multidimensional luminosity space]] of local galaxies, cleanly separating distinct evolutionary modes such as steady star formation and rapid gas accretion [Cooray et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.4976C/abstract).

While the necessity of low-dimensional representations is universally accepted, tensions remain regarding the optimal approach. Global linear projections excel in interpretability and direct physical correlation, whereas non-linear manifold techniques and deep autoencoders often achieve superior compactness for [[objective-classification|Objective classification]], albeit by sacrificing immediate physical transparency. Furthermore, defining the true intrinsic dimensionality of complex survey datasets remains an active area of investigation, with emerging evidence suggesting that the dimensionality of certain astrophysical data (e.g., radio morphologies) may exceed that of natural terrestrial images [Font-Quer Roset et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251111490F/abstract).

## Historical Evolution of Spectral and Morphological Classification

Historically, the categorization of galaxies relied heavily on subjective visual inspection, most notably through the manual placement of objects along the discrete Hubble sequence. Early attempts to objectify astronomical catalogs began with broad whole-sky statistical representations based on apparent brightness and spatial distributions [Allen et al. (1954)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1954MNRAS.114..387A/abstract). This objective approach was further advanced by the application of standardized surface photometry techniques to legacy catalogs, such as the Revised Shapley-Ames and Uppsala catalogs, to produce comprehensive, large-scale structural profile datasets [Schombert et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PASA...29..174S/abstract). However, as dataset sizes expanded, the community recognized the necessity of purely automated [[objective-classification|Objective classification]]. The shift toward automated morphological classification was pioneered by decomposing two-dimensional light distributions into orthogonal mathematical basis functions. For instance, Fourier series fits to azimuthal profiles revealed that up to $90\%$ of the variance in galaxy morphology could be captured by just three principal components. This data compression enabled early artificial neural networks to predict human-assigned Hubble types with a $1\sigma$ scatter of only $1.5$ steps on the 16-step revised Hubble scale [Odewahn et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...568..539O/abstract). Shortly thereafter, "shapelets" were introduced to provide a set of mathematically convenient, orthogonal basis functions (such as polar shapelets) that cleanly separated galaxies of differing Hubble types into compact parameter spaces [Kelly et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127..625K/abstract) [Massey et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.363..197M/abstract). Concurrently, analytical software progressed to compute massive arrays of statistical descriptors; for example, the WND-CHARM algorithm was introduced as a multi-purpose image classifier that computes large sets of image features (up to 2700) to quantitatively analyze morphology and similarity in complex visual objects like galaxy mergers [Shamir et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft12002S/abstract).

In tandem with morphological advances, the analysis of galaxy spectra underwent a quantitative revolution. The application of [[principal-component-analysis-in-astronomy|Principal Component Analysis in astronomy]] enabled the projection of high-dimensional spectroscopic data into a low-dimensional space. Early PCA studies revealed that normal galaxies form a continuous "spectral sequence" in the plane defined by the first two principal components. Crucially, this spectral sequence was found to be closely related to the morphological Hubble sequence, suggesting an evolutionary track where galaxies evolve from gas-rich irregulars into spheroidal systems as their star formation histories progress [Sodre et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..114..149S/abstract). This objective framing replaced ad hoc empirical templates with linear combinations of orthogonal eigenspectra, vastly improving automatic redshift determination and noise reduction [Glazebrook et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...492...98G/abstract).

The utility of large-scale representations was pioneered by foundational CCD-based efforts such as the Las Campanas Redshift Survey, whose 2D representations of redshift distributions were instrumental in mapping large-scale structures like walls and voids [Shectman et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...470..172S/abstract). This utility was subsequently firmly cemented by the advent of massive multiplexed surveys like the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey (2dFGRS) and the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS). In the 2dFGRS, PCA was utilized to distill over 200,000 spectra into a single continuous spectral type parameter, $\eta$, which robustly categorized the galaxy population without human intervention [Madgwick et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10220M/abstract). Extensive analyses of large datasets later confirmed that the spectral and global properties of galaxies are highly correlated and can often be reduced to 2-3 latent dimensions. For instance, the first principal component (PC1) alone frequently accounts for over $99\%$ of the spectral variance in early-type samples, while the second component (PC2) frequently correlates with young stellar populations and near-ultraviolet (NUV) flux [Ferreras et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph.11456F/abstract). Furthermore, applying PCA to global physical parameters from SDSS data revealed that PC1 effectively differentiates galaxies based on whether stellar age is a dominating parameter—which it is for blue galaxies, but notably unimportant for red, early-type galaxies [Li et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJS..207....8L/abstract).

Despite the historical success of global linear projections in defining these sequences, modern analyses increasingly rely on locally biased graph techniques and non-linear manifold learning to better map the intrinsic topologies of galaxy populations.

## Linear Representations and PCA Tomography

The foundation of [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]] rests heavily on linear projection methods, most notably [[principal-component-analysis-in-astronomy|Principal Component Analysis in astronomy]] (PCA). In spectral analysis, PCA decomposes a high-dimensional galaxy spectrum $f(\lambda)$ into a mean spectrum $\mu(\lambda)$ and a linear combination of orthogonal eigenvectors $e_i(\lambda)$ (often termed eigenspectra or eigentemplates):

$$ f(\lambda) \approx \mu(\lambda) + \sum_{i=1}^{k} c_i e_i(\lambda) $$

where $c_i$ are the eigencoefficients and $k$ is the truncated dimensionality. This approach excels at [[feature-extraction|Feature extraction]] because the physical variance of galaxy populations is highly compressible. For instance, in early-type galaxies, the first principal component (PC1) alone can capture over 99% of the spectral variance, typically tracing the old stellar population (age and mass), while PC2 correlates strongly with near-ultraviolet flux and recent star formation [Ferreras et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph.11456F/abstract).

This compression is critical for automated redshift determination and [[objective-classification|Objective classification]] in massive surveys. Replacing discrete empirical templates with a continuous, simultaneous linear combination of orthogonal PCA templates eliminates template mismatch and improves error estimation [Glazebrook et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...492...98G/abstract). In the VIMOS Public Extragalactic Redshift Survey (VIPERS), PCA was used to successfully classify over 27,000 spectra and repair noise gaps, distilling the primary variance into just two physical eigencoefficients related to age and star formation rate [Marchetti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.1424M/abstract).

Beyond 1D spectra, linear representations have been extended to 3D integral field unit (IFU) data cubes through "PCA Tomography." This technique projects spatial-spectral data onto uncorrelated coordinates, producing 2D spatial images known as tomograms that correspond one-to-one with the spectral eigenvectors [Steiner et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.395...64S/abstract). Because the eigenvectors are mutually orthogonal, PCA tomography effectively disentangles uncorrelated physical phenomena. For example, applying this method to the LINER galaxy NGC 4736 isolated the signature of a previously hidden type 1 active galactic nucleus that was spatially displaced from the stellar bulge [Steiner et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.395...64S/abstract) [Steiner et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..267...85S/abstract). PCA has similarly proven valuable for analyzing 3D data cubes in the interstellar medium, specifically using it to identify line profile differences in CO maps to characterize turbulent velocity field energy spectra in molecular regions [Brunt et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...566..289B/abstract).

The utility of PCA naturally extends to diverse global and kinematic parameters. Applying PCA to the rotation curves of low surface brightness galaxies revealed that just two components can describe the sample, highlighting that the gas fraction acts as a fundamental variable linked to the inner mass profile [Alard et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...728L..47A/abstract). In studies of massive structures, PCA has been utilized to characterize shared trends and disentangle variations in the correlated scatter of cluster mass measurements across multiwavelength observations [Noh et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.1829N/abstract).

Recent algorithmic advances have expanded upon standard PCA by utilizing **dictionary learning** and **sparse representations**. While PCA enforces strict global orthogonality, dictionary learning constructs an overcomplete basis of data-driven templates. By enforcing sparsity—meaning each spectrum is reconstructed using only a small number of localized dictionary elements—this approach captures specific physical emission or absorption features better than global eigenvectors. Sparse representations have demonstrated superior accuracy and reliability over classical eigentemplates for automated spectroscopic and photometric redshift estimation, particularly in low signal-to-noise regimes [Frontera-Pons et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...625A..73F/abstract) [Frontera-Pons et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&C....4400735F/abstract). Sparse representations beyond standard dictionary learning, specifically employing wavelets and curvelets, have demonstrated exceptional utility for detecting anisotropic features like cosmic strings [Starck et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0903.3383S/abstract). Similar sparse component separation techniques have also been deployed to deconvolve and map the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect in galaxy cluster outskirts [Baldi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020EPJWC.22800004B/abstract). Additionally, variations such as Independent Component Analysis (ICA) have been utilized to disentangle prototypical spectra of different evolutionary histories in elliptical galaxies without the need for physical priors [Kaban et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005dami.conf..183K/abstract).

Despite its ubiquity, standard PCA has recognized limitations. Its foundational assumption that global Euclidean distance is a valid measure of spectral similarity can obscure subtle, non-linear trends. Consequently, locally biased or graph-based techniques are frequently required to construct a more physically reliable topology for modeling complex galaxy populations [Lawlor et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833...26L/abstract).

## Nonlinear Manifold Learning and Embedding Techniques

To address the limitations of linear methods, which rely on global Euclidean distance, nonlinear manifold learning algorithms assume that high-dimensional data lie on or near a continuous, lower-dimensional manifold. For instance, complex astronomical data—spanning high-resolution spectra, time-series, and multidimensional imaging—reside on non-Euclidean topological structures like the 2D [[galaxy-manifold|Galaxy Manifold]], which has been shown to capture $\sim 93.2\%$ of the variance in local galaxy luminosity space [Cooray et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.4976C/abstract). 

Algorithms such as Isomap and Locally Linear Embedding (LLE) unfold these manifolds by preserving local geometries. The potential of these nonlinear dimensionality reduction techniques in computational astrostatistics was noted early on, with introductory reviews suggesting LLE as a promising, efficient tool for the spectral classification of large data sources like the SDSS [Nichol et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10230N/abstract). LLE works by expressing each data point as a linear combination of its nearest neighbors and reconstructing this neighborhood structure in a lower-dimensional space. In the context of [[objective-classification|Objective classification]], applying LLE to galaxy images successfully projects complex morphological features into interpretable 3D representations. When paired with classical classifiers, this approach yields accuracies on par with simple neural networks; however, subsequent analysis indicates that unsupervised clustering metrics generally do not support a distinct clustering structure matching human-defined Hubble types [Semenov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&C....5200963S/abstract). Similarly, locally-biased spectral graph techniques bypass the global Euclidean assumption to extract subtle, localized physical trends in large spectroscopic samples that standard PCA misses [Lawlor et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833...26L/abstract).

For visual data exploration and clustering, t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) are heavily utilized. These algorithms construct probability distributions over pairs of high-dimensional objects based on their proximity, then iteratively minimize the divergence (e.g., Kullback-Leibler divergence) against a mapped low-dimensional distribution. In astrophysics, applying t-SNE to deep network activations following [[feature-extraction|Feature extraction]] is highly effective for visualizing abstract representations, naturally clustering morphologically similar galaxies, and instantly revealing mislabeled objects [Dai et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180705657D/abstract). For massive spectral classification tasks, the development of single-pass k-means has provided a fast alternative clustering method for large datasets like SDSS-DR7 [Ordovás-Pascual et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...565A..53O/abstract). Beyond morphology, t-SNE has proven to be a robust diagnostic tool for isolating "catastrophic errors" in multi-wavelength property catalogs by spatially separating galaxies with anomalously inferred physical parameters from the main manifold [Hovis-Afflerbach et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908..148H/abstract).

Despite the power of non-linear embeddings for data exploration, the literature reflects a clear tension regarding their absolute superiority over linear [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]] methods. While algorithms like UMAP and t-SNE excel at revealing local substructures and separating discrete classes, they do not preserve global distances or densities. Consequently, for strictly quantitative applications, such as the automated identification of broad absorption line quasars (BALQSOs), standard PCA can still outperform LLE, t-SNE, and Isomap when used as a precursor to robust classifiers like XGBoost [Kao et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PASJ...76..653K/abstract). Furthermore, different manifold learning algorithms—or even identical algorithms initialized with different hyperparameters—can produce significantly different 2D or 3D projections of the same high-dimensional dataset. Therefore, researchers caution that these low-dimensional spatial mappings should be treated as probabilistic samples of the data's underlying structure, rather than a definitive physical truth [Reis et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3400437R/abstract).

## The Galaxy Manifold and Physical Parameter Mapping

The sheer diversity of galaxy morphologies, spectra, and photometric profiles can often be compressed into a remarkably low-dimensional subspace. This concept, formalised as the [[galaxy-manifold|Galaxy Manifold]], posits that the vast majority of macroscopic galaxy variance is governed by a minimal set of fundamental physical parameters [Chang et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1009.0030C/abstract) [Cooray et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.4976C/abstract). Utilizing techniques from [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]], researchers have mapped high-dimensional observational datasets—such as [[multidimensional-flux-space|multidimensional luminosity space]] spanning far-ultraviolet to near-infrared bands—onto continuous low-dimensional surfaces. For local galaxies ($z < 0.1$), it has been shown that a 2D manifold captures $93.2\%$ of the variance in the observed population [Cooray et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.4976C/abstract).

The coordinates of this manifold correlate strongly with fundamental physical quantities. By calibrating against a galaxy's location on the 2D manifold, physical properties can be estimated with minimal scatter, achieving precisions of $0.04$ dex for stellar mass and $0.12$ dex for star formation rate (SFR) [Cooray et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.4976C/abstract). These results align with a broad consensus across [[multivariate-analysis-in-astronomy|Multivariate analysis in astronomy]], particularly studies using [[principal-component-analysis-in-astronomy|Principal Component Analysis in astronomy]] to evaluate global galactic parameters (such as half-light radii, luminosities, and gas masses). Such analyses reveal that the first principal component (PC1) can explain up to $86\%$ of global parameter variation, acting as a proxy for mass or scale, while the second component typically isolates color [Chang et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1009.0030C/abstract). 

This capability for dense physical extraction is highly effective in modern data regimes. For instance, in the GAMMA (Galactic Attributes of Mass, Metallicity, and Age) dataset, PCA has been deployed to achieve extreme dimensionality reduction (by factors of 200-3600) for both 2D maps and 3D data cubes while accurately reconstructing underlying physical parameters like stellar age and metallicity [Çakır et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231206016C/abstract). Spectral datasets reflect similar mappings. In the VIPERS survey ($0.4 < z < 1.0$), galaxy spectra are effectively condensed into two primary coefficients that map directly to stellar age and SFR [Marchetti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.1424M/abstract). Quantitative empirical fits derived from these reduced spaces further link observables to physical states. For example, the relationship for red SDSS galaxies linking stellar mass $M_*$ to the absolute $r$-band magnitude $M_r$ can be expressed as:
$$ \log(M_*) = 4.31 - 0.30 M_r $$
while blue galaxies exhibit a tight correlation between age $t$ and $(g - r)$ color [Li et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJS..207....8L/abstract).

The [[galaxy-manifold|Galaxy Manifold]] is not static; rather, it provides a geometric framework for tracking galaxy evolution. Evolutionary tracks parametrised by different star formation histories map onto distinct trajectories within the manifold space. Models of constant star formation and exponentially decreasing star formation form nearly orthogonal modes of evolution on the 2D manifold, a geometric divergence that is fundamentally tied to a galaxy's gas accretion history and gas content [Cooray et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.4976C/abstract). Similarly, mapping techniques such as PCA combined with an unsupervised fuzzy partition (UFP) method have been applied to zCOSMOS data to characterize galaxy downsizing and trace transition epochs for different mass populations across cosmic time [Coppa et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...535A..10C/abstract).

Despite the success of mapping galaxies to a continuous low-dimensional surface, the literature reflects an active debate regarding the topological nature of galaxy diversity. While earlier preprints suggested that unsupervised clustering approaches could smoothly recover traditional, discrete morphological classes [Semenov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241209358S/abstract), subsequent peer-reviewed evaluations revealed that robust metrics do not support distinct clustering [Semenov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&C....5200963S/abstract). Consequently, researchers argue that forcing galaxies into discrete bins results in a significant loss of information, advocating instead that the galaxy population is fundamentally a continuous manifold [Vinci et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..306...68V/abstract) [Cooray et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.4976C/abstract). Furthermore, to robustly capture subtle evolutionary trends and prevent non-physical selection effects, researchers are increasingly moving beyond global linear projections to adopt locally biased or non-linear graph techniques [Lawlor et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833...26L/abstract) [Reis et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3400437R/abstract).

## Deep Latent Representations and Self-Supervised Learning

The astronomical "Big Data" era has motivated a paradigm shift from manually curated training sets for [[objective-classification|Objective classification]] to self-supervised learning (SSL). By training on massive, unlabeled sky surveys, SSL architectures learn to distill the underlying physical structure of the data into semantically meaningful low-dimensional representations. Once learned, these foundation models can be fine-tuned for a multitude of downstream tasks—such as morphology classification or photometric redshift estimation—often achieving state-of-the-art supervised accuracy with $2$ to $4$ times fewer labeled examples [Hayat et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...911L..33H/abstract).

Contrastive learning frameworks explicitly optimize latent spaces so that augmented views of the same object cluster together while separating from distinct objects. This approach has proven highly effective for [[feature-extraction|Feature extraction]] in large photometric surveys. For instance, contrastive representations applied to the DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys have enabled rapid similarity searches across tens of millions of galaxies, successfully isolating rare anomalies and strong gravitational lenses from single labeled examples [Stein et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv211013151S/abstract) [Stein et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932..107S/abstract). Similar techniques applied to integral field unit (IFU) data from the CALIFA survey have yielded embedding spaces where galaxies separate cleanly according to physical properties like the $H\alpha$ equivalent width and stellar mass, without prior labeling [Martínez-Solaeche et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...688A.160M/abstract). Applying self-supervised machine learning to the MaNGA survey has similarly ordered galaxies in a 2D plane by their resolved physical properties, such as stellar kinematics, allowing researchers to successfully identify distinct assembly channels for disks and slow rotators [Sarmiento et al. ## Anomaly Detection and Discovery in Latent Spaces

The exponential growth of data from modern sky surveys makes visual inspection computationally intractable, burying rare and scientifically valuable objects within millions of normal galaxies. By compressing images or spectra into low-dimensional latent spaces—via [[feature-extraction|Feature extraction]] techniques ranging from linear projections to deep self-supervised foundation models—astronomers can efficiently isolate outliers and perform rapid similarity searches.

**Similarity Search**
In a well-regularized low-dimensional representation of dimension $d$, the similarity between a query object $\mathbf{x}$ and a dataset object $\mathbf{y}$ is typically quantified via a metric such as the Euclidean distance:
$$D(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d} (x_i - y_i)^2}$$ 
Because these latent spaces are optimized to capture semantic information, proximity in the embedding space correlates strongly with the physical or morphological similarity of the original objects. This property enables the "similarity search" paradigm: given a single prototype of a rare object, algorithms can query a massive dataset for its nearest neighbors [Stein et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv211013151S/abstract). This approach drastically reduces the need for large, labeled training sets. For instance, similarity searches operating on self-supervised representations of 76 million galaxy images successfully yielded 1192 new strong gravitational lenses with only a few minutes of CPU time [Stein et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932..107S/abstract). Similarly, deep representations trained on Galaxy Zoo data have demonstrated the ability to locate specific rare morphologies—such as ring galaxies or distinct merger stages—using only a single user-supplied example [Walmsley et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513.1581W/abstract).

**Automated Anomaly Detection**
While similarity search relies on a known query target, blind anomaly detection aims to discover entirely novel phenomena by identifying data points that reside in sparsely populated regions of the latent space. Machine learning frameworks like ASTRONOMALY combine low-dimensional representations (often generated by deep residual networks or self-supervised algorithms like Bootstrap Your Own Latent) with isolation forest algorithms to rank objects by their outlier score [Mohale et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.1274M/abstract). 

When deployed at scale on 4 million images from the Dark Energy Camera Legacy Survey (DECaLS), ASTRONOMALY isolated 1635 scientifically relevant anomalies—including 1609 galaxy mergers, 8 strong gravitational lenses, and 18 highly unusual morphologies—within its top 2000 ranked sources [Etsebeth et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529..732E/abstract). Additionally, more classical probabilistic clustering tools have been effective at discovery in lower-dimensional spaces; for example, mixture models employing Gaussian densities have been successfully used for data mining large infrared catalogs like the UKIDSS Galactic Plane Survey to identify previously unknown, embedded stellar clusters based on multi-band photometric clustering [Solin et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...542A...3S/abstract). Analyzing the local clustering of galaxies in modern foundation representations has also been shown to isolate distinct, rare galaxy classes without any prior supervised labels [Walmsley et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231202910W/abstract).

**Visualizing Complex Cosmic Structures**
Beyond identifying individual anomalous objects, mapping techniques and sophisticated visualization tools are instrumental for macro-level discoveries. For example, utilizing Wiener filter reconstructions to map 3D velocity and density fields has enabled the intuitive, lower-dimensional exploration and visual discovery of complex cosmography in the local universe [Courtois et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AJ....146...69C/abstract).

**Challenges and Active Learning**
A major tension in unsupervised anomaly detection is the algorithm's inherent inability to distinguish between astrophysical discoveries and observational artifacts. Because isolation algorithms purely search for statistical outliers, they frequently flag cosmic rays, satellite trails, stellar diffraction spikes, or instrumental noise as the most anomalous objects in a survey. To overcome this limitation, modern frameworks necessitate an active learning approach, employing a human-in-the-loop to rapidly score a small subset of the highest-ranked anomalies. The algorithm then dynamically updates its weighting of the latent features to suppress data artifacts and prioritize scientifically interesting targets [Etsebeth et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529..732E/abstract).

**Isolating Pipeline Failures**
Beyond discovering unique astrophysics, anomaly detection in low-dimensional spaces is a powerful tool for survey quality control. Complex automated pipelines occasionally produce "catastrophic errors"—such as vastly incorrect photometric redshift estimates or mass inferences—which contaminate population studies. By projecting both the raw data and derived properties into a low-dimensional manifold using algorithms like t-SNE, similar objects are grouped together. Catastrophic errors can then be identified as objects whose inferred physical properties drastically deviate from the statistical consensus of their immediate neighborhood [Hovis-Afflerbach et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908..148H/abstract).

## Cross-Survey Translation and Multi-Modal Representations

The proliferation of large-scale astronomical datasets has necessitated [[feature-extraction|Feature extraction]] methods capable of mapping low-dimensional representations across different instrumental passbands, resolutions, and data modalities. Recent advances in deep generative models and transformer architectures have enabled both cross-survey domain translation and the construction of unified, multi-modal latent spaces.

**Cross-Survey Generative Mapping**
Generative deep learning models provide a robust framework for cross-survey image translation, effectively mapping the observational parameter space of one instrument into that of another. A prominent example is the Survey2Survey model, which utilizes neural networks to translate imaging from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) into the domain of the Dark Energy Survey (DES) [Buncher et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503..777B/abstract). This cross-survey mapping generates synthetic DES representations from SDSS source images, enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio and spatial resolution while preserving intrinsic morphological information and excising survey-specific artifacts [Buncher et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503..777B/abstract).

**Multi-Modal Autoencoders and Transformers**
Beyond image-to-image translation, modern self-supervised architectures allow for the simultaneous embedding of disparate data types—such as 2D morphology and 1D spectroscopy—into a shared latent space. The Multi-Modal Masked Autoencoder (MMAE) achieves this by embedding paired multi-band photometry and optical spectra into a unified representation [Himes et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251022527H/abstract). Trained on a dataset of 134,533 paired observations from HSC-PDR2 (imaging) and DESI-DR1 (spectroscopy), the MMAE masks $75\%$ of the input tokens and learns cross-modal associations by reconstructing the missing data [Himes et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251022527H/abstract). The model successfully recovers broad physical properties, including overall galaxy shapes, continuum slopes, and prominent atomic emission line peaks. When applied to downstream tasks like redshift regression using only imaging data, the multi-modal latent space matches or exceeds the prediction scatter of single-modality models, though current autoencoders still struggle to precisely reconstruct fine image details and specific line strengths [Himes et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251022527H/abstract). 

Concurrent with multi-modal efforts, purely spectral foundation models have demonstrated the power of transformer-based [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]]. For example, the Spectroscopy Pre-trained Transformer (SpecPT) extracts generalizable representations from DESI spectra to perform automated redshift prediction, achieving high precision with a normalized median absolute deviation (NMAD) of $0.0006$ to $0.0008$ across a redshift range of $0 < z < 1.6$ [Pattnaik et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988..139P/abstract).

**The Platonic Representation Hypothesis**
As self-supervised models scale in capacity across varying wavelengths and architectures, evidence suggests that their learned representations are converging. This phenomenon, recently termed the "Platonic Representation Hypothesis" in astronomy, has been quantified using mutual $k$-nearest neighbour analyses across vision transformers and autoencoders trained on diverse datasets, including JWST, HSC, and DESI [UniverseTBD et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919453U/abstract). The observed representational alignment indicates that despite differences in input modalities (e.g., infrared imaging versus optical spectroscopy) and training objectives, these models are independently isolating the same underlying [[galaxy-manifold|Galaxy Manifold]] governing galaxy astrophysics [UniverseTBD et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919453U/abstract). This convergence suggests that future astronomical foundation models can readily adapt pre-trained, general-purpose architectures to map multi-modal observations into a single, universal physical space.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

As astronomy enters the petabyte era with next-generation facilities such as the Vera C. Rubin Observatory and the Square Kilometre Array (SKA), the scalability of representation learning has become a paramount concern [Mohale et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.1274M/abstract). The Rubin Observatory alone is slated to observe nearly $2 \times 10^{10}$ galaxies, necessitating extreme semantic compression and [[feature-extraction|Feature extraction]] to reduce high-dimensional multi-band imaging into manageable latent spaces [Gagliano et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251204145G/abstract). While algorithms like isolation forests and active learning frameworks have successfully scaled to millions of objects to isolate rare anomalies (e.g., strong lenses and galaxy mergers), a major unresolved challenge is scaling unsupervised anomaly detection to billions of sources while mitigating the overwhelming impact of noise and data artifacts [Etsebeth et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529..732E/abstract).

A rapidly developing theoretical frontier is the application of the "Platonic Representation Hypothesis" to extragalactic astronomy. This hypothesis posits that different self-supervised foundation models—despite utilizing varied architectures (e.g., Vision Transformers, convolutional networks) and training on diverse astronomical modalities—are converging toward a shared, universal representation of galaxy astrophysics [UniverseTBD et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919453U/abstract). Recent empirical tests reveal that representational alignment between disparate models increases systematically with model capacity, suggesting that astronomy-specific models are learning a fundamental, modality-agnostic parameterization of physical reality [UniverseTBD et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919453U/abstract). To this end, multi-modal masked autoencoders have been developed to project both photometric images and optical spectra into a shared latent space [Himes et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251022527H/abstract). However, it remains an open question whether these unified representations can successfully reconstruct fine morphological details and specific spectral line strengths, which currently limit the efficacy of cross-modal autoencoders [Himes et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251022527H/abstract).

Finally, the tension between predictive performance and mechanistic interpretability in non-linear representations remains a highly active debate. While classical linear projections like [[principal-component-analysis-in-astronomy|Principal Component Analysis in astronomy]] and shapelets offer immediate physical transparency , deep representations achieve superior classification accuracy at the cost of obscuring the physical meaning of their latent dimensions. Efforts to map ultra-low-dimensional spaces—such as the $5$-dimensional semantic compression achieved by the Minuet diffusion autoencoder [Gagliano et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251204145G/abstract)—back to physical parameters like stellar mass and star formation rate are ongoing. Furthermore, the true intrinsic dimension of astronomical datasets is still actively contested; recent estimates using score-based diffusion models suggest that the intrinsic dimensionality of complex radio galaxy datasets may significantly exceed that of standard terrestrial images [Font-Quer Roset et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251111490F/abstract). Resolving these dimensional constraints and establishing how to best extract human-interpretable physics from foundational representations remain critical future directions for the field.
