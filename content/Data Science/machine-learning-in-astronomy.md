The application of [[artificial-intelligence-and-machine-learning-in-astrophysics|machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence in astronomy]] has fundamentally transformed the analysis of astrophysical data. Driven by the exponential growth of survey volumes and the increasing complexity of multi-wavelength datasets, [[computational-algorithms-and-machine-learning-in-astronomy|data-driven algorithms]] are now deployed across nearly every domain of astrophysics. To manage this transition to petascale data streams, the field increasingly relies on collaborative human-AI discovery paradigms [Djorgovski et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221201493D/abstract). Furthermore, to navigate the specialized jargon of astronomical literature, tools like chaotic_neural employ natural language processing (e.g., Doc2Vec) for context-aware literature surveys [Iyer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021wems.confE..13I/abstract). Comprehensive reviews highlight the utility of these techniques across tasks ranging from star-galaxy separation to identifying the compact object hosts of low-mass X-ray binaries [Kembhavi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JApA...43...76K/abstract). From the real-time classification of transient events to the emulation of complex cosmological simulations, machine learning techniques offer a scalable solution to the "Big Data" challenges posed by next-generation observatories. The rise of ML in the field is supported by the availability of comprehensive hands-on textbooks that provide an introduction to building and optimizing models specifically for research problems in physics and astronomy [Acquaviva et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023mlpa.book.....A/abstract). This article outlines the historical evolution of these techniques and details their modern applications, particularly highlighting deep learning, generative modeling, and unsupervised representation learning within Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Astrophysics.

## Historical Evolution of ML in Astronomy

The application of machine learning in astronomy has evolved in tandem with the exponential growth of survey data, transitioning from simple dimensionality reduction techniques to highly complex, [[computational-algorithms-and-machine-learning-in-astronomy|automated predictive models]]. Early efforts sought to address the statistical challenges of precision cosmology, highlighting the rising importance of Bayesian methods [Feigelson et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003sca..book.....F/abstract).

**Early Statistical and Linear Models**
In the 1990s and early 2000s, automated algorithms were primarily framed as non-linear extensions of classical statistical techniques. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was widely utilized to compress high-dimensional data, such as galaxy spectra or images, into a small number of informative, orthogonal basis vectors [Lahav et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.283..207L/abstract). Later, Non-Negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) was introduced as a physically intuitive alternative to PCA, ensuring that weights and spectral components remained non-negative [Hurley et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.437..241H/abstract). Around the same time, Ensemble Learning for Independent Component Analysis (EL-ICA) was utilized to effectively compress synthetic spectral libraries into physically intuitive components for modeling SDSS galaxy properties [Lu et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..790L/abstract). Early implementations of [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs)]] built upon these foundations to perform [[automated-galaxy-classification|Automated galaxy classification]]. These models relied heavily on a limited set of manually extracted structural parameters and successfully demonstrated that neural computation could replicate human expert classifications to within a scatter of $\sim 2$ Hubble $T$-type units [Lahav et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.283..207L/abstract). Alongside standard ANNs, early researchers utilized neural network ensembles and locally weighted regression to achieve $>91\%$ accuracy for broad galaxy classifications [de la Calleja et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.349...87D/abstract). These methods evolved into integrated platforms like the ASTROMLSKIT toolkit, which provided an early framework for star-galaxy and supernovae classification [Saha et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150407865S/abstract). Early applications on datasets like the KiDS survey also demonstrated the power of ML for deriving photometric redshifts and structural parameters to trace galaxy evolution [Tortora et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSP...42..123T/abstract). Later, deep neural networks expanded on this foundation; for example, the PS1-STRM catalogue provided automated source classifications and photometric redshifts for nearly 3 billion objects in the Pan-STARRS1 DR1 [Beck et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.1633B/abstract). 

**Classical Supervised Learning**
As projects like the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) ushered in the era of massive catalog-level data, traditional supervised machine learning algorithms became essential for processing millions of sources. A milestone in this era was the application of Decision Trees to the SDSS DR3 [Ball et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...650..497B/abstract). By training on a subset of nearly 500,000 objects with secure spectroscopic data, these decision tree models successfully provided star-galaxy classifications for 143 million photometric objects. Crucially, the models demonstrated an ability to extrapolate beyond the photometric limits of the spectroscopic training set, maintaining robust classification efficiencies down to $r \sim 20$ magnitudes [Ball et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...650..497B/abstract). By combining Genetic Algorithms with Support Vector Machines (SVMs), researchers further optimized feature selection for star-galaxy separation and redshift estimation [Heinis et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...821...86H/abstract). To distinguish stars from quasars, GAN-based classifiers were applied to combined GALEX-SDSS photometric catalogs [Makhija et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&C....2900313M/abstract). Random Forest models relying on parameters like the FWHM/PSF became crucial, as demonstrated by the S-PLUS survey [Costa-Duarte et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190908626C/abstract), while nine-color Random Forests achieved $>99\%$ star-galaxy classification accuracy alongside precise stellar temperature regression [Bai et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AJ....157....9B/abstract). XGBoost algorithms were similarly deployed to classify extended versus point-like objects using 60-band photometry in J-PAS/miniJPAS [Jeakel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251120524J/abstract) and to improve classification accuracy by $10\%$ in the darkest SDSS source sets [Li et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ChA&A..43..539L/abstract). In preparation for the CSST, GoogLeNet integrated polar coordinate transformations to provide a rotationally invariant supervised star-galaxy classification method [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24i5012Z/abstract). Around this time, cloud computing platforms began to facilitate the early use of supervised algorithms, such as Logistic Regression and SVMs, for classifying SDSS galaxy spectral subclasses—such as AGNs and starbursts—at scale [Tao et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180104839T/abstract).

Throughout this period, the standard workflow relied almost entirely on "feature engineering." Astronomers were required to manually define, extract, and select summary statistics—such as colors, magnitudes, and structural parameters like the Concentration, Asymmetry, and Smoothness (CAS) metrics—before feeding them into classical algorithms like Random Forests or Support Vector Machines.

**The Deep Learning Paradigm Shift**
The modern landscape of astronomical machine learning is characterized by the dominance of deep learning architectures, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). The defining paradigm shift occurred when researchers demonstrated that CNNs could learn complex, high-dimensional visual representations directly from calibrated pixel values, completely bypassing the need for manual feature extraction for tasks like star-galaxy separation [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4463K/abstract). Early CNN experiments on Galaxy Zoo data explored varying layer depths and activation functions to optimize these deep architectures [Lukic et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..325..217L/abstract). Subsequent comparisons, utilizing transfer learning, demonstrated that specific backbones such as DenseNet121 achieve superior fast and accurate morphological classification on datasets like Galaxy10 DECaLS [Hui et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JPhCS2402a2009H/abstract), [Fielding et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ecet.confE...1F/abstract). Transforming data into alternate color spaces (e.g., RGB to LAB) before input was also found to improve individual network performance [Andrew et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230500002A/abstract). These advances extend to integrated pipelines like Mask R-CNN, which simultaneously perform detection, segmentation, and morphological classification with high centroid precision [Farias et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&C....3300420F/abstract). 

By operating directly on raw or reduced images, deep learning models capture non-parametric morphological information that traditional CAS metrics often miss. In modern [[automated-galaxy-classification|Automated galaxy classification]] tasks, CNNs routinely achieve $>90\%$ accuracy, matching or exceeding the reliability of human experts and traditional algorithmic classifiers [Cheng et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.4209C/abstract), [Barchi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&C....3000334B/abstract). Furthermore, once trained, these deep neural networks can perform inference at remarkable speeds—over $10^3$ times faster than traditional template matching or profile-fitting software like GALFIT [Tuccillo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..325..191T/abstract), [Tohill et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916....4T/abstract). This transition from manual feature engineering to automated representation learning now serves as the foundation for the real-time analysis required by forthcoming synoptic surveys.

## Automated Morphological Classification

[[automated-galaxy-classification|Automated galaxy classification]] represents one of the most mature applications of machine learning within the astronomical literature. While early automated classifiers relied heavily on manual feature engineering, modern structural analysis is dominated by [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|deep learning]]. By learning complex spatial hierarchies directly from raw pixel data, CNNs bypass the limitations of handcrafted parametric models and consistently outperform traditional algorithms in capturing non-linear visual representations [González et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&C....25..103G/abstract). Studies directly comparing deep learning to traditional algorithms confirm that CNNs reliably outperform SVMs and Random Forests, achieving accuracies of $\sim 0.99$ on Dark Energy Survey data [Cheng et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.4209C/abstract). Non-parametric frameworks like CyMorph similarly benchmark deep learning against traditional ML for SDSS-DR7 morphology [Barchi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&C....3000334B/abstract). To handle large volumes, lightweight frameworks like EfficientNet-G3 [Wu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RAA....22k5011W/abstract), models like Xception combined with data augmentation [Khramtsov et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019OAP....32...21K/abstract), and deep metric learning techniques such as Proxy-NCA [Variawa et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022IEEEA..1019539V/abstract) provide fast, robust representation. Using Zernike Moments as invariant descriptors alongside 1D-CNNs or SVMs has also enabled high-performance classification of spirals, ellipticals, and rare objects [Ghaderi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJS..277...10G/abstract), [Ghaderi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250109816G/abstract). Alternatively, XGBoost hierarchical approaches successfully leverage non-parametric structural and color features for 5-class morphologies [Aguilar-Argüello et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.tmp...77A/abstract).

These architectures enable vast value-added catalogs. For example, CNNs generated detailed morphological predictions for over 300,000 SDSS galaxies at low redshift ($0.02 < z < 0.1$) trained on Galaxy Zoo 2 labels [Vavilova et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022KosNT..28a...3V/abstract), [Dobrycheva et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IAUS..362..111D/abstract). In the MaNGA survey, the MDLM-VAC-DR17 deep-learning catalogue mapped T-Types and barred galaxies [Domínguez Sánchez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.4024D/abstract), while PyMorph catalogs revealed insights into stellar angular momentum across galaxy types [Fischer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.2057F/abstract). For S-PLUS, combining multi-band morphometric fitting and CNNs successfully classified morphologies in the Stripe-82 area [Bom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.1937B/abstract), and ensemble CNNs achieved $99\%$ accuracy distinguishing spirals and ellipticals [Cardoso et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210702287C/abstract), culminating in an extended high-reliability catalogue of 164,000 objects in DR3 [Bom et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.4188B/abstract). CNNs routinely categorize broad Hubble types and detect detailed morphological features like galactic bars, rings, and tidal tails [Domínguez Sánchez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.3661D/abstract), [Desmons et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023mla..confE..11D/abstract). For instance, CNNs have evaluated detailed binary morphological features (e.g., edge-on orientations, bar signatures, and mergers) derived from Galaxy Zoo 2 questions with accuracies over $97\%$, alongside enabling continuous large-scale T-type predictions [Domínguez Sánchez et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.3661D/abstract). Furthermore, CNN regression models are deployed to estimate bulge-to-total luminosity ratios (B/T) significantly faster than traditional profile fitting [Grover et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.3313G/abstract). Despite this, ML analysis on GAMA catalogs indicates that some traditional visual categories, apart from 'Little Blue Spheroids', remain difficult to consistently separate across physical features [Nolte et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190307749N/abstract).

Regarding specific sub-populations and rare objects, object detectors like YOLOv5 detect and classify spiral, elliptical, and lensed galaxies [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023SPIE12511E..1CW/abstract). In the DESI Legacy Surveys, Swin Transformer-based models identified over 8,000 new ring galaxies [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250616090Z/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJS..279...52Z/abstract), while semi-supervised deep learning tools like GC-SWGAN produced a massive catalog of 62,000 ringed galaxies using limited standard labels [Chen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250707552C/abstract), [Luo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJS..279...17L/abstract). Other models leverage GAN-augmented imagery to detect ring galaxies in real and simulated data [Krishnakumar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..191K/abstract). CNNs are actively deployed to identify rare objects—including mergers, lenses, and asteroids—in KiDS and Euclid [Verdoes Kleijn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240301613V/abstract), though strong lens searches in Euclid Perseus fields face high purity challenges [Pearce-Casey et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241116808P/abstract). In the Dark Energy Survey, Vision Transformers coupled with interactive ML have uncovered new galaxy-galaxy lenses [González et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250115679G/abstract). CNNs like UDGnet-K flag ultra-diffuse galaxies in KiDS [Su et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250913910S/abstract), and 7-class networks have pinpointed over 100,000 local ($z < 0.02$) dwarf galaxy candidates [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250300109Z/abstract). Furthermore, tools like Faster R-CNN, pre-trained on domain-specific datasets (Zoobot), localize giant star-forming clumps [Popp et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RASTI...3..174P/abstract), and the NASIM pipeline automates the extraction of low surface brightness structures from near-infrared VISTA data [Saremi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250802780S/abstract).

For interacting systems, CNNs and ResNets estimate dynamical parameters like spin and inclination from merger snapshots [Mahor et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv211212604M/abstract) and track merger stages (e.g., pericentric passage, coalescence) in MaNGA using images and H$\alpha$ velocity maps [Chang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937...97C/abstract). Deep learning has been expanded to detect hundreds of thousands of merger candidates via few-shot learning in Legacy Surveys [Wei et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..274...23W/abstract) and to successfully identify major and minor mergers at $z \sim 1$ in CANDELS [Schechter et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251012173S/abstract), extending to low-mass minor mergers out to $z \sim 1.5$ [Schechter et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251115006S/abstract).

Crucially, while CNNs deliver classification reliabilities comparable to human experts, they perform inference significantly faster than classical fitting techniques [Tuccillo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..325..191T/abstract), [Tohill et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916....4T/abstract). Data augmentation techniques, such as rotations and adversarial validation, are often employed to significantly improve generalization to fainter galaxies against varying instrumental noise [Khramtsov et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022KosNT..28e..27K/abstract). To further address limited training sets, few-shot learning and Siamese networks identify rare objects without vast labeled catalogs [Zhang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RAA....22e5002Z/abstract), and semi-supervised learning combined with GANs can improve strong gravitational lens detection precision by up to a factor of 10 [Keerthi Vasan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5368K/abstract).

The success of optical morphological classification has catalyzed the expansion of these algorithms across the electromagnetic spectrum, notably in radio astronomy, where researchers are shifting toward multi-channel data cubes and annotated datasets [Ndung'u et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NewAR..9701685N/abstract). Early efforts using Radio Galaxy Zoo data demonstrated that three-layer CNN architectures could achieve $>94\%$ accuracy in distinguishing compact sources from multi-component extended morphologies [Lukic et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476..246L/abstract). Deep learning frameworks, including custom networks such as MCRGNet, automate the categorization of extended radio sources into Fanaroff-Riley morphological classes (FRI and FRII) directly from interferometric maps [Ma et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..240...34M/abstract), [Becker et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.1828B/abstract). Furthermore, models employing parallel convolutional blocks and specific data augmentation techniques reliably sort radio galaxies into finer categories, such as FRI, FRII, Bent-Tailed, and Compact classes [Maslej-Krešňáková et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.1464M/abstract). Employing PCA-based rotational standardization significantly improves CNN accuracy and training speeds for these classifications [Brand et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522..292B/abstract), and optimizing pre-trained image backbones is already underway for the forthcoming SKAO [Denzel et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260104773D/abstract). Other architectures like YOLOv5 achieve robust automatic detection and classification by explicitly fusing these radio interferometric maps with optical image data [Zhang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASP..134f4503Z/abstract), occasionally integrating attention mechanisms to simultaneously locate and classify FR I and FR II structures [Wang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Univ....7..211W/abstract).

Despite these methodological triumphs, a significant tension persists regarding high-redshift ($z > 2$) galaxies. Supervised learning models are fundamentally constrained by their target labels, which are overwhelmingly derived from the local universe's Hubble sequence. However, ConvNets applied to CANDELS fields achieve $<1\%$ misclassification rates for morphologies at $z \sim 1.25$ [Huertas-Company et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..221....8H/abstract). Specialized architectures address specific observational challenges; for example, in low-to-intermediate redshift samples, frameworks like PSFGAN combined with GAMORNET or GaMPEN successfully decouple central AGN light from host galaxy images, estimating accurate bulge-to-total ratios and morphological classifications for AGN hosts out to $z < 1.4$ [Tian et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944..124T/abstract), [Tian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...981....5T/abstract). Meanwhile, to explore irregular high-redshift ($z > 2$) galaxies independently of local taxonomies, unsupervised tools such as Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) are now applied to JWST/NIRCam data [Tohill et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230211482T/abstract), [Tohill et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962..164T/abstract). Additionally, CNNs identify specialized evolutionary stages like the 'blue nugget' compaction phase [Huertas-Company et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...858..114H/abstract). To entirely circumvent the biases of human-defined labels in [[large-scale-galaxy-classification|Large-scale galaxy classification]], researchers increasingly leverage self-supervised representation learning, suggesting the foundation for entirely new morphological taxonomies [Wei et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASP..134k4508W/abstract), [Tohill et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962..164T/abstract), [Kolesnikov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528...82K/abstract).

## Redshift Estimation and Spectral Analysis

An essential task in modern astronomy is the rapid and accurate inference of distances and physical parameters from large datasets. Within [[artificial-intelligence-and-machine-learning-in-astrophysics|Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Astrophysics]], regression algorithms have been extensively developed to estimate redshifts and extract intrinsic properties—such as stellar mass, metallicity, and star formation history—from both imaging and spectroscopic data.

**Photometric and Spectroscopic Redshifts**
The estimation of photometric redshifts is a critical bottleneck for large-scale cosmological surveys. Machine learning significantly outperforms traditional empirical methods; for instance, nearest-neighbor algorithms reduce the catastrophic outlier rate in quasar photometric redshifts to $<1\%$ [Ball et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...683...12B/abstract). Early tools like PhotoRApToR utilized the MLPQNA algorithm for regression and classification in photo-z estimation [Cavuoti et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..306..307C/abstract), while studies showed that incorporating visual morphologies from Galaxy Zoo improved photo-z accuracy for Luminous Red Galaxies [Way et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...734L...9W/abstract). Integrating morphological features (e.g., smoothness, spiral structure) into photo-z models via XGBoost and CNNs significantly improves prediction accuracy [Chong et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190107544C/abstract). [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|Deep learning]] photo-z models also inherently reduce the color-dependent 'attenuation bias' observed in classical ML methods [Moran et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250706299M/abstract). When applied to DES DR2 data, architectures like Keras combined with GPz have accurately mapped photometric redshifts to create onion-like overdensity maps for cosmological parameter estimation [Abdalla et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250810191A/abstract). Additionally, self-supervised representation learning fine-tuned for galactic distance estimation has been shown to outperform standard supervised methods when labeled redshift data is limited [Abul Hayat et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210104293A/abstract).

A major shift in recent literature is the transition from tabular photometry to pixel-based deep learning. By training CNNs directly on multiband images, models leverage morphological information alongside color, significantly reducing the bias and scatter of redshift predictions compared to photometry-only models [Menou et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.4802M/abstract), [Jones et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..159J/abstract), [Engel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991..124E/abstract). For forthcoming missions, deep CNNs have been tailored for spectroscopic redshift estimation specifically in low signal-to-noise regimes [Stivaktakis et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180909622S/abstract). 

However, deep learning models for redshift estimation face unresolved challenges, including susceptibility to "adversarial attacks" [Campagne et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200210154C/abstract), miscalibration due to unaccounted-for epistemic uncertainties [Lin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A.331L/abstract), and problematic domain shifts [Janiurek et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240720670J/abstract). To mitigate these issues, hybrid approaches are gaining traction. Algorithms like HAYATE hybridize neural networks with traditional template fitting to provide robust extrapolation out to high redshifts [Tanigawa et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.2012T/abstract), while other approaches leverage machine learning to deduce optimal spectral templates directly from broadband photometry [Crenshaw et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....160..191C/abstract). Furthermore, online dictionary learning approaches now jointly estimate redshifts and spectral templates, heavily reducing model mismatch errors [Bryan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231114812B/abstract).

**Spectral Analysis and Physical Properties**
Deriving fundamental galactic parameters typically requires computationally expensive $\chi^2$ minimization through SED fitting. Once trained, ML algorithms regress parameters such as stellar mass, age, and star formation rate exponentially faster [Hemmati et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881L..14H/abstract), [Gilda et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916...43G/abstract). Supervised ML models estimate star formation rates (SFR) for large galaxy samples significantly faster than spectroscopic observations [Delli Veneri et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180506338D/abstract), and comparing architectures like LSTM and Support Vector Regression confirms strong photometric SFR predictions for SDSS-DR7 [Raghav et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241006736R/abstract). Neural networks and Random Forests similarly predict the masses of galactic disks, bulges, and total stellar mass directly from $ugriz$ photometry [López-Sánchez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Univ...10..220L/abstract). Beyond photometry, neural networks decompose optical spectra into single stellar population (SSP) components orders of magnitude faster than Bayesian methods [Murata et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ASPC..532..227M/abstract). In COSMOS2020 data, models like CatBoostClassifier heavily outperform standard SED fitting in distinguishing quiescent from star-forming galaxies [Asadi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250903039A/abstract). Advanced architectures like multibranch CNNs break degeneracies by predicting mass-to-light ratios directly from kinematic maps [Chu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.6354C/abstract), and neural networks like sedNN decompose stellar populations to improve flux determination [Kunsági-Máté et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.1045K/abstract). 

Some studies indicate that ML significantly reduces bias and scatter by more efficiently marginalizing over astrophysical uncertainties [Gilda et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916...43G/abstract), [Asadi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220544A/abstract), though others note that ML accuracy remains influenced by the limitations of the theoretical templates used in training [Simet et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908...47S/abstract). 

Beyond broadband SEDs, ML is highly effective for direct spectral analysis. Codes such as GAME employ supervised learning to map emission-line intensities to metallicity and ionization parameters, notably in dwarf galaxies like IZw18 using MUSE IFU data [Ucci et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.1144U/abstract), [Ucci et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.1484U/abstract), [Ucci et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.1295U/abstract). CNNs trained on synthetic spectra from cosmological simulations predict highly resolved star formation histories [Lovell et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.5503L/abstract). For 3D data, CNNs determine line-of-sight components in IFU spectroscopy to analyze complex merging systems [Rhea et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923..169R/abstract). In IFU spectroscopy like SITELLE, neural networks perform local background interpolation for noise reduction and faint source recovery [Rhea et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240401175R/abstract). Unsupervised clustering (k-means, DBSCAN) and CNNs are also applied to 21cm HI spectral profiles to classify and analyze gas kinematics [Jaimes-Illanes et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250111657J/abstract). Deep learning models retrieve internal velocity dispersions directly from optical images [Hansen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020RNAAS...4..185H/abstract), and machine learning further refines metallicity-dependent isotopic scaling models to trace chemical histories from elemental observations [Sun et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240302678S/abstract). In X-ray astronomy, ML models classify optical/infrared counterparts to eRASS1 X-ray sources as either Galactic or extragalactic AGNs [Salvato et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250902842S/abstract). Advanced approaches such as Recurrent Inference Machines (RIMs) have also been tested to disentangle intrinsic X-ray spectra from instrumental responses in galaxy cluster observations; however, while RIMs accurately reconstruct mock spectra, their application to real cluster observations (such as NGC 1550) currently exhibits discrepancies because the networks implicitly encode rigid prior expectations [Rhea et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240910711R/abstract). In some cases, ML predicts SDSS-quality spectra directly from broadband images [Holwerda et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...914..142H/abstract), while GaSNets identify emission lines from strong gravitational lenses in 1D data [Zhong et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RAA....22f5014Z/abstract).

Finally, ML enables real-time spectral inference for transient events. The DASH software utilizes deep learning to automate supernova spectra classification [Muthukrishna et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...885...85M/abstract). In photometric surveys, random forests distinguish real transients from artifacts [du Buisson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.2026D/abstract), while frameworks like SCONE classify supernovae directly from photometry, and tools such as Photo-zSNthesis perform host-independent redshift estimation entirely from light curves [Qu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240604529Q/abstract). For massive time-domain surveys, pre-training models on galaxy images (e.g., Galaxy Zoo) outperforms ImageNet pre-training when filtering transient alerts [Rehemtulla et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251211957R/abstract). Hierarchical deep learning classifiers like ORACLE have proven highly effective for the LSST, achieving $96\%$ precision in separating transients from variables using only one day of data [Shah et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...995....4S/abstract). Additionally, frameworks like AGNBoost utilize XGBoost on JWST NIRCam and MIRI photometry to simultaneously identify AGNs and estimate their redshifts [Hamblin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603130H/abstract). For early-time classification, recurrent neural networks leverage host-galaxy information soon after discovery [Gagliano et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954....6G/abstract). Furthermore, algorithms such as DELIGHT employ multiresolution images and CNNs to achieve real-time identification of transient host galaxies [Förster et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AJ....164..195F/abstract).

## Stellar and Planetary Parameters

The characterization of stars and their planetary systems has been revolutionized by the application of [[artificial-intelligence-and-machine-learning-in-astrophysics|Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Astrophysics]]. Traditional methods for determining stellar labels—such as detailed spectral template matching or theoretical isochrone fitting—are highly computationally intensive. [[computational-algorithms-and-machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine learning algorithms]] circumvent this computational bottleneck by learning complex, nonlinear mappings directly from observables to physical parameters, enabling parameter inference orders of magnitude faster than conventional techniques [Bellinger et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...31B/abstract). Beyond normal stars, Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) provide a Bayesian framework for classifying pulsars [Lee et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.2832L/abstract). Using ML, researchers derived detailed individual stellar abundances (Fe, Mg, Si, C) from MUSE data in clusters like NGC 1856 [Asa'd et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167..265A/abstract). Neural networks classify Young Stellar Objects (YSOs) to reconstruct 3D dense cloud structures, while separate convolutional networks infer the 3D extinction distribution of the Milky Way [Cornu et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201001431C/abstract). Additionally, Probabilistic Random Forests combined with SMOTE successfully classify YSOs and evolved stars in the Magellanic Clouds with $89\%$ accuracy [Ghaziasgar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024CoBAO..71..377G/abstract). [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|Deep learning models]] like ResNet-50 identified over 1,000 star cluster candidates in M31 from PAndAS survey images, of which nearly 750 are entirely new discoveries [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250308130Z/abstract), and SVM approaches successfully disentangle globular clusters from foreground stars and background galaxies in the Fornax cluster using NGFS filters [Ordenes-Briceño et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215895O/abstract). Imbalanced learning techniques have similarly proven vital for identifying rare RR Lyrae stars across large surveys [Zhang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PASP..132l7001Z/abstract).

**Stellar Atmospheric Parameters**
Supervised learning models are routinely deployed to regress fundamental stellar atmospheric parameters: effective temperature $T_{\rm eff}$, surface gravity $\log g$, and metallicity $\mathrm{[Fe/H]}$. For large-scale spectroscopic surveys, tools such as SLAM rapidly parameterize vast datasets, yielding $T_{\rm eff}$ and $\mathrm{[M/H]}$ predictions that strongly agree with high-resolution APOGEE labels [Li et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..253...45L/abstract). CNNs applied to GIRAFFE spectra in the Gaia-ESO survey precisely predict chemical abundances for Mg, Al, and Fe [Ambrosch et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...672A..46A/abstract). By projecting Gaia DR3 RVS spectra using UMAP, models identify new metal-poor candidate stars by mapping their proximity to known galactic fossils [Kane et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AAS...24120811K/abstract). Furthermore, ML-based photometric methods utilizing specific filters (e.g., NB515) cleanly separate giant stars from Milky Way dwarf contaminants [Ding et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251007005D/abstract). Applying machine learning to big survey data has also proven invaluable for determining stellar rotation and surface gravity, as well as tracing the chemical enrichment history of the Milky Way [Blancato et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200910661B/abstract).

Machine learning techniques demonstrate comparable efficacy when applied purely to broadband photometry, bypassing the need for expensive spectroscopic follow-up. Using Random Forest regressors trained on combined optical, infrared, and ultraviolet photometry, algorithms extract physical labels for tens of millions of stars with excellent precision [Gu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJS..277...19G/abstract). Deep neural networks utilizing densely connected convolutional architectures have also output continuous atmospheric parameter estimates across a wide range of colors and types [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PASP..131h4504W/abstract). In the interstellar medium, CNNs applied to 3D CO datasets successfully determine distances to molecular clouds, resolving the 'near-far' kinematic ambiguity [Fujita et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASJ...75..279F/abstract).

**Asteroseismology and Evolutionary Labels**
Beyond surface properties, ML is highly effective in asteroseismology for deriving deeper evolutionary parameters, such as stellar age, mass, and radius. By training on high-precision photometric time-series from Kepler, models negate the need for exhaustive grid-based modeling. They instantaneously estimate fundamental parameters for solar-like stars with competitive uncertainties [Bellinger et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...31B/abstract). This characterization is vital for maximizing the diagnostic potential of missions like TESS and PLATO, which rely on tight stellar constraints to study planetary systems [Angelou et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839..116A/abstract). Applying age constraints to galactic questions, Bayesian ML frameworks like BINGO estimate accurate stellar ages for APOGEE giants to trace the Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus merger [Ciucă et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528L.122C/abstract). Unsupervised techniques have also been applied to GALAH survey data to separate stellar populations based on chemo-kinematic signatures [da Silva et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ASPC..532..219D/abstract).

**Exoplanet Transit Identification**
In time-series photometry, ML dramatically accelerates exoplanetary transit identification. Space-based observatories generate vast quantities of light curves, necessitating automated frameworks to distinguish true transits from false positives. Deep learning classifiers, particularly CNNs and residual networks, achieve high success rates. Specialized approaches such as Siamese network architectures elevate classification accuracy to $99\%$ by mitigating data-scarcity challenges inherent to rare exoplanet candidates [G et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230509596G/abstract).

## Cosmology and Large-Scale Structure

[[artificial-intelligence-and-machine-learning-in-astrophysics|Machine learning]] is increasingly applied to bridge the gap between cosmological parameters and the physical property distributions extracted from massive simulation runs. [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|Deep learning architectures]] trained on hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate that individual galaxy properties correlate strongly with fundamental constants. For example, models can infer the matter density parameter $\Omega_m$ from a single galaxy to within $\sim 10\%$ precision [Echeverri-Rojas et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954..125E/abstract). Artificial Neural Networks constrain $\Omega_m$ from the two-point correlation function of BOSS galaxies [Veronesi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&C....4200692V/abstract), while Support Vector Regression (SVR) of simulated galaxy ages reliably reconstructs cosmic expansion history and matter density [Sousa-Neto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250517205S/abstract). Neural networks distinguish between $\Lambda$CDM and $f(R)$ gravity models with $97\%$ accuracy using growth rate data [Ocampo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240608351O/abstract), and attention-based emulators model CMB temperature and polarization power spectra to precision levels below cosmic variance limits [Zhu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522574Z/abstract). Implicit Likelihood Inference (ILI) applied to intensity mapping (LIMFAST) also constrains star formation efficiency and gas surface density relations in high-redshift galaxies [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250907060S/abstract). To mitigate weak lensing contaminants, deep learning emulators trace galaxy intrinsic alignment (IA) correlations [Pandya et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240413702P/abstract). In dark matter studies, CNNs disentangle the mass distribution signatures of self-interacting dark matter from those of AGN feedback [Harvey et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240517566H/abstract). Using the CAMELS-SAM suite, neural networks marginalize over nonlinear astrophysical uncertainties, constraining cosmological parameters to $3-8\%$ precision [Perez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954...11P/abstract). The official public data release of the CAMELS project provides thousands of hydrodynamic simulations and multi-wavelength catalogs explicitly designed for ML training [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...54V/abstract).

Machine learning techniques also show great promise in inferring global properties of large-scale structures. [[computational-algorithms-and-machine-learning-in-astronomy|Algorithms]] like Deep-CEE apply deep neural nets directly to color imaging for cluster detection without requiring photometric catalogs [Chan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.5770C/abstract), while the COSMIC algorithm utilizes ML to identify Brightest Cluster Galaxies (BCGs) and estimate cluster richness [Tian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241020083T/abstract). Other ML-based group finders identify galaxy groups and predict their masses in real space while properly accounting for redshift space distortions [Ma et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250401131M/abstract). CNNs trained on Chandra mock images accurately estimate cluster masses from photon counts alone, achieving remarkably low scatter ($8-12\%$) compared to traditional luminosity methods [Ntampaka et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...876...82N/abstract). U-Net architectures infer total mass density maps of galaxy clusters directly from mock X-ray and Sunyaev-Zeldovich images [de Andres et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240405400D/abstract). Applying ML to spectroscopic galaxy samples in the MACSIS simulations yielded a factor-of-4 reduction in cluster mass scatter over traditional $\sigma-M$ relations [Armitage et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.1526A/abstract), and Random Forests utilizing X-ray morphological parameters (like concentration and asymmetry) reduced mass estimation scatter by $20\%$ [Green et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884...33G/abstract). Within clusters, automated supervised learning measures the Intracluster Light (ICL) fraction directly from images [Canepa et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..245C/abstract), and ML algorithms pinpoint BCGs in LSST simulations with $95\%$ accuracy, serving as a superior alternative to red-sequence methods [Chu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315945C/abstract). CNNs utilizing 1D and 2D architectures infer dynamical cluster masses from velocity and radial distance distributions, exhibiting reduced scatter compared to traditional scaling relations [Ho et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887...25H/abstract). Overall, ML algorithms have demonstrated widespread success in predicting the dynamical mass of galaxy clusters, providing a robust alternative to conventional statistical methods [Haider Abbas et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191205316H/abstract). Within galaxy clusters, Random Forests classify distinct stellar components to distinguish the BCG from diffuse Intracluster Light (ICL) [Marini et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3082M/abstract). The LtU-ILI framework provides an all-in-one codebase for implicit likelihood inference across domains [Ho et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..54H/abstract). 

In the early universe, CNNs discriminate between reionization models by analyzing simulated 21cm maps [Hassan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..333...47H/abstract). At intermediate scales, CNNs accurately estimate galaxy distances directly from images to infer peculiar velocities [Quigley et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220409135Q/abstract). ML frameworks populate halos and predict subhalo masses with remarkable fidelity, forecasting $>99\%$ of subhalos to within $0.2\text{ dex}$ across different subgrid physics models [Shao et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927...85S/abstract). However, overspecialized network states present issues when generalizing to alternative simulators [Ntampaka et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926...45N/abstract).

## Generative Modeling and Synthetic Data

Modern astrophysics relies heavily on mock catalogs to calibrate surveys and characterize selection biases. Generative modeling and representation learning bridge the computationally prohibitive gap of running full-physics hydrodynamical simulations over cosmological volumes, forming a rapidly expanding subfield within [[artificial-intelligence-and-machine-learning-in-astrophysics|Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Astrophysics]].

**Populating Halos and Mock Catalogs**
Early efforts to create mock catalogs utilized algorithms such as SVMs and nearest neighbors to predict halo occupation distributions from dark matter-only N-body simulations, like the Millennium run [Xu et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...772..147X/abstract). In these frameworks, algorithms such as Random Forests and extremely randomized trees predicted galaxy properties (e.g., stellar mass, gas mass) from dark matter halo properties via semi-analytical models . [[computational-algorithms-and-machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine learning models]] have since been shown to mimic full hydrodynamical simulations (like Illustris) to predict baryonic properties with high statistical fidelity in minutes rather than months . Modern [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|deep learning frameworks]], such as the MACHINE INFERRED GALAXY (MIG) pipeline, map subhalo properties directly to complex baryonic observables [Das et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240616103D/abstract), [Das et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.tmp.1984D/abstract). Trained on SIMBA, MIG reproduces simulated scaling relations in a fraction of the time required by traditional methods. Other approaches employ conditional Invertible Neural Networks (cINNs) to infer unobservable assembly histories directly from integral galaxy properties [Eisert et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.2199E/abstract). Other approaches employ Normalizing Flows and probabilistic ML to capture the stochasticity of central galaxy property distributions given host halo features [Rodrigues et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241017844R/abstract).

A persistent tension reveals that deterministic ML models often underestimate the intrinsic scatter of physical property distributions, requiring an artificial "scatter bias" re-injection to recover true underlying functions [Das et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240616103D/abstract). Models trained on one specific simulation frequently fail to generalize to others due to fundamental subgrid differences.

**Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and Image Synthesis**
Beyond tabular mock catalogs, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) successfully synthesize realistic galaxy images. For comprehensive benchmarking, mock releases like the GAMMA dataset provide 2D and 3D maps of simulated galaxy mass, metallicity, and age designed explicitly for ML evaluation [Çakır et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231206016C/abstract). Chained GAN architectures render synthetic populations that closely track real structural distributions [Fussell et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.3203F/abstract). Generative frameworks enhance morphological studies; CNNs trained on IllustrisTNG data separate post-mergers from normal star-forming galaxies better than traditional asymmetry metrics [Ferreira et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931...34F/abstract). Mock images are critical for calibrating precision cosmology, and image-to-image models can translate mock observations across photometric bands for datasets with incomplete coverage [Zaazou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250115149Z/abstract).

**Simulation-to-Observation Bridging and REALSIM**
A critical challenge is overcoming the "domain shift" between idealized simulations and noisy observations. Achieving "observational realism"—by injecting realistic sky noise and point spread functions—is far more critical to out-of-sample performance than high-resolution physical modeling [Bottrell et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.5390B/abstract). The REALSIM framework degrades idealized outputs to match survey-specific noise, enabling CNNs to achieve $87.1\%$ merger classification accuracies on survey-realistic data [Bottrell et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.5390B/abstract).

## Unsupervised and Self-Supervised Learning

The exponential growth of survey data has driven a paradigm shift in [[artificial-intelligence-and-machine-learning-in-astrophysics|astronomical machine learning]], moving away from purely supervised models toward unsupervised and [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|self-supervised learning]] (SSL). These approaches leverage massive unlabelled datasets to extract low-dimensional representations, capturing information manual feature engineering often misses. Early frameworks like PINK employed unsupervised mapping of large-scale galaxy data to generate prototypes for visualization and classification [Polsterer et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ASPC..495...81P/abstract). Unsupervised clustering of PAU survey low-resolution spectra successfully identifies starburst and elliptical galaxy groups [González-Morán et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.3569G/abstract), and tools like Fisher-EM clustering identified 47 distinct morphological clusters in the EFIGI dataset based primarily on light distribution and B/T ratios [Fraix-Burnet et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3974F/abstract). Clustering methods applied to autoencoder-extracted features have been compared against citizen scientist classifications, demonstrating their potential as a basis for generating automated, human-like morphological labels [Fielding et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022icec.confE...1F/abstract). Furthermore, the 1-DREAM toolbox extracts complex manifolds in noisy environments, proving effective for structures like jellyfish galaxies and stellar streams [Canducci et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250321584C/abstract).

**Self-Supervised and Contrastive Learning**
Self-supervised learning exploits the intrinsic structure of astronomical data to pre-train models. Contrastive learning frameworks on multiband photometry extract features that correlate strongly with redshift and morphology, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy requiring fewer labeled examples [Hayat et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...911L..33H/abstract). Contrastive networks utilizing vision transformers exceed $94\%$ accuracy on Galaxy Zoo datasets [Wei et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASP..134k4508W/abstract). Tools like MCL-Galaxy utilize momentum contrastive learning to hit $90\%$ accuracy in morphological classification entirely without labels [Shen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASP..135j4501S/abstract). In X-ray astronomy, Nearest Neighbour Contrastive Learning (NNCLR) within the ERGO-ML pipeline maps the continuous distribution of cluster morphologies from relaxed to merging states [Chadayammuri et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241022416C/abstract). SSL detects faint tidal features indicating ongoing galaxy mergers with high completeness [Desmons et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023mla..confE..11D/abstract) and enables [[foundation-models-in-astrophysics|Foundation models in astrophysics]] for wide-field radio images [Lastufka et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240806147L/abstract).

**Autoencoders and Manifold Learning**
Convolutional autoencoders (CAEs) compress raw pixel data into robust representations to improve downstream supervised tasks [Zhu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215142Z/abstract). Manifold reduction algorithms, such as SOMs, t-SNE, and LLE, cluster these latent spaces. t-SNE reveals galaxies group naturally by morphological type [Dai et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180705657D/abstract), and LLE autonomously recovers Hubble sequence classes [Semenov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241209358S/abstract). In spectra, SOMs summarize massive datasets into 2D maps, segregating star-formation histories [Teimoorinia et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AJ....163...71T/abstract). Using SOMs, researchers can predict spectral features, such as the 4000 Å break (D4000), purely from broadband colors [Jafariyazani et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967...60J/abstract). SOMs also effectively highlight selection biases in massive, dust-poor galaxy samples and analyze complex scaling relations like mass-metallicity [Sanjaripour et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..202S/abstract). Unsupervised algorithms on separation metrics matched visual catalogs with $\sim 95\%$ accuracy [Kolesnikov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528...82K/abstract). Unsupervised learning also uncovers hidden physical correlations across spatially resolved nearby galaxies [Baron et al. **Anomaly Detection and Active Learning**
Unsupervised learning targets "unknown unknowns" through anomaly detection. Incorporating the public into this active loop is highly effective; ML systems trained directly on Galaxy Zoo data reveal that accuracy strongly correlates with human consensus levels [Kuminski et al. ## Robustness, Interpretability, and Uncertainty

The predictive success of [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|deep learning]] in astrophysics is counterbalanced by its "black box" nature. For dataset purification, SOMs and Random Forests identify unrecognized galaxy blends in deep ground-based catalogs utilizing catalog-level information, specifically colors, magnitudes, and sizes [Liang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250316680L/abstract). Models must provide explainable outputs, calibrated uncertainties, and robustness against theoretical biases.

**Interpretability and Explainable AI**
To understand what drives a model's predictions, researchers employ Saliency maps and attribution techniques. Grad-CAM spatially visualizes which features influence regression targets [Wu et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...900..142W/abstract), and Shapley value explanations quantify photometric band importance [Gilda et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916...43G/abstract). Dataset Distillation offers a novel visualization strategy by synthesizing prototypical images that explicitly reveal which morphological features the neural networks leverage for classification [Guan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231117967G/abstract). Sparse Feature Networks (SFNets) extract human-understandable features without sacrificing accuracy [Wu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250100089W/abstract), and leave-one-out methods reveal self-calibration modes in cluster mass PDFs [Ntampaka et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926...45N/abstract).

**Uncertainty Quantification**
Standard networks yield miscalibrated predictions. Bayesian Convolutional Neural Networks (BCNNs) offer a framework to generate proper posterior distributions [Jones et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..159J/abstract). Techniques like CLAP resolve miscalibration without intensive computational costs [Lin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A.331L/abstract). Bayesian architectures are evaluated for out-of-distribution detection in radio galaxy classification [Mohan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240518351M/abstract).

**Adversarial Training and Perturbations**
Neural networks are vulnerable to adversarial attacks. Incorporating Adversarial training via FSGM or PGD immunizes models, leading to resilient decision boundaries [Campagne et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200210154C/abstract). Adversarial validation significantly improves generalization for [[automated-galaxy-classification|Automated galaxy classification]] models [Khramtsov et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022KosNT..28e..27K/abstract).

**Simulation Bias and Domain Shift**
Many models rely on mock training data. "Simulation bias" means a network overspecialized to one simulation fails to generalize to another or to real observations [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract), [Ntampaka et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926...45N/abstract). Models inferring parameters across disparate codes exhibit dramatic failures on outlier populations [Echeverri-Rojas et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954..125E/abstract). Injecting rigorous "observational realism" is essential for successful transfer to real data [Bottrell et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.5390B/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While [[artificial-intelligence-and-machine-learning-in-astrophysics|machine learning]] has demonstrated exceptional performance, a critical open question is resilience against "domain shift." Models overspecialized for mock simulation datasets frequently fail when evaluated on differing simulations (e.g., SIMBA versus IllustrisTNG) [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract), [Echeverri-Rojas et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954..125E/abstract), [Gluck et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.52710038G/abstract). Robust inferences require the injection of extensive "observational realism" or sophisticated domain adaptation [Bottrell et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.5390B/abstract).

To mitigate rigid data-dependence, researchers turn to transfer learning. The *Zoobot* architecture, pre-trained on $450,000$ labeled galaxies, has successfully predicted detailed morphological features for Euclid images [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240210187E/abstract). Expanding on this are [[foundation-models-in-astrophysics|Foundation models in astrophysics]], trained via self-supervised learning to extract general-purpose representations [Lastufka et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240806147L/abstract), [Lastufka et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240911175L/abstract). Multi-modal foundation models, such as Multi-Modal Masked Autoencoders (MMAE), embed imaging and spectroscopic data into shared latent spaces [Himes et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251022527H/abstract).

The maturation of adaptable models is a prerequisite for next-generation observatories like Euclid and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory. The democratization of deep learning tools highlights that while computer vision tasks largely reached maturity, refinement is necessary for complex parameter inferences [Huertas-Company et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASA...40....1H/abstract). Moving forward, the field must deploy robust, well-calibrated frameworks for real-time inference, rigorously calibrating probability density functions for epistemic uncertainties, and ensuring generative models recover intrinsic astrophysical scatter.
