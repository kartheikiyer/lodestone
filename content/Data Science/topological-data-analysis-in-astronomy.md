## Article

**Topological data analysis in astronomy** is a mathematical framework used to robustly quantify the [[multi-scale-analysis|multiscale]] connectivity, shape, and morphological features of complex astronomical datasets. Traditionally, the large-scale structure of the Universe has been analyzed using two-point statistics, such as the power spectrum and the standard [[n-point-correlation-function|N-point correlation function]]. However, because the late-time cosmic density field is highly non-linear and non-Gaussian, these conventional spatial metrics fundamentally fail to capture the complex, higher-order geometry of the cosmic web [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract). By employing tools from algebraic topology—principally [[persistent-homology|Persistent homology]] and Betti numbers—topological data analysis (TDA) provides a coordinate-free method to systematically categorize the hierarchical arrangement of dark matter and galaxies. This modern approach represents a significant analytical evolution from early global scalar metrics, such as the [[minkowski-functionals|Minkowski functionals]], allowing researchers to continuously track the "birth" and "death" of structural features across multiple spatial scales [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract).

In practice, TDA transforms discrete point clouds, such as observed galaxy catalogs or $N$-body simulation particles, into continuous topological spaces using nested geometric constructs like [[alpha-shapes-topology|Alpha Shapes (topology)]]. By continuously varying a scale parameter, the evolution of topological invariants (Betti numbers) allows astronomers to extract physically meaningful structures: 0-dimensional components, 1-dimensional loops, and 2-dimensional cavities correspond directly to the dense clusters, interconnected filaments, and cosmic voids of the cosmic web. Alongside discrete graph-theoretic methodologies like the [[minimum-spanning-tree-astronomy|Minimum Spanning Tree (astronomy)]] and local network metrics such as [[betweenness-centrality|Betweenness centrality]], these multiscale statistical summaries have proven remarkably powerful in sparse or noise-dominated regimes. 

Despite its analytical power, the application of TDA to astrophysics faces significant theoretical and computational challenges. As the field prepares for the massive influx of high-dimensional data from next-generation surveys like DESI and Euclid, researchers must grapple with "topological bias"—a well-documented phenomenon where tracers of varying masses or astrophysical states imprint distinctly different multiscale connectivity patterns on the observed large-scale structure. Resolving how this mass-dependent bias affects precision cosmology, determining the ultimate physical extent of cosmic filaments (which currently exceed $130 \, \mathrm{Mpc}$ in measured length), and optimizing [[multi-scale-analysis|Multi-scale analysis]] to overcome the high-dimensional, low-sample-size limitations inherent to both large-scale structure mapping and continuous spectral field analysis are the primary open questions driving the future of astronomical TDA.

## Foundations of Topological Data Analysis in Astrophysics

The large-scale structure of the Universe is a complex, hierarchical network of filaments, walls, and voids, governed by spatial point process models and statistical physics that dictate the galaxy distribution [Martínez et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002sgd..book.....M/abstract). Traditional statistical descriptors, such as the power spectrum or the [[n-point-correlation-function|N-point correlation function]] (specifically the two-point correlation function), are fundamentally limited in this regime because they fail to capture the higher-order, non-Gaussian connectivity of the cosmic web [Pranav et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract), [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251207236L/abstract). Topological Data Analysis (TDA) addresses this limitation by offering a robust, coordinate-free mathematical framework to quantify the shape and connectivity of data manifolds across multiple scales.

To apply TDA to discrete astronomical datasets—such as galaxy catalogs or $N$-body simulation particles—the discrete point cloud must be translated into a continuous topological space. This is achieved by constructing a simplicial complex. Common choices in cosmology include nested [[alpha-shapes-topology|Alpha Shapes (topology)]] derived from a Delaunay tessellation [Bermejo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract). Closely related is the Voronoi tessellation, which serves as a natural mathematical method for the objective space segmentation of the cosmic web pattern, as well as providing a foundation for adaptive smoothing and moving-mesh simulations [Vavilova et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021inas.book...57V/abstract). In this context, [[poisson-voronoi-tessellation|Poisson Voronoi Tessellation]] models provide a baseline for comparing observed clustering. By continuously varying a scale parameter, such as a physical linking radius or a local density threshold, the algorithm generates a nested sequence of these simplicial complexes. This sequence is known as a *filtration*. Note that TDA is often used alongside hierarchical clustering algorithms to reveal the intrinsic nesting of celestial systems across scales, from molecular clouds to galaxy clusters [Yu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&C....4100662Y/abstract).

The topological features of the complexes within a filtration are formalized by homology groups. The ranks of these groups are the Betti numbers, denoted $\beta_k$, which count the number of $k$-dimensional holes in the space. In a three-dimensional cosmological context, the Betti numbers have direct morphological interpretations: 
* $\beta_0$ represents the number of connected components (e.g., isolated clusters or dense nodes),
* $\beta_1$ counts the number of one-dimensional loops or tunnels (tracing the interconnected filamentary network),
* $\beta_2$ quantifies the number of two-dimensional enclosed voids or hollow shells [Pranav et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract), [Kono et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200602905K/abstract). 

These metrics provide a deeper and more complete description of spatial structure than traditional geometric descriptors like the [[minkowski-functionals|Minkowski functionals]] or the topological genus, as they explicitly distinguish and track individual topological features rather than returning a single global average [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract).

The core analytical engine of modern TDA is [[persistent-homology|Persistent homology]], which tracks the "birth" and "death" of these topological features as the filtration parameter evolves. Features that survive over a large range of the filtration scale are considered "persistent" and are generally associated with genuine, underlying physical structures. Conversely, short-lived features are typically attributed to shot noise or small-scale stochastic clustering. This multiscale information is often summarized in a persistence diagram or a one-dimensional Betti curve, which plots $\beta_k$ as a function of the chosen scale parameter [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251207236L/abstract).

This mathematical formalism has proven exceptionally sensitive in low-density or noise-dominated observational regimes. For example, persistent homology was used to successfully detect the Baryon Acoustic Oscillation (BAO) signal—manifesting as a characteristic topological shell ($\beta_2$) at a scale of $\sim 150 \ \text{Mpc}$—in a highly sparse sample of only 2000 quasars, a regime where standard two-point statistics struggle [Kono et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200602905K/abstract). 

## Historical Evolution: From Geometric Measures to Persistent Topology

Historically, characterizing the large-scale distribution of matter in the universe heavily relied on two-point statistics, essentially the simplest form of the [[n-point-correlation-function|N-point correlation function]]. These spatial descriptors evolved significantly over time, progressing from early power-law descriptions to the definitive detection of acoustic peaks in modern redshift surveys [Martínez et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009LNP...665..269M/abstract). While highly successful in establishing the standard cosmological model, these descriptors are intrinsically blind to the higher-order, non-Gaussian features of the complex cosmic web [Einasto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250904252E/abstract). Parallel to large-scale structural metrics, the analysis of individual galaxies similarly progressed from qualitative visual classifications to rigorous, quantitative morphometrics [Holwerda et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021gamo.book.....H/abstract). Early comprehensive frameworks and definitive treatments of observational galactic properties and stellar evolution history laid the groundwork for this shift [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract). Such texts established the primary schemes for historical galaxy morphological classification, which were critical before the transition to fully automated digital analysis paradigms [van den Bergh et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gmc..book.....V/abstract). This transition was largely accelerated by the advent of large digital databases, which led to widespread collaborative morphological projects such as Galaxy Zoo [Masters et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015HiA....16....1M/abstract). More broadly, this shift toward modern astrostatistics is the result of long-standing cross-disciplinary interactions between astronomy, mathematical statistics, and computer science [Feigelson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1004.4148F/abstract).

To capture the shape and connectivity of cosmic structures, cosmologists initially turned to integral geometry and algebraic topology. Central to this early phase were the [[minkowski-functionals|Minkowski functionals]] and the topological genus. These measures provided scalar summaries of the global geometry and connectivity of the cosmic density field, offering an early window into the topology of large-scale structure by identifying transitions between isolated clusters, interconnected filaments, and void-dominated regions. Early efforts to quantify non-linear morphological elements also utilized weighted scaling indices and constrained randomization to distinguish genuine topological features from linear surrogates [Räth et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.337..413R/abstract). Likewise, [[multi-scale-analysis|multi-scale wavelet transforms]] have historically been used as an objective method for identifying structures and voids across a range of scales in large galaxy catalogues [Escalera et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995A&A...298....1E/abstract). However, the universe is inherently multiscale. The fractal-like nesting of structures—from giant voids to local substructures—demanded mathematical tools that did not rely on a single smoothing scale or arbitrary density thresholds.

The modern era of topological data analysis in astronomy addresses these limitations by shifting the focus toward persistent topology. Emanating from Morse theory and algebraic topology, [[persistent-homology|Persistent homology]] emerged as a powerful extension and deepening of the classical topological genus and geometric Minkowski functionals [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract). Instead of measuring topology at a fixed spatial scale, persistent homology tracks the "birth" and "death" of topological features across a continuous sequence of scales, providing a parameter-free, multiscale topological outline of the point distribution [Bermejo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract). 

This transition to persistent topology has moved cosmic web analysis from qualitative morphological classification to rigorous, high-dimensional feature extraction. By moving beyond global averages, the persistence of individual topological features has proven to be remarkably robust to noise and highly sensitive to underlying physical signals, laying the foundation for modern applications in both cosmic web characterization and precision cosmology.

## Characterizing the Cosmic Web: Voids, Filaments, and Sheets

Topological Data Analysis provides a rigorous, [[multi-scale-analysis|multi-scale framework]] for classifying the hierarchical components of the cosmic web. By directly extracting higher-order topological features across varying density thresholds and spatial scales, [[persistent-homology|Persistent homology]] maps these structures systematically: 0-dimensional holes correspond to cluster nodes, 1-dimensional holes to filaments, and 2-dimensional holes to enclosed voids [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract). 

### Skeletonization and Graph-Theoretical Classifications

To explicitly map the connectivity of filamentary structures, astronomers frequently model the cosmic web as a discrete graph. Methods utilizing the $\beta$-skeleton or the [[minimum-spanning-tree-astronomy|Minimum Spanning Tree (astronomy)]] allow for the parameter-free [[galaxy-filament-finding-algorithm|skeletonization]] of the tracer distribution, providing a robust extraction of the cosmic web's underlying network [Fang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.5276F/abstract). 

Once parameterized as a network, the topology of the universe can be quantified globally and locally. The graph entropy of the cosmic web has been measured to range between $1.5$ and $3.2 \text{ bits}$, serving as a single scalar metric that is highly sensitive to the spatial number density of clustered points [Valentina García-Alvarado et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200808164V/abstract). At the local scale, graph-theoretic metrics such as Degree Centrality are utilized to systematically classify galaxies into void, wall, and cluster environments, while [[betweenness-centrality|Betweenness centrality]] isolates structural main branches from dangling leaves, and [[closeness-centrality|Closeness centrality]] distinguishes the dense kernel of a structure from its backbone and outlying fractures, all without relying on arbitrary geometric thresholds [Hong et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.1999H/abstract). 

Additional discrete lattice methods, such as mapping galaxy densities onto 2D hexagon cells, have revealed significant topological shifts between high- and low-density regions in surveys like 2MASS [Wu et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...781..115W/abstract). Furthermore, self-consistent phase-space reconstructions, such as the KIGEN-approach, offer a path toward modeling the dynamic topology of the cosmic web directly from redshift data [Kitaura et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1405.6273K/abstract). Together with geometric approaches like the Lagrangian [[v-web-threshold|V-web]], which relies on adaptive local density measurements rather than fixed grids [Fisher et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.458.1517F/abstract), these multi-scale analyses have demonstrated that major filaments and sheets in the three-dimensional cosmic web can maintain structural coherence over distances of at least $130 \, \mathrm{Mpc}$ [Sarkar et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.3227S/abstract).

### Voids and Topological Bias

While TDA excels at identifying high-density filaments, characterizing cosmic voids presents unique challenges due to severe shot noise in underdense regions. Currently, the literature is divided on the optimal void-finding methodology for sparse spectroscopic surveys. Purely topological approaches employ persistent homology directly on observed galaxy coordinates to identify statistically robust voids; in the SDSS Main Galaxy catalog, such methods have isolated voids with effective radii ranging from $21$ to $56 \, h^{-1} \, \mathrm{Mpc}$ [Aggarwal et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240901905A/abstract). Conversely, competing hybrid algorithms, such as ASTRA, argue that incorporating randomly distributed mock catalogues alongside observed data is necessary to reliably trace underdense regions that are poorly sampled by actual galaxies [Forero-Romero et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025RASTI...4...32F/abstract).

## Topological Bias and the Galaxy-Halo Connection

Galaxies and dark matter haloes are well-known to be biased tracers of the underlying matter density field. While standard linear bias is typically characterized by differences in the amplitude of the [[n-point-correlation-function|N-point correlation function]], *topological bias* refers to the phenomenon where tracers of different masses, formation histories, or astrophysical states imprint fundamentally distinct multiscale geometric patterns on the cosmic web [Bermejo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract). Because the structural connectivity of voids, walls, and filaments depends sensitively on the population of discrete tracers used to define them, quantifying this bias is essential for robust cosmological inference.

**Dark Matter Haloes and Mass Dependence**

The connectivity of the cosmic web varies systematically with dark matter halo mass. Using filtrations of Delaunay tessellations and [[alpha-shapes-topology|Alpha Shapes (topology)]] to capture multiscale topology, studies of large $N$-body simulations have revealed a tight linear scaling relation between halo mass and topological signature [Bermejo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract). Crucially, this topological bias is an environmental structure bias that remains evident even when the spatial clustering is normalized up to the second-order correlation function. 

Traditional two-point statistics often struggle to capture the complex, non-linear dependence between haloes and the underlying continuous matter field. Advanced spatial statistics, such as $k$-nearest neighbour distributions, have been shown to outperform the two-point function in identifying these tracer-field cross-correlations. For example, in noisy regimes on scales of $10 - 30 \, h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$, $k$-nearest neighbour methods can detect the statistical dependence between haloes and the underlying matter field at $>5\sigma$ significance, compared to only $\sim 1\sigma$ for the standard two-point cross-correlation [Banerjee et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4856B/abstract).

**Galaxy Formation and Feedback Physics**

Topological bias extends beyond the dark matter sector, manifesting strongly as a consequence of baryonic physics. Analyses relying on [[persistent-homology|Persistent homology]] demonstrate that the topological summary statistics—such as Betti curves, which track the abundance of connected components, loops, and voids—differ significantly between the galaxy distribution and the dark matter halo distribution [Ouellette et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.5738O/abstract). 

Furthermore, within the galaxy population itself, astrophysical states segregate topologically. In hydrodynamical frameworks like the IllustrisTNG and CAMELS-SAM simulations, star-forming and quiescent galaxies exhibit statistically distinct topological distributions. The spatial topology of quiescent galaxies, in particular, is highly sensitive to the implementation of subgrid physics, showing a strong dependence on the specific strength of supernova feedback [Ouellette et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.5738O/abstract). This indicates that topological summaries can be leveraged as novel constraints on the physics of galaxy formation and quenching mechanisms.

## Cosmological Parameter Constraints and Non-Gaussianity

The late-time distribution of matter in the Universe is a highly non-linear, non-Gaussian field. Because higher-order non-Gaussian information is largely inaccessible to traditional two-point statistics, it can instead be robustly probed via Topological Data Analysis to tighten bounds on cosmological parameters. 

Techniques such as [[persistent-homology|Persistent homology]] systematically trace the formation and destruction of isolated components, loops, and voids across different density thresholds. The resulting summary statistics, such as Betti curves, encode the spatial dimensions and structural hierarchy of the data [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract). Because these topological features arise directly from the non-linear gravitational collapse of large-scale structure, Betti curves exhibit measurable sensitivities to fundamental parameters governing the Universe's evolution. When integrating multiscale topological data with continuous fields to constrain cosmology, robust error estimation in the cross-correlation of sky maps becomes crucial, particularly for detecting signals like the Integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect using both configurations and harmonic space estimators [Cabré et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.381.1347C/abstract). Such advancements in multi-field analysis build directly upon foundational frameworks for Bayesian multilevel modeling of cosmological populations and optimal signal separation in CMB data [Hobson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009bmc..book.....H/abstract).

Recent studies have successfully employed Bayesian inference and automated machine-learning emulators to model the dependence of Betti curves on these variables. This approach yields unbiased constraints on the matter density $\Omega_m$, the clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$, and the scalar spectral index $n_s$ [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251207236L/abstract). Remarkably, integrating redshift-space distortions (RSD) into the topological analysis enhances the sensitivity of Betti curves to growth-related parameters. Joint analyses combining Betti curves with the standard power spectrum significantly tighten constraints on parameters like the dark energy equation of state $w$ compared to employing the power spectrum alone [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251207236L/abstract).

However, extracting cosmological parameters using TDA requires accurately parameterizing the aforementioned "topological bias." Because the topological signature of a tracer scales with its mass and is influenced by local baryonic feedback processes, resolving this bias is an active area of debate. Correctly modeling these effects will be critical for interpreting non-Gaussian TDA statistics in next-generation large-scale structure surveys such as DESI and Euclid [Bermejo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract).

## Graph-Theoretic and Network Approaches to Clustering

Discrete graph structures model the large-scale galaxy distribution by treating individual galaxies or dark matter haloes as nodes, with edges determined by spatial proximity or topological rules. By translating the cosmic web into a formal network, astronomers can bypass grid-based smoothing and extract structural features in a robust, mathematically rigorous manner.

A prominent example of this approach is the $\beta$-skeleton, a proximity graph that connects nodes based on the emptiness of localized intersection regions, scaled by a continuous parameter $\beta$. This methodology provides a self-tuning method to identify the [[galaxy-filament-finding-algorithm|filamentary skeleton]] of the cosmic web without the need to set arbitrary density thresholds [Fang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.5276F/abstract). The statistical properties of $\beta$-skeleton connections—such as edge lengths and directional distributions—are highly sensitive to observational and physical effects, including redshift-space distortions, the Alcock-Paczynski effect, and galaxy bias [Fang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.5276F/abstract).

Closely related is the [[minimum-spanning-tree-astronomy|Minimum Spanning Tree (astronomy)]], which connects all nodes in a dataset such that the total edge length is minimized. Advanced graph-based filament finders frequently employ minimum spanning trees to trace the underlying mass distribution. For instance, algorithms like T-ReX regularize minimum spanning trees by embedding them within a Gaussian mixture framework, allowing for the extraction of a smoothed topological prior of the cosmic web that is robust to noise and outliers [Bonnaire et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...637A..18B/abstract). Similarly, density-ranked topological network finders such as MulGuisin draw on spanning-tree logic and local density rankings to efficiently segment galaxy networks [Ju et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230103278J/abstract). To facilitate the study of these complex networks, interactive visualization tools like Vizic enable researchers to dynamically explore graph structures like the Minimum Spanning Tree and [[poisson-voronoi-tessellation|Voronoi overlays]] atop astronomical images [Yu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&C....20..128Y/abstract).

Once the cosmic web is cast as a network, local node importance can be analyzed using standard network centralities. Studies applying these metrics to large-scale structure have shown that different centrality measures naturally partition galaxies into distinct topological environments [Hong et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.1999H/abstract). Degree centrality, which counts the number of direct connections a node has, can reliably classify galaxies into voids, walls, and clusters. Meanwhile, [[betweenness-centrality|Betweenness centrality]] isolates structural "main branches" from "dangling leaves," and [[closeness-centrality|Closeness centrality]] distinguishes the highly connected "kernel" of a dense structure from its "backbone" and outlying "fractures" [Hong et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.1999H/abstract).

At the macroscopic level, the overall connectivity of the graph can be quantified using information-theoretic graph entropy. Serving as a discrete analogue to continuous connectivity scalars, graph entropy typically yields values between $1.5$ and $3.2$ bits for the cosmic web [Valentina García-Alvarado et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200808164V/abstract), [García-Alvarado et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498L.145G/abstract). However, the literature highlights a critical methodological tension regarding its stability: graph entropy is exceedingly sensitive to the spatial number density of the observed tracers. While variations in survey geometry, redshift evolution, and fundamental cosmological parameters typically induce minor entropy shifts on the order of $\sim 10^{-2}$ bits, a decreasing number density of clustered points can artificially diminish the entropy by up to $0.2$ bits [Valentina García-Alvarado et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200808164V/abstract). Consequently, tracer density variations can easily overwhelm true cosmological signals if the sampling rate is not strictly controlled during the analysis.

## Applications in the Cosmic Microwave Background and ISM

While [[persistent-homology|Persistent homology]] and graph-based approaches are most frequently applied to discrete galaxy surveys, topological and high-dimensional statistical methods have been increasingly extended to characterize continuous scalar and vector fields. In both cosmology and galactic astrophysics, these techniques are utilized to detect non-Gaussian features, isolate signal from complex noise, and trace continuous structures. 

In the study of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB), these methods are uniquely suited to probing non-Gaussianities and performing component separation. For instance, distance-based $k$-nearest neighbour statistics can evaluate the joint clustering of discrete points and continuous fields at all orders; while theoretically applicable to continuous CMB maps, tests of this framework on simulated cosmological matter fields have successfully detected statistical dependencies under highly noisy conditions at $>5\sigma$ significance, vastly outperforming traditional two-point cross-correlations [Banerjee et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4856B/abstract). Furthermore, [[multi-scale-analysis|Multi-scale analysis]] facilitates the identification of galaxy clusters via the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect. By combining wavelet-based denoising with the Joint Approximate Diagonalization of Eigenmatrices (JADE) algorithm, researchers can extract Sunyaev-Zel'dovich clusters from CMB maps with purities exceeding $90\%$, providing a robust, parameter-free alternative to traditional matched-filter algorithms [Novaes et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...545A..34N/abstract). Sparse geometric representations, such as ridgelets and curvelets, have also been proposed to identify anisotropic topological defects, such as cosmic strings, imprinted on the CMB [Starck et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0903.3383S/abstract). Historically, cosmic vacuum strings arising from global symmetry breaking have been extensively investigated as potential seeds for the density fluctuations that ultimately lead to galaxy formation [Vilenkin et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982PhRvL..48.1867V/abstract). In the context of an open universe, defect models normalized to COBE observations have been shown to predict galaxy power spectra that are consistent with broad spatial surveys without necessitating the assumption of extreme galaxy bias [Spergel et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994NuPhS..35...17S/abstract). Furthermore, related physical topological defects, such as cosmic necklaces and monopolonia, have been proposed as potential sources for ultra-high energy cosmic rays within the Galactic halo [Berezinsky et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999NuPhS..75..119B/abstract). Beyond cosmic web connectivity, physical topological defects like electroweak magnetic monopoles and Z-strings are predicted in several cosmological extensions and may yield a significant flux of antiprotons in our galaxy [Kephart et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996PhLB..388..481K/abstract).

On galactic scales, the study of the interstellar medium and molecular clouds often relies on highly complex 3D spectral data cubes generated by instruments like ALMA or integral field spectrographs. These datasets frequently present a high-dimensional low sample size (HDLSS) problem, where the data dimensionality $d$ vastly exceeds the number of independent spatial samples $n$ [Takeuchi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271...44T/abstract). In such regimes, traditional dimensionality reduction discards vital kinematic and structural information. Shapelets, a basis of weighted Hermite polynomials, provide a localized multi-scale framework for analyzing the complex morphology of galaxies and gravitational lensing features [Refregier et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.338...35R/abstract). Recently, this localized framework has been extended into four-dimensional phase space through the use of Wigner Function Shapelets, which better capture the spatial coherence and intricate kinematics of galaxy morphology [Arai et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260201141A/abstract). In parallel, archetypal analysis has been introduced to decompose galaxy spectra into pure representative types, accounting for the superposition of stellar populations and nuclear activity [Chan et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.338..790C/abstract).

To map the local structure of continuous velocity and emission fields, sophisticated variants of principal component analysis—such as PCA tomography and noise-reduction PCA (NRPCA)—are employed. PCA tomography has been successfully utilized to decompose dense data cubes into physically interpretable structures, such as isolating active galactic nuclei emission scattered within ionization cones in galaxies like NGC 7097 [Ricci et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...734L..10R/abstract). Similarly, in starburst galaxies like NGC 253, applying NRPCA to ALMA maps allows for the extraction of subtle spectral features. This high-dimensional approach effectively characterizes the complex spatial structure of global turbulent outflows, successfully disentangling them from the dominant systemic rotation of the galaxy [Takeuchi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271...44T/abstract).

## High-Dimensional Statistics and Sparse Data Challenges

Modern cosmological surveys yield datasets characterized by immense volume and high dimensionality, yet the spatial distribution of discrete tracers—such as galaxies or quasars—is frequently sparse and subject to complex, heteroscedastic noise. Overcoming these fundamental 21st-century challenges in astrostatistics requires new methods for treating heteroscedastic measurement errors and detecting faint signals embedded within complex noise [Feigelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AnRSA...8..493F/abstract). This includes addressing the specific mathematical challenges posed by anisotropic spatial point processes in galaxy clustering, where rigorous statistical methodologies are necessary to link discrete observational data to complex, non-linear astrophysical models [Feigelson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0903.0416F/abstract). In this regime of astrostatistics, traditional metrics like the power spectrum or standard [[n-point-correlation-function|N-point correlation function]]s often discard crucial non-linear information or fail to extract statistically significant signals. To rigorously handle these survey artifacts, techniques such as eigenmode analysis provide a formal framework for evaluating correlation matrices in deep redshift surveys while explicitly accounting for selection effects and peculiar velocity distortions [Matsubara et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...606....1M/abstract). Direct harmonic analysis of discrete galaxy tracers can avoid the aliasing and window function issues inherent to pixelized map-based approaches [Baleato Lizancos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...05..010B/abstract).

A primary computational strength of topological data analysis is preserving signal fidelity in low-density distributions. When applying TDA to identify underdense features, sparse sampling presents a persistent algorithmic challenge. While pure persistence-based algorithms use only the available discrete tracers to locate voids [Aggarwal et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240901905A/abstract), alternative hybrid methods, such as the ASTRA algorithm, employ stochastic topological ranking alongside random simulated catalogs to better trace regions poorly sampled by actual galaxies [Forero-Romero et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025RASTI...4...32F/abstract). The literature currently remains divided on whether pure topological approaches or hybrid probabilistic methods offer the most unbiased void catalogs for sparse surveys. 

Beyond spatial clustering, astronomical feature extraction often suffers from the High-Dimensional Low Sample Size (HDLSS) problem, where the number of dimensions $d$ greatly exceeds the number of samples $n$ ($n \ll d$). Such data—common in high-resolution spectral mapping and functional data analysis—render traditional dimensionality reduction ineffective. Recent applications of high-dimensional statistics, such as noise-reduction principal component analysis (NRPCA), have successfully isolated complex kinematic structures in HDLSS data, bypassing the ill-posed nature of classical covariance estimation [Takeuchi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271...44T/abstract). Similarly, Bayesian functional data analysis (FDA) addresses the challenge of analyzing large ensembles of functions (like light curves and spectra) that are subject to sparse or irregular sampling [Loredo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240814466L/abstract).

Analyzing the intrinsic properties of galaxies introduces further high-dimensional challenges. When attempting to recover structural parameters from heavily noise-dominated observations, advanced generative techniques such as score-based likelihoods and diffusion model priors have emerged as a powerful Bayesian framework for deconvolving noisy images of faint galaxy populations [Adam et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....169..254A/abstract). Furthermore, principal curves (P-curves) have been proposed as a non-linear dimensionality reduction technique to rank and classify galaxies by an arc-length value through a high-dimensional property space [Taghizadeh-Popp et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...755..143T/abstract). Local two-sample hypothesis testing allows for the identification of regions in high-dimensional morphological space where populations, such as high-mass versus low-mass galaxies, significantly differ [Freeman et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471.3273F/abstract). Furthermore, machine learning pipelines are increasingly adopting rotation-invariant self-organizing maps, such as PINK, which utilize GPU acceleration to perform morphological classification of billions of objects from all-sky surveys [Polsterer et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ascl.soft10001P/abstract).

Finally, the computational bottleneck of non-linear manifold learning and topological techniques poses a significant barrier. Methods like Spectral Connectivity Analysis (SCA) and diffusion maps, used for tasks such as empirical photometric redshift estimation, rely on eigen-decompositions that scale poorly, typically limiting training sets to $<10^4$ objects [Freeman et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.2012F/abstract). To apply these methods to survey-scale data, astrostatisticians employ the Nyström extension, an algebraic approximation that estimates diffusion coordinates for out-of-sample objects without recomputing the entire matrix [Freeman et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.2012F/abstract). However, care must be taken: empirical high-dimensional embedding methods remain susceptible to attenuation bias, where measurement errors systematically distort the underlying manifold, leading to systematic offsets in extrapolated physical parameters [Freeman et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.2012F/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The transition of cosmology into an era of massive, high-dimensional datasets driven by large-scale surveys such as DESI, Euclid, and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory necessitates the scaling of [[multi-scale-analysis|multi-scale topological algorithms]] [Einasto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250904252E/abstract), [Bermejo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract). As these next-generation surveys map the universe with unprecedented volume and density, several open questions and methodological challenges define the frontier of topological data analysis in astronomy.

A major unresolved challenge is the quantitative impact of "topological bias"—the phenomenon wherein mass-dependent tracers, such as dark matter haloes, or distinct galaxy populations (e.g., star-forming versus quiescent) exhibit intrinsically different connectivity signatures [Bermejo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract), [Ouellette et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.5738O/abstract). While this bias exhibits a linear mass-scaling relation, its exact effect on precision cosmological constraints remains an open question. Disentangling this topological bias from redshift-space distortions (RSD) and complex survey selection effects is critical. Interestingly, while RSD typically act as a contaminant in standard clustering analyses, incorporating RSD into [[persistent-homology|Persistent homology]] summaries such as Betti curves can actually enhance sensitivity to structure growth parameters [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251207236L/abstract). Similarly, graph-theoretic properties like those of the $\beta$-skeleton also display a clear dependence on RSD [Fang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.5276F/abstract). Determining whether TDA can consistently mitigate these observational effects to yield robust, unbiased parameter estimations is a primary goal for the field.

The integration of unsupervised machine learning with TDA represents another major direction. Machine learning emulators trained on topological summaries are being developed to accelerate Bayesian parameter inference [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251207236L/abstract). Unsupervised machine learning techniques, such as Random Forests and clustering algorithms, are increasingly applied to automated morphological classification and the identification of rare outlier galaxies [Baron et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4530B/abstract), [Tohill et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230211482T/abstract). 

In the realm of information theory, while metrics like graph entropy successfully quantify cosmic web connectivity, their acute sensitivity to tracer number density fuels an ongoing algorithmic debate regarding the optimal identification of low-density environments like voids. The literature remains divided between purely persistence-based void finders that rely solely on tracer particles [Aggarwal et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240901905A/abstract) and hybrid stochastic methods that inject random catalogs to better trace underdense regions [Forero-Romero et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025RASTI...4...32F/abstract). 

Finally, the ultimate physical scale of the cosmic web remains unconstrained. While statistical shuffles of SDSS data have confirmed that coherent filaments and sheets extend to at least $130 \, \mathrm{Mpc}$ [Sarkar et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.3227S/abstract), the finite volume of current survey limits prevents the detection of larger structures. Applying scalable TDA methods to upcoming deep-field data will be essential to determining the maximum topological extent of large-scale structure.
