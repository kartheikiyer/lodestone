## Article

**GPU acceleration in astronomy** refers to the utilization of the massively parallel architecture of Graphics Processing Units (GPUs)—which feature thousands of concurrent processing cores—to execute computationally intensive astrophysical calculations. As modern observational astronomy transitions into a petabyte-scale discipline driven by facilities like the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, traditional CPU-bound data pipelines have become a prohibitive bottleneck. Historically, GPUs were first adopted by astronomers to resolve "embarrassingly parallel" numerical workloads. Early applications exploited GPU architecture to perform brute-force N-body simulations, execute exact inverse ray-shooting for unapproximated microlensing magnification maps [Thompson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ascl.soft03001T/abstract), and conduct real-time, incoherent de-dispersion of massive data streams to detect millisecond radio transients and pulsars [Armour et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASPC..461...33A/abstract), [Ng et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..291...53N/abstract). 

The discipline experienced a profound paradigm shift when hardware capabilities—evolving from the [[maxwell-gpu-architecture|Maxwell GPU architecture]] up through the [[volta-gpu-architecture|Volta GPU architecture]] and modern tensor-core accelerators—converged with the widespread adoption of [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|Machine Learning and Neural Networks in Astrophysics]]. Today, GPU-accelerated deep learning models, such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), routinely achieve processing speedups of $90\times$ to $3000\times$ over legacy CPU codes for tasks ranging from cosmic ray rejection and image deblurring to automated galaxy morphology classification [Tuccillo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475..894T/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889...24Z/abstract). Furthermore, the recent rise of differentiable programming (using libraries like JAX and PyTorch) leverages GPU-accelerated automatic differentiation to evaluate complex cosmological likelihoods. This enables highly scalable Bayesian inference methods, such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC), permitting the calculation of high-order cosmological statistics and stellar population synthesis (SPS) models at speeds that were previously computationally impossible. 

Beyond raw numerical computation and automated classification, the massive memory bandwidth and rendering power of GPUs are now driving advanced 3D visualization and immersive Virtual Reality (VR) environments, which allow astronomers to intuitively interrogate faint morphological features within complex spatial-kinematic data cubes. Despite these dramatic computational gains, the integration of GPU-accelerated workflows introduces several critical open questions. Chief among these is the challenge of "domain shift"—the performance degradation that occurs when neural networks trained on synthetic simulations are deployed on real-world observational data with distinct noise profiles. The article below explores these advanced applications, while also detailing ongoing efforts to extract well-calibrated uncertainties from "black-box" machine learning models, improve the physical interpretability of deep learning architectures, and evaluate the viability of emerging alternative hardware modalities like Graphcore Intelligence Processing Units (IPUs).

## Historical Evolution of GPU Usage in Astronomy

Astronomy entered the big data era in the early 1990s with the advent of the first digital sky surveys. These projects produced terabyte-sized datasets that necessitated the first wave of automated data processing for fundamental tasks such as star-galaxy separation [Djorgovski et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221201493D/abstract). During this period, automated analysis largely relied on manual feature engineering and classical statistical methods—such as [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|Support Vector Machines (SVMs)]]—executed on standard central processing units (CPUs) [González et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&C....25..103G/abstract). 

The subsequent rise of high-cadence time-domain astronomy and synoptic sky surveys precipitated a dramatic transition from Terascale to Petascale data volumes [Djorgovski et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221201493D/abstract). Next-generation observatories, such as the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, are projected to yield exabytes of data, rendering traditional CPU-based analysis and human-aided refinement pipelines mathematically and practically impossible to scale [Jarrett et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3700502J/abstract), [Ndung'u et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NewAR..9701685N/abstract). To manage this historical data tsunami [Cavuoti et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..306..307C/abstract), the astronomical community was forced to adopt highly parallelized hardware architectures.

Initial adoptions of graphics processing units (GPUs) in astrophysics focused on "embarrassingly parallel" algorithmic bottlenecks. Early GPU codes relied on brute-force numerical calculations, exploiting early NVIDIA architectures to perform real-time, incoherent de-dispersion of millisecond radio transients (e.g., the ARTEMIS pipeline) [Armour et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASPC..461...33A/abstract). Similarly, GPUs were rapidly adopted for exact, unapproximated inverse ray-shooting to generate cosmological microlensing magnification maps [Thompson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ascl.soft03001T/abstract). 

The most profound historical pivot occurred when hardware advancements—progressing through the [[maxwell-gpu-architecture|Maxwell GPU architecture]] and up to the [[volta-gpu-architecture|Volta GPU architecture]] and beyond—converged with the widespread adoption of Machine Learning and Neural Networks in Astrophysics. As the complexity of multi-parameter spatial-kinematic data grew, researchers moved away from manual feature extraction. Morphological classification historically relied heavily on expert visual inspection or crowd-sourced citizen science (e.g., Galaxy Zoo). However, modern automated data processing leverages GPU-accelerated deep learning frameworks, such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and the YOLO real-time object detection system [González et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&C....25..103G/abstract), [Ndung'u et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NewAR..9701685N/abstract). This era also saw the broader application of machine learning for diverse classifying tasks, such as distinguishing low-mass X-ray binaries (LMXBs) into those hosting neutron stars versus black holes [Kembhavi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JApA...43...76K/abstract).

This shift to deep learning has yielded orders-of-magnitude improvements in throughput. For instance, single high-end GPUs can now evaluate complex neural networks to process standard Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) images in approximately $50\text{ ms}$ [González et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&C....25..103G/abstract). Consequently, the historical trajectory of computational astronomy has moved from a reliance on human-aided statistical heuristics to end-to-end, GPU-driven automated pipelines. Today, for real-time transient detection and massive demographic surveys, GPU acceleration is no longer considered a supplementary optimization, but an absolute requirement for modern discovery [Jarrett et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3700502J/abstract).

## High-Throughput Signal Processing and Radio Transients

The advent of next-generation facilities such as the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) and the Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder (ASKAP) has driven radio astronomy into a petabyte- to exabyte-scale data regime [Ndung'u et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NewAR..9701685N/abstract). Processing these massive data streams with traditional CPU-based pipelines is computationally prohibitive. Consequently, GPU acceleration has become a strict requirement for high-throughput time-domain signal processing and real-time interferometric analysis.

In time-domain astronomy, detecting millisecond transients—such as pulsars and fast radio bursts—requires continuous, real-time corrections for the frequency-dependent delays introduced by the interstellar medium. This process, known as de-dispersion, is highly parallelizable. The ARTEMIS software pipeline demonstrates this by utilizing GPU architectures to perform real-time, brute-force incoherent de-dispersion, effectively removing interstellar dispersion to detect millisecond radio bursts without bottlenecks [Armour et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASPC..461...33A/abstract). Similarly, the High Time Resolution Universe (HTRU) survey relies on computationally demanding segmented search techniques to comb through petabyte-sized data streams, optimizing the probability of discovering highly-accelerated relativistic binaries, such as pulsar-black hole systems [Ng et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..291...53N/abstract). In the multi-messenger domain, future work is planned at facilities like IceCube to apply [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|deep learning methods]] to improve the modeling of detection media and aid in the search for extra-galactic cosmic sources [Montaruli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230106320M/abstract). Furthermore, machine learning and spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting methods have been successfully deployed to rapidly measure the redshifts of gamma-ray burst (GRB) afterglows and their host galaxies [Li et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023FrASS..1024317L/abstract).

Beyond time-domain searches, GPUs are deeply integrated into the spatial and kinematic analysis of 3D radio interferometry data cubes. Searching for faint morphological features, such as extra-planar H I gas or tidal filaments, is challenging because the signal-to-noise ratio in the outer regions of galaxies routinely drops to $\sim 1$ [Punzo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&C....17..163P/abstract). GPU-accelerated intensity-driven gradient filters enable the real-time, interactive 3D visualization of these faint kinematic structures without degrading the spatial resolution of the brighter, high signal-to-noise source components [Punzo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&C....17..163P/abstract). Similarly, in the optical and near-infrared regimes, GPU acceleration via PyTorch is leveraged by the `capivara` package for the rapid spectral segmentation and decomposition of Integral Field Unit (IFU) data cubes [de Souza et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241021962D/abstract). For massive spectroscopic surveys, deep learning tools like GaSNet-II utilize desktop GPUs to process up to 40,000 spectra per minute, performing spectral classification and redshift predictions to enable real-time analysis for Stage-IV facilities [Zhong et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532..643Z/abstract).

The sheer volume of radio images has also necessitated the widespread adoption of Machine Learning and Neural Networks in Astrophysics for automated source extraction and cataloging. Deep learning algorithms, such as the YOLOv5 object detection model, have been deployed on GPUs to simultaneously localize and classify Fanaroff-Riley (FR) radio galaxies (e.g., FR0, FRI, and FRII morphologies) with a mean average precision ($\text{mAP}@0.5$) of $89.4\%$ [Zhang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASP..134f4503Z/abstract).

Despite these successes, the literature highlights a significant ongoing tension: the standard deep learning models used for radio morphological classification often act as "black boxes" and lack sufficient uncertainty calibration, which is a critical requirement for rigorous scientific adoption [Huertas-Company et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASA...40....1H/abstract). To address this, the community is actively evaluating Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs). By modeling the posterior distribution of network weights, BNNs provide a mathematically principled mechanism to quantify predictive uncertainty and detect distribution shifts between training simulations and real-world radio observations [Mohan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240518351M/abstract).

## Accelerated N-body Simulations and Gravitational Modeling

The calculation of gravitational interactions in dense stellar environments and large-scale structures scales as $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$. This presents an "embarrassingly parallel" workload that is ideally suited for the thousands of concurrent threads available on modern graphics processing units. To exploit this architecture, numerical libraries such as `phi-GPU` (Parallel Hermite Integration on GPU) have been developed to model the highly non-linear dynamical evolution of star clusters and galaxies [Berczik et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ascl.soft06027B/abstract). Utilizing CUDA on NVIDIA hardware, `phi-GPU` achieves high-precision stellar dynamics by implementing a fourth-order Hermite integration scheme. It further optimizes computational efficiency by utilizing hierarchical individual block time-steps and directly incorporating external gravitational fields. 

Beyond direct N-body integration, GPUs have revolutionized the computational tractability of gravitational lensing models. Modeling the magnification patterns produced by an ensemble of stellar-mass compact objects within a foreground macrolens galaxy requires tracing millions of light rays backwards from the observer to the source plane. This inverse ray-shooting technique is highly computationally intensive but perfectly suited for brute-force parallelization. The `GPU-D` software package leverages this by implementing exact inverse ray-shooting, generating cosmological microlensing magnification maps by solving the gravitational lens equation without relying on traditional numerical approximations [Thompson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ascl.soft03001T/abstract). Similarly, GPU-accelerated pipelines enable the rapid processing of ongoing microlensing events, facilitating the real-time identification and characterization of cool exoplanets orbiting low-mass stars in the Galaxy using codes like MORSE [McDougall et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456..565M/abstract). Beyond microlensing, [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|convolutional neural networks (CNNs)]] have been successfully trained on massive astronomical images to automate the identification of strong gravitational lens systems in simulated data representing Euclid-like and KiDS-like surveys [Davies et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.5263D/abstract).

Advances in Machine Learning and Neural Networks in Astrophysics have further augmented gravitational modeling, allowing researchers to map the three-dimensional gravitational potential of the Milky Way without assuming strict prior symmetries. For example, the `ClearPotential` framework uses normalizing flows to solve the equilibrium collisionless Boltzmann equation for Gaia DR3 phase-space data. By treating the potential as a fully differentiable neural network—a method commonly deployed alongside GPU architectures to handle its computational scale—this data-driven model yields a highly precise local dark matter density at the Solar radius of $(0.84 \pm 0.08) \times 10^{-2}\, M_\odot/\mathrm{pc}^3$ [Putney et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209989P/abstract).

Despite these computational advancements, inferring the fundamental nature of galactic gravitational acceleration from observed kinematics remains an active subject of debate. Attempts to use symbolic regression algorithms to model the Radial Acceleration Relation (RAR) of late-type galaxies have yielded ambiguous results regarding the necessity of dark matter versus modified gravity. Specifically, exhaustive algorithmic searches of functional parameter spaces indicate that current observational data (such as the SPARC dataset) lack the dynamical range and precision required to confirm the deep-MOND (Modified Newtonian Dynamics) limit:
$$ g_\text{obs}\propto \sqrt{g_\text{bar}} $$
as the baryonic acceleration $g_\text{bar} \to 0$. The literature explicitly cautions that this limited precision precludes definitive proof of law-like modified gravitational behavior over alternative functional forms, maintaining a tension in theoretical interpretations of galactic dynamics [Desmond et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1817D/abstract).

## Deep Learning for Galaxy Morphology and Image Classification

The sheer volume of data generated by next-generation facilities, such as the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST), has catalyzed a paradigm shift from human-aided morphological classification to fully automated, high-throughput pipelines [Jarrett et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3700502J/abstract), [Ndung'u et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NewAR..9701685N/abstract). To process these petabyte-scale data streams in feasible timeframes, GPU acceleration combined with [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|Machine Learning and Neural Networks in Astrophysics]] has become strictly necessary. Comprehensive guides to quantitative morphometrics underscore the necessity of these automated techniques as near-future surveys prepare to catalog millions of galaxies [Holwerda et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021gamo.book.....H/abstract).

For rapid source detection and morphological classification, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and real-time object detection architectures like YOLO have proven highly effective. For example, the AstroCV repository provides open-source, C- and CUDA-based algorithms that utilize YOLO for end-to-end galaxy detection and classification. Running on a single high-end NVIDIA GPU, these models can process a Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) image in $50\text{ ms}$ and a Dark Energy Camera (DECam) image in under $3\text{ seconds}$ [González et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&C....25..103G/abstract). To process entire all-sky surveys, GPU-accelerated frameworks like PINK deploy rotation- and flipping-invariant self-organizing maps for automated, unsupervised morphological analysis [Polsterer et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ascl.soft10001P/abstract).

Beyond broad classification, GPU-accelerated CNNs are increasingly deployed for quantitative structural profiling. The DeepLeGATo framework utilizes CNNs to derive 2D photometric surface brightness profiles, successfully retrieving one-component Sérsic parameters—such as effective radius, Sérsic index, and axis ratio. By exploiting GPU parallelization, DeepLeGATo operates approximately 3000 times faster than the traditional CPU-based code GALFIT, with comparable or superior accuracy on isolated galaxies [Tuccillo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475..894T/abstract). Similarly, modern tensor-based tools like galmoss achieve computational speeds that are $6\times$ faster than default implementations, capable of fitting thousands of SDSS profiles in roughly 10 minutes on a single A100 GPU [Chen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&C....4700825C/abstract). Beyond simple profile extraction, the ASTROPHOT package acts as a GPU-accelerated, PyTorch-based solver capable of performing joint forward optimization of the sky, stars, and galaxy profiles simultaneously [Stone et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.6377S/abstract). Moreover, morphological analysis is increasingly coupled with distance estimation. Preliminary multi-survey computer vision models, such as Mantis Shrimp, are currently in development to explore feature fusion across UV, optical, and IR imagery, and to investigate model interpretability for photometric redshift estimation across diverse datasets [Engel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240203535E/abstract).

## Differentiable Programming and Bayesian Inference

The transition from random-walk Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods to gradient-based Bayesian parameter estimation represents a major paradigm shift in computational astrophysics. By leveraging differentiable programming frameworks such as JAX and PyTorch, researchers can compute exact derivatives of complex physical models with respect to their input parameters via automatic differentiation (autodiff). When coupled with GPU acceleration, autodiff enables highly scalable inference algorithms, such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) and Variational Inference, which were previously computationally prohibitive for high-dimensional astrophysical models (see [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|Machine Learning and Neural Networks in Astrophysics]]).

In the cosmological domain, this approach has driven the development of end-to-end differentiable libraries like JAX-COSMO [Campagne et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..15C/abstract). JAX-COSMO computes background evolution and non-linear power spectra while allowing exact, tuning-free Fisher matrix evaluations and HMC sampling directly on the GPU. Dramatic performance gains are also seen in high-order summary statistics. For example, the SwiftCl pipeline computes 120 angular power spectra beyond the Limber approximation in just 5 milliseconds on a single GPU core—roughly a 40-fold speedup over previous state-of-the-art non-differentiable codes [Reymond et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522718R/abstract). Similarly, PyBird-JAX generates one-loop Effective Field Theory of Large-Scale Structure (EFTofLSS) redshift-space galaxy power spectrum multipoles in 0.2 milliseconds, achieving a speedup of $10^3$ to $10^4$ over its CPU-based predecessor [Reeves et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250720990R/abstract).

Parallel breakthroughs have occurred in the modeling of galaxy spectral energy distributions (SEDs). Stellar Population Synthesis (SPS) is notoriously computationally intensive, creating a primary bottleneck when inferring physical properties across billion-galaxy datasets. The DSPS (Differentiable Stellar Population Synthesis) package natively implements SPS in JAX, providing exact gradients for parameters governing star formation history, stellar metallicity, and dust attenuation [Hearin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1741H/abstract). On modern GPUs, forward-modeling synthetic galaxy colors with DSPS is 300 to 400 times faster than standard CPU-based SPS codes [Hearin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1741H/abstract). Neural network emulators, such as SPECULATOR, provide an alternative autodiff-compatible approach, substituting direct physical computations with parameterized neural networks to achieve empirical speedups of $10^3$ to $10^4$ [Alsing et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..249....5A/abstract). By parallelizing individual chains across independent GPU threads, modern HMC pipelines can now infer Bayesian posteriors at a rate of approximately 1,000 galaxies per GPU-minute [Zacharegkas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025OJAp....851255Z/abstract). Beyond cosmology and SED modeling, Bayesian inference is crucial for demographic studies. Specialized GPU-accelerated C++ frameworks now implement advanced algorithms, such as Metropolis-Hastings-within-Gibbs sampling, to estimate galaxy luminosity functions from flux-limited observations while correctly accounting for photometric noise and Eddington bias [Szalai-Gindl et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&C....25..247S/abstract).

While the vast majority of these differentiable pipelines are heavily optimized for NVIDIA hardware (such as the [[volta-gpu-architecture|Volta GPU architecture]]), the escalating computational demands of stage-IV surveys have spurred exploratory benchmarks on alternative accelerator modalities. Initial studies indicate that architectures like Graphcore Intelligence Processing Units (IPUs) may provide a viable long-term alternative to standard GPUs for cosmological inference and deep learning tasks, though the community consensus on optimal hardware remains unresolved as the ecosystem rapidly evolves [Arcelin et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210602465A/abstract).

## Advanced Image Restoration and Signal Recovery

As observational astronomy enters an era of petabyte-scale surveys, traditional CPU-bound algorithms for image processing are increasingly becoming computational bottlenecks. To address this, the field has rapidly adopted [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|Machine Learning and Neural Networks in Astrophysics]], leveraging the highly parallel architecture of GPUs to perform advanced image restoration, artefact removal, and signal recovery at unprecedented speeds.

A primary application of GPU-accelerated deep learning is the identification and removal of detector artefacts, such as cosmic rays. Historically, this task relied on iterative, CPU-based algorithms like LACosmic, which scale poorly on massive datasets. The deepCR framework provides a neural alternative for both cosmic ray identification and subsequent image inpainting [Zhang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889...24Z/abstract). Operating on a single GPU, deepCR can predict masks up to $90\times$ faster than a multicore CPU implementation of LACosmic. At a fixed false-positive rate of $0.5\%$, the model achieves near $100\%$ detection rates in extragalactic and globular cluster fields, and $91\%$ in resolved galaxy fields. Furthermore, the GPU-accelerated image inpainting yields mean squared errors (MSE) that are significantly improved over non-neural techniques, reducing MSE by a factor of $2.5$ in extragalactic fields and up to $20$ in globular cluster fields [Zhang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889...24Z/abstract).

Beyond artefact removal, generative models are increasingly used for deblurring and resolution enhancement. Atmospheric seeing inherently degrades ground-based astronomical images, complicating the morphological classification of distant galaxies. To mitigate this, algorithms like SeeingGAN utilize Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to map blurred ground-based observations—such as those from the Subaru Telescope—to a quasi-space-based resolution mimicking the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) [Gan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210309711G/abstract). This deblurring technique provides up to an $18\%$ improvement in the Complex Wavelet Structural Similarity Index (CW-SSIM), effectively recovering fine galactic structures without the need for expensive space-based follow-up [Gan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210309711G/abstract).

GPU acceleration also facilitates the automated recovery of extremely faint physical features, such as the intracluster light (ICL) in galaxy clusters. It is also being developed to recover the types of faint tidal features and archaeological records of galaxy collisions that have historically been mapped by manual deep imaging surveys, such as the MATLAS survey of massive elliptical galaxies [Bilek et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021POBeo.100..211B/abstract). ICL measurement traditionally requires laborious manual preprocessing due to its extremely low surface brightness. Recent machine learning approaches eliminate this manual bottleneck; fully automated models can now process up to $500$ Hyper Suprime-Cam images in a matter of seconds on a single GPU [Canepa et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..245C/abstract).

However, models trained exclusively on simulations often suffer performance degradation when applied to real observational data characterized by complex instrumental noise [Ćiprijanović et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201103591C/abstract). To reliably generalize these models, researchers must employ domain adaptation techniques. For instance, the automated ICL model can be fine-tuned on a small sample of $101$ real clusters to successfully bridge this domain gap, while fine-tuning the model for new imaging surveys, such as LSST, takes merely three minutes when accelerated by a GPU [Canepa et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..245C/abstract). 

## Interactive Visualization and Virtual Reality

The advent of major wide-field and high-resolution observatories has transitioned astrophysics into a remarkably data-rich discipline, producing datasets of unprecedented volume and multi-dimensional complexity. In particular, the spatial-kinematic data cubes generated by radio interferometers and integral field spectrographs require novel approaches to data interrogation. While automated pipelines—often driven by [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|Machine Learning and Neural Networks in Astrophysics]]—are highly efficient at bulk classification and anomaly detection, the careful disentanglement of complex physical behaviors, systematics, and false positives still necessitates direct human inspection [Jarrett et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3700502J/abstract). To address this, researchers are increasingly relying on GPU acceleration to power advanced **interactive visualization** and immersive virtual environments.

One of the primary challenges in visual data interrogation is the detection of morphologically complex, low-surface-brightness structures, such as extra-planar gas, tidal tails, and filaments. In neutral hydrogen (H I) surveys from instruments like APERTIF and ASKAP, the signal-to-noise ratio ($\text{S/N}$) of these structures typically drops from $\sim 10$ in the inner galactic regions to $\sim 1$ in the outskirts [Punzo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&C....17..163P/abstract). To isolate these features, Punzo et al. [Punzo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&C....17..163P/abstract) investigated the use of optimized **3D kernels** for data filtering. They demonstrated that an intensity-driven gradient filter serves as the optimal choice, adaptively enhancing the $\text{S/N}$ of faint components without degrading the spatial resolution of the high-$\text{S/N}$ emission. Because the manual tuning and application of these filters across large 3D data cubes is computationally expensive, the algorithms were accelerated using multi-core CPUs (OpenMP) and GPUs (OpenGL). This GPU-accelerated filtering was subsequently embedded into the **SlicerAstro** software, providing a 3D visualization environment that supports the real-time, interactive exploration of faint H I structures [Punzo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&C....17..163P/abstract).

Beyond desktop-based interactive rendering, the availability of mainstream **Virtual Reality** (VR) headsets and the massive parallel processing power of modern graphics hardware (such as the [[volta-gpu-architecture|Volta GPU architecture]] or the earlier [[maxwell-gpu-architecture|Maxwell GPU architecture]]) have enabled a paradigm shift toward fully immersive data exploration. Because astrophysical data is inherently multi-parameter, stereoscopic VR environments allow researchers to intuitively leverage human visual perception to interrogate 3D spatial and kinematic relationships that are easily obscured in 2D projections. 

To facilitate this, Jarrett et al. [Jarrett et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3700502J/abstract) developed the **iDaVIE** (Immersive Data Visualisation Interactive Explorer) software suite. Built upon video game development engines, iDaVIE exploits GPU rendering to maintain the high frame rates necessary for a seamless VR experience while handling massive astronomical data cubes. The software allows researchers to interactively step through multi-dimensional parameter spaces to study galaxy evolution, cosmic large-scale structure, and the kinematics of interacting galaxies [Jarrett et al. ## Hardware Benchmarking and Emerging Architectures

The processing demands of petabyte-scale astronomical surveys have established hardware acceleration as a prerequisite for modern data analysis. While graphics processing units (GPUs) currently dominate the field, the computational scaling requirements of emerging algorithms have prompted the evaluation of novel hardware architectures.

**CUDA and Computational Scaling**
Traditional numerical tasks, such as high-order Hermite integration for star cluster evolution and inverse ray-shooting for microlensing magnification maps, are often "embarrassingly parallel." These algorithms are highly optimized for NVIDIA's CUDA platform, allowing for exact, brute-force numerical solutions without approximation [Thompson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ascl.soft03001T/abstract), [Berczik et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ascl.soft06027B/abstract). 

For high-order spatial statistics, algorithmic complexity remains a primary bottleneck. By expanding N-point correlation functions in a separable basis of isotropic functions, GPU-accelerated estimators like ENCORE reduce the computational scaling for $N_\mathrm{g}$ particles to:
$$ \mathcal{O}(N_\mathrm{g}^2) $$
or, when utilizing Fast Fourier Transforms with $N_\mathrm{FFT}$ grid points, to $\mathcal{O}(N_\mathrm{FFT}\log N_\mathrm{FFT})$ [Philcox et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.2457P/abstract). 

When applied to [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|Machine Learning and Neural Networks in Astrophysics]], GPU deployment yields dramatic improvements in computational scaling compared to traditional CPU setups. For example, deep learning models for surface brightness profile fitting (DeepLeGATo) execute up to $3000\times$ faster on GPUs than standard CPU-based GALFIT implementations [Tuccillo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475..894T/abstract). Similarly, the deepCR framework achieves a $90\times$ speedup on a single GPU over the multicore CPU software LACosmic for cosmic ray rejection [Zhang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889...24Z/abstract). In cosmological inference, fully differentiable pipelines compute 120 angular power spectra across 103 $\ell$-multipoles in just 5 ms on a single GPU core—an acceleration factor of roughly $40\times$ over the winning code of the N5K challenge [Reymond et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522718R/abstract).

**OpenMP vs. OpenGL in Interactive Visualization**
Interactive data exploration also relies heavily on comparative hardware paradigms. For real-time signal processing and 3D visualization of faint H I structures in radio data cubes, filtering algorithms require carefully tuned hardware implementations. To this end, researchers have successfully implemented both multi-core CPU (OpenMP) and GPU (OpenGL) versions of intensity-driven gradient filters, utilizing both pathways to achieve the interactive frame rates needed to inspect complex morphological components without degrading the resolution of high signal-to-noise sources [Punzo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&C....17..163P/abstract).

**Emerging Architectures: IPUs vs. GPUs**
While NVIDIA GPUs and the CUDA ecosystem currently enjoy a near-monopoly in astrophysical software infrastructure, the literature exhibits an active debate regarding long-term hardware diversification. Novel processing modalities, such as Graphcore Intelligence Processing Units (IPUs) are being evaluated as alternatives for deep learning workloads. 

A foundational benchmark study compared a Graphcore MK1 (GC2) IPU against an Nvidia V100 (based on the [[volta-gpu-architecture|Volta GPU architecture]]) across several cosmological use cases, including Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs) for galaxy shape estimation and generative networks for image production [Arcelin et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210602465A/abstract). Although IPU benchmarks remain in an exploratory phase, these results suggest that Graphcore IPUs offer a viable architectural alternative to handle the increasing computational loads of modern cosmology. However, whether IPUs will secure widespread adoption or displace the highly optimized CUDA-based codes that currently permeate the field remains an unresolved question within the astronomical community.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The advent of Stage-IV facilities, such as the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST), represents a paradigm shift in astrophysical data volume. With the SKA expected to generate data on the exabyte scale daily [Ndung'u et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NewAR..9701685N/abstract), traditional CPU-based pipelines will be mathematically and computationally intractable. While GPU acceleration is an undisputed prerequisite for processing in this regime [Jarrett et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3700502J/abstract), several critical methodological and infrastructural questions remain unresolved.

One of the most persistent tensions in applying [[machine-learning-and-neural-networks-in-astrophysics|Machine Learning and Neural Networks in Astrophysics]] is the issue of domain adaptation. Deep learning models are frequently trained on synthetic data from cosmological simulations, but their performance can degrade unpredictably when applied to real observational data containing complex instrumental noise and heteroscedastic measurement errors [Ćiprijanović et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201103591C/abstract), [Feigelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AnRSA...8..493F/abstract). The literature currently presents conflicting views on the necessary degree of adaptation to mitigate this domain shift. Some studies suggest that standard transfer learning using a very small subset of real data—comprising just $0.1\%$ to $0.8\%$ of the training set size—is sufficient to recover baseline accuracy in morphological profiling [Tuccillo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475..894T/abstract). Others argue that explicit domain adaptation techniques, such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) or Domain Adversarial Neural Networks (DANN), are strictly necessary to robustly bridge the simulation-to-reality gap [Ćiprijanović et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201103591C/abstract), [Huertas-Company et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASA...40....1H/abstract). This challenge is tightly coupled to the bottleneck of data labeling; optimal exploitation of next-generation surveys will likely require self-supervised or unsupervised algorithms capable of leveraging multi-channel data cubes without relying on massive, manually annotated datasets [Ndung'u et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NewAR..9701685N/abstract).

Another major hurdle for the community adoption of GPU-accelerated neural networks is rigorous uncertainty quantification. Traditional "black-box" deep learning models often lack statistically calibrated posterior distributions for their predictions, which are essential for precision cosmology. Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs) have been proposed as a principled solution to extract well-calibrated uncertainties, demonstrating early promise in tasks like radio galaxy classification [Mohan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240518351M/abstract).

To address the interpretability deficit inherent in massive neural networks, the field is increasingly moving toward frameworks that facilitate human-AI collaboration. Rather than relying on opaque parameter estimation, recent developments include agent-based Large Language Model (LLM) architectures, such as Mephisto, which interface directly with spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting codes. These systems iteratively refine physical models against observational data via tree search, providing a transparent, human-aligned reasoning process [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251008354S/abstract). Such innovations point to a future where AI acts as a research copilot, augmenting human discovery across petascale data streams [Djorgovski et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221201493D/abstract). As these techniques mature, foundational textbooks emphasize that the deployment of machine learning in astronomy must remain grounded in rigorous scientific methodology and critical thinking [Acquaviva et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023mlpa.book.....A/abstract).

Finally, the long-term trajectory of hardware optimization remains an open question. While much of the existing astrophysical software ecosystem was built and optimized around NVIDIA hardware via CUDA—spanning from the legacy [[maxwell-gpu-architecture|Maxwell GPU architecture]] and [[volta-gpu-architecture|Volta GPU architecture]] to modern tensor-core accelerators—the field may need to diversify. Exploratory benchmarks suggest that alternative architectures, such as Graphcore Intelligence Processing Units (IPUs), could provide a viable and potentially superior path to addressing the escalating computational needs of cosmological deep learning [Arcelin et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210602465A/abstract).
