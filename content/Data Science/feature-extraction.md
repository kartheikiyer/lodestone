In modern astrophysics, **feature extraction** refers to the mathematical and computational processes used to transform raw, high-dimensional observational data—such as high-resolution spectra, two-dimensional detector images, and variable light curves—into a reduced set of informative physical or statistical parameters. As a cornerstone of [[astronomical-data-science-and-reduction|Astronomical Data Science and Reduction]], this process isolates the most relevant signals required for classification and physical modeling while discarding instrumental noise and redundancies. Historically, this involved manually engineered metrics, such as structural compactness parameters for star-galaxy separation [Mamon et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997astro.ph.12169M/abstract) or specific equivalent widths like the Lick/IDS index system used to measure stellar populations [Worthey et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJS...94..687W/abstract). Today, robust feature extraction is an essential prerequisite for applying machine learning algorithms to the exabyte-scale datasets generated by large-scale surveys like the Vera Rubin Observatory (LSST) and the Square Kilometre Array (SKA).

The methodologies for extracting features span a wide spectrum of complexity. Traditional approaches rely on parametric decomposition, such as fitting Sérsic profiles to galaxy light distributions, or optimal forward-modeling extraction techniques that preserve the native resolution of complex spectrographs [Bolton et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PASP..122..248B/abstract). To further compress these metrics, astronomers extensively employ [[multivariate-analysis-in-astronomy|Multivariate analysis in astronomy]]. Techniques like [[principal-component-analysis-in-astronomy|Principal Component Analysis in astronomy]] (PCA) and Independent Component Analysis (ICA) are standard tools for [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]], capable of capturing the vast majority of variance in complex spectral and morphological data within just a few dominant eigenvectors [Madgwick et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10220M/abstract). More recently, the field has experienced a major paradigm shift toward deep representation learning. Unsupervised models, such as variational autoencoders (VAEs) and self-supervised convolutional networks, can now autonomously construct [[low-dimensional-representations|Low-dimensional representations]] directly from pixel-level data, frequently matching or exceeding the classification accuracy of human-designed indices.

Despite the rapid success of automated extraction pipelines, several critical challenges and open questions remain. A primary tension in the current literature is the trade-off between the predictive power of "black-box" deep learning models and the fundamental need for physical interpretability. This conflict is driving the development of specialized frameworks like Sparse Feature Networks and active learning attribution methods, which attempt to map abstract latent variables back to comprehensible physical properties like metallicity. Furthermore, as models are deployed across diverse observational facilities, researchers must grapple with the "domain gap"—the degradation of a model's extracted features when applied to datasets with different noise properties or telescope systematics. Resolving these cross-survey biases and ensuring that extracted features can reliably isolate genuine astrophysical anomalies from instrumental artifacts are critical active research areas as the community prepares for the next generation of automated discovery.

## Historical Development of Astronomical Feature Extraction

The historical development of feature extraction in astrophysics reflects a steady transition from manually engineered, physically intuitive metrics to increasingly automated and mathematically abstracted representations. In the era of early digitised photographic plates and first-generation CCD surveys, feature extraction was heavily reliant on explicit geometric and photometric measurements. 

A primary challenge in early wide-field imaging was reliable star-galaxy separation. Initially, this was accomplished using simple structural parameters derived from source compactness. For instance, the ratio of peak pixel intensity to isophotal area was demonstrated to be a highly effective discriminator; in early near-infrared survey data, this single extracted feature achieved $98.5\%$ completeness and $92.5\%$ reliability for sources down to a magnitude of $I < 16.5$ [Mamon et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997astro.ph.12169M/abstract). However, achieving homogeneous galaxy extraction from wide-angle near-infrared surveys like DENIS presented significant early challenges, highlighting the pitfalls of varying observing conditions and atmospheric limits [Mamon et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASSL..210..239M/abstract). 

In the spectroscopic domain, feature extraction was driven by the need to quantify the physical properties of stellar populations, such as age and metallicity. This led to the widespread adoption of the Lick/IDS index system, a set of manually defined equivalent widths targeting specific atomic and molecular absorption lines. To systematise this, 21 distinct optical absorption features were automatically measured across a sample of 460 stars and parameterised as functions of effective temperature, surface gravity, and metallicity ($\rm [Fe/H]$) [Worthey et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJS...94..687W/abstract). These carefully engineered Lick indices allowed astronomers to extract physical parameters from the integrated light of distant, unresolved galaxies. Simultaneously, early efforts to process multi-object spectroscopy relied on automated ridge-finding algorithms to locate and extract individual spectra from complex fiber-optic spectrometers [Hill et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986SPIE..627..693H/abstract).

The exponential growth of survey data in the 1990s necessitated a move away from bespoke manual extraction toward standardized, automated software pipelines. A watershed moment in this evolution was the introduction of SExtractor (Source Extractor), which provided a fast, optimal framework for detecting, de-blending, and measuring sources in large images [Bertin et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996A&AS..117..393B/abstract). Crucially, SExtractor pioneered the use of a neural network—trained on simulated images—to automate star-galaxy separation, cementing its status as a foundational tool in [[astronomical-data-science-and-reduction|Astronomical Data Science and Reduction]]. As observational projects expanded, the importance of automated source extraction and the creation of comprehensive band-merged catalogs became increasingly apparent in large-area surveys, such as the Spitzer South Pole Telescope Deep Field (SSDF) . During this time, the introduction of Bayesian source extraction and signal separation provided essential statistical frameworks for robust, probabilistic feature analysis in cosmology [Hobson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009bmc..book.....H/abstract).

As datasets continued to scale in complexity, the limitations of extracting pre-defined indices or basic isophotal shapes became apparent. Researchers increasingly turned to [[multivariate-analysis-in-astronomy|Multivariate analysis in astronomy]] to automatically capture variance across highly dimensional datasets. For example, instead of relying solely on scalar measurements like isophotal area, classifiers began applying [[principal-component-analysis-in-astronomy|Principal Component Analysis in astronomy]] to the Fourier series fits of multi-radial isophotal profiles. This approach demonstrated that up to $90\%$ of the variance in galaxy morphology could be compressed into as few as three principal components [Odewahn et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...568..539O/abstract). This successful mapping of structural data into [[low-dimensional-representations|Low-dimensional representations]] marked the beginning of a paradigm shift from manual feature engineering toward the automated [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]] and deep representation learning that dominates modern analytical pipelines.

## Parametric and Morphological Decomposition

Traditional approaches to quantifying galaxy structure rely on parametric and morphological decomposition. These techniques [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|reduce the complex two-dimensional light distributions of galaxies into a tractable set]] of geometric and physical parameters. While the field is increasingly shifting toward automated representation learning via deep neural networks to minimize human bias [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4463K/abstract), extracting specific geometric features remains highly relevant for researchers prioritizing physically interpretable models over "black-box" accuracy [Wu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250100089W/abstract). 

A foundational method of morphological feature extraction is the fitting of analytical functions, such as the Sérsic profile, to galaxy surface brightness distributions. Advanced implementations like GALFITM have extended traditional single-band fitting into the multi-wavelength regime. By simultaneously using multiple images of the same galaxy to constrain a wavelength-dependent model, GALFITM significantly improves parameter extraction from low signal-to-noise (S/N) observations (such as the $u$ and $z$ bands) by utilizing constraints from higher-S/N bands [Vika et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435..623V/abstract). This multi-wavelength decomposition effectively captures physical trends; for example, the extracted Sérsic indices of spiral galaxies increase toward redder wavelengths, which is expected for composite bulge-disc systems. However, users must account for systematic biases when extracting these parameters, as the recovered Sérsic index can drift when characterizing identical galaxies at successively higher redshifts [Vika et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435..623V/abstract).

Beyond global parametric profiles, fine-grained morphological features can be extracted through the analysis of elliptical isophotes and Fourier series fits. In this approach, a galaxy's two-dimensional light distribution is reconstructed by fitting Fourier series to azimuthal profiles within concentric elliptical annuli. The phase and amplitude of these Fourier components are highly compressible; applying [[principal-component-analysis-in-astronomy|Principal Component Analysis in astronomy]] can capture up to $90\%$ of the variance in these Fourier profiles using as few as three principal components [Odewahn et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...568..539O/abstract). Such Fourier-based feature extraction enables the automated detection of morphological bars at an $85\%$--$90\%$ confidence level and can predict a galaxy's position on the revised Hubble stage with a $1\sigma$ scatter of just 1.5 steps [Odewahn et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...568..539O/abstract). For analyzing even broader scale structures, particularly in large-volume cosmological surveys, methods such as Spherical Fourier-Bessel (SFB) decomposition provide an effective radial and angular separation technique to extract critical cosmological parameters [Grasshorn Gebhardt et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104l3548G/abstract).

Alternatively, the sequential properties of elliptical isophotes themselves can be treated as an extracted feature set. Collecting isophotal light distributions into 1D sequences significantly reduces data dimensionality while preserving distinguishing morphological characteristics. When fed into automated machine learning classifiers, this sequence-based extraction yields accuracies of $86\%$ for early-type galaxies and $93\%$ for late-type galaxies, provided the image S/N is $> 300$ [Tarsitano et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.3330T/abstract). 

Finally, standard non-parametric morphological features—such as concentration, asymmetry, and the Gini coefficient—continue to serve as essential structural quantifiers. These traditional summary statistics are frequently used to benchmark the feature spaces generated by modern unsupervised clustering algorithms, confirming that automated systems successfully learn to separate distinct physical structures like early-type and late-type galaxies [Cheng et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.4446C/abstract).

## Dimensionality Reduction via Multivariate Analysis

As astronomical surveys generate datasets of increasingly high dimensionality, techniques for [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]] have become essential for data compression and physical interpretation. Within the broader framework of [[multivariate-analysis-in-astronomy|Multivariate analysis in astronomy]], statistical methods such as [[principal-component-analysis-in-astronomy|Principal Component Analysis (PCA)]] and Independent Component Analysis (ICA) are routinely employed to extract essential features from complex datasets.

PCA conceptually transforms a system of correlated variables into a set of orthogonal basis vectors, or eigenvectors, ordered by decreasing variance. This high-dimensional compression is highly efficient in practice: it is typical to capture up to $90\%$ of the variance in galaxy morphologies using as few as three principal components [Odewahn et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...568..539O/abstract), or $86\%$ of the variation in global dynamical parameters with just one dominant component [Chang et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1009.0030C/abstract). Beyond morphological modeling, PCA has been applied to Strömgren photometry to distinguish between starburst and Seyfert galaxies and to isolate their underlying star formation histories [Steindling et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJS..132...19S/abstract). In radio astronomy, PCA serves as a powerful diagnostic tool for extracting the energy spectra and turbulent velocity fluctuations within molecular clouds [Brunt et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...566..289B/abstract). Recent applications to 3D integral field unit (IFU) data cubes and theoretical simulations have achieved dimensionality reduction factors of up to 3650 while maintaining a reconstruction error of $<5\%$ [Çakır et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231206016C/abstract). 

A prominent application of PCA is the derivation of continuous classification metrics. For example, in the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey, PCA was used to construct the $\eta$ parameter, a continuous measure of spectral type that reflects the actual distribution of spectra in the observed galaxy population [Madgwick et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10220M/abstract). Combining PCA with Unsupervised Fuzzy Partition (UFP) clustering provides a robust statistical method for classifying these extracted features across large samples, such as galaxies in the zCOSMOS survey [Coppa et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...535A..10C/abstract). Similarly, the kinematics of low surface brightness galaxies can be parameterised using just two components; the ratio of the second to the first component correlates strongly with the galaxy's gas fraction and inner mass profile, independent of other parameters [Alard et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...728L..47A/abstract).

When applied to spatially resolved spectra, this technique is known as PCA Tomography. The projection of the data cube onto the eigenvectors produces spatial images known as tomograms. Because the eigenvectors are mutually orthogonal, PCA tomography is uniquely capable of separating uncorrelated physical phenomena. This method has been used successfully to isolate the signature of a hidden type-1 active galactic nucleus from the stellar bulge in the LINER galaxy NGC 4736 [Steiner et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.395...64S/abstract), [Steiner et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..267...85S/abstract).

While PCA enforces orthogonality, Independent Component Analysis (ICA) seeks basis vectors that are statistically independent. ICA has been used to distill massive parameter spaces—comprising emission lines, Lick indices, and photometric data—into a fundamental set of features. For instance, just 9 independent components can provide a nearly complete physical description of a galaxy's structure, kinematics, and stellar population [Chattopadhyay et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PASP..131j8010C/abstract). On an even larger scale, component separation frameworks leverage ICA alongside Maximum Entropy methods to systematically isolate the cosmic microwave background (CMB) from galactic and extragalactic foregrounds, a technique famously utilized for the PLANCK mission [Leach et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...491..597L/abstract).

Beyond PCA and ICA, wavelet and mode projection methods frequently supplement dimensionality reduction. Wavelet-based rotation measure (RM) synthesis operates as a multi-scale technique to identify structural features, such as magnetic fields, in Faraday space [Beck et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...543A.113B/abstract). Wavelet reconstruction is also widely utilized for isolating curl-free (E-mode) and divergence-free (B-mode) components in polarization and weak lensing datasets [Leistedt et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.3728L/abstract). These multi-scale algorithms are frequently paired with mode projection techniques integrated into power spectrum estimation to effectively remove astrophysical foregrounds and observational systematics [Elsner et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.1847E/abstract).

Despite the mathematical success of these techniques, tensions remain regarding their physical interpretation. Standard empirical heuristics (such as optical colors or specific line ratios) do not always correlate significantly with the primary principal or independent components [Chang et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1009.0030C/abstract), [Chattopadhyay et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PASP..131j8010C/abstract). This discrepancy indicates that traditional, visually intuitive parameters may fail to capture the hidden complexities of galaxy evolution. To address nonlinear relationships that standard linear techniques might miss, researchers frequently explore manifold learning methods, such as Locally Linear Embedding (LLE), to construct nonlinear [[low-dimensional-representations|Low-dimensional representations]] of data [Nichol et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10230N/abstract).

## Optimal Extraction in Spectroscopy and IFU Data

In the context of astronomical spectroscopy, the fundamental objective of feature extraction is to reduce raw two-dimensional (2D) detector signals—or three-dimensional (3D) data cubes from Integral Field Units (IFUs)—into one-dimensional (1D) spectra while preserving the maximal signal-to-noise ratio. Historically, standard optimal extraction has relied on weighting the flux across the spatial cross-section of the spectrum. However, these classical approaches generally assume that the instrumental point spread function (PSF) is separable in the spatial and dispersion directions, an assumption often violated by the complex optics of modern spectrographs [Bolton et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PASP..122..248B/abstract). 

To overcome the limitations of profile-based extraction, the paradigm for fiber and slit spectroscopy has shifted toward 2D forward modeling, a technique famously termed "spectro-perfectionism" [Bolton et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PASP..122..248B/abstract). This algorithmic framework directly models the raw pixel data using an accurate, arbitrarily complicated 2D PSF. Unlike traditional 1D optimal extraction, spectro-perfectionism yields statistically independent extracted samples and preserves the native resolution of the spectrograph without degradation [Bolton et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PASP..122..248B/abstract). By providing accurately determined statistical errors and resolution matrices, it enables rigorous $\chi^2$ comparisons between physical model spectra and the extracted data [Bolton et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PASP..122..248B/abstract). Properly accounting for error correlations and precise sky background levels is critical for deblending overlapping signals and detecting faint features, such as high-redshift emission lines [Chen et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..191..217C/abstract). For large-scale multi-wavelength processing, automated routines such as the Herschel SPIRE Fourier Transform Spectrometer (FTS) Spectral Feature Finder are deployed to systematically detect and extract specific spectral features, like N II and C I gas lines, from far-infrared observations [Benson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.3711B/abstract). On a broader stellar scale, automated pipelines such as the Gaia All-Sky Stellar Parameters Service (GASPS) perform comprehensive spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting to extract effective temperatures and luminosities for massive, uniform samples [McDonald et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260103978M/abstract).

The extraction of distinct source spectra from 3D IFU data cubes presents additional complexities, particularly in crowded fields. Traditionally, extracting 1D spectra from these volumes relied heavily on spatial priors derived from ancillary photometric imaging. However, recent automated frameworks, such as the AUTOSPEC software, perform optimal source identification and spectral extraction utilizing the wealth of information within the IFU data cube itself [Griffiths et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869...68G/abstract). This direct 3D approach can improve the signal-to-noise ratio of the extracted spectra and successfully resolve source blending from nearby contaminants without requiring external imaging catalogs [Griffiths et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869...68G/abstract).

Beyond isolating 1D spectra, analyzing IFU data often requires separating complex, spatially resolved physical components. "PCA Tomography" applies [[principal-component-analysis-in-astronomy|Principal Component Analysis in astronomy]] to data cubes to isolate uncorrelated physical phenomena [Steiner et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.395...64S/abstract), [Steiner et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..267...85S/abstract). By projecting the 3D data onto mutually orthogonal eigenvectors and visualizing their corresponding spatial tomograms, researchers can effectively deblend overlapping signatures. Additionally, applying PCA directly to specific molecular line maps enables the clear identification of localized structural features, such as biconical outflow peaks and circumnuclear disk concentrations in active galaxies like NGC 1068 [Saito et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935..155S/abstract). Such methods represent a powerful application of [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]] to spatially resolved spectroscopy.

Despite advancements in forward modeling and automated extraction, the literature highlights persistent challenges in fully automated pipelines. Algorithms often struggle to distinguish true faint features from false sources or artifacts, particularly when extracting objects clustered around bright stars or situated in regions of highly variable surface brightness [Solin et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...542A...3S/abstract). Consequently, there is an ongoing tension between the desire for fully automated pipelines and the practical necessity of residual visual screening to separate genuine embedded sources from complex instrumental noise fluctuations [Solin et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...542A...3S/abstract).

## Representation Learning and Deep Neural Networks

The volume and complexity of data expected from next-generation surveys have catalyzed a major shift in [[astronomical-data-science-and-reduction|Astronomical Data Science and Reduction]]. There is a growing movement away from manual feature engineering—such as fitting predefined Sérsic profiles or calculating Lick indices—toward automated representation learning [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4463K/abstract). In this paradigm, deep neural networks ingest raw or minimally processed pixel arrays and autonomously learn optimal representations, significantly reducing human bias and expert effort.

Supervised Convolutional Neural Networks (ConvNets) have become foundational for automated classification tasks. By extracting hierarchical features directly from two-dimensional images, ConvNets consistently match or exceed traditional catalog-based machine learning approaches. For instance, architectures such as GoogLeNet have demonstrated star-galaxy separation accuracies of $99.6\%$ for stars and $99.9\%$ for galaxies when trained on high-quality survey data [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24i5012Z/abstract). To improve the training efficiency of these networks, dimensionality tools like [[principal-component-analysis-in-astronomy|PCA]] are sometimes used beforehand to perform rotational standardization—aligning galaxy principal components with coordinate axes to normalize orientation prior to ingestion [Brand et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522..292B/abstract). As classification schemas become more complex, researchers have evaluated the robustness of these extracted feature sets using ensemble decision tree distributions, successfully classifying specific and rare morphologies such as bent-double radio galaxies [Proctor et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJS..165...95P/abstract).

However, the reliance on massive, human-annotated training sets has increasingly driven the field toward unsupervised and self-supervised learning algorithms. Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) are now widely used for [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]], compressing high-dimensional imaging or spectral data into a compact bottleneck of [[low-dimensional-representations|latent variables]]. It has been shown that a $40$-dimensional latent space can effectively encode and reproduce complex morphological features from the DECaLS survey [Xu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.6391X/abstract). Unsupervised machine learning approaches excel at extracting these features from high-redshift galaxy images while simultaneously accounting for observational biases related to size, orientation, and instrumental noise [Tohill et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230211482T/abstract). Recent architectures further combine hierarchical feature extraction via ConvNeXt CNNs with non-linear manifold learning through Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) to achieve highly optimized, topology-aware dimensionality reduction [Fang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215137F/abstract). 

In the spectroscopic domain, dictionary learning algorithms for sparse representation and denoising autoencoders have been employed to learn non-linear representations of galaxy spectra, significantly facilitating automated redshift estimation [Frontera-Pons et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...625A..73F/abstract). When dealing with imaging, Vector-Quantized VAEs (VQ-VAEs) paired with hierarchical clustering can separate galaxies into distinct groups based purely on intrinsic structure, achieving binary early/late-type classification accuracies of $\sim 87\%$ on imbalanced datasets [Cheng et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.4446C/abstract). These data-driven latent representations often correlate strongly with underlying physical properties, leading some authors to argue that unsupervised representation learning provides a more physically meaningful taxonomy than visually assigned Hubble types [Cheng et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.4446C/abstract). Furthermore, self-supervised learning frameworks like Bootstrap Your Own Latent (BYOL) have proven highly effective for extracting robust features that enable the discovery of morphological clusters and rare anomalies without requiring any labeled data [Mohale et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.1274M/abstract).

As these models are deployed across different observatories, they frequently encounter the "domain gap"—a degradation in model performance caused by varying noise properties or seeing conditions. Universal Domain Adaptation (UDA) techniques explicitly address this by aligning the extracted feature distributions across different datasets [Ćiprijanović et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MLS&T...4b5013C/abstract), [Xu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.6391X/abstract).

Despite these successes, an active tension remains regarding the "black-box" nature of deep neural networks. While some researchers consider manual feature extraction largely obsolete in the deep learning era, others emphasize the strict necessity of physical interpretability for understanding galaxy evolution. This conflict has motivated the development of explicitly interpretable architectures, such as Sparse Feature Networks, which are explored further in the context of feature attribution.

## Feature Extraction for Time-Domain and Transient Events

Time-domain astronomy, particularly in the context of high-cadence large-scale surveys, relies heavily on automated feature extraction to process and classify variable events in real time. Because raw astronomical light curves are typically irregularly sampled time-series data, they are often processed by translating them into fixed-length vectors of summary statistics.

The primary methodology for parameterizing variable data involves extracting a comprehensive suite of photometric time-series features. These include time-domain magnitude statistics, such as peak amplitude, variance, and skewness, alongside frequency-domain metrics like periodogram power peaks [Malanchev et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AAS...24111703M/abstract). These summary statistics capture the fundamental periodicities, outburst rise times, and decay rates necessary for the rapid identification of transient events. Other feature-based classifiers, such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), have proven exceptionally effective for selecting active time-domain candidates, like quasars, from extensive photometric surveys including the SDSS and UKIDSS [Peng et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.2599P/abstract).

In addition to manually engineered statistical metrics, there is a growing reliance on deep learning architectures to autonomously learn morphological light-curve features. Variational autoencoders (VAEs), for example, are frequently utilized to project light curves into domain-specific [[low-dimensional-representations|latent spaces]]. Through such [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]], the resulting low-dimensional representations encode subtle photometric behaviors that make classifiers significantly more sensitive to the distinctions between highly similar transient subclasses, such as different types of supernovae [Malanchev et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AAS...24111703M/abstract).

However, light-curve features alone are rarely sufficient for optimal classification. State-of-the-art transient classifiers dynamically combine these photometric metrics with contextual metadata. Features such as the line-of-sight Milky Way extinction, host galaxy color, and host redshift are consistently ranked among the most critical variables for achieving high-precision classification, particularly at early times before a transient's light curve has fully evolved [Malanchev et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AAS...24111703M/abstract). 

The robustness of these feature extraction pipelines is rigorously tested through simulated data streams, most notably the Extended LSST Astronomical Time-series Classification Challenge (ELAsTiCC). ELAsTiCC simulates the real-time alert infrastructure required for upcoming massive surveys, utilizing a complex astrophysical taxonomy composed of 32 distinct physical models. These models span a wide parameter space, from local Milky Way variable stars and microlensing events to distant active galactic nuclei and superluminous supernovae [Malanchev et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AAS...24111703M/abstract). In this environment, community alert brokers ingest the continuous alert stream, applying gradient-boosting algorithms and neural networks to the extracted features to assign probabilistic classifications on the fly. Finally, translating variable data into robust feature vectors is also a prerequisite for automated anomaly detection frameworks, which leverage active learning to efficiently sift through millions of routine alerts to identify rare, misclassified, or physically unknown phenomena [Lochner et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3600481L/abstract).

Looking toward the future of large-scale transient and sequential survey analyses, specialized machine learning frameworks—incorporating CNNs and sophisticated clustering techniques—are actively being developed to automate the extraction and classification of integrated HI spectral profiles, laying necessary groundwork for the vast data influx expected from the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) [Jaimes-Illanes et al. ## Interpretability and Feature Attribution

As astronomical datasets reach exabyte scales, the transition from manual feature engineering to deep representation learning has dramatically improved classification accuracies. However, this shift introduces a fundamental tension in the literature regarding interpretability. While deep Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) can act as "black boxes" that match or exceed human-designed indices—achieving $>98\%$ accuracy for star-galaxy separation [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4463K/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24i5012Z/abstract)—they obscure the underlying physical mechanisms driving the classification. Some authors argue that manual feature extraction is now redundant [Polsterer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASPC..461..561P/abstract), while others emphasize the necessity of maintaining physically meaningful metrics to build a coherent understanding of galaxy evolution.

To reconcile high predictive performance with the need for physical insight, emerging architectures such as Sparse Feature Networks (SFNets) have been developed. SFNets are designed to produce explicitly interpretable features that can be combined linearly to estimate complex physical properties directly from image cutouts. For instance, SFNets have successfully predicted optical emission line ratios and gas-phase metallicities without sacrificing the accuracy typically associated with opaque deep learning models [Wu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250100089W/abstract), [Wu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..183W/abstract). By bridging the gap between raw pixel representations and physical parameters, SFNets provide a transparent framework for [[multivariate-analysis-in-astronomy|Multivariate analysis in astronomy]].

In the context of standard machine learning and decision-tree ensembles, feature attribution algorithms are deployed to "open the black box" and assess feature relevance. Techniques for assessing feature relevance date back to early back-propagation neural networks, where regularization parameters were used to explicitly prune unnecessary input features during morphological galaxy classification [Bazell et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000MNRAS.316..519B/abstract). Today, more complex techniques such as the Mutual Information based Transductive Feature Selection (MINT) algorithm and tree-interpreters allow researchers to quantify exactly how strongly a given metric drives a modern classification. By visualizing decision boundaries and pruning irrelevant features, refined models have been shown to reduce misclassifications in star-galaxy separation by up to $\approx 33\%$ compared to standard photometric pipelines [Morice-Atkinson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.4194M/abstract). 

Interpretability is especially critical for outlier detection. When an automated pipeline flags an anomalous object in massive surveys, determining exactly *which* features render the object an outlier is a non-trivial inverse problem. Models often perform poorly on anomalies because out-of-distribution test data exceed the limits of the training set. To address this, specialized feature attribution methods like Inverse Multiscale Occlusion have been developed. Applied to galaxy spectra from the Dark Energy Survey Instrument (DESI), this technique identifies the specific spectral features driving the anomaly classification, providing much higher interpretability than alternative attribution approaches [Shen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231020012S/abstract). 

Finally, because unguided anomaly detection often yields a high fraction of irrelevant instrumental artifacts, active learning frameworks are increasingly utilized to guide feature attribution alongside [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]]. Software such as ASTRONOMALY integrates expert human feedback into the anomaly detection loop, dynamically personalizing the feature relevance weighting. By discarding systematic artifacts, this active learning approach has been shown to roughly double the rate of genuine astrophysical anomaly discoveries within the first 100 objects inspected by a user [Lochner et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3600481L/abstract).

## Cross-Survey Domain Adaptation

As automated feature extraction pipelines scale to process data from next-generation surveys, a critical challenge emerges known as the "domain gap." Machine learning models trained to extract features and classify objects on a specific survey often suffer severely degraded performance when applied to new datasets. This degradation is driven by differences in telescope systematics, varying signal-to-noise ratios, spatial resolutions, and survey depths. Cross-survey domain adaptation (DA) provides an algorithmic framework to ensure that extracted features remain robust and invariant to these instrumental differences, facilitating unbiased classification across disparate observational domains.

A prominent approach to this problem is the use of semi-supervised universal domain adaptation. The DeepAstroUDA algorithm, for example, aligns the feature distributions of a labeled "source" domain and an unlabeled "target" domain, even in the presence of differing class overlaps or completely unknown classes [Ćiprijanović et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MLS&T...4b5013C/abstract). This framework has been successfully demonstrated on morphological classification tasks of varying complexity (e.g., three-class and ten-class problems) across highly discrepant observational scenarios. DeepAstroUDA can successfully transfer models between entirely different surveys (such as from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey to DECaLS), across different observing depths within a single survey (SDSS wide field versus the deeper Stripe 82 field), and across temporal depths (mock $1$-year versus $10$-year observations for the Legacy Survey of Space and Time). By bridging this cross-survey gap, DeepAstroUDA increases classification accuracy by up to $40\%$ on unlabeled target data, yielding consistent model performance and allowing for robust anomaly detection of unknown classes [Ćiprijanović et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MLS&T...4b5013C/abstract).

Domain adaptation is also actively combined with deep generative models to perform [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]]. When compressing galaxy images into [[low-dimensional-representations|lower-dimensional representations]], the latent space can be explicitly penalized or tuned to be domain-invariant. Xu et al. demonstrated this by coupling DA with Variational Auto-encoders (VAEs) to extract a $40$-dimensional latent vector that securely captures morphological features [Xu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.6391X/abstract). By training on the overlapping footprint of the DECaLS and BASS+MzLS surveys, the DA-enhanced network successfully aligned the latent feature distributions of both datasets. This alignment removes survey-specific instrumental signatures from the extracted features, enabling unbiased classification when these latent variables are fed into classical algorithms like random forests [Xu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.6391X/abstract).

Ultimately, cross-survey domain adaptation is becoming a foundational requirement in [[astronomical-data-science-and-reduction|Astronomical Data Science and Reduction]]. As the community transitions from relying on heavily curated, survey-specific training catalogs to applying pre-trained models on exabyte-scale data from upcoming observatories (such as the Square Kilometre Array or the China Space Station Telescope), DA ensures that previously learned physical representations retain their diagnostic power regardless of the underlying observational parameters.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The advent of next-generation facilities, most notably the Vera Rubin Observatory and the Square Kilometre Array (SKA), is ushering in an era of exabyte-scale data collection [Lochner et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3600481L/abstract), [Ndung'u et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NewAR..9701685N/abstract). At this volume, manual feature engineering and visual classification are entirely intractable, necessitating fully automated pipelines within [[astronomical-data-science-and-reduction|Astronomical Data Science and Reduction]]. However, managing this data deluge presents several open methodological challenges, particularly concerning anomaly detection, cross-survey generalization, and model interpretability.

A critical open question is how to identify rare or unknown astrophysical phenomena when the vast majority of the data will never be visually inspected. While unsupervised machine learning is highly effective at identifying outliers, it frequently struggles to differentiate genuine astrophysical anomalies from instrumental artifacts or noise fluctuations [Lochner et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3600481L/abstract). To address this, current research heavily emphasizes active learning frameworks, such as ASTRONOMALY, which incorporate sparse, expert human feedback to iteratively refine machine learning classifications and dynamically guide anomaly detection across images, light curves, and spectra [Lochner et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3600481L/abstract), [Mohale et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.1274M/abstract).

Another major challenge in feature extraction is the "domain gap" that arises between different astronomical surveys or between simulated training data and real observations. Deep learning models optimized for specific photometric depths or instrument point spread functions (PSFs) often fail to generalize to novel data. Bridging this gap requires universal domain adaptation (UDA) techniques that align feature distributions across disparate surveys, ensuring that previously learned physical representations retain their diagnostic power regardless of the observational domain [Ćiprijanović et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MLS&T...4b5013C/abstract). 

Furthermore, the literature exhibits a significant, unresolved tension regarding the trade-off between the high accuracy of "black-box" deep representation learning and the fundamental requirement for physical interpretability. While some authors argue that manual feature extraction is becoming redundant given the superior performance of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4463K/abstract), others caution that opaque feature spaces hinder physical understanding. To resolve this, future directions point toward the refinement of explicitly interpretable networks, such as SFNets, which map pixel-level representations directly to physical parameters without sacrificing predictive accuracy [Wu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250100089W/abstract), [Wu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..183W/abstract).

Finally, as [[dimensionality-reduction-in-astrophysics|Dimensionality reduction in astrophysics]] creates increasingly complex [[low-dimensional-representations|latent spaces]], the scalability of subsequent clustering algorithms remains an active area of research. Algorithms must process petabytes of data without losing sensitivity to small-scale, localized astrophysical structures. Approaches such as ASTROLINK attempt to hierarchically classify these structures while dynamically filtering out stochastic density fluctuations using a statistically motivated significance threshold $S$ [Oliver et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.2637O/abstract). Scaling such algorithms to the data rates of the SKA and the Vera Rubin Observatory while preserving high-fidelity feature extraction will be a defining computational challenge of the coming decade.
