## Article

**Physics-informed neural networks** (PINNs) represent a major paradigm shift in astrophysical modeling, moving beyond the purely data-driven "black box" algorithms that historically dominated early morphological classification and parameter estimation tasks [Naim et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.275..567N/abstract), [Phillips et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12359P/abstract). Unlike standard neural network architectures that rely solely on empirical data, PINNs integrate known governing equations—such as the Vlasov-Poisson, Navier-Stokes, or magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) equations—directly into their training process. This is typically achieved by formulating custom loss functions that penalize any network predictions deviating from established conservation laws, thermodynamic relations, or differential equations. For example, by anchoring a custom loss function to the astrophysical entropy ($K \propto P/\rho^{5/3}$), researchers can enforce exact thermodynamic consistency in complex fluid and outflow models [Nguyen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231102057N/abstract).

The mathematical foundation of PINNs relies heavily on differentiable programming. Because neural networks are inherently analytic and differentiable, their partial derivatives with respect to spatial and temporal input coordinates can be computed exactly using automatic differentiation. This allows any system of partial differential equations (PDEs) or ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to be evaluated continuously over the network's domain. By forcing their outputs to remain physically viable even when extrapolating into regimes with sparse, noisy, or incomplete training data, PINNs provide an intrinsic regularization that significantly improves their robustness and physical fidelity over traditional empirical models [Auddy et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MLS&T...5b5014A/abstract).

Within astrophysics, PINNs and associated differentiable architectures are primarily utilized to solve complex inverse problems and drastically accelerate computationally expensive forward simulations. In the domain of fluid dynamics and structure formation, architectures like the Gravity-Informed Neural Network (GRINN) model 3D self-gravitating hydrodynamic systems by embedding the Euler and Poisson equations directly into the loss function, achieving an order of magnitude speedup over conventional grid codes while matching standard numerical solvers to within $5\%$ [Auddy et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MLS&T...5b5014A/abstract). In galactic dynamics, the "Deep Potential" framework treats the collisionless Boltzmann equation (CBE) as a strict constraint, successfully demonstrating the recovery of 3D gravitational potentials directly from discrete mock stellar phase-space snapshots, laying the groundwork for mapping the real Milky Way [Green et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942...26G/abstract). Similar continuous-time approaches using Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (NODEs) have accurately emulated the secular orbital evolution of supermassive black hole binaries at a fraction of the computational cost of full $N$-body simulations [Chan et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260113019C/abstract).

As these architectures scale to handle high-dimensional, survey-level data, they are increasingly integrated into Simulation-Based Inference (SBI) pipelines to extract precise cosmological constraints directly from continuous cosmic fields, bypassing analytically intractable likelihood functions. However, the broader implementation of physics-informed machine learning faces several critical open challenges. Chief among these is the "domain shift" problem, where models trained on specific hydrodynamical simulations fail to generalize to real observational data. Resolving these tensions, safely propagating intrinsic network approximation errors into the tight error budgets of Stage-IV cosmological surveys, and developing hybrid models capable of evaluating alternative gravitational regimes (paralleling purely empirical efforts like [[deep-mond|Deep-MOND]]) remain foremost priorities in the field.

## Evolution from Empirical Classifiers to Physics-Driven Models

The application of neural networks in astrophysics has undergone a profound evolution, transitioning from simple, empirical pattern recognition tools to sophisticated architectures that explicitly embed physical laws. 

In the 1990s and early 2000s, shallow artificial neural networks (ANNs) were primarily utilized for morphological classification and basic parameter estimation. Early pioneering works demonstrated that neural networks could successfully automate star-galaxy separation in digital sky surveys [Odewahn et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994VA.....38..281O/abstract) and classify galaxy morphologies with an accuracy comparable to human experts, achieving an rms error of 1.8 Revised Hubble types [Naim et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.275..567N/abstract). Around the same time, ANNs were introduced to cosmological parameter estimation, demonstrating that network-based inference of cosmic microwave background (CMB) anisotropies could scale computationally as $\mathcal{O}(N^{3/2})$ while maintaining maximum-likelihood precision [Phillips et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12359P/abstract). A major milestone in this era was the development of the ANNz algorithm, which became a foundational tool for estimating photometric redshifts by mapping multi-band photometric catalogs to spectroscopic redshift spaces (applied in works like [Banerji et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.386.1219B/abstract) and recently upgraded for modern surveys as ANNZ+ [Pathi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...01..097P/abstract)). 

However, these early empirical classifiers were heavily dependent on manual feature extraction and catalog-level summary statistics. The mid-2010s marked a paradigm shift with the widespread adoption of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). By operating directly on calibrated pixel values, CNNs bypassed the need for human-engineered features and achieved superhuman consistency in classification tasks. For example, rotation-invariant CNN architectures demonstrated the ability to replicate Galaxy Zoo consensus classifications with $>99\%$ accuracy [Dieleman et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.1441D/abstract), while other deep CNN frameworks were developed to robustly automate star-galaxy separation directly from pixel values [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4463K/abstract). Early deep learning efforts in radio astronomy similarly utilized CNNs to automate the classification of compact and extended sources within the Radio Galaxy Zoo citizen science project [Lukic et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476..246L/abstract). Furthermore, neural models like DeepLeGATo automated the fitting of 2D galaxy surface brightness profiles, reaching speeds up to 3000 times faster than GALFIT [Tuccillo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475..894T/abstract). Beyond static images, the introduction of recurrent neural networks (RNNs) enabled models to learn from complex sequential information, such as automating the classification of supernova light curves [Charnock et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837L..28C/abstract). Expanding upon time-domain capabilities, the ORACLE framework introduces hierarchical deep-learning for real-time transient classification, utilizing context-aware gated recurrent units for the early identification of variable phenomena [Shah et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250101496S/abstract). Subsequently, CNN-based architectures have demonstrated the ability to estimate photometric redshifts directly from SDSS images with very low bias and dispersion, outperforming traditional feature-based machine learning methods [Pasquet et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...621A..26P/abstract). Furthermore, new machine learning approaches are enabling the simultaneous prediction of X-ray cluster properties, such as central cooling time and entropy, providing a path to automated cool-core cluster classification [Sadikov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250104081S/abstract).

As deep learning matured, the astronomical community shifted its focus from purely empirical classification to the emulation of computationally expensive physical models and high-dimensional spectral embeddings. Advanced neural architectures are being utilized for chemical tagging, learning disentangled spectral representations that successfully isolate chemical abundance factors from atmospheric parameters [de Mijolla et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913...12D/abstract). Deep neural networks were also successfully trained to map baryonic properties onto dark-matter-only simulations, with emulators like Mangrove replicating semi-analytic models (SAMs) $10^4$ times faster than traditional numerical solvers [Jespersen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941....7J/abstract). Similarly, neural emulators were developed to accelerate stellar population synthesis models and radiative transfer calculations by orders of magnitude while preserving percent-level accuracy [Alsing et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..249....5A/abstract).

The most recent stage in this historical development is the move toward true physics-driven models, where the network acts not merely as a "black box" regressor, but as a solver for the governing physical equations. This transition from phenomenological fitting to differentiable, physics-informed architectures—ranging from CBE-constrained models of the Milky Way [Green et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942...26G/abstract) to NODE formulations of hot galactic winds [Nguyen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231102057N/abstract)—represents a fundamental maturation in how the universe is modeled computationally.

## Dynamical Modeling and Gravitational Potential Recovery

A classic inverse problem in galactic dynamics is recovering the underlying gravitational potential $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$, and thereby the total mass distribution (baryonic plus dark matter), from a snapshot of stellar positions and velocities. Traditional dynamical modeling often relies on strict symmetry assumptions (e.g., spherical or axisymmetry) and specific analytic forms for both the potential and the phase-space distribution function. Physics-informed neural networks offer a flexible, data-driven alternative by enforcing the collisionless Boltzmann equation (CBE) as a physical constraint on the neural network outputs, bypassing the need for geometric assumptions [Green et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942...26G/abstract).

The foundational method in this domain is the "Deep Potential" framework. This approach divides the problem into two learned components. First, a normalizing flow is trained on the observed 6D phase-space coordinates of a stellar tracer population to learn a smooth, fully differentiable approximation of the phase-space distribution function, $f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v})$. Second, a feed-forward neural network is used to parameterize the gravitational potential $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$. To train the potential network, the system enforces the steady-state CBE, which dictates that the distribution function of a stellar system in dynamical equilibrium must be stationary:
$$ \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} f - \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \Phi \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{v}} f = 0 $$
By minimizing the residual of this equation across the sampled phase space, the network converges on the $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$ that best maintains the steady state of the observed stars, a concept initially demonstrated using mock phase-space snapshots to lay the groundwork for subsequent real-world applications [Green et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942...26G/abstract).

The advent of the *Gaia* mission has provided unprecedented kinematic data, enabling the application of these physics-informed architectures to map the Milky Way in 3D. Recent extensions of the Deep Potential framework have mapped the local gravitational potential and pattern speed in the solar neighborhood, successfully disentangling the local dark matter density from baryonic matter without assuming fixed analytic background models [Kalda et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...992...84K/abstract). Subsequent iterations, such as the "ClearPotential" model, have incorporated the simultaneous learning of observational selection functions to correct for dust extinction, allowing the network to map the dark matter halo even through the dust-obscured Galactic disk [Putney et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241214236P/abstract), [Putney et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209989P/abstract). These 3D, symmetry-free models have provided some of the clearest maps of the local Galactic potential to date, identifying features such as a tilted oblate dark matter halo and yielding local dark matter density estimates of $\sim 0.0084\, M_\odot/\text{pc}^3$ [Putney et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209989P/abstract). 

Beyond full phase-space modeling, the optimization architectures that power neural networks have also been applied to 1D kinematic observables. For example, machine learning gradient descent algorithms can fit the Milky Way's Grand Rotation Curve across five orders of magnitude in radius, drastically reducing the computational time required to optimize complex, non-analytic dark matter profiles (such as fermionic core-halo models) [Argüelles et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Univ....9..372A/abstract). In a similar vein, physics-informed neural networks can be combined with pseudo-spectral methods to construct solitonic dark matter solutions, such as Q-balls, that effectively reproduce observed galactic rotation curves [Pombo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251109634P/abstract).

Despite these successes, PINN-based dynamical modeling faces physical challenges. A core assumption of the CBE constraint is that the Galaxy is in a state of statistical equilibrium. However, the Milky Way is known to be non-stationary, exhibiting features like phase spirals and bulk motions in response to satellite impacts. Applying strict stationarity constraints to non-equilibrium data can induce artificial spatial fluctuations in the recovered potential, making the handling of Galactic disequilibrium an active area of investigation in interpreting neural network-derived mass maps [Kalda et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...992...84K/abstract), [Putney et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209989P/abstract).

## Accelerated Emulation of Cosmological Simulations

Modern cosmological and astrophysical simulations—ranging from $N$-body dynamics to full radiative hydrodynamics—are fundamentally bottlenecked by the computational cost of their numerical solvers. To alleviate this, neural networks are increasingly deployed as surrogate models, or "emulators," designed to bypass expensive procedural calculations while maintaining high fidelity to the underlying physics. 

A prominent application of surrogate models in cosmology is the emulation of Boltzmann solvers, such as CLASS or CAMB, which are used to calculate the linear power spectrum $P(k)$ of density perturbations. While traditional numerical integration of these equations takes on the order of seconds per cosmological model, neural network emulators can evaluate the linear and non-linear matter power spectra in roughly a millisecond. These networks achieve fractional accuracies of $\sim 0.1-0.2\%$ across a broad range of scales and redshifts [Aricò et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210414568A/abstract). Similarly, the CosmoPower framework provides high-accuracy neural emulators for both CMB and matter power spectra, enabling order-of-magnitude speedups in parameter extraction for Stage-IV surveys [Bolliet et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.1351B/abstract). Surrogate models have also been developed to emulate the cross-spectra of linear fields predicted by Lagrangian Perturbation Theory, enabling the rapid model iterations required for Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analyses of galaxy clustering data [DeRose et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...04..056D/abstract).

Beyond the linear regime, neural networks are used to emulate the highly non-linear outputs of semi-analytic models (SAMs) and hydrodynamical simulations. Mapping baryonic properties onto dark matter structures is conventionally expensive, but graph neural networks trained on dark matter merger trees (e.g., the Mangrove framework) can emulate SAM predictions for stellar mass, cold gas mass, and star formation histories up to $10^4$ times faster than traditional methods, while successfully capturing the intrinsic scatter in the galaxy-halo connection [Jespersen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941....7J/abstract). Similarly, the EMBER-2 framework employs modulated convolutions to simultaneously emulate multiple baryon channels, including gas density and temperature, across a large redshift range directly from dark-matter-only inputs [Bernardini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.538.1201B/abstract). At the field level, the CAMELS project—a vast suite of hydrodynamical simulations varying both cosmological parameters (such as $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$) and subgrid feedback physics—has facilitated the training of emulators that rapidly predict the cosmic star formation rate density and stellar mass functions directly from governing physical parameters [Jo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...67J/abstract). Concurrently, other CAMELS emulators predict spatial clustering summary statistics, including the two-point correlation, count-in-cells, and void probability functions [Perez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954...11P/abstract).

Emulators are also used to accelerate specific microphysical modules embedded within larger simulations. Evaluating the radiative transfer of stellar light through dusty interstellar media is notoriously slow. Artificial neural networks trained on numerical radiative transfer codes like GRASIL have demonstrated speedups of $\sim 10^2$ for generating synthetic spectral energy distributions (SEDs) [Silva et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410.2043S/abstract). Similarly, neural operators have been successfully applied to non-equilibrium interstellar medium (ISM) chemistry, executing more than 100 times faster than stiff ordinary differential equation (ODE) solvers [Branca et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...684A.203B/abstract).

Despite these massive computational gains, the literature highlights persistent limitations. Emulators are highly susceptible to out-of-distribution errors and can fail catastrophically when queried with input parameters outside their training grid. For instance, radiative transfer emulators struggle to accurately predict SEDs for extreme starbursting galaxies with unusually high optical depths if such objects are underrepresented in the training data [Silva et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423..746S/abstract). Furthermore, while integrated quantities heavily governed by the depth of the gravitational potential (such as stellar mass) are efficiently mapped from dark matter halos, instantaneous properties like specific star formation rates and colors exhibit large intrinsic scatter and remain significantly harder to emulate robustly without introducing systematic biases [de Santi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.2463D/abstract).

## Physics-Informed Lensing and Image Reconstruction

The recovery of intrinsic galaxy properties from survey imaging is fundamentally limited by the convolution of the signal with an optical and atmospheric point spread function (PSF). Traditional approaches to removing this blur treat it as an ill-posed linear inverse problem. Physics-informed deep learning techniques incorporate the forward-modeling of the PSF directly into the network architecture to regularize this inversion. For example, by unrolling the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) algorithm into a neural network, models can learn optimal denoising priors and hyperparameters directly from simulated images [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522L..31L/abstract). This plug-and-play approach effectively handles space-variant deconvolution [Sureau et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...641A..67S/abstract) and translates directly to improved weak lensing shape measurements, reducing shear ellipticity errors by $38.6\%$ to $45.0\%$ compared to classical methods [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522L..31L/abstract). When Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are applied to direct shear measurement, they can achieve negligible multiplicative biases ($m < 10^{-3}$) while predicting shape parameters in fractions of a millisecond per galaxy [Ribli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.4847R/abstract).

Beyond measuring individual galaxy shapes, physics-informed architectures are utilized for weak lensing mass mapping—reconstructing the underlying dark matter convergence field ($\kappa$) from observed shear catalogs. Deep learning models, such as the U-Net based DeepMass algorithm, learn to map $\kappa$ using simulated data realizations that include non-Gaussian shape noise. By naturally capturing the non-linear structure of the dark matter distribution, these networks can outperform standard Wiener filtering by $11\%$ in mean squared error, even when the traditional filter is supplied with the exact underlying power spectrum [Jeffrey et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.5023J/abstract). Similarly, Bayesian Graph Neural Networks have been used to extract weak lensing convergence from photometric catalogs, hierarchically aggregating line-of-sight information to tightly constrain external convergence for time-delay cosmography [Park et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953..178P/abstract).

In the strong lensing regime, disentangling the mass distribution of the foreground deflector from the highly distorted background source requires complex, high-dimensional parameter inference. Physics-informed neural networks provide a solution via "differentiable physics." By coupling a CNN-based source model with an exact, fully differentiable implementation of gravitational lensing physics within a probabilistic programming framework, networks can optimize nearly 100 source and lens parameters simultaneously using efficient gradient-based sampling, achieving percent-level accuracy [Chianese et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496..381C/abstract). More recent advances use continuous neural fields to perform non-parametric source reconstructions while inferring the foreground lens configuration [Mishra-Sharma et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022mla..confE..34M/abstract). 

Furthermore, Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs) trained on synthetic lenses can directly estimate the posterior probability density functions of lens mass parameters. BNNs are increasingly used to accelerate traditional parametric profile fitting, either by providing highly accurate priors that reduce fitting errors by up to $37\%$ [Pearson et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4362P/abstract), or by directly propagating network-inferred posteriors into ensemble models to measure the Hubble constant ($H_0$) with sub-percent precision [Park et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910...39P/abstract).

## The Galaxy-Halo Connection and Environmental Constraints

Elucidating the mapping between the properties of galaxies and their host dark matter structures is a central challenge in modeling galaxy formation. While hydrodynamical simulations and semi-analytic models (SAMs) provide physically motivated prescriptions for this mapping, their computational expense limits their application across large cosmological volumes. Consequently, deep learning architectures have emerged as powerful, high-speed emulators that learn the complex, stochastic relationship between dark matter and baryons. 

Because the hierarchical assembly of dark matter halos is inherently topological, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) serve as a natural choice for modeling merger trees. Frameworks such as Mangrove deploy GNNs directly on merger trees to predict galactic stellar mass, cold gas mass, and star formation rates (SFRs). This approach achieves computational speedups of four orders of magnitude over traditional SAMs while simultaneously reducing root-mean-square errors by up to a factor of two [Jespersen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941....7J/abstract). In parallel with graph-based approaches, the dm2gal framework utilizes CNNs to efficiently map galactic stellar masses onto the dark matter fields of gravity-only simulations [Kasmanoff et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201200186K/abstract). Similarly, semi-recurrent neural networks that evaluate time-resolved halo accretion histories have been shown to successfully reproduce key features of the galaxy-halo connection, such as downsizing and color bimodality [Chittenden et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.5670C/abstract).

These networks also excel at integrating the influence of the local cosmic web environment. A galaxy's evolution is not strictly dictated by its host subhalo in isolation; rather, message-passing GNNs demonstrate that explicitly incorporating the positions, kinematics, and masses of neighboring subhalos significantly improves the accuracy of stellar mass inferences [Wu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230612327W/abstract). Interpretability tools applied to these models suggest that simple spherical overdensities on $\sim 3 \rm \, Mpc$ scales contain the majority of the environmental signal regulating star formation, though GNNs with larger linking lengths can successfully extract additional constraining power from structures up to $10 \rm \, Mpc$ away [Wu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240207995W/abstract). For particularly massive host halos (peak mass $>10^{12.5} M_\odot$), environmental data within just a $1 \rm \, Mpc$ radius is strongly correlated with the underlying subhalo mass [Bowden et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..37B/abstract).

Despite these successes, the literature highlights notable limitations and ambiguities when mapping dark matter to baryons. While neural networks predict stellar mass with exceptional fidelity (yielding Pearson correlation coefficients of $r \approx 0.98$), they struggle to fully capture properties highly reliant on complex baryonic feedback, such as specific star formation rate (sSFR), galaxy color, and structural size, where correlations routinely drop to $r \sim 0.7-0.8$ [de Santi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.2463D/abstract). This discrepancy indicates that dark-matter-only catalogs lack sufficient information to deterministically predict these features. To address this, current research is shifting towards probabilistic architectures that utilize Gaussian loss functions to capture the intrinsic scatter in the galaxy-halo connection [Stiskalek et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.4026S/abstract), as well as semi-stochastic corrections to model the inherently bursty variability of short-timescale star formation [Behera et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240916548B/abstract).

Beyond emulation, these algorithms are being used to discover novel physical correlations. By applying symbolic regression to trained neural networks, researchers have identified universal, virial-like analytic expressions that predict subhalo mass from internal velocity dispersions and maximum circular velocities—relations that hold across diverse subgrid physics models [Shao et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927...85S/abstract). Furthermore, by combining GNNs with symbolic regression, researchers have derived a universal analytic equation that predicts the matter density parameter $\Omega_m$ directly from tracer phase-space distributions [Shao et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956..149S/abstract). In some regimes, machine learning models even challenge established assumptions; for instance, the reinforcement-learning framework GalaxyNet predicts a stellar-to-halo mass relation at high redshift ($z \gtrsim 0.5$) featuring a lower normalization and a shallower low-mass slope than is assumed in standard empirical models [Moster et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.2115M/abstract).

## Stellar Population Synthesis and Spectral Emulation

Stellar population synthesis (SPS) models are a cornerstone of extragalactic astrophysics, used to physically relate the observed spectral energy distributions (SEDs) of galaxies to their intrinsic properties, such as stellar mass, metallicity, and star formation history (SFH). However, traditional SED fitting requires repeatedly computing complex physical models—including stellar evolution tracks, initial mass functions, and dust radiative transfer—which is computationally prohibitive for the billions of galaxies observed by modern large-scale surveys. To overcome this bottleneck, neural networks have been widely adopted to emulate SPS codes, acting as high-speed surrogate models.

In the forward-modeling approach, neural networks learn the mapping from physical galaxy parameters to the predicted observables. A pioneering architecture in this domain is SPECULATOR [Alsing et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..249....5A/abstract), which employs a principal component analysis (PCA) to compress the high-dimensional space of synthetic spectra, followed by a neural network that predicts the PCA basis coefficients as a function of SPS parameters. This strategy yields a computational speedup of $10^3$ to $10^4$ over direct SPS calculations while maintaining percent-level accuracy [Alsing et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..249....5A/abstract). Similar emulation strategies have been extended to broadband photometry, such as the ProMage network, which accelerates the generation of observer- and rest-frame magnitudes by a factor of $10^4$ [Tortorelli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250900150T/abstract). Emulators are also particularly valuable for bypassing the stiff ordinary differential equations and intense computational demands of dust radiative transfer. For example, neural networks integrated into codes like GRASIL and STARDUSTER can predict multi-wavelength dust attenuation and emission up to two orders of magnitude faster than exact numerical solvers [Silva et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423..746S/abstract), [Qiu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930...66Q/abstract).

Conversely, neural networks are extensively used to solve the inverse problem: directly mapping observed photometry or spectra to physical parameters. Simulation-based inference and amortized neural posterior estimation (ANPE) have emerged as powerful frameworks for this task. Tools such as SEDFLOW [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract) and Synference [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110640H/abstract) leverage neural density estimators to generate full Bayesian posterior distributions for SED model parameters. Once trained, these models can infer the properties of a new galaxy in milliseconds—reducing the processing time from tens of CPU hours per galaxy to around a second, matching the accuracy of traditional Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract). 

Beyond integrated SEDs, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been deployed to extract stellar population properties directly from optical spectra. Models like STARNET significantly outperform traditional full-spectrum fitting codes in computational speed while recovering the mean stellar age, metallicity, and dust extinction $E(B-V)$ with an error scatter of less than $0.08$ dex [Woo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.4260W/abstract). Similar results have been achieved for high-throughput parameterization of Sloan Digital Sky Survey spectra [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.52710557W/abstract). Taking generative models further, the StarFlow approach uses normalizing flows to recover full likelihood distributions for stellar ages, producing the largest evolved star age catalog to date with robust uncertainty characterization [Stone-Martinez et al. ## Simulation-Based Inference and Uncertainty Quantification

In modern cosmology, the likelihood function for complex observables—such as non-linear structure formation or hydrodynamical feedback—is often computationally intractable or analytically unknown. This bottleneck has driven a paradigm shift toward likelihood-free inference, also known as simulation-based inference (SBI). SBI bypasses the need for explicit likelihoods by forward-modeling mock data and using neural networks as massive data compressors or direct density estimators [Jeffrey et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501..954J/abstract). By exploiting raw field-level data, such as 3D galaxy distributions or weak lensing mass maps, neural networks can extract non-Gaussian information that is lost when using traditional two-point summary statistics (e.g., the power spectrum). Consequently, field-level SBI can yield significantly tighter constraints on cosmological parameters; for instance, determining the amplitude of matter fluctuations, $\sigma_8$, with an error margin more than twice as tight as standard methods [Lemos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109h3536L/abstract). Field-level reconstructions are also powerful tools for correcting observational distortions; the DarkAI framework uses a U-Net architecture to reconstruct the mass density, velocity, and tidal fields of dark matter from galaxy surveys, effectively addressing systematic challenges like geometric selection and redshift-space distortions (RSD) [Shi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJS..280...53S/abstract). To further mitigate these effects, neural networks can accurately estimate the line-of-sight velocities of individual galaxies directly from environmental characteristics, effectively eliminating RSD artifacts at large scales [Chen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.3947C/abstract). At the cluster scale, CNNs have been successfully applied to Planck Compton-$y$ maps to provide cluster mass estimates that are entirely independent of traditional hydrostatic equilibrium assumptions [de Andres et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6.1325D/abstract).

To handle the vast data volumes of upcoming Stage-IV surveys, researchers increasingly utilize Amortized Neural Posterior Estimation (ANPE). Once a network is trained on a prior distribution of simulations, ANPE models can instantaneously evaluate the posterior probability for new observations without requiring repeated Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling. In spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting, ANPE pipelines can generate full multi-dimensional parameter posteriors in approximately one second per galaxy, bypassing thousands of CPU hours while maintaining excellent agreement with traditional MCMC results [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract). Likewise, simulation-based inference pipelines have achieved massive speed-ups over Hamiltonian Monte Carlo in constraining fundamental galactic parameters, such as the initial mass function (IMF) slope, from stellar abundances [Buck et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...702A.184B/abstract). Generalized open-source frameworks for implicit likelihood inference now allow these amortized methods to be rapidly scaled across varied astrophysical domains [Ho et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..54H/abstract).

Robust uncertainty quantification is critical for precision astrophysics, rendering simple deep learning point estimates insufficient. Bayesian neural networks (BNNs) address this limitation by learning probability distributions over their internal weights, providing well-calibrated posterior probability density functions (PDFs) that capture both aleatoric (data-driven) and epistemic (model-driven) uncertainties. In photometric redshift estimation, BNNs fed with multiband images capture morphological cues to produce reliable uncertainties that meet stringent survey requirements while significantly reducing catastrophic outlier rates [Jones et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..130J/abstract), [Jones et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..159J/abstract). BNNs are also highly effective in strong gravitational lens modeling, characterizing the posterior PDFs of lens mass profiles to achieve sub-percent precision in ensemble Hubble constant ($H_0$) inferences [Park et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910...39P/abstract). Similarly, deep generative models like normalizing flows have been employed alongside neural networks to infer the dynamical masses of galaxy clusters directly from phase-space distributions, yielding full mass PDFs [Kodi Ramanah et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.1985K/abstract).

Despite these advances, the transition to neural network-based posteriors is accompanied by unresolved tensions. A primary concern is properly propagating the intrinsic approximation error of neural networks into the strict error budgets required by precision cosmology [Grandón et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022OJAp....5E..12G/abstract). Furthermore, SBI frameworks are highly vulnerable to the "domain shift" problem: models trained on specific hydrodynamical simulations (e.g., IllustrisTNG) often suffer severe performance degradation or introduce biases when evaluated on real observational data or simulations with different subgrid physics prescriptions [Ntampaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251009748N/abstract). Current research seeks to mitigate these biases through domain-generalized networks [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975...38L/abstract) and model-insensitive estimators designed to marginalize over the variance in simulation physics [Jo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991..120J/abstract), ensuring that the resulting posteriors reflect robust physical truths rather than simulation-specific artifacts.

## Interpretability, Robustness, and Domain Adaptation

Despite the widespread adoption of deep learning in astrophysics, the "black box" nature of neural networks poses significant challenges for scientific inference. To establish trust in these models, researchers are increasingly utilizing Explainable AI (XAI), rigorously evaluating model robustness against adversarial attacks, and employing domain adaptation to bridge the gap between simulated training sets and observational data.

**Explainable AI and Saliency Mapping**
Understanding the internal logic of neural networks is crucial for ensuring that models rely on physical features rather than dataset artifacts. Saliency mapping techniques, such as SMOOTHGRAD, have demonstrated that XAI can effectively isolate physical parameters. For example, using saliency maps to extract galactic bar lengths yields a lower root-mean-square error (RMSE $= 1.69$) compared to direct convolutional neural network (CNN) regression [Bhambra et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.5032B/abstract). Similarly, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values have been integrated into interpretable frameworks like Katachi to decode the specific morphological features that correlate with a galaxy's star formation history [Alfonzo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967..152A/abstract). 

In cosmological contexts, leave-one-out methods and average saliency maps have been used to discover previously unknown self-calibration modes for galaxy cluster mass estimation [Ntampaka et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926...45N/abstract). Beyond post-hoc analysis of standard architectures, researchers are developing intrinsically interpretable models, such as Sparse Feature Networks (SFNets), which evaluate linear combinations of transparent features without sacrificing the predictive accuracy typical of deep learning [Wu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250100089W/abstract). 

**Robustness and Adversarial Attacks**
Deep learning models are highly sensitive to minor data perturbations. In astronomical imaging, processing errors or sensor artifacts can act as one-pixel adversarial attacks. Studies show that a single altered pixel, or higher levels of Poisson noise, can easily flip morphological classifications into incorrect categories [Ćiprijanović et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MLS&T...3c5007C/abstract). Mitigating these vulnerabilities requires specialized architectures and training routines. For instance, incorporating inductive biases via group-equivariant convolutional networks—such as those preserving $E(2)$ Euclidean symmetries (translations, rotations, and reflections)—naturally improves a network's robustness against both noise and one-pixel perturbations [Pandya et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231101500P/abstract). These explicitly preserved symmetries have also been leveraged to improve classification robustness for Fanaroff-Riley radio galaxies [Scaife et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.2369S/abstract).

**Domain Adaptation**
A critical unresolved tension in astrophysical machine learning is the "domain shift" problem: models trained on idealized or specific hydrodynamical simulations often degrade in performance when applied to different simulations or real observational data. The severity of this issue is significant; a model trained to estimate cluster masses using the Magneticum simulation performed $40\%$ worse when tested on out-of-domain IllustrisTNG data [Ntampaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251009748N/abstract).

To ensure that models generalize effectively, researchers employ domain adaptation techniques such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) and Domain Adversarial Neural Networks (DANN), which enforce cross-domain knowledge sharing by aligning the latent space representations of the source and target domains [Ćiprijanović et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201103591C/abstract). The quantitative impact of these methods is substantial. Training with domain adaptation has been shown to improve classification accuracy by up to $23\%$ on noisy observational data, while increasing the latent space distance between a baseline image and an adversarially perturbed image by a factor of $\approx 2.3$, thereby creating a more robust decision boundary [Ćiprijanović et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MLS&T...3c5007C/abstract). Additionally, parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods like Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) provide an efficient mechanism to generalize redshift estimation models by combining ground truths from precise spectroscopic data with broader photometric datasets, mitigating discrepancies between data domains [Seenivasan et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260100146S/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The impending arrival of data from next-generation surveys, such as the Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST) and the Euclid mission, presents a dual challenge of unprecedented data volume and stringent precision requirements. While deep learning models can rapidly process these large datasets, ensuring that their outputs remain physically sound necessitates the integration of physics-informed architectures. However, transitioning Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and related techniques from idealized proof-of-concept scenarios to complex, survey-scale analysis remains a significant hurdle.

A primary open question is the computational scalability of PINNs when handling the full complexity of astrophysical environments. Current implementations, such as the gravity-informed neural network (GRINN) framework, have demonstrated that PINNs can achieve an order-of-magnitude speedup over traditional grid-based codes for 3D self-gravitating hydrodynamic systems [Auddy et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MLS&T...5b5014A/abstract). Yet, a known tension exists in the literature: for lower-dimensional (1D or 2D) calculations, PINNs can sometimes be computationally slower than conventional numerical solvers due to the overhead of network training and optimization [Auddy et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MLS&T...5b5014A/abstract). It remains to be seen whether physics-informed approaches can be efficiently scaled to accommodate the full dynamic range of 3D magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulations with radiative feedback at the high resolutions required by modern cosmological surveys.

Another critical challenge is the issue of model misspecification and "domain shift"—the degradation of a network's performance when applied to data outside its training distribution. Deep learning estimators trained on specific cosmological hydrodynamic simulations (e.g., IllustrisTNG) often fail to generalize when evaluated on different subgrid physics models (e.g., SIMBA) or when applied directly to real observational data [Ntampaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251009748N/abstract), [Ćiprijanović et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MLS&T...3c5007C/abstract). Addressing this requires active research into domain generalization techniques and the development of "model-insensitive estimators." These methods aim to extract universal physical relations—such as mapping the matter density parameter $\Omega_m$ from galaxy phase-space—that remain robust to underlying variations in subgrid prescriptions [Jo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250213239J/abstract), [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240902256L/abstract).

Future directions heavily emphasize the construction of hybrid pipelines that seamlessly combine procedural physics solvers with neural networks. By enforcing strict adherence to physics, frameworks leveraging Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (NODEs) can embed deep learning directly into the governing differential equations of a system. This approach has already proven effective in discovering hidden physics, such as characterizing complex mass-loading functions in the hot galactic winds of M82 [Nguyen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231102057N/abstract) and modeling the secular orbital evolution of supermassive black hole binaries [Chan et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260113019C/abstract). A major future direction is embodied by the DREAMS project, which utilizes thousands of hydrodynamical simulations to train models capable of disentangling complex baryonic feedback from alternative dark matter physics [Rose et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982...68R/abstract). Furthermore, end-to-end "differentiable physics" pipelines allow for simultaneous, gradient-based optimization of physical parameters (e.g., in strong gravitational lensing), explicitly uniting gravitational physics with generative neural networks [Chianese et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496..381C/abstract). Such flexible, physics-embedded architectures could eventually be extended to test alternative dynamical theories, complementing purely data-driven efforts like [[deep-mond|Deep-MOND]].

Finally, safely propagating the intrinsic approximation errors of neural networks into cosmological parameter inferences is an unresolved bottleneck. To meet the tight error budgets demanded by Stage IV cosmology, future PINNs and hybrid pipelines must incorporate rigorous uncertainty quantification—such as Bayesian error propagation [Grandón et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022OJAp....5E..12G/abstract)—to prevent network-induced biases from compromising precision parameter estimation.
