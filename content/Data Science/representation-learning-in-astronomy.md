## Article

In the era of petabyte-scale sky surveys, extracting scientific insight from complex, high-dimensional datasets is a central challenge in astrophysics. **Representation learning** refers to the use of machine learning algorithms—predominantly deep neural networks—to automatically discover the optimal features or patterns required to perform a given task directly from raw data, such as multi-band images or 1D spectra. Historically, astronomers relied heavily on manual feature engineering, compressing survey data into human-defined summary statistics like magnitudes, colors, and Sérsic profiles before applying traditional statistical classifiers [Banerji et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..342B/abstract). However, foundational applications of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) in astronomy demonstrated that end-to-end automated feature extraction could achieve performance highly competitive with conventional machine learning techniques without the need for manual feature engineering [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4463K/abstract). By processing raw pixel or spectral data directly, representation learning models compress astronomical objects into low-dimensional **latent embeddings**—continuous mathematical spaces where physical proximity tightly correlates with intrinsic astrophysical properties like stellar mass, metallicity, and morphology.

While early deep learning in astronomy focused primarily on supervised tasks, such as discrete morphological classification or photometric redshift estimation [Hoyle et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&C....16...34H/abstract), the field is currently undergoing a major paradigm shift toward unsupervised and self-supervised frameworks. Because acquiring expert-annotated labels for millions of objects is a severe bottleneck, modern approaches train "foundation models" that learn general-purpose features without explicit human supervision. Techniques such as autoencoders, generative adversarial networks (GANs), and [[momentum-contrastive-learning|Momentum Contrastive Learning]] allow models to build semantically rich latent spaces directly from unlabelled datasets. These learned representations naturally recover fundamental astrophysical relationships—such as the star-formation main sequence or the chemical signatures of accreted stellar populations—and can be fine-tuned to achieve state-of-the-art accuracy on downstream tasks with significantly fewer labels. 

This article explores the application of representation learning across diverse domains of astronomy, tracking its historical evolution and detailing its use in both imaging and spectroscopic surveys. It covers how compressed neural representations enable automated chemical tagging, cosmological inference, and the unsupervised discovery of rare phenomena like Odd Radio Circles. Furthermore, the article examines the critical open questions currently defining the field. Chief among these is the "reality gap" or domain shift problem—the challenge of successfully adapting models trained on idealized cosmological simulations to real, heterogeneous observational data. Finally, it addresses the ongoing effort to "open the black box" using Explainable AI (XAI) and Bayesian frameworks, which are essential for ensuring that learned representations remain physically interpretable, uncertainty-aware, and robust enough for deployment on petabyte-scale streams from next-generation facilities like the Vera C. Rubin Observatory and the Square Kilometre Array.

## Introduction to Representation Learning in Astrophysics

Modern astronomical surveys produce petabyte-scale datasets characterized by complex, multi-band, high-dimensional data. Traditionally, machine learning applications in astrophysics relied heavily on manual feature extraction. Astronomers pre-calculated summary statistics—such as magnitudes, colors, Sérsic indices, and non-parametric morphological indicators like the Concentration-Asymmetry-Smoothness (CAS) or Gini-$M_{20}$ parameters—which were then fed into shallow classifiers. However, these hand-crafted features inevitably result in information loss, as they compress the complex spatial and spectral distributions of raw survey data into a limited set of human-defined metrics.

The advent of deep learning has driven a paradigm shift from catalog-based feature engineering to end-to-end representation learning directly from raw pixels [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4463K/abstract). Neural architectures are explicitly designed to handle high-dimensional data by automatically learning optimal, hierarchical feature representations. By operating on raw image cutouts or 1D spectra, these models capture intricate structural and physical information that was previously discarded.

A critical conceptual shift enabled by automated feature extraction is the transition from discrete, rigid classification schemes (such as the traditional Hubble sequence) to continuous latent embeddings. Unsupervised machine learning methods provide independent, data-driven classification schemes. For instance, Vector-Quantized Variational Autoencoders (VQ-VAEs) combined with hierarchical clustering have successfully grouped galaxies into discrete morphological clusters, highlighting the intrinsic vagueness of rigid, human-defined Hubble types [Cheng et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.4446C/abstract). Within these learned latent embeddings, proximity correlates strongly with physical properties such as stellar mass, star formation rate, and metallicity, providing a purely data-driven, physics-based taxonomy of the galaxy population [Martínez-Solaeche et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...688A.160M/abstract).

Despite the success of supervised deep learning, acquiring human-annotated labels for millions of objects remains a primary bottleneck. Consequently, the field is rapidly adopting self-supervised learning (SSL) frameworks to create generalized "foundation models" for astronomy. Techniques such as [[momentum-contrastive-learning|Momentum Contrastive Learning]] [Shen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASP..135j4501S/abstract) allow models to learn rich, low-dimensional latent representations entirely without explicit human labels. These self-supervised representations can recover scientifically meaningful relationships and can match the accuracy of fully supervised models while requiring significantly fewer labeled examples for downstream fine-tuning [Hayat et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...911L..33H/abstract). 

## Historical Evolution: From Hand-Crafted Features to Latent Spaces

Historically, astronomical representation learning relied heavily on catalog-based summary statistics. Before the advent of deep learning, astronomers pre-calculated hand-crafted features such as magnitudes, colors, and morphological parameters. To process these multi-dimensional datasets, dimensionality reduction techniques like Principal Component Analysis (PCA) became standard practice. For instance, early automated finders for galaxy-scale strong lenses employed PCA-based basis vectors to model and subtract the smooth light of luminous red galaxies, allowing researchers to enhance and detect residual Einstein rings via curvelet filtering [Joseph et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A..63J/abstract). Similarly, early automated morphology classifiers utilized a Fourier approach, reconstructing 2D light distributions via Fourier series fits to azimuthal profiles and using PCA to compress the variance before classification [Odewahn et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...568..539O/abstract). These extracted features were subsequently fed into shallow, supervised machine learning algorithms. Classifiers such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs) and Random Forests were widely deployed and achieved notable success in tasks such as star-galaxy separation [Krakowski et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...596A..39K/abstract) and photometric redshift estimation [Heinis et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...821...86H/abstract).

Howeever, the standard textbook approach of utilizing template-fitting and human-engineered color-cuts was ultimately challenged by the advent of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). Deep learning models allowed for end-to-end representation learning directly from raw pixel data, effectively capturing hierarchical spatial information that is frequently lost in catalog summaries [Hoyle et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&C....16...34H/abstract), [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4463K/abstract). CNNs rapidly established a new baseline in the field, consistently outperforming SVMs trained on manual features for binary morphological classification (e.g., elliptical versus spiral) [Cheng et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.4209C/abstract). 

More recently, the field has undergone a second paradigm shift: moving from discrete supervised classification to unsupervised latent spaces. Modern unsupervised and self-supervised architectures project raw image data into low-dimensional latent manifolds that reveal the physical distribution of galaxies. Techniques utilizing denoising autoencoders [Frontera-Pons et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...603A..60F/abstract) have shown that galaxies naturally organize along continuous sequences of structural and physical evolution, whereas models like Vector-Quantized Variational Autoencoders (VQ-VAEs) algorithmically identify discrete structural categories without human bias [Cheng et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.4446C/abstract).

The current frontier of representation learning utilizes self-supervised frameworks, including [[momentum-contrastive-learning|Momentum Contrastive Learning]] and other contrastive architectures [Hayat et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...911L..33H/abstract), to learn robust, physics-based representations without human labels. By extracting optimal, domain-invariant features directly from the data manifold, these foundation models can achieve supervised-level accuracies on downstream tasks while requiring significantly fewer explicit labels.

## Supervised and Generative Architectures for Astronomical Imaging

The transition from manual feature engineering—such as deriving Sérsic indices or using concentration-asymmetry-smoothness (CAS) statistics—to end-to-end representation learning directly from raw pixels represents a major paradigm shift in astronomical imaging. Supervised deep learning models, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), are now the standard for extracting morphological and physical information from optical and near-infrared surveys. 

CNNs consistently outperform traditional machine learning algorithms in morphological classification. For binary classification tasks, such as separating elliptical from spiral galaxies, optimized CNNs routinely achieve accuracies exceeding $99\%$ [Cheng et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.4209C/abstract). They also exhibit greater robustness to observational noise compared to alternative architectures like Capsule Networks [Lukic et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.1729L/abstract). As datasets grow, the field has increasingly adopted lightweight network variants, such as EfficientNet-G3, which drastically reduce the parameter count while maintaining highly accurate classification performance [Wu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RAA....22k5011W/abstract). Recently, the Vision Transformer (ViT) has emerged as a competitive alternative to CNNs. By utilizing self-attention mechanisms rather than localized convolutional kernels, ViTs have shown particular promise in classifying faint and smaller-sized galaxies, achieving results commensurate with the best CNN ensembles [Yao-Yu Lin et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv211001024Y/abstract). Furthermore, neural network classification models are now increasingly utilized to separate local low-mass dwarf galaxies from massive high-redshift contaminants in wide-field photometric surveys with high precision [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250300109Z/abstract). These architectures extend seamlessly to the radio regime, where 3-layer CNNs have been utilized to classify compact versus multi-component extended radio sources with over $97\%$ accuracy on Radio Galaxy Zoo data [Lukic et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476..246L/abstract). To circumvent the immense labeling costs required by these deep architectures, supervised fine-tuning is increasingly combined with self-supervised pre-training, such as [[momentum-contrastive-learning|Momentum Contrastive Learning]], bridging the gap between unsupervised representations and highly accurate morphological taxonomies [Shen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASP..135j4501S/abstract).

Beyond classification, deep learning has revolutionized image processing and parameter modeling tasks. Removing optical and atmospheric blurring (the point spread function, or PSF) is an ill-posed linear inverse problem critical for weak gravitational lensing measurements. Modern approaches demonstrate that utilizing U-Net architectures to post-process classical Tikhonov deconvolutions is highly computationally efficient compared to standard Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) methods [Sureau et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...641A..67S/abstract), while newer architectures such as unrolled plug-and-play ADMM networks provide state-of-the-art accuracy improvements over both classic and modern methods [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522L..31L/abstract). Deep learning has also superseded classical profile fitting; methods like DeepLeGATo, a CNN-based approach for 2D photometric profile modeling, can retrieve Sérsic parameters $3000$ times faster than traditional fitting software like GALFIT [Tuccillo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475..894T/abstract). For robust image denoising, self-supervised restoration processes provide critical quantitative control over denoising errors. The TDR (Training, Denoising, and Restoring) method, based on the Self2Self architecture, has been successfully applied to clarify weak structures in diverse astronomical contexts, ranging from solar magnetograms to HST galaxy images [Liu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..608L/abstract).

For photometric parameter estimation, deep learning methods such as LUMOS have been developed for narrow-band surveys to double the signal-to-noise ratio in galaxy photometry by accurately predicting the background-subtracted flux probability density function [Cabayol et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.4048C/abstract). Additionally, deep neural networks and random forests have been applied to predict the neutral hydrogen (H I) content of galaxies directly from multi-band optical photometry, successfully recovering H I mass trends as demonstrated on MUFASA simulations and ALFALFA survey data [Morice-Atkinson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.4194M/abstract). Similarly, in photometric redshift estimation, machine learning regressors like CATBOOST have demonstrated superior performance over classical template-fitting methods (e.g., EAZY) for standard optical and infrared galaxy samples in the DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518..513L/abstract). However, it is important to note that template-fitting methods like EAZY still provide more accurate photometric redshift estimates for high-redshift galaxies, particularly when extrapolating beyond the redshift range of the training sample [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518..513L/abstract). To further optimize these estimates, multi-modal architectures like the Hybrid-z framework fuse 4-band image cutouts directly with 9-band tabular magnitudes, reducing photometric redshift scatter by $20\%$ compared to traditional neural networks [William et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250101942W/abstract). Further advancing multi-modal applications, architectures such as the Mantis Shrimp model fuse multi-survey imagery from GALEX, PanSTARRS, and UnWISE to improve photometric redshift estimates. Such frameworks demonstrate that early and late fusion strategies perform comparably when handling the varying resolutions across multi-wavelength bands .

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) are frequently deployed to address the domain shift problem and enhance observational realism. A common application of GANs is cross-survey mapping and domain adaptation. For example, generative deep learning can successfully translate images from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) into mock Dark Energy Survey (DES) images, retaining essential morphological information while emulating the target survey's noise and signal-to-noise properties [Buncher et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503..777B/abstract). GANs are also highly effective at image deblurring; models like SeeingGAN have been used to computationally remove atmospheric seeing from ground-based Subaru Telescope observations, generating quasi-Hubble Space Telescope (HST) images to reveal fine substructures like spiral arms and clumps [Gan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210309711G/abstract). Furthermore, chained GAN architectures are utilized to model the joint distribution of galaxy properties, producing physically realistic synthetic galaxy images that provide vital data augmentation for calibrating shape measurements in cosmological weak lensing pipelines [Fussell et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.3203F/abstract).

## Self-Supervised and Contrastive Learning Frameworks

With the exponential growth of astronomical datasets, reliance on human-annotated labels has become a severe bottleneck for machine learning applications. Self-supervised learning (SSL) addresses this limitation by extracting low-dimensional latent representations directly from unlabelled data. Pre-training strategies are not strictly limited to self-supervised frameworks; highly robust supervised representations, such as those from models like Zoobot, enable few-shot downstream tasks like morphology-based similarity searches and finding researcher-specific anomalies using only a few hundred explicit labels [Walmsley et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513.1581W/abstract). Additionally, architectures like Siamese Networks enable few-shot learning for morphological classification, achieving over $90\%$ accuracy with roughly half the training labels required by traditional deep architectures such as ResNet-50 [Zhang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RAA....22e5002Z/abstract). However, to fully utilize massive, unlabelled sky surveys, researchers have turned to contrastive learning—training models to maximize the representational similarity between augmented views of the same astronomical object (positive pairs) while minimizing similarity with different objects (negative pairs). 

Several contrastive frameworks originally developed for computer vision have been successfully adapted for astrophysics, notably SimCLR, Bootstrap Your Own Latent (BYOL) [Hossain et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PrCS..222..601H/abstract), and [[momentum-contrastive-learning|Momentum Contrastive Learning]]. For instance, BYOL has been applied to Galaxy Zoo DECaLS imaging to extract features that group morphologically similar galaxies and identify rare anomalies, such as interacting mergers, entirely without explicit supervision [Mohale et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.1274M/abstract). Similarly, momentum contrastive architectures utilizing ResNet-50 backbones have achieved automated morphological classification accuracies exceeding $90\%$, effectively bridging the performance gap between unsupervised algorithms and traditional supervised regimes [Shen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASP..135j4501S/abstract).

A major finding in the recent literature is that the latent spaces constructed by these contrastive frameworks naturally encode the underlying astrophysics. When applied to multiband photometry and Integral Field Unit (IFU) spatial data, contrastive models autonomously recover the continuous distribution of galaxy populations, cleanly separating the star-forming blue cloud, the red sequence, and the green valley [Martínez-Solaeche et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...688A.160M/abstract). Furthermore, self-supervised frameworks applied directly to MaNGA IFU data have allowed for the visualization of internal kinematics, driving the data-driven clustering of galaxies into main-sequence disks and slow rotators [Sarmiento et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...921..177S/abstract). The learned embedding dimensions exhibit strong quantitative correlations with fundamental physical parameters such as the H$\alpha$ equivalent width, stellar mass, stellar metallicity, and luminosity-weighted age [Martínez-Solaeche et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...688A.160M/abstract). The application of contrastive learning is not restricted to galaxy morphology; it has also been successfully extended to 1D stellar spectra (e.g., Gaia XP and RVS) to extract physically meaningful, cross-modal representations of stellar properties [Buck et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016081B/abstract).

By pre-training on massive unlabelled datasets to create "foundation models," researchers can fine-tune these semantic representations for diverse downstream tasks. For complex challenges like photometric redshift estimation and morphological classification, SSL representations have been shown to match or outperform classical supervised state-of-the-art methods while requiring $2$ to $4$ times fewer labeled training examples [Hayat et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...911L..33H/abstract). Advanced feature extraction layers utilizing combinations of Vision Transformers (ViT) and CNNs in contrastive setups have yielded classification accuracies up to $96.5\%$ on standard datasets like SDSS [Wei et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASP..134k4508W/abstract).

Despite these successes, there remains an active debate regarding the necessity of domain-specific pre-training. While custom models pre-trained on survey data demonstrate excellent performance, standard foundation models trained on natural images (e.g., ImageNet) often provide surprisingly robust baselines for astronomical tasks. Recent investigations into this phenomenon propose a "Platonic Representation Hypothesis," which suggests that varying architectures and data modalities—such as imaging and spectroscopy from JWST, HSC, and DESI—ultimately converge toward a shared, objective representation of the underlying astrophysics, scaling predictably with model capacity [UniverseTBD et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919453U/abstract).

## Representation Learning in Spectroscopy and Chemical Tagging

Representation learning provides a powerful framework for extracting physical information from high-dimensional 1D spectra, shifting the paradigm from traditional template matching and synthetic spectral modeling toward purely data-driven approaches.

In extragalactic astrophysics, neural networks are increasingly used to compress complex spectral energy distributions (SEDs) into low-dimensional latent spaces. **Autoencoders**, particularly denoising variants, have been trained to learn non-linear representations of rest-frame emission lines directly from data, significantly improving the accuracy of **spectroscopic redshift** estimation across various signal-to-noise regimes [Frontera-Pons et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...625A..73F/abstract). Latent variable models, such as conditional variational autoencoders, demonstrate that the diversity of SDSS galaxy spectra can be effectively compressed into as few as four latent variables, driven strongly by the continuum below $5000\,\mathrm{\AA}$ and prominent emission lines [Iwasaki et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231117414I/abstract). End-to-end architectures like GaSNet-II leverage these learned features for simultaneous spectral classification and redshift prediction, achieving mean spectroscopic redshift errors as low as $0.23\%$ for galaxies and $2.1\%$ for quasars [Zhong et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532..643Z/abstract). Supervised models are highly effective for extracting these population properties. For instance, CNNs can simultaneously predict stellar age, metallicity, color excess, and velocity dispersion directly from SDSS spectra with computational speeds 10 times faster than traditional synthesis methods [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.52710557W/abstract).

For stellar astrophysics, representation learning effectively bypasses the theoretical uncertainties inherent in synthetic spectral models. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on high-resolution spectra can extract atmospheric parameters and chemical abundances with remarkable precision. For example, networks applied to GIRAFFE spectra achieve precisions of $37\,\mathrm{K}$ for effective temperature ($T_{\rm eff}$) and $0.06\,\mathrm{dex}$ for surface gravity ($\log g$), alongside multi-element abundance precisions such as $0.04\,\mathrm{dex}$ for [Fe/H] [Ambrosch et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...672A..46A/abstract). Cross-domain representation learning enables the transfer of these predictive models across different wavelength regimes. Techniques have successfully mapped near-infrared APOGEE models onto optical BOSS spectra, creating unified representations over an expansive parameter space ranging from $1700 < T_{\rm eff} < 100{,}000\,\mathrm{K}$ [Sizemore et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167..173S/abstract).

A major application of these techniques is **chemical tagging**—the effort to reconstruct disrupted star clusters based on their conserved chemical signatures. Because traditional tagging is limited by systematic errors in abundance pipelines, researchers have developed networks that learn *disentangled* spectral representations. By explicitly separating chemical factors of variation from standard atmospheric parameters ($T_{\rm eff}$, $\log g$, [Fe/H]), these models enable "abundance-free" chemical tagging directly from the latent space [de Mijolla et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913...12D/abstract). Furthermore, following the initial discovery of rare chemical anomalies like phosphorus-rich giant stars via conventional detailed abundance analysis, researchers are currently exploring the use of unsupervised machine learning algorithms to identify additional P-rich stars that challenge current nucleosynthetic models [Brauner et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250303569B/abstract).

In the context of Galactic archaeology, learned representations successfully differentiate accreted **stellar populations** from in-situ stars. Machine learning classifiers utilizing chemo-kinematic representations can recover $>90\%$ of accreted stars in the Milky Way disc beyond $5\,\mathrm{kpc}$, even where in-situ stars overwhelmingly dominate the local distribution [Sante et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4363S/abstract). To handle massive, heterogeneous datasets like Gaia XP coefficients and RVS spectra, the field is turning to self-supervised frameworks. Methods utilizing contrastive architectures—analogous to [[momentum-contrastive-learning|Momentum Contrastive Learning]] in computer vision—can project multi-modal stellar spectra into highly structured, physically meaningful spaces without requiring explicit labels [Buck et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016081B/abstract).

Despite these successes, the literature highlights ongoing tensions regarding the robustness of spectral representation learning. While network gradients and saliency mapping confirm that these models base their predictions on true physical absorption features rather than instrumental artifacts [Ambrosch et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...672A..46A/abstract), purely data-driven models remain highly susceptible to systematic biases in their training sets. 

## Cross-Survey Mapping and Domain Adaptation

A persistent challenge in applying machine learning to astronomy is the "domain shift" or "reality gap" problem. Models trained on synthetic data from cosmological simulations, or on observational data from a specific survey, often experience a severe degradation in performance when applied to real data or a different survey [Ntampaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251009748N/abstract). Specific observational environments exacerbate these challenges; for example, models trained outside the Galactic plane suffer significant misclassifications when applied to the 'Zone of Avoidance,' where high extinction and severe star crowding introduce a unique domain shift [Marchant Cortés et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..18M/abstract).

To bridge the gap between simulations and reality, the inclusion of observational realism in training sets is critical. Research indicates that mimicking the instrumental footprint—such as adding appropriate Gaussian or Poisson noise, sky background, and the point spread function (PSF)—is often more important than the exact astrophysical prescriptions of the underlying simulation. In the classification of galaxy mergers, models trained on fully realistic mock images achieved $87.1\%$ accuracy, whereas simplifying the underlying physics from complex dust-inclusive radiative transfer models to basic stellar maps resulted in a relatively modest performance drop (to $79.6\%$). Conversely, training on idealized images without observational noise led to poor performance when applied to survey-realistic images [Bottrell et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.5390B/abstract).

When direct forward-modeling of observational effects is insufficient, explicit domain adaptation techniques are employed to align the feature distributions of the source (training) and target (testing) domains. Techniques such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) and Domain Adversarial Neural Networks (DANN) encourage the model to learn domain-invariant latent representations [Ćiprijanović et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201103591C/abstract), [Ćiprijanović et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MLS&T...3c5007C/abstract). Similar unsupervised domain adaptation strategies have been successfully applied to Graph Neural Networks (DA-GNNs) to extract scale-free cosmological parameters robustly across different hydrodynamic simulation suites [Roncoli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231101588R/abstract). Advanced self-supervised frameworks, such as [[momentum-contrastive-learning|Momentum Contrastive Learning]], are also increasingly explored to extract foundational, domain-invariant physical features without relying on heavily curated labels [Shen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASP..135j4501S/abstract). In many applications, domain adaptation leverages knowledge from entirely non-astronomical domains. Transfer learning from models pre-trained on terrestrial photographs (such as ImageNet) has proven highly effective for identifying complex structures like rare galaxy mergers, providing a significant performance improvement over traditional, non-parametric CAS statistics [Ackermann et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479..415A/abstract).

In addition to simulation-to-real transfer, deep learning enables direct cross-survey mapping. Generative architectures, such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), have been utilized to translate images from one survey's domain to another. The "Survey2Survey" approach demonstrated that generative models can map Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) images to Dark Energy Survey (DES) equivalent quality, effectively generating false DES representations that recover morphological information and artificially increase the signal-to-noise ratio [Buncher et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503..777B/abstract). This image-to-image translation has even been extended to interpolate and extrapolate galaxy observations across entirely different photometric bands, bridging ultraviolet, optical, and infrared datasets [Zaazou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250115149Z/abstract). 

Transfer learning also facilitates the application of established models to new, deeper observations. Vision transformers trained on DES data have been successfully applied without any fine-tuning to detect low surface brightness galaxies (LSBGs) and ultra-diffuse galaxies in deeper Hyper Suprime-Cam (HSC) observations, provided the input data is rigorously standardized by pixel-level surface brightness [Thuruthipilly et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250203142T/abstract). Ultimately, mastering domain adaptation and cross-survey translation is necessary to prevent systematic biases and to maximize the scientific return of petabyte-scale, multi-wavelength surveys like those from the Vera C. Rubin Observatory and the Euclid mission.

## Anomaly Detection and Unsupervised Discovery

As next-generation telescopes reach petabyte-scale data volumes, identifying rare, unmodeled astrophysical phenomena or subtle instrumental artifacts cannot rely on manual inspection. Unsupervised anomaly detection algorithms leverage compressed representations to automatically flag statistical outliers without requiring prior labels or specialized training sets. 

In a typical pipeline, high-dimensional astronomical data—such as raw pixels or 1D spectra—are first compressed into a lower-dimensional latent space. This extraction can be achieved through deep generative models like Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), Self-Organizing Maps (SOMs), or self-supervised architectures utilizing frameworks like [[momentum-contrastive-learning|Momentum Contrastive Learning]]. Outlier detection algorithms, such as the Isolation Forest or the Local Outlier Factor (LOF), are then applied to these latent features to rank objects based on their deviation from the majority distribution. Alternatively, generative models like Vector-Quantised Variational Autoencoders (VQ-VAEs) can directly identify anomalies via high reconstruction errors, as they are trained to optimally reconstruct only "normal" astronomical sources [Ventura et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260113901V/abstract). This clustering logic builds upon earlier foundational work, which demonstrated the power of unsupervised learning by applying Gaussian Mixture Models for pulsar classification, successfully identifying distinct clusters of millisecond and normal pulsars within the $P-\dot{P}$ diagram for the Fermi 2FGL catalogue [Lee et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.2832L/abstract).

A fundamental tension in unsupervised discovery is that algorithms frequently assign the highest anomaly scores to uninteresting instrumental artifacts, satellite trails, or data processing errors. Defining what constitutes a "scientifically interesting" anomaly is inherently subjective and varies depending on the research domain. To address this, current state-of-the-art frameworks employ active learning to integrate human-in-the-loop feedback. For example, in the Galaxy Zoo DECaLS project, active learning cycles utilizing Bayesian CNN ensembles intelligently focus human volunteer effort on the most informative unlabeled objects, drastically increasing overall morphology classification accuracy [Walmsley et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.3966W/abstract). 

The ASTRONOMALY framework provides a generalized example of this hybrid approach [Lochner et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3600481L/abstract). It extracts latent features, applies an Isolation Forest for initial unsupervised ranking, and then requests an expert to visually grade a small batch of the most anomalous targets. A machine learning regressor uses these manual grades to dynamically update the anomaly scores, effectively personalizing the search to the scientist's specific criteria. Empirical tests show that this active learning step can double the rate of relevant discoveries within the first 100 inspected objects [Lochner et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3600481L/abstract).

Scaling these frameworks has led to several notable discoveries in both optical and radio surveys. When applied to 4 million galaxy images from the Dark Energy Camera Legacy Survey (DECaLS), ASTRONOMALY identified 1,635 genuine anomalies among the top 2,000 actively ranked candidates, uncovering 1,609 uncatalogued galaxy merger candidates and 8 strong gravitational lens candidates [Etsebeth et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529..732E/abstract). In the search for specific rare objects, such as strong gravitational lenses, combining semi-supervised learning with GAN-generated synthetic images in ResNet architectures has been shown to achieve a 10-fold increase in lens candidate discovery efficiency [Keerthi Vasan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5368K/abstract). In the radio regime, unsupervised SOMs applied to ASKAP data successfully identified Odd Radio Circles (ORCs) [Gupta et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASA...39...51G/abstract). Similarly, applying ASTRONOMALY to MeerKAT images resulted in the discovery of a unique quadrilateral, ring-like radio source with a luminosity of $\sim 10^{25}\text{ W Hz}^{-1}$ that defies simple physical categorization [Lochner et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.1439L/abstract).

Beyond imaging, anomaly detection is highly effective for large spectroscopic datasets. Probabilistic auto-encoders trained on Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) data isolate outliers by evaluating the likelihood of a spectrum under a learned distribution, successfully identifying rare populations such as E+A galaxies, LINERs, and overlapping objects [Böhm et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.3072B/abstract). Recent advancements have coupled these detections with Explainable AI (XAI) techniques, such as Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME), to compute explanation weights for individual spectral bins. By explicitly highlighting the emission lines or continuum features that drive the high anomaly score, these methods bridge the gap between algorithmic outlier detection and physical astrophysics [Ortiz Manrique et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251005235O/abstract).

## Interpretability, Uncertainty, and Robustness in Learned Representations

As deep learning architectures grow in complexity, their utility in astrophysics relies increasingly on the ability to interpret their representations, rigorously quantify their uncertainties, and guarantee their robustness against data domain shifts. 

**Explainable AI and Interpretability**

A central challenge in representation learning is bridging the gap between high-dimensional latent spaces and physically meaningful parameters. Explainable AI (XAI) techniques, encompassing both post-hoc analysis and intrinsically interpretable architectures, are increasingly used to "open the black box." Post-hoc attribution methods like saliency mapping (e.g., SMOOTHGRAD) have successfully aligned neural network decision-making with human intuition. In morphological studies, saliency maps have enabled the direct measurement of physical features, such as galactic bar lengths, directly from classification network gradients [Bhambra et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.5032B/abstract). Similarly, Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) have been adapted to explain radio morphology classifications [Tang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230703453T/abstract) and to identify anomalous SDSS spectra by weighting the specific emission lines that drive the anomaly score [Ortiz Manrique et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251005235O/abstract). 

Conversely, intrinsic interpretability seeks to design architectures with physically transparent representations. Recent work has introduced Sparse Feature Networks (SFNets), which constrain learned representations to map linearly to physical properties—like gas-phase metallicity and emission line ratios—without sacrificing overall predictive accuracy [Wu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..183W/abstract). Furthermore, causal analysis and mutual information decomposition can explicitly quantify how non-photometric features influence deep learning predictions of stellar mass [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250923901Z/abstract). Even when models utilize unsupervised frameworks like [[momentum-contrastive-learning|Momentum Contrastive Learning]] to cluster morphological features [Shen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASP..135j4501S/abstract), XAI techniques are necessary to map those latent representations to established physical parameters.

**Uncertainty Quantification**

For rigorous cosmological inference, models must output well-calibrated probability distribution functions (PDFs) rather than deterministic point estimates. Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs) offer a formalized framework to capture epistemic uncertainty. In photometric redshift estimation, BNNs output Gaussian PDFs that closely match expected coverage statistics—for instance, achieving a $67.2\%$ coverage rate for $1\sigma$ uncertainties across $0 < z < 2.5$, meeting stringent science requirements for upcoming surveys [Jones et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..130J/abstract). Ensembles of neural networks trained with Gaussian loss functions have proven highly capable of modeling the stochasticity and intrinsic scatter within the galaxy-halo connection across hydrodynamical simulations, natively incorporating physical uncertainties into the network output [Stiskalek et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.4026S/abstract). Recent milestones in statistical robustness include the first use of simulation-based inference (SBI) combining weak lensing and galaxy clustering maps within deep graph convolutional networks to successfully break parameter degeneracies in $w$CDM cosmology for Dark Energy Survey Year 3 results [Thomsen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251104681T/abstract).

However, the literature is conflicting on the reliability of BNNs across different astronomical domains. Studies on radio galaxy classification reveal that BNNs can suffer from a "cold posterior effect," wherein models require an ad-hoc down-weighting of the posterior to achieve competitive predictive performance [Mohan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.3722M/abstract). This implies that overly aggressive data curation causes likelihood misspecification, leading to poorly calibrated uncertainties and complicating the deployment of fully Bayesian pipelines on real observational data [Mohan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.3722M/abstract), [Mohan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv211111654M/abstract).

**Robustness and Domain Shift**

A major bottleneck in applying simulation-trained models to real data is the fragility of neural networks when faced with unmodeled subgrid physics, differing noise profiles, or point spread functions (PSFs). Minor adversarial perturbations or observational Poisson noise can catastrophically flip morphological classifications [Ćiprijanović et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MLS&T...3c5007C/abstract). While domain adaptation techniques (such as DANNs) can significantly mitigate this reality gap and recover accuracy lost to noise [Ćiprijanović et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MLS&T...3c5007C/abstract), simulation-based inference (SBI) ultimately remains vulnerable to fundamental model misspecifications. For instance, in the estimation of galaxy cluster masses from X-ray profiles, standard and scatter-augmented neural networks can suffer a $40\%$ performance degradation when tested on an out-of-domain hydrodynamical simulation, sometimes underperforming traditional scaling relations like $Y_X$ [Ntampaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251009748N/abstract). This tension highlights that while learned representations are powerful, achieving true domain invariance requires carefully addressing biases embedded within the training simulations.

## Open Questions and Future Directions in the Big Data Era

The advent of data-intensive astronomy, heralded by next-generation facilities such as the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, the Euclid space telescope, and the Square Kilometre Array (SKA), presents unprecedented challenges and opportunities for machine learning. These observatories will generate petabyte-scale datasets containing $\sim 10^9$ astronomical sources, rendering manual inspection and catalog-based feature engineering computationally intractable. As the field transitions to end-to-end automated pipelines, several open questions and future directions dominate the representation learning landscape.

**Foundation Models and Multi-Modal Learning**
A major frontier is the development of astronomical foundation models. Instead of training custom architectures from scratch for individual surveys, researchers are increasingly leveraging massive, pre-trained vision models. By employing self-supervised algorithms, such as [[momentum-contrastive-learning|Momentum Contrastive Learning]], models can learn rich, low-dimensional latent representations from vast quantities of unlabelled data [Shen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASP..135j4501S/abstract). While models trained on natural images often provide surprisingly strong baselines, structural differences in fields like radio astronomy suggest that domain-specific pre-training may still be required to achieve peak performance on specific tasks [Lastufka et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240911175L/abstract). Another critical direction is extending representations beyond 2D projections; the application of deep 3D convolutional networks directly to volumetric representations of dark-matter simulations has shown significant promise for estimating cosmological parameters, indicating a shift toward fully 3D structural analysis [Ravanbakhsh et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv171102033R/abstract). Ultimately, resolving this debate will dictate how effectively the field builds universal representations of underlying astrophysics [UniverseTBD et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919453U/abstract).

**The Domain Shift Challenge**
Bridging the "reality gap" between simulated training data and observational reality remains a critical hurdle. Although embedding observational realism directly into training suites drastically improves generalizability [Bottrell et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.5390B/abstract), modern simulation-based inference can still suffer from severe, hidden biases when the underlying simulator is misspecified [Ntampaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251009748N/abstract). Developing increasingly robust architectures capable of genuine domain invariance across heterogenous surveys—using techniques like Domain Adversarial Neural Networks—will be a primary focus over the next decade.

**Anomaly Detection at Scale**
As data volumes exceed human capacity, finding the "unknown unknowns" requires robust anomaly detection frameworks capable of isolating objects via latent space isolation or high reconstruction errors [Gupta et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASA...39...51G/abstract), [Lochner et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.1439L/abstract). However, distinguishing between scientifically interesting anomalies and uninteresting instrumental artifacts is inherently subjective. Consequently, scaling these frameworks to millions of objects necessitates active learning. By incorporating a "human-in-the-loop" to periodically query the model and personalize the detection algorithm, researchers can drastically accelerate the rate of genuine discoveries compared to purely unsupervised methods [Lochner et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3600481L/abstract), [Etsebeth et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529..732E/abstract).

**Computational Efficiency**
Finally, the sheer computational cost of deep learning necessitates architectural efficiency. While deep convolutional neural networks provide state-of-the-art accuracy, their parameter-heavy nature is resource-intensive. Future directions include the optimization of lightweight models, such as EfficientNet-G3, which maintain supervised classification accuracies of $>96\%$ while utilizing a fraction of the parameters of standard networks [Wu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RAA....22k5011W/abstract). Further automation is required for complex large-scale measurements; for example, supervised learning and fine-tuning are now applied to automatically measure the intracluster light (ICL) fraction across large cluster samples without the need for intensive manual preprocessing [Canepa et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..245C/abstract). Balancing model complexity, physical interpretability, and inference speed will be essential to effectively process the real-time alert streams generated in the Big Data era.
