Persistent homology is a highly robust mathematical framework within [[topological-data-analysis-in-astronomy|Topological data analysis in astronomy]] used to characterize the multi-scale connectivity and [[hierarchical-clustering|hierarchical morphology]] of the cosmic web. Historically, the large-scale structure of the Universe has been quantified using spatial point processes, such as the two-point correlation function [Peebles et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981lssu.book.....P/abstract), or single-scale geometric measures like [[minkowski-functionals|Minkowski functionals]]. While traditional summary statistics successfully describe Gaussian random fields, they often fail to capture the complex, non-linear phase information inherent to the late-time cosmic web. Persistent homology overcomes this limitation by formalizing the topology of the matter distribution across an entire continuum of spatial scales or density thresholds, providing a parameter-free description of how cosmic structures assemble, connect, and hierarchically nest [van de Weygaert et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011LNCS.6970...60V/abstract), [Pranav et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract).

At the core of this methodology is the tracking of topological invariants known as Betti numbers ($\beta_k$). In a three-dimensional cosmic density field, these numbers directly correspond to the structural components of the cosmic web: $\beta_0$ counts isolated connected components (dense clusters or nodes), $\beta_1$ measures independent one-dimensional loops (the filamentary network), and $\beta_2$ enumerates enclosed two-dimensional cavities (cosmic voids). To measure these features in discrete galaxy surveys or N-body simulations, algorithms construct a *filtration*—a nested sequence of simplicial complexes typically derived from a [[delaunay-tessellation|Delaunay tessellation]] and parameterized by [[alpha-shapes-topology|Alpha Shapes (topology)]]. As the filtration scale evolves, topological features are "born" and eventually "die." The lifespan of a feature across this filtration is its *persistence*, a metric that allows computational extractors like [[disperse|DISPERSE]] to rigorously distinguish the genuine, large-scale physical backbone of the Universe from local shot noise [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract).

In recent years, persistent homology has transitioned from a theoretical descriptor to a powerful tool for precision cosmology. By capturing higher-order non-Gaussian information, persistence-based statistics can break standard parameter degeneracies. For instance, combining topological summaries with traditional power spectra has been shown to tighten constraints on the matter density ($\Omega_{\rm m}$) and clustering amplitude ($S_8$) by up to 70% compared to standard weak lensing analyses [Prat et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.tmp.2029P/abstract). Furthermore, persistent homology has successfully detected the Baryon Acoustic Oscillation (BAO) signal as a topological shell in highly sparse quasar datasets, constrained primordial non-Gaussianity ($f_{\rm NL}$) using intermediate-scale filament loops, and distinguished between Cold and Warm Dark Matter models by identifying topological divergences in sub-galactic subhalo populations.

Integrating persistent homology into standard cosmological pipelines for upcoming Stage IV surveys (such as Euclid and DESI) is an active area of research. A critical challenge is understanding "topological bias"—the phenomenon where dark matter halos of different masses inherently trace fundamentally different topological environments, complicating the interpretation of luminous tracers. Additionally, the field faces profound open questions, including reconciling topological measurements with [[multifractal-analysis|Multifractal analysis]] regarding the transition scale to cosmic homogeneity, and investigating highly anomalous deviations in the Betti numbers of the Cosmic Microwave Background at large angular scales.

## Historical Context: From Correlation Functions to Topology

The quantitative study of large-scale structure traditionally relied on spatial point processes and second-order statistics. Following the foundational work of Peebles [Peebles et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981lssu.book.....P/abstract) and comprehensive foundational texts exploring galaxy distribution functions and early uses of [[percolation-analysis|percolation]] or graph-based topology [Saslaw et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999diga.book.....S/abstract), the two-point correlation function and its Fourier transform, the power spectrum, became the standard tools for cosmological analyses. These Gaussian summary statistics were highly successful in characterizing the early universe and validating models of [[hierarchical-clustering|Hierarchical clustering]] [White et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997evun.work..227W/abstract). Furthermore, prominent observational campaigns such as the REFLEX Cluster Survey leveraged these measures to probe large-scale structure statistics on scales up to $500 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$, providing key evidence to favor low-density universe models [Böhringer et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002luml.conf...12B/abstract). However, while the power spectrum fully describes a Gaussian random field, the late-time universe exhibits a highly non-linear, non-Gaussian cosmic web. Standard two-point statistics fail to capture the phase information responsible for the rich connectivity of clusters, filaments, and voids [van de Weygaert et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011LNCS.6970...60V/abstract). Even in the early stages of characterizing these elements, the origin of such large-scale features was fiercely debated; researchers frequently contrasted the top-down 'fragmentation theory' of pancake and void formation against the bottom-up 'continuous clustering theory' regarding the origin of large-scale voids [Doroshkevich et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982ComAp...9..265D/abstract).

Recognizing the limitations of two-point statistics, researchers in the 1990s and 2000s explored higher-order statistics and scale-invariant frameworks. The move toward [[multifractal-analysis|multifractal analysis]] in the 1990s provided a natural explanation for the differing clustering properties of cosmic objects via the concept of multiscaling [Martinez et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995Sci...269.1245M/abstract). Multifractal analysis was introduced to describe the self-similar distribution of galaxies, yielding a measured fractal dimension of $D \approx 2.0$ to $2.2$ on scales up to $\sim 40 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Sylos Labini et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009EL.....8529002S/abstract), [Sylos Labini et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..1151S/abstract). To deepen the understanding of multiscaling, researchers utilized 1D gravitational simulations, which allowed for precise multifractal analysis of particle distributions in configuration space and effectively characterized regions of low density using equal-mass partitions [Miller et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150104674M/abstract). This fractal approach highlighted a significant tension in the literature regarding the scale at which the universe transitions to homogeneity. Some authors argued that galaxy distributions showed no convergence to homogeneity up to $75-100 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$, challenging standard $\Lambda$CDM predictions [Sylos Labini et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009EL.....8529002S/abstract). This sparked debates over the influence of cosmic variance in limited survey volumes, though other studies demonstrated that despite the sensitivity of large-scale morphology to finite-size effects, the $\Lambda$CDM model remains favored [Dominguez et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph..2197D/abstract).

To explicitly quantify the geometry and morphology of these large-scale patterns independently of power spectra, cosmologists adapted tools from differential geometry and graph theory. [[minkowski-functionals|Minkowski functionals]] and the Euler characteristic were extensively used to measure the surface area, volume, and connectivity of [[isodensity-analysis|isodensity surfaces]] [Dominguez et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph..2197D/abstract), [Coles et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991MNRAS.250...75C/abstract). Concurrently, discrete algorithms such as the [[minimal-spanning-tree|Minimal spanning tree]] [Pearson et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.272..231P/abstract) and [[percolation-analysis|Percolation analysis]] emerged to skeletonize the cosmic web into graph networks. Graph theory and percolation theory approaches have since been utilized to successfully identify critical density and length-scale thresholds at which robust galaxy filamentary structures form [Strey et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231020184S/abstract). In these graph-theoretic studies, network construction algorithms relying solely on spatial proximity were found to provide the most effective correlations between the physical characteristics of connected galaxies [Coutinho et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160403236C/abstract). Related graph-based methods like the $\beta$-skeleton have also been used to identify filamentary structures and probe redshift space distortions and galaxy bias without parameter fine-tuning [Fang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.5276F/abstract). Furthermore, information theoretic measures, such as graph entropy, have been introduced as discrete analogues to topological scalars to quantify the connectivity and complexity of the cosmic web [García-Alvarado et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498L.145G/abstract). 

Other automated methods were developed using the velocity shear tensor (e.g., the [[v-web-threshold|V-web threshold]] [Olex et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...696A.142O/abstract)) or marked point processes (e.g., [[bisous-filaments|Bisous filaments]] [Tempel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...572A...8T/abstract)). While these early structural methods successfully identified distinct elements, they often required arbitrary density thresholds or linking lengths, and they struggled to objectively quantify the nested, multi-scale nature of the cosmic mass distribution.

The transition to modern [[topological-data-analysis-in-astronomy|Topological data analysis in astronomy]] (TDA) resolved many of these thresholding ambiguities by introducing the mathematical framework of persistent homology. Before detailing this framework, it is important to briefly distinguish "persistent homology" from the historical use of "homology" in galaxy morphology, where the latter referred to structural scaling relations like the Fundamental Plane and Sérsic profiles [Graham et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997MNRAS.287..221G/abstract). For example, dissipationless collapse simulations in live dark matter halos demonstrated that stellar systems robustly follow the Sérsic $R^{1/m}$ law, contributing to the 'weak homology' commonly observed in elliptical galaxies [Nipoti et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.370..681N/abstract). Furthermore, historical galaxy homology was frequently defined by 3D scaling constants, and simulated structural remnants appear more homologous when using specific geometric radii that contain equal amounts of baryonic and dark matter mass [Novak et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424..635N/abstract). Pioneered in a cosmological context in the early 2010s [van de Weygaert et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011LNCS.6970...60V/abstract), persistent homology instead tracks the geometric evolution of topological invariants across a continuous filtration of spatial scales or density thresholds [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract). This paradigm shift allowed researchers to separate robust structural features from shot noise without parameter fine-tuning.

## Methodological Framework: Filtrations and Simplicial Complexes

To apply [[topological-data-analysis-in-astronomy|Topological data analysis in astronomy]] to the discrete distribution of galaxies or dark matter halos, point sets must first be transformed into continuous topological spaces. This is achieved by constructing parameterized families of simplicial complexes, a mathematical procedure known as a filtration [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract). By tracking the evolution of homology groups across this filtration, persistent homology characterizes the multiscale geometry of the cosmic web in a parameter-free manner.

The most common computational procedure for discrete cosmic datasets begins with the [[delaunay-tessellation|Delaunay tessellation]] of the spatial point process. From this tessellation, algorithms extract [[alpha-shapes-topology|Alpha Shapes (topology)]], which constitute an ordered sequence of nested simplicial complexes defined by a spatial scale parameter, $\alpha$ [van de Weygaert et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011LNCS.6970...60V/abstract), [van de Weygaert et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.3640V/abstract). The $\alpha$-shape filtration is homotopy equivalent to the sublevel sets of the distance field of the point distribution. This equivalence makes it an exact and computationally efficient tool for analyzing discrete datasets without the need for artificial grid smoothing or binning [van de Weygaert et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.3640V/abstract), [Bermejo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract). Alternatively, when working with continuous data—such as smoothed matter density fields, grid-based hydrodynamical simulations, or Cosmic Microwave Background temperature maps—filtrations are directly defined by evaluating the superlevel or sublevel sets across varying density or temperature thresholds [van de Weygaert et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011LNCS.6970...60V/abstract), [Xu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&C....27...34X/abstract).

As the filtration parameter (either the spatial scale $\alpha$ or a density threshold) monotonically increases, topological features appear ("birth") and eventually merge or fill in ("death"). These invariant features are formalized by the Betti numbers, $\beta_k$:
*   $\beta_0$ measures the number of connected components, physically corresponding to isolated clusters or nodes.
*   $\beta_1$ measures the number of independent loops or tunnels, corresponding to the filamentary network.
*   $\beta_2$ measures the number of enclosed cavities, corresponding to cosmic voids [Pranav et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract), [Kono et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200602905K/abstract).

The evolutionary history of these features is summarized in a persistence diagram, a two-dimensional scatter plot where each topological generator is represented by the coordinates of its threshold values $(x_{\rm birth}, x_{\rm death})$. The topological lifetime, or persistence $\Pi$, of a feature is conventionally defined as:
$$ \Pi = |x_{\rm death} - x_{\rm birth}| $$
This metric serves as a rigorous measure of the feature's robustness against shot noise and local perturbations [Xu et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&C....27...34X/abstract). Generators near the diagonal are typically treated as topological noise, while highly persistent features represent the fundamental, large-scale structures of the cosmic web [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract). 

The practical extraction of these structures frequently relies on Discrete Morse theory. Software implementations such as the Discrete Persistent Structures Extractor ([[disperse|DISPERSE]]) compute the discrete Morse-Smale complex directly from the [[delaunay-tessellation|Delaunay tessellation]] or gridded manifolds [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract). By identifying the critical points of the density field (maxima, minima, and saddle points) and calculating the gradient paths connecting them, Discrete Morse theory explicitly traces the persistent structural skeleton—peaks, voids, walls, and filamentary bridges.

## Characterizing the Cosmic Web: Voids, Filaments, and Nodes

The large-scale structure of the universe forms a complex, multi-scale network. Persistent homology offers a mathematically rigorous framework to quantify this hierarchical connectivity. Unlike traditional global summary statistics such as [[minkowski-functionals|Minkowski functionals]] or the Euler characteristic, persistent homology localizes and tracks the survival of individual structural components [van de Weygaert et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011LNCS.6970...60V/abstract).

In a three-dimensional cosmic density field, the physical elements of the cosmic web map directly to the Betti numbers. Isolated connected components ($\beta_0$) correspond to overdense cosmic web nodes or galaxy clusters. Within these topological nodes, clusters located in rich supercluster environments—particularly those exhibiting a multibranched or "spider" morphology—show significantly higher probabilities of possessing intricate substructure and multimodality [Einasto et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...542A..36E/abstract). One-dimensional non-homologous loops ($\beta_1$) trace the branching filamentary network. Finally, two-dimensional enclosed shells ($\beta_2$) rigorously define the boundaries of cosmic voids [Xu et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&C....27...34X/abstract). By utilizing discrete persistence across [[alpha-shapes-topology|Alpha Shapes (topology)]], astronomers can filter out spurious structures generated by survey shot noise [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract), [van de Weygaert et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.3640V/abstract). In addition to discrete galaxy surveys, persistent homology has been applied to 3D continuous reconstructions of the Lyman-alpha forest to map the topology of the high-redshift intergalactic medium [Cisewski et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440.2599C/abstract). Furthermore, luminous quasar systems at high redshifts ($1.0 \leq z \leq 1.8$) have been utilized successfully to trace supercluster-scale features and outline the skeletal structure of the high-redshift cosmic web [Einasto et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...568A..46E/abstract).

The multiscale topology of an entire survey volume is often summarized using Betti curves, which plot the values of $\beta_k$ as a function of the filtration scale. These curves capture essential non-Gaussian information regarding the network's connectivity that is entirely missed by the standard two-point correlation function. By capturing the precise hierarchy of these structures, persistent homology provides a rich, localized description of the universe's mass distribution that underpins many modern cosmological applications.

Applying this multiscale topology to observational datasets has yielded explicit confirmations of structural evolution. For example, an analysis of the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) DR12 fields using persistent homology confirms that void structures become increasingly distinct from random distributions at lower redshifts, supporting the hierarchical growth of structure [Kimura et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AdSpR..60..722K/abstract). Furthermore, a dedicated PH-based approach has identified robust cosmic voids in the SDSS Main Galaxy catalog, finding features with effective radii between 21 and $56 \ h^{-1}{\rm Mpc}$ that align precisely with mock catalog predictions [Aggarwal et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240901905A/abstract).

## Cosmological Constraints and Primordial Non-Gaussianity

The application of persistent homology to large-scale structure provides a powerful statistical framework for extracting non-Gaussian information from the late-time mass distribution. While traditional two-point statistics fully describe a Gaussian random field, the non-linear evolution of the cosmic web generates complex, multi-scale phase couplings. By tracking the birth and death of clusters, filament loops, and voids across spatial scales, persistent homology captures these higher-order structural details [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251207236L/abstract).

Recent studies have demonstrated that combining topological summary statistics, such as Betti curves, with the power spectrum can yield significantly tighter constraints on fundamental cosmological parameters. Analyses of N-body simulations have shown that topological data can break standard parameter degeneracies, improving constraints on the matter density $\Omega_{\rm m}$, the clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$, the scalar spectral index $n_{\rm s}$, and the dark energy equation of state $w$ [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251207236L/abstract). When applied to observational data, such as the Dark Energy Survey Year 3 (DES Y3) weak lensing maps, spherical persistent homology combined with second moments yields $S_8 = 0.821 \pm 0.018$ and $\Omega_{\rm m} = 0.304 \pm 0.037$. These combined topological constraints are approximately $70\%$ tighter in the $\Omega_{\rm m}$-$S_8$ plane than those derived from conventional cosmic shear two-point statistics alone [Prat et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.tmp.2029P/abstract), [Prat et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250613439P/abstract).

Persistent homology also offers a novel approach to probing primordial non-Gaussianity, specifically the local-type parameter $f_{\rm NL}^{\rm loc}$. Traditionally, $f_{\rm NL}^{\rm loc}$ is constrained by measuring the scale-dependent bias of halos at extremely large, linear scales, which requires massive survey volumes. Persistent homology, however, captures the imprint of $f_{\rm NL}^{\rm loc}$ on the topology of the cosmic web at intermediate scales ($\mathcal{O}(10)\ h^{-1}{\rm Mpc}$). By evaluating the persistence of filament loops and voids in simulated volumes of $40\ (h^{-1}{\rm Gpc})^3$, topological pipelines have successfully detected $f_{\rm NL}^{\rm loc} = 10$ at $97.5\%$ confidence, successfully breaking the degeneracy between $f_{\rm NL}^{\rm loc}$ and $\sigma_8$ without the strict requirement of sampling extreme long-wavelength modes [Biagetti et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...04..061B/abstract).

Additionally, persistence-based techniques have proven highly effective at extracting the Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO) signal from sparse observational datasets. By calculating the persistent homology of galaxy and quasar distributions, the BAO signature manifests as a statistically significant 2-dimensional topological hole (a hollow shell) at a characteristic scale of $\sim 150\ {\rm Mpc}$. Notably, this topological BAO signal has been robustly detected in highly undersampled regimes, such as a subsample of merely 2000 quasars at $z < 1.0$ from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) [Kono et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200602905K/abstract).

## Distinguishing Dark Matter Models at Small Scales

The identity of dark matter leaves distinct structural imprints at sub-galactic scales. While standard Cold Dark Matter (CDM) models predict a bottom-up [[hierarchical-clustering|Hierarchical clustering]] process resulting in a rich abundance of low-mass subhalos, Warm Dark Matter (WDM) models predict a suppression of small-scale structure due to the free-streaming of lighter dark matter particles. Traditional clustering metrics often struggle to fully capture the complex, non-Gaussian spatial arrangements of these localized subhalos. Persistent homology provides a robust alternative by tracking the formation and destruction of topological features across a continuous range of spatial scales [Cisewski-Kehe et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106b3521C/abstract).

To differentiate between these models, persistent homology has been applied to the subhalo populations of Milky Way-analog environments in high-resolution $N$-body simulations, such as the Copernicus Complexio suite. By treating the discrete distribution of dark matter halos as a spatial point process and extracting functional summaries from their persistence diagrams, researchers can explicitly quantify the small-scale topology of the subhalo network [Cisewski-Kehe et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106b3521C/abstract).

The topological signatures of CDM and WDM diverge robustly at sub-galactic scales. Hypothesis tests applied to persistence diagram summaries yield statistically significant differences ($p \leq 0.001$) between the two dark matter scenarios. The distinguishing power of persistent homology is most pronounced at filtration scales of $r \sim 100 \text{ kpc}$. At these specific physical scales, the CDM model generates a substantially larger number of isolated, unconnected halo clusters (zero-dimensional topological features, $\beta_0$). Conversely, the subhalo distribution in WDM models exhibits a fundamentally different connectivity pattern, preferentially generating one-dimensional topological loops ($\beta_1$) [Cisewski-Kehe et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106b3521C/abstract).

Crucially, persistent homology isolates these geometric differences at physical scales and structural configurations that are distinct from those detected by standard spatial point process summaries. Where the traditional two-point correlation function averages over the geometry of the field, persistent homology retains the higher-order connectivity and multi-scale hierarchy, making it a highly sensitive tool for dark matter model selection. Translating these topological methods from dark matter halos in $N$-body simulations to realistic, observable satellite galaxy catalogues offers a mathematically rigorous pathway to empirically constrain the physical nature and mass of the dark matter particle [Cisewski-Kehe et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106b3521C/abstract).

## Applications to Scalar Fields and Projected Maps: EoR and Weak Lensing

Beyond the structural classification of the late-time galaxy distribution in 3D, the framework of [[topological-data-analysis-in-astronomy|Topological data analysis in astronomy]] has recently been extended to track complex, evolving scalar fields and projected continuous maps. Two prominent examples include the topology of ionized bubbles during the Epoch of Reionization (EoR) and the spherical topology of weak lensing convergence maps.

**The Epoch of Reionization**
During the EoR, the intergalactic medium underwent a profound phase transition as expanding HII regions merged to fully ionize the universe. Persistent homology provides a parameter-free formalism to track the formation, merging, and complex connectivity of this bubble network over time. By utilizing "persistence fields"—a statistical summary of the persistence data tracking how topological features survive across spatial scales and varying neutral fractions—researchers can identify distinct evolutionary stages of reionization [Elbers et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.1523E/abstract). 

Regardless of the underlying degree of galaxy clustering or the specific ionizing source model, persistent homology identifies a universal topological sequence. Following an initial pre-overlap and overlap phase, the topology of the universe enters a "filament stage," where the structure is dominated by a percolating network of neutral filaments. This eventually transitions into a "patch stage," characterized by enclosed, isolated islands of neutral hydrogen that undergo outside-in ionization [Elbers et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.1523E/abstract). Persistence fields also provide a rigorous mathematical basis for analyzing the fractal-like topology of the largest ionized regions and extracting the effective bubble size distribution.

**Weak Lensing and Spherical Homology**
While 3D persistence is typically computed on Euclidean grids or via the [[delaunay-tessellation|Delaunay tessellation]] of discrete points, photometric surveys like weak gravitational lensing require adaptations for the curved sky. Recently, spherical persistent homology has been applied to weak lensing convergence maps to capture the multi-scale evolution of peaks, filaments, and voids across density thresholds. Because weak lensing maps project the integrated mass distribution along the line of sight, capturing their non-Gaussian topological features is critical for breaking standard cosmological degeneracies.

In the aforementioned analysis of Dark Energy Survey Year 3 (DES Y3) data, researchers utilized the HEALPix-compatible TopoS2 algorithm to extract topological summary statistics from convergence maps across multiple smoothing scales and redshift bins [Prat et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.tmp.2029P/abstract). By combining Betti numbers and second moments in a simulation-based inference framework, this spherical approach generated the highly tightened parameter constraints discussed previously. Furthermore, analyses relying on the Gower Street simulation suite have demonstrated that these topological summaries are highly resilient to baryonic feedback effects, introducing systematic parameter biases of less than $0.3\sigma$ [Prat et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250613439P/abstract). This establishes persistent topology as a robust tool for extracting non-Gaussian cosmological information from upcoming wide-field surveys.

## Topological Bias and Environment-Dependent Galaxy Evolution

The multiscale topology of the cosmic web profoundly influences the evolutionary trajectories of the galaxies within it. Utilizing persistent structure extractors such as [[disperse|DISPERSE]], which computes the discrete Morse-Smale complex to identify robust topological features in noisy data [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract), observational studies have quantified the impact of topological environments on galaxy properties. In the local Universe, central galaxies situated closer to cosmic web nodes, filaments, and walls exhibit systematically lower specific star formation rates than mass-matched counterparts in more isolated field environments [Winkel et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4920W/abstract). Localized studies using DISPERSE have further quantified the 'connectivity' of individual massive clusters; for example, the Coma cluster acts as a dense node connected to three primary filaments [Malavasi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...634A..30M/abstract). At these massive nodes, the detailed analysis of the spatial distribution and kinematics of member galaxies is reliably used to determine cluster mass profiles and trace their internal dynamics [Biviano et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10053B/abstract). Furthermore, galaxies proximal to these persistent structural features are, on average, older, more metal-rich, and $\alpha$-enhanced. This phenomenon, termed "cosmic web quenching," indicates that the topological environment drives galaxy evolution through nurture effects—such as ram-pressure stripping and strangulation—inherent to the hierarchical infrastructure of the cosmic web [Winkel et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4920W/abstract). Intensive galaxy transformation and building processes prominently occur as well in the outskirts and suburbs of massive clusters, representing a critical transitional environment as galaxies interface with the broader web [Diaferio et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ogci.conf.....D/abstract).

The relationship between galaxy evolution and the cosmic web is fundamentally underpinned by how dark matter haloes populate these topological features. Recent applications of [[topological-data-analysis-in-astronomy|Topological data analysis in astronomy]] have identified an intrinsic mass-topology relation known as "topological bias" [Bermejo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract). By tracking the connectivity of the cosmic web using persistent homology—specifically through filtrations of the [[delaunay-tessellation|Delaunay tessellation]] and nested [[alpha-shapes-topology|Alpha Shapes (topology)]]—researchers have uncovered a robust linear scaling relation between halo masses and the topological signatures of their local environments [Bermejo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract). 

Crucially, topological bias is an environmental structure bias that operates independently of the standard halo clustering bias associated with the two-point correlation function. Haloes of varying masses inherently trace structural patterns (such as elongated filamentary bridges versus sheet-like walls) with distinct topological connectivity. This mass-dependent segregation manifests in the physical properties of the web itself; for instance, the filamentary network can be divided into statistically distinct populations, such as short ($L_f < 9 \text{ Mpc}$), dense bridges connected to massive clusters, and long ($L_f \geq 20 \text{ Mpc}$), thinner filaments that trace less massive environments and shape the broader skeleton of the cosmic web [Galárraga-Espinosa et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...641A.173G/abstract). To properly account for the relationship between luminous tracers and these halo mass dependencies, astronomers have introduced parameter-free group identification methods that probabilistically link galaxies based on hosting dark matter halo mass assumptions, directly mapping observed galaxy groups to their underlying topology [Muñoz-Cuartas et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1203.4846M/abstract). Furthermore, at sub-Mpc scales, halo alignment and ellipsoidal shapes have been shown to significantly influence clustering, thereby complicating standard spherical halo occupation models and emphasizing the need to account for structural geometry in environmental statistics [van Daalen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.2954V/abstract). Beyond direct topological extraction, network centrality measures (such as degree, closeness, and [[betweenness-centrality|betweenness]]) allow for the complementary classification of galaxies into discrete topological populations, such as 'backbone' or 'fracture' regions [Hong et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.1999H/abstract).

The existence of topological bias has significant implications for observational cosmology. Because the inferred shape, connectivity, and infrastructure of large-scale features depend strongly on the specific mass of the galaxy sample used to trace them, cosmological probes utilizing voids and filaments must explicitly account for this mass-dependent topology. Accurately modeling this scaling relation allows researchers to determine the overall connectivity of the dark matter density field from a limited sample of biased luminous tracers, a requirement that will be critical for interpreting data from upcoming Stage IV wide-field surveys such as DESI and Euclid [Bermejo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract).

## Software and Computational Tools for TDA

Astronomical datasets—whether generated by $N$-body simulations or observed in galaxy redshift surveys—are inherently discrete, sparse, and subject to systematic artifacts. Extracting meaningful topological information from these catalogs requires an algorithm capable of constructing continuous filtrations from discrete points and tracking the birth and death of homological features across scales [van de Weygaert et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011LNCS.6970...60V/abstract), [van de Weygaert et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.3640V/abstract). A critical requirement for any such software is noise robustness, which allows the persistent homology framework to separate genuine cosmic web structures from stochastic shot noise.

The most widely adopted software package for this purpose is [[disperse|DISPERSE]] (the Discrete Persistent Structures Extractor) [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract). Rather than relying on visually motivated or arbitrary density thresholds, DISPERSE utilizes discrete Morse-Smale complexes to formalize the topology of the underlying density field. The software identifies critical points (maxima, minima, and saddle points) and the integral lines connecting them, effectively skeletonizing the cosmic web into its constituent nodes, filaments, walls, and voids. By quantifying the structural longevity of each topological feature as it appears and disappears across the filtration, DISPERSE allows users to filter out topological pairs with low persistence ratios. It operates seamlessly on continuous 2D and 3D manifolds, unstructured grids, and discrete point samples [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract). As a complement to these strictly geometric constructions, alternative Lagrangian approaches to cosmic web classification have been developed to classify haloes based on nearest-neighbor properties using smoothed particle hydrodynamics techniques [Fisher et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.458.1517F/abstract). Additionally, tools like T-ReX offer a graph-based [[galaxy-filament-finding-algorithm|filament detection method]] utilizing regularized [[minimal-spanning-tree|minimum spanning trees]] and Gaussian mixtures to automatically retrieve continuous filamentary paths directly from 2D and 3D galaxy distributions [Bonnaire et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...637A..18B/abstract).

As [[topological-data-analysis-in-astronomy|Topological data analysis in astronomy]] expands from cubic simulation boxes to observational light cones and full-sky maps, computational tools have adapted to spherical geometries and complex survey masks. For instance, the TopoS2 algorithm was specifically developed to compute spherical persistent homology directly on curved-sky data [Prat et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250613439P/abstract), [Prat et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.tmp.2029P/abstract). Optimized for HEALPix compatibility, TopoS2 extracts multiscale topological summary statistics from observational datasets like weak lensing convergence maps, enabling the tight parameter constraints achieved in recent dark energy surveys.

Beyond structure identification, modern computational pipelines increasingly integrate persistent homology solvers with machine learning and simulation-based inference. Automated emulators are now routinely trained on summary statistics—such as Betti curves and persistence diagrams—generated from large simulation suites like Quijote and Gower Street [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251207236L/abstract), [Prat et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250613439P/abstract). These computational pipelines allow theorists to evaluate the sensitivity of the topology to fundamental physics, effectively utilizing persistent homology to constrain primordial non-Gaussianity ($f_{\rm NL}$) or the sum of neutrino masses directly from the multiscale structure of the cosmic web [Xu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&C....27...34X/abstract), [Biagetti et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...04..061B/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While persistent homology has firmly established itself as a robust framework within [[topological-data-analysis-in-astronomy|Topological data analysis in astronomy]], its transition into a tool for precision cosmology presents several ongoing challenges. Early topological studies of the large-scale structure often relied on [[minkowski-functionals|Minkowski functionals]] and the Euler characteristic, mathematically defined for a three-dimensional spatial distribution as
$$ \chi = \beta_0 - \beta_1 + \beta_2 $$
where $\beta_k$ are the Betti numbers representing connected components, loops, and voids, respectively. By tracking these individual topological generators across density filtrations rather than collapsing them into a single metric, modern persistent homology extracts substantially more information, allowing it to break parameter degeneracies that traditionally limit two-point clustering statistics [Biagetti et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...04..061B/abstract), [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251207236L/abstract).

As the field prepares for the unprecedented data volumes of Stage IV surveys like Euclid and DESI, quantifying observational and physical systematics is paramount. One major hurdle is the accurate modeling of baryonic feedback, which can alter the multiscale topology of the cosmic web. While recent simulation-based inference methods applying spherical persistent homology indicate that baryonic feedback induces minimal parameter biases [Prat et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250613439P/abstract), confirming this robustness across a wider range of feedback prescriptions and tracer types remains an active area of research. In parallel, interpreting these surveys requires accurately calibrating "topological bias"—the mass-dependent linear scaling relation dictating how haloes uniquely sample the multiscale environments of the cosmic web [Bermejo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract).

Algorithmically, assigning rigorous statistical significance to topological features in noisy, sparse datasets is mathematically demanding. Current leading methods use persistence diagrams in conjunction with bootstrap sampling to isolate true cosmological structures. By resampling the spatial data, researchers can assign $p$-values to individual homology group generators, successfully differentiating genuine filament loops and voids from shot noise [Xu et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&C....27...34X/abstract).

Finally, persistent homology continues to expose explicit tensions within the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm. The literature remains conflicted regarding the transition scale to cosmic homogeneity. While standard models predict homogenization on large scales, [[multifractal-analysis|Multifractal analysis]] of samples up to $75-100 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ indicate persistent large-scale fluctuations with a fractal dimension of $D \approx 2.2$ [Sylos Labini et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009EL.....8529002S/abstract), as well as unexplained quasi-regular supercluster spacings of $120-140 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Einasto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522082E/abstract). Furthermore, topological analysis of the cosmic microwave background reveals $3\sigma$ to $4\sigma$ deviations in Betti numbers at $3^{\circ}-7^{\circ}$ scales compared to Gaussian $\Lambda$CDM predictions [Pranav et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...627A.163P/abstract). Determining whether these persistent anomalies arise from late-time physical effects, primordial non-Gaussianity, or non-trivial cosmic topology remains one of the most profound open questions in the field.
