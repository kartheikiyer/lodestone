**DISPERSE** (Discrete Persistent Structure Extractor) is an open-source geometric formalism and computational algorithm designed to identify and characterize the multi-scale topology of the cosmic web. Developed as a foundational tool for [[topological-data-analysis-in-astronomy|Topological data analysis in astronomy]], DISPERSE operates directly on discrete tracer distributions—such as galaxy catalogs from large-scale redshift surveys or dark matter particles in $N$-body simulations—to extract a mathematically robust, scale-independent "skeleton" of the universe [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract). Acting as a highly precise [[galaxy-filament-finding-algorithm|Galaxy Filament-Finding algorithm]], DISPERSE typically begins by estimating a continuous density field from discrete points via a [[delaunay-tessellation|Delaunay tessellation]]. It then applies discrete Morse theory to partition this field into a discrete Morse-Smale complex. This topological framework rigorously categorizes the spatial manifold into its constituent critical points: local density maxima (nodes/clusters), connecting ridges (filaments), two-dimensional sheets (walls), and local minima (cosmic voids) [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract).

A fundamental challenge in mapping the large-scale structure of the universe is the inherent presence of Poisson and observational noise, which can generate a massive overcount of spurious topological structures. DISPERSE overcomes this by utilizing [[persistent-homology|Persistent homology]], a branch of algebraic topology that quantifies the robustness of structural features across varying density thresholds. By tracking the "birth" and "death" of topological components through a nested filtration process, the algorithm calculates persistent Betti numbers to securely encode the true multiscale connectivity of the cosmic network. When a user applies a specific persistence threshold, DISPERSE systematically cancels out low-significance critical point pairs, isolating genuine physical structures from background noise without altering the global geometry. The accuracy of this topological filtering has been thoroughly validated against theoretical models, including fractal distributions and [[poisson-voronoi-tessellation|Poisson Voronoi Tessellation]] clustering frameworks [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract).

The structural skeletons extracted by DISPERSE have driven significant advances in understanding how the macroscopic environment governs galaxy evolution and kinematics. Spatial mapping with the algorithm has quantified "cosmic web quenching," revealing that central galaxies residing closer to filament spines and structural nodes exhibit systematically lower specific star formation rates, older stellar populations, and higher metallicities than their equal-mass counterparts located at larger distances from these structural features [Winkel et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4920W/abstract). Furthermore, the extracted filamentary network provides a critical reference frame for galactic kinematics, highlighting a mass-dependent "spin-flip" transition where galaxies alter their rotational alignment relative to their host filaments as they grow via mergers [Welker et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.2864W/abstract).

Despite its widespread adoption, the application of DISPERSE highlights several open questions and methodological tensions in modern cosmology. A primary ongoing debate centers on the exact physical mechanisms driving cosmic web quenching at filament boundaries, with competing theories suggesting either traditional "nurture" effects like ram pressure stripping or starvation driven by high angular momentum in vorticity-rich filament edges. Additionally, the algorithm's outputs are sensitive to tracer bias; applying DISPERSE to simulated galaxies often underestimates the connectivity of massive clusters compared to underlying dark matter fields [Zakharova et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.4079Z/abstract). Finally, the choice of the initial density estimator remains a critical variable. Recent research demonstrates that replacing the standard [[delaunay-tessellation|Delaunay tessellation]] with alternative estimators—such as slime-mold-inspired models—can fundamentally alter the recovery of low-prominence filaments, opening new avenues for mapping the diffuse matter of the universe [Hasan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..177H/abstract).

## Mathematical Foundations: Morse Theory and Persistent Homology

DISPERSE is built upon the mathematical frameworks of discrete Morse theory and [[persistent-homology|Persistent homology]]. This methodology marks a transition from global statistical measures—such as the two-point correlation function or Minkowski functionals—toward [[topological-data-analysis-in-astronomy|Topological data analysis in astronomy]], enabling the objective, multiscale extraction of the cosmic web's connectivity [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract).

To identify coherent physical structures, DISPERSE computes the discrete Morse-Smale complex of a density field defined over a manifold [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract). In cosmological applications, this continuous density field is typically discretized over a cell complex generated via a [[delaunay-tessellation|Delaunay tessellation]] of a discrete point sample, such as galaxies or dark matter particles. Through the computation of the discrete Morse-Smale complex, DISPERSE directly identifies distinct features of the cosmic web, notably peaks (nodes or clusters), voids, walls, and in particular filamentary structures [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract). The resulting Morse-Smale complex partitions the spatial manifold into regions of uniform flow, mathematically formalizing the boundaries and skeletal structure of the cosmic web.

Because astronomical data sets are inherently populated with Poisson and observational noise, straightforward extraction of features yields a massive overcount of spurious structures. DISPERSE mitigates this via topological persistence [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract). By analyzing a sequence of nested subcomplexes—a filtration—created by sweeping a density threshold across the data, DISPERSE tracks the appearance ("birth") and disappearance ("death") of topological features. The persistence of a given topological feature acts as a quantitative measure of its robustness [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract). Features with low persistence are statistically indistinguishable from noise. DISPERSE simplifies the underlying Morse-Smale complex by systematically cancelling these low-persistence pairs, thereby preserving only the robust, high-persistence physical structures without altering the global geometry of the remaining skeleton. 

The global topology of the simplified field is further quantified using Betti numbers ($\beta_k$). Tracking persistent Betti numbers across a density filtration provides a robust, quantitative summary of hierarchical clustering. Analyses of template datasets, including [[poisson-voronoi-tessellation|Poisson Voronoi Tessellation]] models and fractal-like point distributions, demonstrate that persistence diagrams—scatter plots of the birth and death values of topological features—contain clear, identifiable imprints of cluster nodes, filaments, walls, and voids [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract). By leveraging these formalisms, DISPERSE provides a mathematically rigorous foundation for extracting filaments and nodes, independent of any ad-hoc geometric tuning.

## Algorithmic Implementation and Data Processing

The algorithmic implementation of DISPERSE represents a fundamental shift from global geometric measures toward [[topological-data-analysis-in-astronomy|Topological data analysis in astronomy]]. The computational pipeline is designed to extract the multiscale structural "skeleton" by applying discrete Morse theory and [[persistent-homology|Persistent homology]] directly to noisy, discretely sampled distributions [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract).

**Density Field Construction and Domain**
The first step in the DISPERSE pipeline is the construction of a continuous density field, $\rho(\mathbf{x})$, from a discrete set of tracers, such as dark matter particles or galaxies. The software is highly versatile and can operate on structured grids, unstructured meshes, Healpix tessellations of the sphere, or directly on discrete point samples [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract). For discrete point sets, DISPERSE standardly employs a [[delaunay-tessellation|Delaunay tessellation]] to estimate the underlying density field, utilizing the Delaunay Tessellation Field Estimator (DTFE) framework [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract), [Zakharova et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.4079Z/abstract). The fundamental geometric constraint is that the input distribution must be defined over a valid mathematical manifold, which may include manifold boundaries [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract).

While DTFE remains the standard choice, the optimal density field estimator for topological structure extraction is an area of active debate. Recent studies suggest that alternative density estimators, such as the Monte Carlo Physarum Machine (MCPM)—a model inspired by slime mold behavior—may outperform standard Delaunay methods in recovering low-prominence and diffuse filaments by better tracing the underlying matter distribution [Hasan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..177H/abstract).

**Discrete Morse Theory and Critical Points**
Once the density field is established, DISPERSE computes its discrete Morse-Smale complex. This framework identifies the underlying features of the cosmic web, allowing the rigorous definition of structural elements such as peaks (nodes or galaxy clusters), voids, walls, and interconnected filaments [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract), [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract).

**Filtration and Persistence Thresholding**
Because astrophysical datasets are inherently noisy and galaxies act as biased tracers of the underlying dark matter field [Zakharova et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.4079Z/abstract), identifying physically meaningful structures requires robust noise filtration. DISPERSE achieves this through topological persistence. By constructing a density field filtration, the algorithm tracks the creation (birth) and destruction (death) of topological features across a continuous range of density thresholds. 

In practice, a user-defined persistence ratio is applied to filter out spurious topological features arising from Poisson sampling noise or local tracer artifacts. Features with a persistence below the chosen threshold are mathematically paired and canceled out, simplifying the Morse-Smale complex. Systematic analyses using [[poisson-voronoi-tessellation|Poisson Voronoi Tessellation]] clustering models and fractal sets have demonstrated that this thresholding technique cleanly isolates the true physical imprint of cluster nodes, filaments, walls, and voids from background noise [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract).

## Characterizing the Cosmic Skeleton: Nodes, Filaments, and Voids

DISPERSE extracts the cosmic web by computing the discrete Morse-Smale complex of a sampled density field, thereby partitioning the large-scale matter distribution into distinct topological components: *nodes*, filaments, walls, and *cosmic voids* [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract). By leveraging [[persistent-homology|Persistent homology]], the algorithm filters out local sampling noise to isolate genuine structural features, providing a robust quantification of the multiscale *connectivity structure* of the Universe through persistent Betti numbers [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract).

At the core of this structural partition are nodes—local density maxima that generally correspond to massive dark matter halos and galaxy clusters. Connecting these nodes is the *filamentary spine*, an interconnected network of one-dimensional density ridges that serve as the primary conduits for matter accretion. The two-dimensional boundaries spanning between these filaments make up the cosmic walls, while the sprawling underdense regions bounded entirely by the network are identified as cosmic voids. While DISPERSE is traditionally applied to density fields estimated via a [[delaunay-tessellation|Delaunay tessellation]] (DTFE) of discrete point distributions, recent studies suggest that utilizing alternative density estimators can yield higher-fidelity maps of these structures, particularly for the faintest, most diffuse filaments in the network [Hasan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..177H/abstract).

## Environmental Impact on Galaxy Evolution and Quenching

The extraction of the cosmic web's multiscale skeleton using DISPERSE—a widely utilized [[galaxy-filament-finding-algorithm|Galaxy Filament-Finding algorithm]]—has enabled detailed quantitative studies of **cosmic web quenching**. This phenomenon refers to the suppression of star formation driven by a galaxy's macroscopic topological environment, distinct from the effects of standard local isotropic density. Observational data and cosmological hydrodynamic simulations both indicate that a galaxy's proximity to filaments and nodes correlates strongly with its evolutionary state, gas supply, and stellar age. To further quantify the impact of environment on evolution, researchers have utilized statistical classification tools like Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Gaussian mixture models to categorize the diverse stellar mass histories of galaxies within different cosmological formation models [Cohn et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.3253C/abstract).

In the local Universe, analyses of the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) demonstrate that central galaxies located closer to cosmic web features (nodes, filaments, and walls) exhibit a systematically lower specific star formation rate [Winkel et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4920W/abstract). Even when controlling for stellar mass and local environmental density, galaxies near these topological features are on average older, metal richer, and $\alpha$-enhanced compared to their equal-mass counterparts located at larger distances from these structural features [Winkel et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4920W/abstract). This gradient suggests that proximity to large-scale structure imparts measurable "nurture" effects—potentially through mechanisms like ram pressure stripping or strangulation—that operate independently of the local group or cluster environment.

While the occurrence of cosmic web quenching is well-documented, the exact physical mechanisms driving it remain debated. Simulations offer conflicting views on the dominant mode of gas removal and starvation. Using the Horizon-AGN simulation at $z \sim 2$, researchers found that while proximity to filaments generally enhances stellar mass assembly, star formation is actively suppressed at the immediate edges of the filaments [Song et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.4635S/abstract). This study posits that gas accretion into dark matter halos becomes highly inefficient due to the excess angular momentum supplied by the "vorticity-rich" edges of the filaments, effectively starving the galaxies of fuel [Song et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.4635S/abstract).

Recent studies utilizing the IllustrisTNG simulation underscore that quenching is sensitive to filament properties beyond mere proximity. By replacing the standard [[delaunay-tessellation|Delaunay tessellation]] density field with a Monte Carlo Physarum Machine estimator to better trace diffuse matter, it was shown that over 90\% of galaxies reside within $\sim 1.5\, \mathrm{Mpc}$ of a filament spine [Hasan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..177H/abstract). In this framework, the distance to a filament is a poor predictor of median star formation. Instead, the local **filament line density**, $\Sigma_{\rm fil}$ (defined as the total overdensity per unit length along the filament segment), is the primary environmental driver of gas depletion at $z \le 1$ [Hasan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..177H/abstract). This effect is highly mass-dependent: while massive galaxies ($\log(M_*/M_\odot) \gtrsim 10.5$) are predominantly mass-quenched, the specific star formation rate of lower-mass galaxies is significantly curtailed near high-line density filaments, with satellite galaxies proving far more susceptible than centrals [Hasan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..177H/abstract).

The evolutionary impact of the cosmic web extends to the nodes where filaments intersect. Halo occupation distribution (HOD) modeling reveals a mass-dependent dichotomy in these high-density intersections. While high-mass halos at DISPERSE nodes display properties characteristic of older, virialized systems, low-mass halos in the same regions exhibit an excess of faint, blue galaxies [Perez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.3186P/abstract). This suggests that low-mass systems near nodes are not yet fully virialized and represent an active population continuously falling into the cluster environment along the filamentary network.

## Galaxy Kinematics and Spin Alignment

The orientation of a galaxy's angular momentum vector—or spin—relative to the large-scale cosmic web provides a critical test for models of structure formation. Utilizing DISPERSE as a multiscale [[galaxy-filament-finding-algorithm|Galaxy Filament-Finding algorithm]] rooted in [[topological-data-analysis-in-astronomy|Topological data analysis in astronomy]], observational studies have reconstructed 3D filamentary networks on Megaparsec scales to examine the alignment between the kinematic axis of galaxies and their host filaments. Using integral field spectroscopy (IFS) kinematics from the SAMI Galaxy Survey across GAlaxy and Mass Assembly fields, researchers have detected a mass-dependent transition in galaxy spin orientation with $>2\sigma$ confidence [Welker et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.2864W/abstract). 

This mass-dependent alignment exhibits a distinct bifurcation based on stellar mass. Low-mass galaxies preferentially align their rotational axes parallel to the spine of their nearest cosmic filament. In contrast, higher-mass galaxies tend to orient their spins orthogonal to the local filamentary axis. The transition between these two kinematic states, often referred to as a "spin-flip," occurs at a stellar transition mass bracketed between $10^{10.4}$ and $10^{10.9} M_{\odot}$:
$$ 10^{10.4} M_\odot \lesssim M_* \lesssim 10^{10.9} M_\odot $$
[Welker et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.2864W/abstract). There are also observational hints that this transition mass scales with the overall size of the surrounding filamentary structure.

The physical mechanism driving this transition is understood to reflect the different stages of angular momentum build-up during galaxy evolution. At low masses, early angular momentum build-up occurs in the vorticity-rich quadrants at the filament edges, imparting a net spin to the forming low-mass galaxies that aligns parallel to the filament axis [Song et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.4635S/abstract), [Welker et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.2864W/abstract). As galaxies grow, their evolutionary pathways become dominated by a progressive spin flip orthogonal to the filaments, driven by mergers as the galaxies increase in stellar mass [Welker et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.2864W/abstract).

The physical drivers of this alignment are further illuminated by comparing observational data with cosmological simulations. Full hydrodynamic simulations, such as Horizon-AGN, successfully reproduce the parallel-to-orthogonal spin transition when their structural skeletons are extracted using DISPERSE [Welker et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.2864W/abstract). However, standard dark matter-only simulations post-processed with semi-analytical models (SAMs) struggle to reproduce the observed spin orientation signals. This discrepancy indicates that gravitational dynamics and dark matter halo mergers alone are insufficient to account for the observed trends. The failure of SAMs in this regime strongly suggests that explicitly modeling the local physics of baryonic matter—specifically gas dissipation and accretion in vorticity-rich environments—is an essential component for capturing the true kinematic alignment of galaxies with the cosmic web [Welker et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.2864W/abstract).

## Comparative Analysis: Tracers and Methodologies

The robust extraction of the cosmic web is highly sensitive to the underlying data distribution, prompting extensive comparative studies on how DISPERSE performs across different structural tracers and relative to alternative algorithms. 

### Tracer Bias: Dark Matter versus Galaxies
A critical consideration in [[topological-data-analysis-in-astronomy|Topological data analysis in astronomy]] is the effect of galaxy bias. While cosmological simulations provide access to the continuous distribution of dark matter particles, observational surveys are restricted to discrete galaxies, which act as biased tracers of the underlying mass density. Comparisons of $z=0$ filaments extracted from simulated dark matter particles and from semi-analytical galaxy models reveal that the large-scale skeletons generally align, but they never perfectly coincide [Zakharova et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.4079Z/abstract). Notably, galaxy-based extractions tend to systematically underestimate the number of filaments connecting to massive, Coma- or Virgo-like clusters when compared to dark matter-based extractions [Zakharova et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.4079Z/abstract).

Furthermore, topological node identification in DISPERSE does not trivially map to purely density-based halo definitions. When matching dark matter cluster halos to DISPERSE nodes in simulations smoothed at scales $\leq 2.5 \ h^{-1} \mathrm{Mpc}$, approximately $3/4$ of the clusters consistently exhibit a direct node counterpart [Cohn et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513..624C/abstract). However, a significant fraction of clusters lack a matching node, exhibiting different local shear trends and merger histories, which highlights a fundamental physical discrepancy between spherical overdensity peaks and Morse-Smale critical points [Cohn et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513..624C/abstract). For matched pairs, a tight correlation exists between cluster mass and the local DISPERSE node density [Cohn et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513..624C/abstract).

The fidelity of the extraction also depends heavily on the density field estimator used to convert discrete tracers into a continuous manifold. While the standard implementation relies on a [[delaunay-tessellation|Delaunay tessellation]] Field Estimator (DTFE), recent advancements utilizing Monte Carlo Physarum Machine (slime mold) density fields have proven superior to DTFE in tracing the true underlying matter distribution. This alternative approach allows DISPERSE to identify low-prominence, diffuse filaments with significantly higher fidelity [Hasan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..177H/abstract).

### Comparison with Alternative Methodologies
DISPERSE leverages discrete Morse theory and [[persistent-homology|Persistent homology]] to isolate the topological skeleton of the density field [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract), [Pranav et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract). This contrasts with several other prominent structure extractors:

*   **Point-process models:** The Bisous model treats the cosmic web as a marked point process with interactions, resulting in a "pearl necklace" geometry where filaments are defined by roughly uniformly spaced galaxy groups with a characteristic length scale of $\sim 7 \ h^{-1} \mathrm{Mpc}$ [Tempel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...572A...8T/abstract). This contrasts with DISPERSE's representation of filaments as continuous topological ridges connecting distinct critical points.
*   **Hessian cosmic web identifiers:** Algorithms like the velocity shear tensor-based V-web separate the universe into kinematic environments. The COWS (COsmic Web Skeleton) method applies thinning algorithms directly to these Hessian identifiers to isolate individual curved cylindrical filaments at local density ridges, ensuring strict alignment with the underlying velocity field rather than relying on topological persistence [Pfeifer et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514..470P/abstract).
*   **Graph-based models:** Methods like the Beta-skeleton provide a parameter-free approach to constructing connectivity graphs. Unlike the manifold-based Morse-Smale complex, the Beta-skeleton's statistical properties are highly sensitive to—and thus useful for probing—redshift space distortions and the Alcock-Paczynski effect [Fang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.5276F/abstract). Other graph-based methods, such as T-ReX, utilize minimum spanning trees regularized within a Gaussian mixtures framework to extract structures [Bonnaire et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...637A..18B/abstract).
*   **Friends-of-friends models:** Parameter-free modified friends-of-friends (FoF) methods provide an alternative approach for identifying galaxy groups and dark matter haloes without the need for ad-hoc density thresholds [Muñoz-Cuartas et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1203.4846M/abstract). These rely on clustering linkage rather than resolving topological topology mappings.

Ultimately, while Hessian identifiers excel at dynamical classification and point processes capture discrete spatial regularities, DISPERSE remains the preeminent tool for multiscale connectivity and hierarchical structure extraction due to its robust, mathematically formalized handling of noise via persistence [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract).

## Historical Evolution of Topological Indicators

Historically, the characterization of the large-scale cosmic web relied heavily on global geometric and statistical metrics. Before the advent of discrete multi-scale structure extractors, cosmologists primarily utilized the two-point correlation function, the topological genus, and Minkowski functionals to quantify the distribution of matter. Early theories of structure formation relied heavily on statistical physics and the Harrison-Zeldovich spectrum to understand the fundamental properties of the galaxy distribution [Pietronero et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhyA..306..395P/abstract). Before assuming homogenization at the largest scales, historical debates explored whether galaxy distributions exhibited fractal properties with a dimension of approximately 2 [Sylos Labini et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..1151S/abstract), [Pietronero et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhyA..306..395P/abstract). To probe these structure statistics on scales up to 500 Mpc, early researchers utilized large-scale cluster surveys, such as the REFLEX Cluster Survey, as cosmic high-energy laboratories [Böhringer et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002luml.conf...12B/abstract). As observational efforts scaled up to huge astronomical data sources like the SDSS, the field of computational astrostatistics drove the development of fast and efficient tools, including kd-trees and mixture models, to handle density estimation [Nichol et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10230N/abstract). 

While Minkowski functionals provided a robust mathematical framework to describe the geometric and morphological properties of the density field, they have been extensively applied to identify supercluster morphologies—such as "spiders" and "filaments"—and to demonstrate that multimodal clusters tend to reside in higher-density environments [Einasto et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...542A..36E/abstract). While valuable, these early indicators ultimately provided global statistical averages and lacked the multi-scale connectivity information required to objectively isolate and trace individual structural components, such as discrete filaments or voids, across complex spatial hierarchies.

To address the limitations of global averaging, the field experienced a paradigm shift toward [[topological-data-analysis-in-astronomy|Topological data analysis in astronomy]]. This theoretical evolution was characterized by an extension and deepening of the cosmologically familiar topological genus measure into a more comprehensive multiscale description using Betti numbers ($\beta_k$). By utilizing Betti numbers ($\beta_k$), researchers could objectively map the clear imprints of cluster nodes, filaments, walls, and voids [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract). However, applying classical Betti numbers directly to discrete, noisy datasets—such as galaxy surveys or $N$-body simulations—proved challenging, as sampling noise introduces a multitude of spurious topological features.

The critical mathematical leap that resolved this issue and enabled the creation of DISPERSE was the integration of discrete Morse theory with [[persistent-homology|Persistent homology]] [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract). By computing the discrete Morse-Smale complex over a density field filtration—typically constructed using a [[delaunay-tessellation|Delaunay tessellation]] of the discrete point sample—topological persistence assigns a quantitative measure of robustness to every topological feature. This persistence threshold allows researchers to objectively separate the true physical "skeleton" of the cosmic web from localized Poisson noise, preserving the underlying connectivity without relying on arbitrary density thresholds or ad-hoc smoothing parameters.

The robustness of these modern topological indicators has been systematically validated using diverse spatial point processes. For example, studies applying persistent Betti numbers to Voronoi clustering models—analogous to a [[poisson-voronoi-tessellation|Poisson Voronoi Tessellation]]—and fractal-like Soneira-Peebles models demonstrated that distinct web-like morphologies leave clear, quantifiable imprints in persistence diagrams [Pranav et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract). The distinct topological signatures of cluster nodes, filaments, walls, and voids can be clearly identified within these diagrams, mapping perfectly to the nested hierarchy of multiscale cosmic structures. This historical evolution from scalar Minkowski functionals to persistent topological skeletons laid the foundational mathematics for DISPERSE, transforming the cosmic web from a qualitative visual concept into a rigorously defined set of multiscale topological features.

## Recent Advances and Future Directions

The performance of DISPERSE relies heavily on the fidelity of the initial density field estimation. While the standard implementation uses the [[delaunay-tessellation|Delaunay tessellation]] Field Estimator (DTFE) [Sousbie et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ascl.soft02015S/abstract), recent studies have explored alternative estimators to improve the extraction of topological features. Notably, the Monte Carlo Physarum Machine (MCPM)—an algorithm inspired by the foraging behavior of slime mold—has been successfully integrated into the DISPERSE framework [Hasan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..177H/abstract). When applied to cosmological simulations, the MCPM density field outperforms traditional DTFE in tracing the underlying matter distribution. Specifically, it enables the identification of low-prominence and diffuse filaments that are otherwise lost to noise in standard Delaunay-based extractions.

This enhanced sensitivity has allowed for more granular studies of galaxy evolution within the cosmic web. By defining a local filament line density, $\Sigma_{\rm fil}$ (the MCPM overdensity per unit length), researchers have identified a differential effect on galaxy quenching. At $z \le 1$, galaxies—particularly satellites—are found to be highly quenched and gas-poor when located near high-line density filaments. Interestingly, this environmental quenching effect appears to vanish at earlier times, suggesting that the high-redshift cosmic web does not significantly restrict galactic gas supply [Hasan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..177H/abstract). This observation adds necessary nuance to the ongoing debate over the precise quenching mechanisms at filament edges, where alternative models suggest that high angular momentum supply at vorticity-rich boundaries limits gas accretion efficiency [Song et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.4635S/abstract). 

Another major recent advance is the combination of DISPERSE's topological classification with Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD) modeling. By separating the inner regions of filaments and nodes from their lower-density surroundings, HOD modeling can constrain how dark matter halo assembly varies by cosmic web environment [Perez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.3186P/abstract). Recent HOD analyses using DISPERSE have revealed an excess of faint, blue galaxies residing within low-mass halos located at cosmic web nodes. This indicates that these specific environments are not yet fully virialized and act as active sites of continuous galaxy infall along filaments. In contrast, higher-mass halos within nodes exhibit the classical characteristics of mature, virialized systems.

Looking forward, there is immense potential to expand the application of algorithms like DISPERSE to alternative high-redshift tracers. For instance, the high-redshift cosmic web ($z = 1.0-1.8$) has been successfully mapped using quasar systems identified via friend-of-friend algorithms [Einasto et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...568A..46E/abstract). Applying refined [[galaxy-filament-finding-algorithm|Galaxy Filament-Finding algorithm]] pipelines to such quasar datasets and the next generation of massive spectroscopic datasets, including the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) survey, will be critical for empirically testing the mass-dependent and tracer-dependent biases of cosmic web extraction [Zakharova et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.4079Z/abstract), [Hasan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..177H/abstract), ultimately providing a more rigorous test of structure formation models across cosmic time.
