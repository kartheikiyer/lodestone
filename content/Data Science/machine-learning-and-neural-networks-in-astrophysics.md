**Machine Learning and Neural Networks in Astrophysics** represent a paradigm shift in how astronomers process, classify, and interpret the petabyte-scale data streams generated by modern observatories. In the context of astrophysics, [[machine-learning-in-astronomy|machine learning]] involves the deployment of data-driven computational models—ranging from early Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs) to modern deep Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)—to identify non-linear patterns in complex, high-dimensional observational and theoretical data. As the field transitions into an era dominated by high-cadence surveys from facilities like the Vera C. Rubin Observatory and the *Euclid* mission, traditional human visual inspection and computationally expensive template-fitting algorithms have become intractable. Driven by recent advances in [[gpu-acceleration-in-astronomy|GPU acceleration in astronomy]] and [[computational-algorithms-and-machine-learning-in-astronomy|Computational Algorithms and Machine Learning in Astronomy]], deep learning architectures now serve as essential tools for everything from real-time transient alert filtering to likelihood-free cosmological inference.

The integration of these algorithms has yielded transformative results across multiple astrophysical domains. In extragalactic astronomy, CNNs have revolutionized [[automated-galaxy-classification|Automated galaxy classification]], consistently achieving accuracies exceeding 90% for broad morphological taxonomy while operating orders of magnitude faster than classical algorithms [Domínguez Sánchez et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.3661D/abstract), [Huertas-Company et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..221....8H/abstract). Beyond raw imaging, neural networks are now routinely used to bypass traditional Bayesian spectral energy distribution (SED) modeling, rapidly estimating photometric redshifts and automating spectral analysis with high precision. In theoretical cosmology, GNNs and generative models are heavily utilized to construct high-fidelity emulators. These architectures map computationally inexpensive dark-matter-only N-body simulations directly to realistic baryonic galaxy distributions, accelerating theoretical predictions by several orders of magnitude (e.g., a factor of $10^4$) [Jespersen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941....7J/abstract), and enabling the rapid traversal of parameter spaces required for [[large-scale-galaxy-classification|Large-scale galaxy classification]] and structure analysis [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...915...71V/abstract). In addition to cosmology and extragalactic astrophysics, machine learning has also permeated exoplanetary science, where Siamese architectures have been successfully applied to low-data scenarios for exoplanet identification in Kepler datasets [G et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230509596G/abstract).

Despite these profound successes, the application of neural networks introduces significant challenges and open questions that are actively reshaping theoretical paradigms. Data-driven methods have begun to challenge traditional textbook assumptions; for example, recent models suggest that precise stellar chemical tagging can be achieved without relying on traditional synthetic spectral libraries [de Mijolla et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913...12D/abstract), and unsupervised learning has demonstrated that the classical Hubble sequence is largely inadequate for the highly irregular morphologies observed at high redshifts ($z > 5$) by JWST [Tohill et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962..164T/abstract). Currently, the most pressing hurdle in the field is the "domain shift" problem: models trained on specific hydrodynamical simulations often overspecialize to underlying subgrid physics, suffering catastrophic performance drops when applied to real survey data or disparate simulations. Moving forward, the pursuit of domain-independent architectures, the integration of Bayesian uncertainty quantification, and the development of Explainable AI (XAI) to interpret the physical mechanisms learned by these "black box" networks constitute the primary frontiers of [[machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine Learning in Astronomy]].

## Historical Development: From Perceptrons to Deep Learning

The application of neural networks in astronomy traces its roots to the mid-1990s, driven by the exponential growth of data from digital sky surveys. Early implementations primarily focused on [[automated-galaxy-classification|Automated galaxy classification]] and parameter estimation using "shallow" network architectures that relied on explicit feature extraction.

During this period, early morphological classification relied heavily on the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), a foundational feed-forward artificial neural network. Because processing raw pixel data was computationally prohibitive, astronomers engineered "catalog-based" pipelines. Images were first reduced to tabular parameters—such as isophotal shapes, surface brightnesses, or Concentration-Asymmetry-Smoothness (CAS) metrics. To optimize these inputs and eliminate redundant information, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was frequently employed to compress the feature space prior to network ingestion [Lahav et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.283..207L/abstract), [Andreon et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000MNRAS.319..700A/abstract). Alongside these developments, broader probabilistic machine learning frameworks were explored; for example, Gaussian Processes (GPs) combined with PCA for model reduction were introduced for star-galaxy separation, providing a robust approach that inherently quantified prediction uncertainty [Goumiri et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201006094G/abstract). Using training sets classified by human experts, networks trained via Backpropagation or Quasi-Newton algorithms successfully assigned Bayesian *a posteriori* probabilities to galaxy types, achieving agreement levels comparable to the variance between distinct human experts [Lahav et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995Sci...267..859L/abstract), [Lahav et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.283..207L/abstract).

Beyond morphology, these early networks were quickly adapted for broader astrophysical parameter estimation. Unsupervised architectures, such as Kohonen Self-Organizing Maps, were utilized to isolate early-type galaxy populations in cluster cores [Molinari et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&A...330..447M/abstract). For large cosmological data sets, committees of neural networks demonstrated the ability to map photometric data to cosmological parameters, scaling computationally as $N^{3/2}$ [Phillips et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12359P/abstract). In the realm of photometric redshifts, MLP networks trained on multi-color catalogs demonstrated remarkable computational efficiency, capable of estimating redshifts for $10^5$ galaxies in mere seconds with a scatter of $\sigma_z \simeq 0.022$ [Vanzella et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004A&A...423..761V/abstract), effectively establishing neural networks as a competitive alternative to traditional template-fitting [Li et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ChJAA...7..448L/abstract).

The modern era of [[machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine Learning in Astronomy]] is defined by a paradigm shift from catalog-based feature engineering to pixel-based, end-to-end deep learning. This transition was catalyzed by the availability of massive labeled data sets, such as the citizen-science project Galaxy Zoo, and breakthroughs in computer vision. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) superseded the traditional MLP by natively ingesting multi-band raw pixel arrays, thereby allowing the network to automatically discover and extract abstract spatial features that are otherwise lost in standard cataloging [Hoyle et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&C....16...34H/abstract). Furthermore, modern multimodal neural networks combine image morphology with SED information to achieve high accuracy in separating stars, galaxies, and quasars in wide-field surveys like KiDS [Feng et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240603797F/abstract).

Deep learning architectures rapidly revolutionized morphological taxonomy. Early applications of CNNs on deep fields, such as the CANDELS survey, successfully predicted visual-like morphologies (e.g., spheroid, disk, irregular) with near-zero bias [Huertas-Company et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..221....8H/abstract). Today, there is strong consensus in the literature that deep learning models consistently achieve classification accuracies exceeding $90\%$-$95\%$ for broad morphological categories, matching or surpassing human performance while operating several orders of magnitude faster [Domínguez Sánchez et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.3661D/abstract). Moreover, these deep convolutional architectures have been shown to outperform traditional non-parametric structural measurements, exhibiting greater stability even at low signal-to-noise ratios [Tohill et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916....4T/abstract).

## Morphological Classification and Structural Analysis of Galaxies

The exponential growth of imaging data from surveys such as SDSS, KiDS, and CANDELS has driven the transition from manual visual inspection—pioneered by citizen science projects like Galaxy Zoo—to [[automated-galaxy-classification|Automated galaxy classification]]. [[machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine Learning in Astronomy]], and particularly the use of CNNs, has revolutionized this domain. Modern CNNs routinely achieve classification accuracies exceeding $90-95\%$ for broad taxonomic tasks, such as distinguishing elliptical from spiral galaxies, operating several orders of magnitude faster than traditional visual or parametric fitting methods [Domínguez Sánchez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.3661D/abstract), [Huertas-Company et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..221....8H/abstract). 

Deep learning models also outperform traditional algorithms in extracting continuous structural parameters. CNNs can directly predict non-parametric morphological metrics like Concentration, Asymmetry, and Smoothness (CAS) from pixel data, demonstrating superior stability at low signal-to-noise ratios compared to classical algorithms [Tohill et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916....4T/abstract). While 2D CNNs dominate the literature, researchers have also explored computationally efficient alternatives; for instance, applying automated machine learning (AutoML) to 1D sequences derived from elliptical isophotes provides a highly efficient method for image feature extraction and galaxy classification without the overhead of full 2D convolutions [Tarsitano et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.3330T/abstract). Furthermore, models predicting parametric profiles have revealed new physical insights; for instance, CNN-derived classifications of SDSS images suggest that the Sérsic index of a galaxy's bulge correlates strongly with its global morphology, contradicting some traditional assumptions [Fischer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.2057F/abstract). To further understand network behavior, t-SNE visualization has been employed to identify clustering patterns and easily isolate mislabeled sources within these vast morphological catalogs [Dai et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180705657D/abstract). 

As a crucial data quality control step prior to high-level structural analysis, CNNs are now deployed to identify and remove "galaxy shreds"—fragments of large galaxies misidentified as separate sources—thereby cleaning massive photometric catalogs like SDSS and DESI [Di Teodoro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....165..123D/abstract). Morphological analysis is also particularly challenging for Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN), where the brilliant central point source obscures underlying structures. The PSFGAN+GAMORNET framework addresses this by utilizing generative models to decouple and subtract the central point source light, enabling the accurate characterization of the host galaxy's underlying morphology out to $z < 1$ [Tian et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944..124T/abstract).

Beyond optical imaging, deep learning has been successfully applied to radio-interferometric data to differentiate between compact and extended morphology classes in datasets like the Radio Galaxy Zoo [Lukic et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476..246L/abstract). Recent studies have expanded this domain by comparing CNN- and transformer-based algorithms for radio galaxy classification, demonstrating their particular efficacy in identifying and filtering out foreground contaminants [Denzel et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260104773D/abstract). Further improving on this, the application of semi-supervised Group Equivariant CNNs (G-CNNs) has been shown to increase classification accuracy by mathematically preserving rotational symmetries directly within the radio image feature maps [Hossain et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PrCS..222..601H/abstract).

Despite these successes, performance often degrades on rare or complex morphological classes due to extreme class imbalance in training data. Identifying ring galaxies—critical for understanding atypical galaxy dynamics—has historically been challenging. By utilizing transfer learning and data augmentation via Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), CNNs have successfully extracted ring galaxy catalogs with $7.4 \times$ the precision and $15.4 \times$ the detection rate of conventional algorithms [Krishnakumar et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221011428K/abstract). However, more recent analyses emphasize that extreme class imbalance in real survey data still produces significant false-positive rates (e.g., $\sim41\%$), underscoring the need for further model refinement before applying these pipelines to billions of objects [Krishnakumar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..191K/abstract).

Merger detection and the analysis of complex tidal features are other areas significantly enhanced by deep learning. CNNs trained on mock observations generated from cosmological simulations (e.g., IllustrisTNG) can classify major interacting systems out to $z \sim 3$ [Ferreira et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...895..115F/abstract), while recent models have expanded this to accurately identify minor mergers down to stellar masses of $10^8 M_\odot$ at $z \sim 1$ [Schechter et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251012173S/abstract). Moreover, frameworks like DeepGalaxy have demonstrated the ability to predict the physical properties of galaxy mergers directly from images, achieving computational speedups of a factor of $10^5$ over traditional methods [Cai et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201011630C/abstract). Similarly, the automated detection of faint tidal features (arms, streams, shells, diffuse structures) in DECaLS data using CNNs is now possible, providing vital insights into hierarchical galaxy assembly histories [Gordon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240406487G/abstract). By combining multi-band imaging with kinematic data, such as MaNGA $\text{H}\alpha$ velocity maps, algorithms can even distinguish fine-grained interaction stages, including incoming pairs, pericentric passages, and final coalescence [Chang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937...97C/abstract). However, the literature highlights a critical tension: models are highly sensitive to "domain shift" and observational realism. CNNs trained on idealized simulated stellar maps fail dramatically on real survey data unless realistic sky noise and point spread functions (PSFs) are explicitly included in the training set [Bottrell et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.5390B/abstract).

To address architectural limitations of standard CNNs—which are not inherently rotation-invariant—researchers have introduced Capsule Networks to [[large-scale-galaxy-classification|Large-scale galaxy classification]]. Capsule Networks inherently capture spatial hierarchies and rotational equivariance, ensuring robust morphological predictions without relying on exhaustive rotational data augmentation [Katebi et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.1539K/abstract).

Finally, the applicability of the traditional Hubble sequence is actively debated for high-redshift ($z > 2$) galaxies observed by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). Visual classifications are often inadequate for the irregular morphologies prevalent in the early Universe. To prevent low-redshift nomenclature biases, recent studies have turned to unsupervised machine learning, such as contrastive learning and variational autoencoders. These self-supervised methods extract physically meaningful morphological clusters directly from feature representations, dynamically redefining galaxy taxonomy for the high-redshift regime [Tohill et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962..164T/abstract). Similarly, contrastive learning has also been successfully applied to redefine features in large-scale low-redshift surveys, such as SDSS and Galaxy Zoo DECaLS [Wei et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASP..134k4508W/abstract).

## Photometric and Spectroscopic Redshift Estimation

Accurate distance estimation is a fundamental prerequisite for extragalactic astrophysics and cosmology. With the advent of next-generation imaging and spectroscopic surveys such as the Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST) and the Euclid mission, the unprecedented volume of data necessitates automated, highly efficient redshift pipelines. The application of [[machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine Learning in Astronomy]] has fundamentally shifted how distances are derived, moving from computationally expensive template-fitting algorithms to rapid, data-driven neural network inference.

**Photometric Redshift (photo-z) Estimation**

Photometric redshift (photo-z) algorithms traditionally map multi-band catalog photometry (e.g., magnitudes and colors) to distance estimates. However, recent deep learning approaches bypass intermediate catalog generation, directly ingesting raw pixel data from multi-band image cutouts [Hoyle et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&C....16...34H/abstract). The literature has historically debated the utility of adding spatial and morphological information to redshift prediction; early studies utilizing standard artificial neural networks suggested that morphological shape parameters offered no statistically significant benefit over photometry alone and could even introduce noise [Singal et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PASP..123..615S/abstract). 

More recent consensus, however, demonstrates that modern CNNs can successfully disentangle weak morphological features from dominant photometric ones. Jointly training models on galaxy fluxes, colors, and raw images significantly reduces the overall photo-z scatter ($\sigma_z$) and the catastrophic outlier fraction [Menou et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.4802M/abstract). For example, networks trained on Kilo-Degree Survey (KiDS) data achieved a normalized median absolute deviation of $\sigma_{\mathrm{NMAD}} = 0.014$ for low-redshift galaxies, suppressing the overall outlier fraction to just $0.3\%$ when correctly isolated from stars and active galactic nuclei [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...666A..85L/abstract). These robust deep learning pipelines are now generating vast, survey-level databases; a prime example is the PS1-STRM catalog, which deployed neural networks to perform simultaneous source classification and photometric redshift estimation for billions of objects across the Pan-STARRS1 3$\pi$ survey [Beck et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.1633B/abstract).

To meet the rigorous precision requirements of precision cosmology, models must go beyond point estimates to provide full probability distributions. Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs) have been deployed to infer accurate posterior probability distributions for photometric redshifts. These networks evaluate prediction uncertainty directly, helping to identify and isolate catastrophic outliers in survey data [Jones et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..130J/abstract). To further refine these outputs, recent pipelines incorporate conformal predictions to calibrate BNN uncertainties, ensuring statistical coverage that strictly adheres to LSST science requirements [Jones et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..159J/abstract).

**Spectroscopic Redshift and Spectral Analysis**

While photometry provides scalable distance estimates for [[large-scale-galaxy-classification|Large-scale galaxy classification]], Spectroscopic redshift (spec-z) measurements remain the gold standard for precision. Deep learning models, particularly 1D CNNs and autoencoders, are increasingly utilized to automate the extraction of parameters from spectra. Architectures such as SPENDER combine convolutional encoders with decoders to compress spectra into low-dimensional latent spaces and generate rest-frame representations, seamlessly handling observational artifacts like skyline residuals without requiring extensive preprocessing [Melchior et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....166...74M/abstract).

Furthermore, generative networks can simultaneously reconstruct spectra, estimate redshifts, and perform anomaly detection within a single unified framework. These models achieve classification and spec-z accuracies exceeding $98\%$ for galaxies, operating at significantly higher computational efficiency than classical PCA and traditional template-fitting methods [Zhong et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241221130Z/abstract). By rapidly processing massive datasets, machine learning models relieve a major computational bottleneck, enabling real-time spectral analysis for current and future spectroscopic sky surveys.

## Cosmological Emulators and N-body Simulation Acceleration

Constraining cosmological parameters from large-scale structure (LSS) observations requires comparing immense observational datasets with precise theoretical predictions. Because high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations—which model both collisionless dark matter and complex baryonic physics—require millions of CPU hours per volume, they are computationally intractable for traversing large parameter spaces. To circumvent this bottleneck, researchers are increasingly utilizing [[machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine Learning in Astronomy]] to construct high-fidelity emulators. These algorithms either dramatically accelerate theoretical predictions or map computationally inexpensive dark-matter-only N-body simulations directly to observable baryonic distributions.

At the level of theoretical summary statistics, neural networks can be trained as surrogate models to accelerate analytical calculations. For example, neural network emulators can predict Lagrangian Perturbation Theory (LPT) matter and galaxy power spectra across a broad $\omega$CDM parameter space with $\sim 0.1\%$ accuracy. These LPT surrogates reduce evaluation times to approximately one millisecond, representing a speedup of a factor of $10^3$ compared to full Boltzmann and LPT solvers [DeRose et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...04..056D/abstract). In the realm of gas dynamics, the `deepCool` framework introduces artificial neural networks for the fast and accurate estimation of radiative cooling and heating rates in irradiated gas, significantly reducing the computational overhead of large-scale hydrodynamical simulations [Galligan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190101264G/abstract). Moving beyond perturbation theory, deep learning models such as $\mathrm{D}^3\mathrm{M}$ can generate non-linear structure formation directly from linear theory inputs, accurately capturing the non-linear suppression of the power spectrum by massive neutrinos down to scales of $k = 0.7\ h\ \mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$ while operating $10^4$ times faster than traditional N-body techniques [Giusarma et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...950...70G/abstract). The LULO framework also introduces a gradient-free deep learning approach for reconstructing 3D cosmic initial conditions using non-differentiable structure formation models [Doeser et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250213243D/abstract). Transformer-based architectures are increasingly used for emulating multi-probe data vectors, providing high precision for forecasting cosmological parameters beyond Lambda CDM [Zhong et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240217716Z/abstract).

To bypass hydrodynamics entirely, researchers employ "baryonic painting," wherein machine learning models map dark matter halos and density fields directly to galaxy properties. CNNs and diffusion models can ingest 3D dark matter density fields to predict the spatial distribution of stellar mass and galaxy populations, significantly outperforming traditional empirical halo occupation distribution (HOD) models [Kasmanoff et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201200186K/abstract), [Sether et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241205131S/abstract). Because cosmic webs and dark matter merger trees are intrinsically sparse and non-Euclidean, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as the optimal architecture for this mapping task [Jespersen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941....7J/abstract). The GNN-based `Mangrove` framework, for instance, learns the physical relations encoded in dark matter merger trees to predict galaxy stellar mass, cold gas mass, and metallicity, executing four orders of magnitude faster than the semi-analytic models (SAMs) it emulates [Jespersen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941....7J/abstract). Additionally, CNNs have been utilized to discriminate between Galaxy-dominated and AGN-dominated reionization models using simulated 21cm maps [Hassan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..333...47H/abstract), and can even reconstruct the local cosmic web and dark matter distribution from galaxy positions and velocities in surveys like Cosmicflows-3 [Hong et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913...76H/abstract).

These accelerated forward-models serve as the foundation for Simulation-Based Inference (SBI), allowing field-level constraints on cosmological parameters via likelihood-free inference. A central pillar of this effort is the CAMELS (Cosmology and Astrophysics with MachinE Learning Simulations) project, which provides thousands of standard and hydrodynamical simulations utilizing varying subgrid physics (e.g., IllustrisTNG and SIMBA) across a vast volume of cosmological ($\Omega_m, \sigma_8$) and astrophysical parameter space [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...915...71V/abstract). Within this project, researchers have utilized Normalizing Flows to construct hierarchical models mapping the probability of galaxy and halo properties explicitly conditioned on underlying cosmological parameters [Lovell et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023mla..confE..21L/abstract). Using CAMELS data, GNNs can infer $\Omega_m$ to $12-13\%$ accuracy directly from the spatial distribution and kinematics of just $\sim 1000$ galaxies in a $(25\ h^{-1}\ \mathrm{Mpc})^3$ volume [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract). Extended suites, such as CAMELS-SAM, enable neural networks to marginalize over highly uncertain astrophysical feedback prescriptions to recover cosmological parameters to within $3-8\%$ precision [Perez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954...11P/abstract).

Despite these successes, the literature highlights a persistent tension regarding "domain shift" and generalizability. Neural networks trained to map dark matter to baryons often overspecialize to the unique subgrid physics of their training set. Consequently, models that achieve high precision on IllustrisTNG data frequently experience severe performance degradation when tested on disparate hydrodynamical frameworks. For instance, cosmological parameter inference models struggle when cross-evaluated between IllustrisTNG and SIMBA [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract), while estimators for X-ray cluster masses suffer similar performance drops between the Magneticum and IllustrisTNG simulations [Ntampaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251009748N/abstract). Developing robust, domain-independent architectures that can successfully apply simulation-trained weights to genuine observational survey data remains a major open challenge in the application of cosmological emulators.

## Graph Neural Networks and the Halo-Galaxy Connection

The relationship between dark matter halos and their resident galaxies is fundamentally complex. Historically, this relationship has been modeled using empirical Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD) frameworks or semi-analytic models (SAMs). However, the spatial distribution of galaxies and the hierarchical assembly of dark matter merger trees present highly irregular, sparse, and non-Euclidean data structures. For these domains, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as the optimal architecture within [[machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine Learning in Astronomy]], outperforming traditional grid-based convolutional networks by naturally capturing the topology of the cosmic web.

By representing halos or galaxies as nodes and their spatial or kinematic relationships as edges, GNNs can directly map dark matter merger trees to realistic baryonic properties. For example, the Mangrove emulator utilizes a GNN to predict galactic stellar mass, cold gas mass, metallicity, and star formation rate directly from merger trees across a $(75 \, h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc})^3$ volume. This approach achieves lower root-mean-square errors than traditional SAMs while operating four orders of magnitude faster [Jespersen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941....7J/abstract). Similarly, message-passing GNNs that incorporate information from neighboring subhalos can accurately predict stellar mass, demonstrating that the extended galaxy environment contains critical constraining power [Wu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230612327W/abstract). Conversely, GNNs have been highly successful at the inverse problem: inferring the underlying halo mass ($M_{\mathrm{halo}}$) from observable galaxy properties—such as stellar mass, radius, and kinematics—with an accuracy of $\sim 0.2$ dex [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935...30V/abstract), [Garuda et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241112629G/abstract). To address the interpretability issues inherent in these "black box" predictions, researchers have increasingly turned to symbolic regression. This artificial intelligence approach successfully discovers explicit analytic expressions for subhalo properties like mass, providing a physically interpretable alternative to pure neural network inference [Shao et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927...85S/abstract).

Beyond individual halos, GNNs facilitate simulation-based, likelihood-free inference at the field level, extracting cosmological parameters from non-linear scales that evade traditional summary statistics. Trained on expansive simulation suites like the CAMELS project, GNNs can infer the matter density parameter $\Omega_m$ to within $12\%-13\%$ accuracy using only the 3D positions of $\sim 1000$ galaxies. When baryonic properties such as stellar mass and metallicity are included as node features, this precision improves dramatically to $4\%-8\%$ [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract). GNNs have also demonstrated up to $99\%$ accuracy in binary classification tasks distinguishing between different dark energy models, providing tight regression constraints on the dark energy equation of state parameter $w_0$ [Farsian et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221011487F/abstract). Furthermore, advanced equivariant GNNs that explicitly preserve 3D rotational and translational symmetries (e.g., $E(3)$ equivariance) are now being deployed to model complex non-Euclidean features like galaxy intrinsic alignments [Jagvaral et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240918761J/abstract).

Despite their representational power, the application of GNNs to real observational data is currently limited by the "domain shift" problem. Models trained on simulations are often highly sensitive to the specific subgrid physics prescriptions used to generate the training data. For instance, a GNN trained to infer cosmology on the IllustrisTNG model often suffers a severe degradation in accuracy when tested on data from SIMBA or EAGLE, as the network overspecialize to simulation-specific baryonic feedback signatures [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract). To bridge this gap, recent efforts have introduced Domain Adaptive GNNs (DA-GNNs). By employing unsupervised domain adaptation techniques—such as minimizing the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) between different simulation suites—these networks are forced to learn domain-invariant topological features, significantly improving cross-simulation robustness [Roncoli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231101588R/abstract). Overcoming this domain shift remains the most critical hurdle before likelihood-free inference with GNNs can be robustly applied to data from upcoming large-scale surveys.

## Spectroscopy, SED Fitting, and Chemical Tagging

The application of [[computational-algorithms-and-machine-learning-in-astronomy|Computational Algorithms and Machine Learning in Astronomy]] to spectroscopy has fundamentally altered how researchers extract physical parameters from high-dimensional spectral data. Traditional Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) fitting relies on computationally expensive Bayesian inference, often requiring $>10$ to $100$ CPU hours per galaxy to sample the complex parameter space of star formation histories and dust attenuation curves [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract). Neural network emulators drastically reduce this computational burden while maintaining comparable precision. 

For instance, Amortized Neural Posterior Estimation (ANPE) frameworks, such as SEDFLOW, can produce full posterior distributions in $\sim 1$ second per galaxy [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract). Other machine learning methods, like the Parametric t-SNE (Pt-SNE) algorithm, estimate stellar masses with a root-mean-square error of $0.169$ dex, operating $\sim 3.2 \times 10^3$ times faster than standard template-fitting tools like LePhare while significantly reducing bias [Asadi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220544A/abstract). CNN pipelines have similarly been developed to efficiently search for extremely metal-poor galaxies (XMPs) in multi-band imaging based on their predicted N2 index [Cheng et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250201638C/abstract). Tools like MIRKWOOD utilize ensemble supervised learning and Shapley values to improve interpretability and marginalize over poor individual model performance [Gilda et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916...43G/abstract). However, the literature notes a trade-off: small neural network architectures can produce flux residuals with significant correlations, potentially inducing dangerous systematic errors in predicted colors if not carefully optimized [Mathews et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954..132M/abstract).

Deep unsupervised learning and autoencoders have also emerged as powerful tools for analyzing raw spectra without explicit physical models. The SPENDER architecture utilizes a convolutional encoder to compress galaxy spectra into a low-dimensional latent space representing up to 256 spectral features, paired with a decoder that generates a rest-frame representation [Melchior et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....166...74M/abstract). This allows for super-resolution reconstruction that exceeds the native resolution of the observing instrument—successfully resolving features like the [O II] doublet—while remaining robust to observational artifacts such as skyline subtraction residuals.

Supervised models are increasingly applied to emission line ratios to extract interstellar medium (ISM) properties. Algorithms such as GAME (GAlaxy Machine learning for Emission lines) infer density, metallicity, and ionization parameters directly from UV, optical, and far-infrared spectra, providing higher accuracy and broader applicability than traditional empirical diagnostics like $R_{23}$ [Ucci et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.1484U/abstract), [Ucci et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.1144U/abstract). Similarly, neural networks can simultaneously handle multiple physical properties (e.g., UV slope, magnitude, mass) to predict Ly$\alpha$ emission from distant galaxies with high reliability [Yoshioka et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241214676Y/abstract). CNNs have also been deployed on integral field unit (IFU) data to disentangle multiple kinematic line-of-sight components in H II regions, a task critical for analyzing merging galaxy systems [Rhea et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923..169R/abstract).

In stellar astrophysics, [[machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine Learning in Astronomy]] allows for the rapid estimation of fundamental parameters (ages, masses, radii) for solar-like main-sequence stars from classical and asteroseismic observations [Bellinger et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...31B/abstract). Furthermore, hierarchical deep learning tools like STELNET can automatically predict stellar mass and age directly from absolute luminosity and effective temperature [Garraffo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....162..157G/abstract). By applying CNNs to high-resolution stellar spectra, researchers can predict effective temperatures with a precision of $37$ K, $\log g$ to $0.06$ dex, and individual abundances like [Mg/Fe] and [Fe/H] to $\sim 0.04-0.05$ dex [Ambrosch et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...672A..46A/abstract). Notably, machine learning enables disentangled representation learning, effectively isolating chemical factors of variation from standard atmospheric parameters ($T_{\rm eff}$, $\log g$, [Fe/H]) [de Mijolla et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913...12D/abstract). This data-driven approach allows for precise chemical tagging without relying on traditional synthetic spectral libraries or ab initio stellar evolution isochrones, challenging the conventional textbook paradigm that synthetic models are strictly necessary for accurate abundance measurements [de Mijolla et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913...12D/abstract).

## Strong Gravitational Lensing: Detection and Mass Modeling

Upcoming wide-field surveys such as those conducted by the Vera C. Rubin Observatory and the *Euclid* space telescope are expected to observe hundreds of thousands of strong gravitational lensing systems. Because visual identification on this scale is computationally intractable, the automation of strong lens discovery has become a major application of [[machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine Learning in Astronomy]]. CNNs are now routinely deployed to search for gravitational arcs and multiple-image configurations directly from multi-band pixel data [Petrillo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.1129P/abstract), [Jacobs et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..243...17J/abstract). These deep learning pipelines have successfully extracted hundreds of new lens candidates from current-generation imaging in the Dark Energy Survey (DES) [Jacobs et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..243...17J/abstract) and the Kilo-Degree Survey (KiDS) [Li et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...30L/abstract). Recent architectures employing semi-supervised learning and data augmentation via Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have achieved precision improvements by a factor of $5$ to $10$ across all recall values when compared to conventional supervised algorithms [Keerthi Vasan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5368K/abstract). In addition to broadband morphological searches, deep Residual Networks (ResNets) have been successfully applied to search for gravitational lens candidates by detecting spectroscopic signatures of anomalous Ly$\alpha$ emission lines [Li et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482..313L/abstract).

Despite these high theoretical accuracies, the literature reflects some tension regarding the generalizability of these models to real survey data. Systematic benchmarking of independent CNNs on Hyper Suprime-Cam (HSC) imaging reveals that network performance is highly sensitive to the properties of the simulated training sets. While algorithms perform exceptionally well on their own bespoke mock test sets, they often exhibit comparable, lower efficiencies when confronted with real astronomical candidates and complex false positives [More et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533..525M/abstract). Furthermore, neural network lens finders introduce distinct selection functions that must be characterized for precise population statistics. Studies indicate that CNNs are biased toward selecting systems with a larger Einstein radius (e.g., $\theta_{\rm E} \gtrsim 1.0^{\prime\prime}$ for high-significance thresholds), higher lens ellipticities, and larger, more concentrated source light distributions [Herle et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230710355H/abstract).

Beyond detection, neural networks are accelerating the mass reconstruction of lenses, a vital step for constraining the Hubble constant ($H_0$) via time-delay cosmography. Precise mass modeling necessitates a joint analysis of lensing observables and the deflector's stellar kinematics to break parameter degeneracies. Because traditional axisymmetric dynamical modeling constitutes a severe computational bottleneck, neural network emulators have been introduced to synthesize kinematic maps from given mass models. Frameworks like the Stellar Kinematics Neural Network (SKiNN) predict spatially resolved kinematic maps with sub-1% precision, yielding speedups in likelihood evaluations by a factor of $\sim 200$ [Gomer et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...679A..59G/abstract).

In addition to modeling the primary deflector, precise time-delay cosmography relies on estimating the external convergence ($\kappa_{\rm ext}$) contributed by large-scale structure along the line of sight. Recent hierarchical inference pipelines utilize Bayesian Graph Neural Networks (BGNNs) to estimate $\kappa_{\rm ext}$ directly from the photometric measurements of line-of-sight galaxies [Park et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953..178P/abstract). By ingesting all available structural information of the sightline into a graph representation, these networks accurately recover the population mean of $\kappa$ without bias. This approach effectively handles sample variance and reduces the contribution of line-of-sight mass fluctuations to the $H_0$ error budget to well under 1% [Park et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953..178P/abstract). While much of the focus is on strong lensing, neural network classifiers are also revolutionizing weak lensing analyses. For example, neural networks can optimize tomographic binning for $3 \times 2$ pt weak lensing surveys by effectively identifying and grouping galaxies with high-accuracy photometric redshift estimates, thereby tightening overall cosmological constraints [Moskowitz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...950...49M/abstract).

## Real-time Transient Detection and Supernova Classification

The deployment of next-generation, high-cadence synoptic surveys, such as the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST), is expected to yield millions of transient alerts per night. Processing this continuous data stream requires automated, real-time identification pipelines that bypass the latency of traditional template matching, accelerating detection by several orders of magnitude using [[machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine Learning in Astronomy]]. 

Because transient light curves are inherently sequential and exhibit temporal dependencies, Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and their variants—such as Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs)—have become the architecture of choice for photometric supernova (SN) classification. For example, deep RNNs trained strictly on filter fluxes and observational times can distinguish Type Ia from non-Ia supernovae with $94.7\%$ accuracy and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of $0.986$ [Charnock et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837L..28C/abstract). Crucially, these networks excel at "early-epoch" classification (predicting transient classes prior to maximum light), achieving $93.1\%$ accuracy on partial, early-phase light curves [Charnock et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837L..28C/abstract). 

Early-time classification is paramount for prioritizing scarce spectroscopic follow-up resources. Recent architectures have demonstrated that injecting contextual data—specifically host-galaxy properties—drastically improves these early-phase predictions. The ORACLE model, utilizing an RNN with GRUs and a hierarchical cross-entropy loss function, incorporates host galaxy photometric redshift, spatial offset, ellipticity, and brightness to achieve a top-level (transient versus variable) macroaveraged precision of $0.96$ using only a single day of photometric observations post-discovery [Shah et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...995....4S/abstract). Similarly, shallow learning approaches employing a single recurrent layer alongside host-galaxy photometry and transfer learning have achieved overall multi-class accuracies of $82\% \pm 2\%$ within 3 days of an event's discovery, improving to $87\% \pm 5\%$ within 30 days [Gagliano et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954....6G/abstract). Other state-of-the-art photometric pipelines, such as SCONE, can classify six distinct supernova types with $75\%$ accuracy on the very night of discovery, and reach $>99\%$ accuracy for fully sampled simulated SN Ia light curves [Qu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240604529Q/abstract).

Beyond photometry, machine learning has also revolutionized the classification of transient spectra. The DASH (Deep Automated Supernova and Host classifier) software leverages deep CNNs to concurrently classify supernova type, age, redshift, and host galaxy from spectral data [Muthukrishna et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...885...85M/abstract). By framing spectral analysis as a feature-learning problem rather than iterative template matching, CNNs can classify hundreds of objects in seconds, decoupling inference speed from the size of the training library.

The reliance of rapid transient classifiers on host-galaxy context strictly requires the automated, real-time association of a transient with its host. Algorithms such as DELIGHT (Deep Learning Identification of Galaxy Hosts of Transients) address this by processing multiresolution image cutouts centered on a transient candidate through a CNN regression model [Förster et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AJ....164..195F/abstract). The network outputs 2D offset vectors pointing to the center of the predicted host. By using multiresolution inputs, the model accurately associates transients with both extended ($10^{\prime\prime} < r < 60^{\prime\prime}$) and compact ($r \le 10^{\prime\prime}$) hosts while compressing the required data bandwidth—a critical optimization for live alert streams. DELIGHT has demonstrated extremely low contamination rates ($<0.86\%$) in recovering cross-matched redshifts [Förster et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AJ....164..195F/abstract), seamlessly feeding contextual features into downstream [[automated-galaxy-classification|Automated galaxy classification]] and transient typing pipelines. Together, these machine learning frameworks drastically reduce the human visual inspection load, ensuring that observatories can confidently trigger rapid multi-messenger follow-up observations for rare and scientifically critical events [Shandonay et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...925...44S/abstract).

## Galaxy Clusters and Multimessenger Observables

Galaxy clusters are powerful cosmological probes, but extracting their total mass is notoriously difficult due to the complex, non-linear nature of baryonic feedback within the intracluster medium. The application of [[machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine Learning in Astronomy]] offers a robust framework for inferring galaxy cluster mass while mitigating these complex astrophysical systematics.

In the X-ray regime, CNNs trained on mock Chandra observations can estimate galaxy cluster mass with an intrinsic scatter of just $8-12\%$ and a bias of $-0.02$ dex [Ntampaka et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...876...82N/abstract). Saliency mapping techniques reveal that these networks naturally learn to excise the highly scattered, AGN-dominated central cores to focus on more reliable large-scale features [Ntampaka et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...876...82N/abstract). Similarly, deep learning applied to optical member galaxy kinematics—using 1D line-of-sight velocities or 2D projected radial distances—achieves scatters as low as $0.132$ dex, outperforming traditional $M-\sigma$ scaling relations by more than a factor of two [Ho et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887...25H/abstract). In optical weak-lensing surveys, multilayered CNNs can perform mass reconstruction for galaxy clusters, outperforming traditional inversion methods in suppressing noise and boundary artifacts [Hong et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923..266H/abstract).

Deep learning is extensively applied to microwave observations, particularly the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect. CNNs can infer cluster masses directly from Planck SZ maps without assuming spherical symmetry or hydrostatic equilibrium [de Andres et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022EPJWC.25700013D/abstract). Furthermore, U-Net architectures and other deep emulators can rapidly generate high-fidelity thermal and kinematic SZ maps, as well as X-ray surface brightness maps, directly from dark-matter-only simulations [Thiele et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..129T/abstract), [Caro et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241004229C/abstract). Additionally, frameworks utilizing the Three Hundred project have trained machine learning models to effectively populate dark-matter-only halos with realistic baryonic properties, such as intracluster gas and stellar distributions, further circumventing the computational expense of full hydrodynamical runs [de Andres et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518..111D/abstract). These networks successfully learn to "paint" electron pressure and density onto dark matter halos without the need for extensive hydrodynamic models.

In optical imaging, Intracluster Light (ICL) and Brightest Cluster Galaxies (BCGs) provide a fossil record of cluster assembly. Because ICL has an exceptionally low surface brightness, traditional measurement methods require heavy manual intervention. Fully supervised CNNs can now automatically measure ICL fractions in survey images in a matter of seconds [Canepa et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..245C/abstract). In simulated phase space, algorithms like Random Forests can effectively disentangle the dynamically distinct stellar populations of the ICL and the BCG using features such as normalized cluster-centric distance and rest-frame velocity [Marini et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3082M/abstract).

Beyond mass estimation, neural networks can extract cosmological parameters directly from cluster observables. Machine learning models trained on multi-cosmology hydrodynamical simulations have demonstrated the ability to infer fundamental parameters like the matter density $\Omega_m$ and clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$ directly from integrated properties such as cluster gas mass and luminosity [Qiu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230409142Q/abstract). They are even capable of distinguishing the subtle morphological imprints of dark matter self-interactions from the confounding effects of baryonic feedback [Harvey et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240517566H/abstract). Despite these successes, models trained on one set of subgrid physics (e.g., the Magneticum simulation) often exhibit degraded performance or unexpected biases when evaluated on different hydrodynamical models (e.g., IllustrisTNG) or on real observational data [Ntampaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251009748N/abstract). Ensuring that deep learning architectures are robust against these specific astrophysical uncertainties remains a primary hurdle before they can be routinely applied to precision cosmology.

## Self-Supervised Learning and Generative Models

The exponential growth of data from modern sky surveys has made relying exclusively on human-annotated training sets increasingly intractable. To overcome the bottleneck of labeled data, astrophysical applications of [[machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine Learning in Astronomy]] are increasingly adopting self-supervised learning, unsupervised representation learning, and generative modeling. These frameworks allow neural networks to learn intrinsic, physically meaningful representations directly from raw, unlabeled pixel or spectral data.

### Self-Supervised and Contrastive Learning
Self-supervised learning (SSL) constructs supervisory signals from the data itself, entirely bypassing the need for manual labels. Contrastive learning, a prominent SSL paradigm, maps images into a lower-dimensional embedding space such that augmented views of the same object are pulled together while distinct objects are pushed apart. In astrophysics, incorporating domain-specific augmentations—such as simulated observational noise, point-spread function (PSF) blurring, and galactic dust attenuation—allows models to learn highly robust feature representations [Abul Hayat et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210104293A/abstract). 

Contrastive learning frameworks applied to multi-band Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) photometry have matched or exceeded the performance of fully supervised methods in downstream tasks like [[automated-galaxy-classification|Automated galaxy classification]] and photometric redshift estimation, while requiring factors of $2$ to $4$ fewer labeled examples [Hayat et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...911L..33H/abstract). Recent architectures have also fused vision transformers with contrastive convolutional networks to capture both spatial and multi-scale geometric features, achieving morphological classification accuracies exceeding $94\%$ using predominantly unlabeled data [Wei et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASP..134k4508W/abstract). Contrastive methods are also being used beyond optical imaging; for instance, Nearest Neighbour Contrastive Learning (NNCLR) has been deployed to organize the continuous structural variations of X-ray galaxy clusters in the TNG-Cluster simulations [Chadayammuri et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241022416C/abstract) and to infer the merger and assembly histories of galaxies from mock images [Eisert et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260119148E/abstract).

### Autoencoders and Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)
Autoencoders and Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) compress high-dimensional inputs into a compact latent space, forcing the network to isolate the most salient features governing the data distribution. VAEs have proven particularly powerful for characterizing irregular or complex structures that defy standard visual taxonomies. For example, VAEs applied to JWST/NIRCam imaging of high-redshift ($z > 2$) galaxies successfully bypass orientation and background biases, clustering galaxies into novel, physically motivated morphological classes that traditional low-redshift Hubble sequence nomenclature fails to adequately capture [Tohill et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962..164T/abstract). 

In spectral analysis, specialized autoencoders like SPENDER compress rest-frame galaxy spectra into low-dimensional latent spaces while explicitly modeling redshift and instrumental resolution transformations. This allows for highly accurate reconstructions and de-noising of large spectral databases without prior feature engineering [Melchior et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....166...74M/abstract). 

### Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and Diffusion Models
Generative models, particularly Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), are heavily utilized to synthesize high-fidelity mock data. This is especially vital for mitigating the severe class imbalances that plague the detection of rare objects. By augmenting training sets with GAN-generated images, researchers have drastically improved the precision and detection rates of rare morphological phenomena, such as ring galaxies [Krishnakumar et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221011428K/abstract), [Krishnakumar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..191K/abstract) and strong gravitational lenses [Keerthi Vasan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5368K/abstract).

Beyond dataset augmentation, generative models are critical for creating synthetic galaxy catalogs and emulating complex baryonic physics at a fraction of the traditional computational cost. Within the CAMELS project, GANs have been used to generate realistic mock observables directly from underlying parameter spaces, capturing non-linear cosmological dependencies [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...915...71V/abstract). More recently, the field has seen a shift toward deep generative models utilizing diffusion processes and equivariant Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). These models are capable of learning complex symmetries—such as the $SO(3) \times \mathbb{R}^n$ distributions governing 3D galaxy orientations and intrinsic alignments—allowing for the robust population of large-scale dark matter simulations with highly realistic, correlated baryonic tracers [Jagvaral et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240918761J/abstract).

To facilitate the continuous improvement of both self-supervised and generative models, standardized benchmarks are increasingly critical. The GAMMA (Galactic Attributes of Mass, Metallicity, and Age) dataset has been introduced specifically for this purpose, providing a rigorous benchmark for scientific machine learning tasks involving 2D maps and 3D data cubes of galactic properties [Çakır et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231206016C/abstract).

## Interpretability, Uncertainty, and Domain Adaptation

While neural networks have proven highly effective for tasks such as parameter estimation and [[automated-galaxy-classification|Automated galaxy classification]], their utility in rigorous scientific inference is often limited by their "black box" nature. To extract reliable astrophysical constraints, models must not only be accurate but also provide formal error budgets, interpretability, and robust generalization capabilities.

**Uncertainty Quantification**
Standard multi-layer perceptrons and convolutional neural networks typically output deterministic point estimates, lacking the uncertainty margins required for precision cosmology. Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs) address this limitation by replacing deterministic weights with probability distributions, thereby yielding posterior probability distributions for their predictions. For example, BNNs have been successfully deployed for photometric redshift (photo-$z$) estimation, where they output a Gaussian probability distribution characterized by a mean $z$ and standard deviation $\sigma_z$. This approach satisfies stringent statistical coverage tests and reduces catastrophic outlier rates for upcoming large-scale surveys [Jones et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..130J/abstract), [Jones et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220207121J/abstract). To further guarantee reliable coverage, modern frameworks incorporate "conformal predictions," mathematically calibrating BNN outputs to yield statistically rigorous uncertainty intervals [Jones et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..159J/abstract). In spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting, techniques like Amortized Neural Posterior Estimation (ANPE) accelerate Bayesian inference by factors of $\sim 10^3$ to $10^6$ while maintaining the fidelity of traditional Markov Chain Monte Carlo posterior distributions [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract).

**Explainable AI (XAI)**
Alongside uncertainty quantification, Explainable AI (XAI) techniques are increasingly utilized to interpret the physical relationships learned by deep learning models. Tools such as Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) and saliency maps allow researchers to visualize the specific pixel-level features driving a network's decision. For instance, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Maps (Grad-CAM) have been used to identify which optical morphological features are most strongly associated with a galaxy's neutral hydrogen (H I) gas mass fraction [Wu et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...900..142W/abstract) and to pinpoint structural signatures of major and minor galaxy mergers [Schechter et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251115006S/abstract). LIME has been similarly applied to validate that deep learning classifiers of radio galaxy morphologies isolate the same physical features prioritized by human experts [Tang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230703453T/abstract). Furthermore, novel architectures like Sparse Feature Networks are being developed to produce inherently interpretable, linearly combinable features without sacrificing predictive accuracy [Wu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..183W/abstract), [Wu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250100089W/abstract).

**Domain Adaptation**
As previously noted across various subfields, a major ongoing challenge in [[machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine Learning in Astronomy]] is "domain shift." Due to the lack of vast, perfectly labeled observational datasets, models are frequently trained on synthetic data from cosmological hydrodynamic simulations (e.g., IllustrisTNG, SIMBA, or the CAMELS suite). However, the literature reveals significant tensions regarding how well these models generalize. Networks often "overspecialize" to the specific subgrid physics or noise properties of their training set, suffering catastrophic performance drops when applied to simulations with different underlying physics or to real survey data [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract), [Perez et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954...11P/abstract), [Ntampaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251009748N/abstract). For instance, recent studies evaluating the ASTRID model highlight the tantalizing potential of determining cosmological parameters from the properties of a single galaxy; however, these highly precise models face severe robustness and domain shift challenges when their predictions are cross-validated against independent suites like IllustrisTNG [Echeverri-Rojas et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954..125E/abstract).

To bridge the gap between simulations and observations, researchers employ domain adaptation techniques. Approaches such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) and Domain Adversarial Neural Networks (DANN) are used to force the network to learn a domain-invariant latent space. By penalizing the network if it relies on features unique to the source domain (e.g., simulation artifacts), adversarial training significantly improves cross-domain robustness [Ćiprijanović et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201103591C/abstract), [Roncoli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231101588R/abstract), [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240902256L/abstract). Despite these advances, ensuring true domain-independence—where a model trained on simulations can flawlessly process real observational data—remains a highly active and unresolved debate in the field.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

As astrophysics enters an era of petabyte-scale surveys, big data scalability is a paramount concern for [[machine-learning-in-astronomy|Machine Learning in Astronomy]]. Facilities such as the Vera C. Rubin Observatory (Rubin-LSST) and the Euclid mission will detect billions of galaxies and millions of transients, necessitating robust pipelines for [[large-scale-galaxy-classification|Large-scale galaxy classification]] and [[automated-galaxy-classification|Automated galaxy classification]] [Broussard et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922..153B/abstract), [Tohill et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916....4T/abstract). While current neural network architectures offer computational speedups of $10^3$ (three orders of magnitude) over traditional methods [Li et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929..152L/abstract), deploying them consistently across distributed, massive observational data sets remains a significant engineering challenge.

A critical tension in current research, as highlighted throughout various subfields, is the problem of simulation-to-observation bias, also known as domain shift. Because models often overspecialize to the specific subgrid physics of their training simulations (e.g., IllustrisTNG, SIMBA), they can fail dramatically on disparate datasets [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract), [Ntampaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251009748N/abstract). Developing true domain-independent inference is a highly active area of research. Proposed solutions include domain adaptation techniques—such as Domain Adaptive Graph Neural Networks (DA-GNNs) and Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) optimization—which attempt to force networks to extract only domain-invariant physical features [Ćiprijanović et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201103591C/abstract), [Roncoli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231101588R/abstract).

Model robustness is further challenged by the discovery of novel object classes at new observational frontiers. For example, recent observations by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have demonstrated that the conventional Hubble sequence nomenclature is largely inadequate for the highly irregular morphologies of galaxies at high redshifts ($z > 5$) [Tohill et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962..164T/abstract), [Tohill et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230211482T/abstract). This inadequacy necessitates a shift toward unsupervised and self-supervised machine learning techniques (such as contrastive learning) that are capable of discovering physically relevant morphological clusters from raw pixels, without relying on biased, low-redshift human taxonomies [Tohill et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962..164T/abstract), [Hayat et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...911L..33H/abstract). 

Finally, several theoretical and practical open questions remain unresolved. The automated detection of extremely rare objects—such as dual active galactic nuclei (AGNs) or highly specific merger stages—is hindered by severely imbalanced training datasets where rare classes are vastly underrepresented [Krishnakumar et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221011428K/abstract). Furthermore, while neural networks provide exceptional parameter constraints, extracting physical meaning from their latent spaces is non-trivial. Ensuring that deep learning architectures are fully interpretable will be essential to confirm that models are learning true physical mechanisms, rather than exploiting numerical artifacts or spurious correlations in the training data [Ntampaka et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926...45N/abstract). Addressing these challenges will ultimately define the theoretical limit of cosmological parameter precision achievable by field-level machine learning inference.
