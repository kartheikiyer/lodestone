The **Paα emission line** (Paschen-alpha), located at a rest-frame vacuum wavelength of 1.875 μm, is a prominent near-infrared hydrogen recombination transition originating from the $n=4$ to $n=3$ atomic energy level of the [[paschen-series|Paschen series]]. Produced primarily in the ionized gas of H II regions and the accretion environments of active galactic nuclei (AGN), Paα is a fundamental, direct tracer of a system's intrinsic ionizing radiation field. Its defining astrophysical advantage is its extremely low susceptibility to interstellar dust extinction compared to optical [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]]. In deeply enshrouded environments where traditional [[h-alpha-astronomy|H-alpha Astronomy]] and [[optical-emission-line-diagnostics|Optical Emission-Line Diagnostics]] are severely attenuated or rendered completely opaque, near-infrared Paα photons can effectively penetrate dense dust columns. By comparing observed Paα fluxes with heavily attenuated optical measurements to anchor [[steep-balmer-decrements|Steep Balmer decrements]], astronomers can robustly quantify line-of-sight visual extinction ($A_V$) and derive intrinsic, dust-corrected line luminosities.

Historically hindered by telluric water vapor absorption in the Earth's atmosphere, the advent of space-based near-infrared surveys [Böker et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJS..124...95B/abstract) and adaptive optics-assisted spectroscopy has established Paα as a premier observational tool. It is widely utilized as a "gold standard" to calibrate mid-infrared star formation rate (SFR) indicators—such as the 24 μm dust continuum and [[ne-ii-emission-lines|[Ne II] emission lines]]—in dusty Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs) [Tateuchi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..217....1T/abstract). Beyond tracing star formation, Paα is highly effective at identifying dust-obscured AGN by revealing "hidden" broad-line regions and compact nuclear disks that suffer from severe host-galaxy dilution in the optical. Within our own Milky Way, high-resolution Paα imaging has been instrumental in mapping the complex sub-parsec kinematics around the supermassive black hole Sagittarius A*, resolving the ionized "minispiral" and cataloging massive, wind-driving stars in an environment obscured by over 30 magnitudes of visual extinction [Scoville et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...594..294S/abstract), [Wang et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.402..895W/abstract).

In the era of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), Paα studies have expanded from the local universe to "Cosmic Noon," where the redshifted line allows for spatially resolved kinematic and SFR mapping of early galaxies. However, the application of Paα across diverse physical scales and cosmic epochs has unveiled significant complexities. This article reviews the fundamental physics and observational milestones of Paα, while exploring current literature tensions, such as scale-dependent discrepancies in hybrid SFR calibrations. Furthermore, it details ongoing open questions regarding the rapid dust-clearing timescales of emerging star clusters, the debated origins of broad Paα wings in certain starbursts, and the fundamental limitations of the line in the most extreme high-redshift ultraluminous infrared galaxies, where up to 90% of star formation may remain deeply buried and invisible even to near-infrared tracers.

## Fundamental Physical Properties and Atomic Context

The Pa$\alpha$ emission line is a fundamental hydrogen recombination feature originating from the $n=4$ to $n=3$ atomic transition of the [[paschen-series|Paschen series]]. The vacuum rest wavelength of this transition is governed by the Rydberg formula:
$$ \frac{1}{\lambda} = R_\text{H} \left( \frac{1}{3^2} - \frac{1}{4^2} \right) $$
where $R_\text{H}$ is the Rydberg constant. This yields a wavelength of $\lambda \approx 1.875\ \mu\text{m}$, placing the line squarely in the near-infrared regime. The emission is primarily produced in ionized gas clouds (H II regions) where neutral hydrogen is photoionized by extreme ultraviolet radiation ($E > 13.6$ eV) from massive young stars or active galactic nuclei. As free electrons recombine with protons, they cascade down the quantized energy levels of the hydrogen atom. Under standard Case B recombination conditions—assuming an optically thick Lyman continuum, an electron temperature $T_e \approx 10^4$ K, and an electron density $n_e \approx 10^2 - 10^4$ cm$^{-3}$—the intrinsic flux ratios of these recombination lines are fixed by quantum mechanical transition probabilities. This fixed intrinsic ratio makes the comparison between Pa$\alpha$ and optical lines an invaluable astrophysical tool. However, in the extreme environments of active galactic nuclei, high radiation densities in the broad-line region lead to large cloud column densities and anisotropic line emission, with most radiation emitted from the inner, irradiated face of the clouds [Ferland et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...387...95F/abstract).

The defining observational advantage of Pa$\alpha$ is its extremely low susceptibility to interstellar dust extinction compared to optical [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]] such as those utilized in conventional [[h-alpha-astronomy|H-alpha Astronomy]]. Because the extinction coefficient $A_\lambda$ decreases significantly at longer wavelengths, near-infrared Pa$\alpha$ photons can penetrate columns of dust that would completely obscure optical emission. For instance, in local Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs), the visual extinction toward star-forming regions averages $A_V \sim 4.3$ mag [Tateuchi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..217....1T/abstract). In even more extreme environments, such as the ionized gas surrounding the Milky Way's supermassive black hole, the median visual extinction reaches $A_V = 31.1$ mag, with local variations ranging from 20 to 50 mag [Scoville et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...594..294S/abstract). In such heavily obscured environments, optical lines are entirely suppressed, and near-infrared transitions like Pa$\alpha$ (or the $n=5 \rightarrow 4$ [[brackett-alpha-line|Brackett-alpha line]]) become essential for tracing the kinematics and spatial distribution of the ionized gas. 

By comparing the observed H$\alpha$/Pa$\alpha$ flux ratio to its theoretical Case B value, researchers can derive the degree of reddening and precisely measure the line-of-sight dust extinction. This method is particularly critical in targets exhibiting [[steep-balmer-decrements|Steep Balmer decrements]], where the H$\beta$ line is too heavily attenuated to serve as a reliable anchor for traditional [[optical-emission-line-diagnostics|Optical Emission-Line Diagnostics]]. However, in objects such as Seyfert 1 galaxies with steep Balmer decrements, Pa$\alpha$ fluxes can also deviate sharply from standard recombination theory, necessitating the measurement of at least two upper-level lines for robust reddening diagnostics [McAlary et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...301..105M/abstract). Note that in objects like the Seyfert 1.9 galaxy V Zwicky 317, Pa$\alpha$ fluxes significantly lower than Case B predictions suggest that radiative transfer effects, rather than dust alone, can drive these steep Balmer decrements [Rudy et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...267L..69R/abstract). Spatially resolved studies comparing Hubble Space Telescope Pa$\alpha$ and H$\alpha$ imaging have further demonstrated that the luminosity-weighted mean extinctions of local H II regions scale directly with local gas surface density and metallicity [Quillen et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....121.2095Q/abstract). This diagnostic utility extends into the early universe; in the gravitationally lensed submillimeter galaxy SMM J163554.2+661225 at $z = 2.515$, the H$\alpha$/Pa$\alpha$ ratio successfully constrained the nebular extinction to $A_V = 3.6 \pm 0.4$ mag [Papovich et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704.1506P/abstract), demonstrating the line's capability as a universal tracer of obscured star formation and AGN activity.

## Historical Development and Observational Milestones

Observing the Paschen-$\alpha$ transition ($\lambda = 1.875\ \mu\mathrm{m}$) of the [[paschen-series|Paschen series]] from the ground was historically hindered by deep telluric water vapor absorption in the Earth's atmosphere. Early ground-based NIR spectroscopy of Pa$\alpha$ was therefore largely restricted to targets at sufficient redshift to shift the line into cleaner atmospheric windows, such as the $K$-band. For example, early long-slit observations successfully detected both broad and narrow Pa$\alpha$ components in the $z=0.083$ infrared luminous galaxy IRAS 14348-1447, enabling the early kinematic characterization of its obscured active nucleus [Nakajima et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...373..452N/abstract).

The installation of HST/NICMOS bypassed atmospheric opacity entirely, inaugurating a new era of high-resolution Pa$\alpha$ imaging. A major observational milestone was the NICMOS Snapshot Survey of nearby galaxies [Böker et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJS..124...95B/abstract), which systematically mapped Pa$\alpha$ emission in 94 local galaxies using the F187N and F190N narrowband filters. By providing unprecedented $0.2''$ spatial resolution, this survey firmly established Pa$\alpha$ as a premier tracer of obscured star formation. Furthermore, NICMOS observations were instrumental in discovering compact nuclear structures that were heavily extincted and completely invisible in optical [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]]. Notable discoveries included a $40\ \mathrm{pc}$ diameter nuclear disk of ionized gas in Centaurus A [Schreier et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...499L.143S/abstract) and the detailed "minispiral" structure around Sagittarius A* in the Galactic Center, where the median extinction reaches $A_V = 31.1\ \mathrm{mag}$ [Scoville et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...594..294S/abstract).

In parallel, advancements in adaptive optics and ground-based NIR spectroscopy enabled detailed kinematic studies from Earth. The advent of near-infrared integral field spectrographs allowed researchers to move beyond simple integrated line ratios. Utilizing instruments like the Palomar Integral Field Spectrograph, spatially resolved Pa$\alpha$ observations facilitated composite modeling of complex merger kinematics, revealing phenomena such as young tidal tails, massive outflows, and dual kinematical axes [Murphy et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...559..201M/abstract). Later, instruments such as VLT/SINFONI and VLT/ISAAC extended this capability; for instance, deep integral field spectroscopy revealed warped Pa$\alpha$ disks and complex velocity fields in heavily interacting systems such as the Super-antennae [Reunanen et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.382..951R/abstract).

The development of Pa$\alpha$ as a quantitative diagnostic was further solidified through synergistic observations with mid- and far-infrared space observatories. Data from missions equipped with high-resolution spectrometers, such as ISO/SWS, were crucial for characterizing the broader infrared spectral energy distributions and fine-structure lines of obscured active galaxies. Extensive data from the Spitzer Space Telescope relied heavily on Pa$\alpha$ to anchor mid-infrared SFR tracers, such as the $24\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ dust continuum and the $3.3\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ PAH feature. These coordinated efforts demonstrated a tight correlation between Pa$\alpha$ fluxes and bolometric infrared luminosity, cementing Pa$\alpha$ as the gold standard for SFR calibration in dusty Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs) where traditional [[optical-emission-line-diagnostics|Optical Emission-Line Diagnostics]] fail [Tateuchi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..217....1T/abstract), [Díaz-Santos et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...685..211D/abstract).

## Tracing Star Formation in Dust-Obscured Environments

Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs) and Ultraluminous Infrared Galaxies (ULIRGs) are characterized by intense starburst activity that is heavily enshrouded by dust. In these environments, traditional optical tracers such as those used in [[h-alpha-astronomy|H-alpha Astronomy]] are severely attenuated. Because dust corrections derived from optical [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]] often fail in deeply buried regions, the [[paschen-series|Paschen series]] recombination line Pa$\alpha$ ($1.875\ \mu\text{m}$) serves as a critical, extinction-insensitive alternative for mapping H II regions and measuring the star formation rate (SFR) [Tateuchi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..217....1T/abstract), [Herrero-Illana et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...786..156H/abstract). 

Because of its reliability, Pa$\alpha$ is extensively used to calibrate mid-infrared (MIR) SFR indicators, including the $21\ \mu\text{m}$ and $24\ \mu\text{m}$ dust continuum and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) features. Notably, the $3.28\ \mu\text{m}$ PAH emission feature serves as a robust discriminator between star formation and Seyfert activity in integrated galactic spectra [Moorwood et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986A&A...166....4M/abstract). Robust star formation rate (SFR) calibrations have also been established using ionic fine-structure lines and the [[c-ii-158-m-line|[C II] 158 μm line]] [Mordini et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A..36M/abstract). High spatial resolution imaging of LIRGs demonstrates that the [[ne-ii-emission-lines|[Ne II] emission lines]] at $12.81\ \mu\text{m}$ exhibit a tight, linear correlation (slope $\approx 1.00$) with Pa$\alpha$ surface density, independent of the Pa$\alpha$ equivalent width or the age of the ionizing stellar populations [Díaz-Santos et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711..328D/abstract). In contrast, the relationship between the $3.3\ \mu\text{m}$ PAH luminosity and the SFR traced by the [[brackett-alpha-line|Brackett-alpha line]] (Br$\alpha$) is sublinear, characterized by a power-law index of $\sim 0.75$, with studies noting an unexplained discrepancy between the relations derived from Br$\alpha$ and Pa$\alpha$ [Gregg et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971..115G/abstract). This PAH relationship exhibits scatter driven by variations in local PAH heating and stochastic sampling of the stellar initial mass function at intermediate luminosities [Gregg et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971..115G/abstract).

The literature highlights a significant tension regarding the scale dependence of these MIR calibrations. The scaling constant for hybrid SFR indicators (e.g., combining H$\alpha$ and $24\ \mu\text{m}$ emission) is observed to be 3 to 5 times larger for individual, compact H II regions than for integrated whole-galaxy measurements [Calzetti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971..118C/abstract), [Calzetti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991..198C/abstract). This discrepancy arises because, on larger galactic scales, stellar populations older than $\sim 5\text{--}6$ Myr contribute substantially to diffuse dust heating, enhancing the MIR continuum relative to the instantaneous Pa$\alpha$ emission [Calzetti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971..118C/abstract), [Calzetti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991..198C/abstract). Consequently, the $L(24\ \mu\text{m})$-$L(\text{Pa}\alpha)$ relationship is intrinsically nonlinear across the six decades of luminosity that separate individual star clusters from luminous galaxies [Calzetti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991..198C/abstract). 

In some unique environments, star formation feedback and clearing mechanisms are highly complex. For example, in the low-metallicity galaxy SBS 0335-052E, high-density stellar winds in super star clusters can absorb ionizing photons, effectively quenching photoionization feedback and delaying the clearing of natal gas [Thompson et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...638..176T/abstract). Furthermore, in certain ultra-luminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs) such as IRAS F11119+3257, very narrow Pa$\alpha$ components with a full width at half-maximum (FWHM) of $\sim 260$ km s$^{-1}$ have been detected, likely originating from heavily obscured star formation [Pan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...883..173P/abstract). Mid-infrared spectroscopy of these deeply enshrouded ULIRGs commonly reveals strong water ice absorption (6-8 μm), a feature that is often weak or completely absent in classical Seyfert galaxies, reflecting their exceptionally high molecular gas columns [Spoon et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...385.1022S/abstract). In other extreme systems, such as the prototypical ULIRG Arp 220, broad recombination lines (FWHM $\sim 1300$ km s$^{-1}$) have been detected via the [[brackett-alpha-line|Brackett-alpha line]], suggesting underlying Seyfert-like activity or extreme kinematics [Depoy et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJ...316L..63D/abstract). Additionally, longer-wavelength radio recombination lines like H53$\alpha$ in M82 provide an extinction-independent measure of the ionization rate, implying profound dust extinctions reaching $A_V \sim 27$ mag in the starburst core [Puxley et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ApJ...345..163P/abstract).

Beyond the local universe, Pa$\alpha$ is an essential tool for mapping star formation in high-redshift environments. For instance, in the $z = 2.515$ lensed galaxy SMM J163554.2+661225, Pa$\alpha$ luminosities yield a dust-corrected instantaneous SFR of $171 \pm 28\ M_{\odot}\text{ yr}^{-1}$ and an extinction of $A_V = 3.6 \pm 0.4$ mag [Papovich et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704.1506P/abstract). However, Pa$\alpha$ faces a fundamental limitation in the most extreme high-redshift ULIRGs. In systems like GN20 at $z = 4.05$, the SFR derived from Pa$\alpha$ accounts for only $\sim 7.7\%$ of the total infrared-derived SFR. This discrepancy indicates extreme nuclear attenuations (up to $A_V \approx 44$ mag), implying that nearly 90% of the star formation in such extreme environments remains deeply "buried" and invisible even to near-infrared recombination lines [Bik et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A...3B/abstract).

## Probing the Nuclei of Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN)

Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) residing in gas-rich, dusty environments often possess heavily obscured central engines. In these "hidden" AGN, optical recombination lines and standard [[optical-emission-line-diagnostics|Optical Emission-Line Diagnostics]] frequently fail to detect the central accreting supermassive black hole due to severe dust extinction and host-galaxy dilution [Balmaverde et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...549A.144B/abstract), [Zuther et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007A&A...466..451Z/abstract). Pa$\alpha$ provides a highly effective diagnostic tool to bypass this obscuration and probe the innermost regions of active galaxies.

The detection of a broad Pa$\alpha$ component (e.g., FWHM $> 2500$ km s$^{-1}$) is a primary diagnostic for identifying a hidden Broad Line Region (BLR) in sources otherwise classified as Type 2 Seyferts [Nakajima et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...373..452N/abstract). Similarly, detections of broad Paschen-$\beta$ ([[pa-emission-line|Pa$\beta$]]) components in galaxies like NGC 5506 resolved earlier controversies over hidden broad-line regions in Seyfert 2s [Blanco et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990MNRAS.242P...4B/abstract). For example, in the "protoquasar" PKS 1549-79, Pa$\alpha$ spectroscopy reveals a broad component with an FWHM of $1745 \pm 40$ km s$^{-1}$ originating from a central source hidden behind $A_V \sim 3$--$7$ magnitudes of visual extinction [Bellamy et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.344L..80B/abstract). In some cases, Pa$\alpha$ emission can even resolve complex BLR kinematics; in the local Seyfert 1 galaxy III Zw 002, Pa$\alpha$ exhibits a broad, double-peaked profile characteristic of a rotating accretion disk [Dias dos Santos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953L...3D/abstract). This builds on earlier foundational work; for example, optical and near-infrared double-peaked profiles in Arp 102B provide direct kinematic evidence for relativistic Keplerian rotation in the central engine [Chen et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ApJ...339..742C/abstract). Beyond broad-line profiles, time-domain studies such as He II lag measurements provide complementary evidence that high-ionization emission originates from gas in virial motion within the innermost radii of the active nucleus [Grier et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...744L...4G/abstract). Furthermore, Pa$\alpha$ observations of IRAS 23060+0505 show that line widths can increase at longer wavelengths, with Pa$\alpha$ FWHMs reaching $\sim 2000$ km s$^{-1}$, revealing obscured broad-line regions where optical lines show only narrow components [Nakajima et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...375L...1N/abstract). Conversely, in objects like Cygnus A, Pa$\alpha$ measurements have been used to derive a narrow-line region reddening of $E(B-V) \approx 0.85$, finding no evidence for a significant obscured broad-line component [Lilly et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJ...315L.103L/abstract), and broad Pa$\alpha$ flux limits imply a minimum extinction of $A_V > 24$ mag toward the broad line region, which is consistent with the massive gas columns inferred from X-ray observations [Ward et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...382..115W/abstract). Similarly, Pa$\alpha$ measurements in the broad-line radio galaxy 3C 234 show that heavy reddening is necessary to explain observed hydrogen line ratios, despite the narrow-line region appearing only slightly reddened [Carleton et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApJ...284..523C/abstract).

However, the literature remains divided on whether broad Pa$\alpha$ wings in ULIRGs are uniquely indicative of a BLR. In some powerful starbursts, such as IRAS 07598+6508, the presence of a broad Pa$\alpha$ component ($\mathrm{FWHM} \sim 3900$ km s$^{-1}$) has been interpreted as evidence of an AGN, but it may alternatively be produced by extreme supernova-driven winds or starburst outflows [Taniguchi et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994AJ....107.1668T/abstract).

To disambiguate these scenarios, Pa$\alpha$ is frequently analyzed alongside near-infrared "coronal lines" such as [Si VI] 1.962 $\mu$m. Because species like Si$^{5+}$ require a hard ionizing continuum (ionization potential of $167$ eV) that is absent in star-forming regions, the spatial coincidence of Pa$\alpha$ and [Si VI] emission strongly confirms AGN photoionization [Murphy et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AJ....120.1675M/abstract). A broad near-infrared coronal line survey further confirmed that [Si VI] emission is exclusively related to Seyfert activity and photoionized by the central AGN engine [Marconi et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994A&A...291...18M/abstract). Furthermore, mid-infrared AGN lines such as [[ne-ii-emission-lines|[Ne II]]] 12.81 μm exhibit a strong Baldwin effect, characterized by an anticorrelation between line equivalent width and continuum luminosity [Hönig et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...485L..21H/abstract). Furthermore, mid-infrared [[diagnostic-emission-line-ratios|ionic line ratios]], such as [[neon-emission-lines|[Ne III]]]/[Ne II] and [[oxygen-spectral-lines|[O IV]]]/[Ne III], act as powerful diagnostics to distinguish AGN from pure star-forming galaxies in heavily dust-obscured environments [Weaver et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...716.1151W/abstract). Using integral field spectroscopy (IFS) of these lines, researchers can map out AGN ionization cones and decouple them from circumnuclear star formation. 

Near-infrared analysis of the QSO I Zw 1 illustrates this interplay, suggesting that the nuclear continuum is a mixture of a QSO nucleus and an extincted stellar component, with the latter contributing up to 20% of the flux at 2.2 $\mu$m [Eckart et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...424..627E/abstract). The 1-μm continuum in these active nuclei is often dominated by the accretion disc, enabling the use of near-infrared virial products to estimate AGN black hole masses [Landt et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414..218L/abstract). Strong near-infrared continuum bumps, such as those seen in Mrk 1239 peaking at 2.2 μm, provide direct evidence of dust heated to near sublimation temperatures ($\sim 1200$ K) within the dusty torus [Rodríguez-Ardila et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.367L..57R/abstract). Additionally, observations of the extended host environment can reveal hidden luminous sources; for example, the 22 kpc 'X'-shaped morphology of the anisotropic ionizing radiation field in IC 5063 exposes a hidden luminous active nucleus despite heavy central dust obscuration [Colina et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...370..102C/abstract). Furthermore, high-resolution Pa$\alpha$ imaging has proven essential for resolving the compact structural morphology of galactic nuclei. Space-based observations have uncovered highly obscured, sub-kiloparsec features, including an inclined, $\sim 40$ pc diameter nuclear disk of ionized gas at the core of Centaurus A [Schreier et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...499L.143S/abstract). 

## Mapping the Galactic Center and Sgr A*

Mapping the complex nuclear environment of the Milky Way is severely hindered at optical wavelengths due to extreme line-of-sight dust extinction. With a median visual extinction of $A_V = 31.1$ mag toward the Galactic Center, optical [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]] are entirely obscured [Scoville et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...594..294S/abstract). Consequently, Paschen-alpha emission serves as a premier, high-resolution tracer of ionized gas dynamics and massive stellar populations in the immediate vicinity of the supermassive black hole, Sgr A*.

The HST/NICMOS Paschen-$\alpha$ Survey of the Galactic Centre has provided an unprecedented panoramic view of this region, mapping the central parsec with a spatial resolution of $\sim 0.01$ pc [Wang et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.402..895W/abstract). A primary feature traced by this survey is the "minispiral," a structure of thermally emitting ionized gas orbiting Sgr A*. High-resolution mapping resolves previously diffuse thermal features in the minispiral into fine, linear filaments. The free thermal expansion time of these structures is remarkably short, estimated at $\sim 3000$ yr, implying that they must be actively sculpted and confined by local magnetic fields or the powerful winds of nearby massive stars [Scoville et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...594..294S/abstract), [Wang et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.402..895W/abstract). 

In addition to mapping the interstellar medium, the survey demonstrates that Pa$\alpha$ emission is a robust diagnostic for identifying massive stars exhibiting strong, ionized mass-loss winds. Observations have cataloged dozens of such emission-line stars, revealing that roughly half of the newly discovered massive stars reside outside the established Arches, Quintuplet, and Central clusters [Wang et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.402..895W/abstract). Based on the extinction-corrected Pa$\alpha$ flux, the minimum Lyman continuum emission rate within a 40 arcsecond radius of Sgr A* is calculated to be $3.9 \times 10^{50} \text{ s}^{-1}$, corresponding to a continuous ionizing luminosity of $L_{\rm LyC} \approx 2.7 \times 10^6 L_\odot$ [Scoville et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...594..294S/abstract).

Tracing the kinematics of the Pa$\alpha$-emitting gas also allows for precise dynamical measurements of the central gravitational potential. The centroid of the Pa$\alpha$ emission, once corrected for extinction, confirms that Sgr A* is located within $\le 0.04$ pc of the dynamical center of the galaxy's stellar and gas distribution [Scoville et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...594..294S/abstract). Furthermore, Pa$\alpha$ and other near-infrared recombination lines have been critical in monitoring the Dusty S-cluster Object (DSO/G2) during its close peribothron passage around Sgr A*. While some models propose it is a coreless gas cloud undergoing tidal disruption, spatially compact hydrogen recombination emission observed at extreme radial velocities (up to $\sim -3320 \text{ km s}^{-1}$) strongly favors the interpretation of the DSO as a young, accreting star [Valencia-S. et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800..125V/abstract).

## Multi-wavelength Correlations and Scaling Relations

Because Pa$\alpha$ is largely insensitive to dust extinction—or can be robustly corrected via the [[steep-balmer-decrements|Balmer decrement]]—it serves as a baseline reference for calibrating other multi-wavelength SFR indicators [Tateuchi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..217....1T/abstract). 

**Radio Continuum and Free-Free Emission**
In star-forming regions, thermal free-free (bremsstrahlung) radio emission is physically linked to the same ionized gas that produces hydrogen recombination lines. High-resolution Pa$\alpha$ imaging can therefore be used as a direct spatial template for this thermal emission. By appropriately scaling and subtracting the Pa$\alpha$-derived free-free component from total radio continuum maps (e.g., at 8.4 GHz), the intrinsic non-thermal synchrotron power law can be isolated. This technique is critical for disentangling compact starbursts from obscured active galactic nuclei, as demonstrated in the circumnuclear ring of NGC 1614 [Herrero-Illana et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...786..156H/abstract).

**Millimeter Recombination Lines**
While Pa$\alpha$ offers critical near-infrared constraints, extending observations to even longer wavelengths provides further density diagnostics. Millimeter recombination lines like H30$\alpha$ in M82 suggest that most recombination emission occurs in dense star-forming clouds ($10^4$ cm$^{-3}$), while forbidden lines originate in lower density regions [Seaquist et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...429..612S/abstract). For instance, unbiased 1.3 mm line surveys of Arp 220 find that molecular line contributions account for 28% of the measured flux, though radio recombination lines remain elusive at these frequencies [Martín et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...527A..36M/abstract). Beyond thermal emission, there is also evidence for the first extragalactic hydrogen recombination line maser (e.g., in H26$\alpha$) towards the nuclear region of the starburst galaxy NGC 253. This maser emission can be identified using the spectral index between H30$\alpha$ and H26$\alpha$ [Báez-Rubio et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...867L...6B/abstract).

**Mid-Infrared Fine-Structure Lines**
Pa$\alpha$ exhibits a tight, linear correlation with several mid-infrared fine-structure lines. The surface density of [[ne-ii-emission-lines|[Ne II] emission lines]] at 12.81 $\mu$m scales directly with Pa$\alpha$ (power-law slope $1.00 \pm 0.02$) in LIRG star-forming regions [Díaz-Santos et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711..328D/abstract). Crucially, the $L([\mathrm{Ne~II}])/L(\mathrm{Pa}\alpha)$ ratio does not strongly depend on the Pa$\alpha$ equivalent width. This indicates that the 12.81 $\mu$m line is relatively insensitive to the age of the ionizing stellar population, validating it as a robust, independent tracer of the local SFR [Díaz-Santos et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711..328D/abstract).

**Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs)**
The relationship between Pa$\alpha$ and PAH emission is more complex and highly age-dependent. Recent high-resolution JWST observations reveal a tight but sublinear correlation (power-law index $\sim 0.75$) between the 3.3 $\mu$m PAH luminosity and the SFR traced by the [[brackett-alpha-line|Brackett-alpha line]] (Br$\alpha$) on scales of $\sim 40$ pc, noting an unexplained discrepancy when compared to Pa$\alpha$ [Gregg et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971..115G/abstract). At longer wavelengths, the $L_{8\mu\mathrm{m}}/L_{\mathrm{Pa}\alpha}$ ratio varies by up to a factor of 10 among individual H II regions. This scatter is primarily driven by the age of the stellar population (spanning roughly 4 to 7.5 Myr) rather than variations in metallicity [Díaz-Santos et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...685..211D/abstract). Similarly, the 11.3 $\mu$m PAH to Pa$\alpha$ ratio increases for regions with smaller Pa$\alpha$ equivalent widths, demonstrating that older stars contribute significantly to PAH excitation independent of the immediate ionizing flux [Díaz-Santos et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711..328D/abstract).

**Near-Infrared Continuum Excess**
In addition to recombination lines and PAH emission, deep surveys of high-redshift galaxies have detected a broad 2-5 μm near-infrared continuum excess. This excess likely originates from the integrated light of circumstellar disks around massive young stellar objects, serving as another complementary tracer of star formation in these systems [Mentuch et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...706.1020M/abstract).

**The $L$-SFR Relation and Hybrid Tracers**
As discussed above, Pa$\alpha$ is extensively used to anchor the $L-SFR$ relation for mid-infrared dust continuum emission, but significant scale-dependent tension exists when constructing hybrid SFR indicators (e.g., combining optical H$\alpha$ with 24 $\mu$m dust continuum). Because older stellar populations provide an increasing contribution to the general dust heating on larger galactic scales, the required scaling constant for these hybrid indicators is approximately 3 to 5 times larger for individual H II regions than for integrated whole galaxies [Calzetti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971..118C/abstract), [Calzetti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991..198C/abstract).

## Paα as a High-Redshift Diagnostic

Historically, tracing high-redshift star formation was incredibly challenging; early milestones included the first discovery of a high-redshift star-forming galaxy identified primarily by its absorption signature in a damped Ly$\alpha$ system [Hunstead et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ApJ...356...23H/abstract). As observational astronomy pushes into the epoch of "Cosmic Noon" ($z \sim 1 - 3$), the rest-frame near-infrared Pa$\alpha$ line experiences redshifted emission that moves it into the mid-infrared bands. Because it is highly resilient to dust attenuation, Pa$\alpha$ serves as a critical diagnostic of star formation rates and kinematics in the distant universe.

Early detections of high-redshift Pa$\alpha$ heavily relied on gravitational lensing to amplify the fluxes of distant sources. A landmark example is SMM J163554.2+661225, a submillimeter-selected galaxy at $z = 2.515$. Using Spitzer spectroscopy, researchers detected the Pa$\alpha$ line and compared it to H$\alpha$ fluxes to derive a moderate nebular extinction of $A_V = 3.6 \pm 0.4$ mag [Papovich et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704.1506P/abstract). The dust-corrected Pa$\alpha$ luminosity implies an instantaneous SFR of $171 \pm 28\ M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$, confirming the efficacy of Pa$\alpha$ in tracing intense, obscured starbursts that are typically missed by optical surveys [Papovich et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704.1506P/abstract). When comparing these varied techniques, it is crucial to recognize that ground-based near-infrared spectroscopy may underestimate total emission line fluxes compared to space-based observations, which lack atmospheric absorption and variable slit losses [Tilvi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..14T/abstract).

The deployment of JWST has revolutionized the use of Pa$\alpha$ by enabling spatially resolved spectroscopy of unlensed galaxies. For instance, the first JWST NIRSpec data of $z \sim 8$ galaxies reveal a strong resemblance to local low-metallicity star-forming analogs, highlighting the continuity of star-forming properties across cosmic time [Schaerer et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...665L...4S/abstract). Observations of ASPECS-LP.3mm.06, a main-sequence spiral at $z = 1.1$, combined JWST NIRCam Pa$\alpha$ maps with ALMA CO(2-1) data to trace both the ionized and molecular gas dynamics [Liu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...69L/abstract). These observations show a galaxy with modest dust extinction ($A_V = 0.8$ mag) that adheres to local Kennicutt-Schmidt star formation relations, demonstrating that Pa$\alpha$ can effectively map stable, rotationally supported disks in the early universe [Liu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...69L/abstract). Serving as a bridge to higher redshifts, studies of Lyman break galaxy analogs at $z \sim 0.2$ show high Pa$\alpha$ velocity dispersions and low rotation-to-dispersion ratios ($V/\sigma \sim 1.2$), indicating strong feedback from recent star formation [Wu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887..251W/abstract).

Despite the low dust susceptibility of the [[paschen-series|Paschen series]], the literature highlights significant tensions when Pa$\alpha$ is applied to the most extreme ultra-luminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs) at high redshift. In GN20, a dusty star-forming galaxy at $z = 4.05$, MIRI/JWST observations successfully resolved clumpy Pa$\alpha$ emission. However, the Pa$\alpha$-derived SFR of $144 \pm 9\ M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$ accounts for only $7.7\% \pm 0.5\%$ of the total infrared-derived SFR ($1860 \pm 90\ M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$) [Bik et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A...3B/abstract). This severe discrepancy implies that the nuclear starburst is deeply buried behind extreme columns of dust, with visual extinctions estimated at $A_V = 17.2 \pm 0.4$ mag, and potentially reaching up to 44 mag under mixed-extinction models [Bik et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A...3B/abstract). Thus, while Pa$\alpha$ is vastly superior to optical diagnostics in dusty environments, nearly 90% of the star formation in such high-redshift ULIRGs remains completely obscured even at rest-frame $1.875\ \mu\text{m}$.

Looking beyond individual galaxy studies, large-scale intensity mapping simulations predict that redshifted H$\alpha$ will become a vital cosmological tool for missions such as SPHEREx and CDIM [Silva et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.1587S/abstract); however, whether Pa$\alpha$ can be leveraged in a similar manner at higher redshifts remains an open question.

## Recent Advances with JWST and Integral Field Spectroscopy

The advent of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has fundamentally transformed the utility of the Pa$\alpha$ emission line as a highly resolved tracer of star formation. Utilizing instruments such as NIRCam and MIRI alongside integral field spectroscopy (IFS), researchers can now map Pa$\alpha$ down to parsec scales in nearby galaxies and extend spatially resolved diagnostics to the distant Universe. The application of integral field spectroscopy extends across a variety of environments. Spatially resolved [[diagnostic-emission-line-ratios|line ratios]] in luminous infrared galaxies (LIRGs) demonstrate that enhanced [[n-ii-emission-lines|[N II]]]/[[h-alpha-astronomy|H$\alpha$]] and [[s-ii-emission-lines|[S II]]]/H$\alpha$ regions are often coincident with low-surface-brightness diffuse Pa$\alpha$ emission [Alonso-Herrero et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...522A...7A/abstract). Furthermore, integral field spectroscopy of cool cluster cores reveals complex Pa$\alpha$ morphologies, including filaments associated with X-ray ghost bubbles and broad nuclear components reaching FWHMs of 1700 km s$^{-1}$ [Wilman et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.395.1355W/abstract). In active galaxies, for instance, researchers utilize [[fe-ii-emission-lines|[Fe II]]]/[[pa-emission-line|Pa$\beta$]] diagnostic diagrams derived from near-infrared integral field spectroscopy to successfully disentangle shock excitation from photoionization in Seyfert nuclei [Riffel et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.5161R/abstract).

In the local Universe, the JWST-FEAST (Feedback in Emerging extrAgalactic Star clusTers) survey relies on Pa$\alpha$ as a robust, extinction-insensitive reference to calibrate mid-infrared star formation rate (SFR) indicators down to $\sim 40-120$ pc scales [Calzetti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971..118C/abstract), [Calzetti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991..198C/abstract). By comparing Pa$\alpha$—often corrected for residual dust using [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]]—to mid-infrared dust emission, FEAST studies have established a tight, nearly linear relation between 21 $\mu$m emission and Pa$\alpha$ (power-law exponent $\approx 1.07$) [Calzetti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971..118C/abstract). Similarly, a sublinear relation (exponent $\sim 0.75$) has been found between the 3.3 $\mu$m PAH luminosity and the SFR traced by the [[brackett-alpha-line|Brackett-alpha line]] (Br$\alpha$), though an unexplained discrepancy remains between the derived relations for Pa$\alpha$ and Br$\alpha$ [Gregg et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971..115G/abstract). Crucially, high-resolution Pa$\alpha$ mapping has resolved the long-standing tension in hybrid SFR calibrations, quantitatively attributing the discrepancy between H II region and whole-galaxy scalings to diffuse dust heating contributed by older ($>5-6$ Myr) stellar populations on larger galactic scales [Calzetti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991..198C/abstract).

High-resolution Pa$\alpha$ imaging is also central to characterizing emerging young star clusters (eYSCs). Because eYSCs are initially heavily embedded, they are missed by optical campaigns but appear as compact Pa$\alpha$ sources. In galaxies like M83 and NGC 3256, JWST observations of Pa$\alpha$ and 3.3 $\mu$m PAH emission trace an evolutionary sequence from fully embedded clusters to exposed optical YSCs [Knutas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993...13K/abstract), [Linden et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974L..27L/abstract). These data reveal a rapid dust clearing timescale. Clusters typically remain associated with 3.3 $\mu$m PAH emission for only $3-4$ Myr before transitioning into purely Pa$\alpha$-emitting sources [Knutas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993...13K/abstract). More massive clusters ($>10^5 M_\odot$) in merging systems like NGC 3256 are observed to clear their natal dust even faster, in $<3-4$ Myr [Linden et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974L..27L/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The fraction of ionizing photons that escape from star-forming galaxies into the intergalactic medium—the escape fraction ($f_{\rm esc}$)—remains a critical open parameter in models of cosmic reionization. While ultraviolet resonance lines like Ly$\alpha$ are strongly affected by resonant scattering and neutral hydrogen column densities, infrared recombination lines such as Pa$\alpha$ offer an extinction-insensitive measure of the intrinsic ionizing photon production rate. Comparing the intrinsic recombination rate with the observed UV continuum allows for robust estimations of Lyman continuum leakage. For instance, recent studies of extreme emission-line galaxies with highly ionized interstellar mediums show that pathways for Lyman continuum escape are often dictated by mechanical feedback, such as superbubble blowouts, which manifest as broad line wings in nebular emission [Martin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966..190M/abstract). Determining the exact relationship between mechanical feedback, the morphology of H II regions, and the resulting escape fraction requires spatially resolved infrared spectroscopy capable of penetrating the local dust column.

Another major frontier is the identification of the first generation of massive stars, or Pop III stars, in the early universe. Discoveries of high-redshift galaxies exhibiting extreme ionization states—traced by features like the [[he-ii-1640-line|He II 1640 line]]—suggest the presence of either low-metallicity very massive stars or small pockets of Pop III stars [Mondal et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988..171M/abstract). In these unpolluted environments, Pa$\alpha$ (shifted into the mid-infrared at high redshift) will serve as a definitive anchor for the [[paschen-series|Paschen series]] and [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]], allowing researchers to disentangle true pristine starbursts from obscured active galactic nuclei (AGN). Accurately calibrating evolving dust-to-gas ratios is crucial in these regimes, as the production and rapid clearing of early dust drastically affect the escape of rest-frame optical and UV emission. Recent observations of emerging star clusters indicate that dust clearing timescales can be remarkably short, on the order of $3-4$ Myr [Linden et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974L..27L/abstract), but the physical mechanisms driving this rapid clearing—and whether these timescales hold at high redshifts—remain open questions.

On smaller physical scales, the sub-parsec kinematics of Pa$\alpha$-emitting gas present ongoing challenges, particularly in the immediate vicinity of supermassive black holes. In the Milky Way's Galactic Center, high-resolution Pa$\alpha$ surveys have mapped complex, filamentary structures shaped by magnetic fields, but the exact nature of features like the DSO/G2 object remains contested. Kinematic data from its peribothron passage suggest it may be a compact, young accreting star rather than a coreless gas cloud [Valencia-S. et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800..125V/abstract). Resolving sub-parsec kinematics is equally important in external AGN, where broad Pa$\alpha$ components can originate either from a true broad-line region or from other high-velocity mechanisms (though in the broad-line radio galaxy 3C 445, recent X-ray emission models favor an origin in an outflowing but clumpy accretion disk wind [Reeves et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...725..803R/abstract)). Finally, while recent JWST mapping has largely explained the scaling differences in hybrid SFR derivations between individual H II regions and entire galaxies [Calzetti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971..118C/abstract), [Calzetti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991..198C/abstract), fully disentangling these localized feedback effects from global galaxy properties will dictate the future utility of Pa$\alpha$ as a precision cosmological probe.
