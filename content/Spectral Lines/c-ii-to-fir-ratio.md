## Article

The **[C II]-to-FIR ratio** is a fundamental astrophysical diagnostic that compares the luminosity of the [[c-ii-158-m-line|[C II] 158 μm line]] to the underlying far-infrared (FIR) dust continuum. Because the [C II] fine-structure transition is a primary cooling channel for the neutral interstellar medium (ISM) and the FIR continuum traces thermal dust emission heated by ultraviolet photons, this ratio serves as an observational proxy for the photoelectric heating efficiency of the gas [Crawford et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985ApJ...291..755C/abstract). In normal [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], the ratio typically ranges from $0.1\%$ to $1\%$ of the bolometric luminosity [Gong et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...745...49G/abstract), reflecting a stable energy balance between gas heating in photodissociation regions (PDRs) and dust cooling [De Looze et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2712D/abstract).

However, this linear relationship breaks down dramatically in high-luminosity environments, a phenomenon widely known as the "[C II] deficit." In systems with intense radiation fields and high star formation rate surface densities, such as [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] and ultra-luminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs), the [C II] line emission becomes severely depressed relative to the [[total-ir-luminosity|Total IR luminosity]]. In the most extreme starbursts, the ratio can plummet below $2 \times 10^{-4}$ [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract). This deficit complicates the use of [[far-infrared-fine-structure-lines|Far-infrared fine structure lines]] as [[star-formation-rate-indicators-and-metrics|Star Formation Rate Indicators and Metrics]]. The precise physical driver of the deficit remains actively debated; leading theories suggest it arises either from the excessive positive charging of dust grains—which raises the work function and reduces photoelectric heating efficiency—or from the thermal saturation of the C$^+$ fine-structure levels when gas temperatures exceed $\sim 91$ K.

Interpreting the [C II]-to-FIR ratio also requires disentangling the multiphase origins of carbon emission. Although PDRs produce the bulk of the integrated [C II] flux, significant contributions can arise from dense ionized gas in H II regions and "CO-dark" molecular clouds. Variations in [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]] and ISM porosity heavily modulate these phase fractions. For instance, metal-poor dwarf galaxies often exhibit elevated [C II]-to-FIR ratios due to the deeper penetration of ultraviolet radiation, which creates extensive envelopes of CO-dark gas. To isolate the neutral PDR conditions and accurately quantify the [C II] deficit, observers frequently employ complementary high-ionization diagnostics, such as [[n-ii-emission-lines|[N II] emission lines]], to subtract the ionized gas contribution.

The advent of modern [[far-infrared-astronomy|Far-infrared astronomy]] facilities like ALMA has pushed studies of the [C II]-to-FIR ratio into the Epoch of Reionization ($z > 6$), where early observations revealed severe [C II] deficits in hyper-luminous quasars [Maiolino et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...440L..51M/abstract). Today, research focuses on several pressing open questions, including the origin of unusually high [[o-iii-88-m-line|[O III] 88 μm line]] to [C II] ratios in early galaxies, the physical mechanisms driving the [C II] deficit across cosmic time, and the recent discovery of extended [C II] halos that stretch far beyond the rest-frame ultraviolet continuum. Resolving these tensions is crucial for calibrating next-generation line intensity mapping surveys and understanding the multiphase thermodynamics of the first galaxies.

## Physical Interpretation and Cooling Efficiency

The [[c-ii-158-m-line|[C II] 158 μm line]] is one of the dominant coolants of the cold, neutral interstellar medium (ISM), while the far-infrared (FIR) continuum primarily traces the thermal re-emission of dust heated by ultraviolet (UV) photons. Consequently, the ratio of [C II] to FIR luminosity, $L_{\rm [C II]}/L_{\rm FIR}$, serves as an observational proxy for the photoelectric heating efficiency of the ISM, particularly within photodissociation regions (PDRs) [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract).

Within PDRs, far-UV photons eject electrons from dust grains and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs). These photoelectrons carry kinetic energy that collisionally heats the surrounding gas. To maintain thermal equilibrium, the gas cools primarily through [[far-infrared-fine-structure-lines|Far-infrared fine structure lines]], with [C II] frequently dominating the cooling budget in the neutral atomic and molecular medium. The $L_{\rm [C II]}/L_{\rm FIR}$ ratio thus approximates the fraction of total absorbed UV energy that is converted into gas heating. In normal [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], this process yields a typical photoelectric heating efficiency of $\epsilon_{\rm PE} \approx 0.1\% - 3\%$ [Mookerjea et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...532A.152M/abstract), [Kramer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...553A.114K/abstract).

In environments with intense radiation fields or high star formation rate surface densities ($\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$), such as [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] and [[hyper-luminous-infrared-galaxies|Hyper-luminous infrared galaxies]], the $L_{\rm [C II]}/L_{\rm FIR}$ ratio is observed to drop precipitously, sometimes falling below $2 \times 10^{-4}$ [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract), [Maiolino et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...440L..51M/abstract). A leading physical interpretation for this "[C II] deficit" centers on dust grain charging. When the intensity of the interstellar radiation field ($G_0$) is high relative to the gas density ($n$), dust grains become increasingly positively charged. As the positive charge builds up, the Coulomb barrier increases, requiring more energetic UV photons to eject subsequent photoelectrons. This diminishes the photoelectric heating efficiency, leading to a suppression of the [C II] cooling line relative to the FIR dust continuum [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract), [Ibar et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449.2498I/abstract). This transition in PDR properties exhibits a critical break at an infrared surface brightness of $\Sigma_{\rm IR} \approx 5 \times 10^{10} L_\odot \, {\rm kpc}^{-2}$, above which the radiation field intensity to gas density ratio ($G_0/n$) rapidly increases [Díaz-Santos et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...32D/abstract).

The literature, however, presents an active debate regarding the singular role of grain charging in driving the deficit. Alternatively, it has been proposed that the [C II] deficit can arise simply from the thermal saturation of the C$^+$ fine-structure levels. At gas temperatures exceeding $\sim 91$ K, the upper level of the $158 \, \mu{\rm m}$ transition becomes saturated, inherently limiting the [C II] luminosity even as the FIR continuum continues to scale with the radiation field [Muñoz et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.2085M/abstract). In active galactic nucleus (AGN) environments, alternative physics may dominate; for instance, modeling of the luminous AGN Markarian 231 suggests that the [C II] deficit is compatible with a high average abundance of C$^+$ residing in a luminous AGN-dominated region with a high luminosity-to-gas mass ratio [González-Alfonso et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...675..303G/abstract). Furthermore, in specific radio-loud systems like the 3C 326N radio galaxy, the dissipation of mechanical energy from AGN-driven turbulence can serve as a primary heating source for warm [C II]-emitting gas, effectively decoupling the fine-structure emission from traditional UV-driven photoelectric heating [Guillard et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...574A..32G/abstract). In stark contrast to the deficit, certain environments can exhibit extreme [C II] excesses; for example, in the Lyman Alpha Reference Sample galaxy LARS 5, the [C II] line alone accounts for roughly $14\%$ of the total far-infrared luminosity [Puschnig et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...644A..10P/abstract).

Furthermore, while the [C II]-to-FIR ratio is traditionally treated as a pure PDR diagnostic, velocity-resolved spectroscopy and photoionization modeling indicate that the [C II] emission is multiphase. Although PDRs produce the bulk of the emission (typically $60\% - 80\%$), dense ionized gas from H II regions can contribute $20\% - 30\%$ of the total [C II] flux [Mookerjea et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...532A.152M/abstract), [Langer et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...590A..43L/abstract). Exact determinations of the PDR cooling efficiency therefore require isolating the neutral contribution by accounting for the ionized gas fraction, which is often achieved by comparing [C II] to other high-ionization tracers like the [[n-ii-emission-lines|[N II] emission lines]] [Decarli et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782L..17D/abstract).

## The [C II] Deficit in Luminous Infrared Galaxies

In normal [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], the [[c-ii-158-m-line|[C II] 158 μm line]] is a dominant cooling channel for the interstellar medium, with the $L_{\rm [C II]}/L_{\rm FIR}$ ratio typically residing around a few times $10^{-3}$. However, in [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] and their more massive counterparts, ultra-luminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs), this ratio undergoes a pronounced, order-of-magnitude depression known as the "[C II] deficit" [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract). While [C II] luminosity often scales linearly with the [[total-ir-luminosity|Total IR luminosity]] in less extreme environments, this relationship fundamentally breaks down at high luminosities, with the $L_{\rm [C II]}/L_{\rm FIR}$ ratio frequently dropping below $2 \times 10^{-4}$ in extreme local starbursts and high-redshift ULIRGs [Maiolino et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...440L..51M/abstract), [De Looze et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...568A..62D/abstract). Interestingly, this phenomenon is not exclusively extragalactic; a [C II] deficit is also observed locally within the Milky Way's Galactic Center, likely driven by a combination of soft radiation fields and high gas densities [Nakagawa et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...455L..35N/abstract).

The magnitude of the line deficit correlates strongly with both the dust temperature—parameterized by far-infrared colors such as $F(60\mu{\rm m})/F(100\mu{\rm m})$—and the infrared luminosity surface density, $\Sigma_{\rm IR}$ [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract), [Díaz-Santos et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...32D/abstract). Studies of local LIRGs reveal a critical transition at $\Sigma_{\rm IR} \approx 5 \times 10^{10} \, L_\odot \, {\rm kpc}^{-2}$ [Díaz-Santos et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...32D/abstract). Below this threshold, the ratio of the interstellar radiation field to the gas density ($G_0/n_{\rm H}$) remains relatively constant, and increases in total luminosity are driven by a larger volume filling factor of normal star-forming regions. Above this critical surface density, $G_0/n_{\rm H}$ rises rapidly, shifting the interstellar medium into a compact, intense "starburst" mode that characterizes the deficit regime [Díaz-Santos et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...32D/abstract). High-resolution spatial mapping confirms this picture: the [C II] deficit in local LIRGs is often strictly confined to their warm, compact nuclei, whereas the extended extra-nuclear disks exhibit normal $L_{\rm [C II]}/L_{\rm FIR}$ ratios of $\ge 4 \times 10^{-3}$ [Díaz-Santos et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...788L..17D/abstract).

While the spatial and photometric properties of the [C II] deficit are increasingly well-characterized, its precise physical origin remains an active subject of debate. Proposed mechanisms—ranging from the microphysics of dust grain charging and the thermal saturation of fine-structure transitions, to the presence of optically thick "FIR excesses" in embedded cores—are explored in detail in Section 5.

## Multiphase ISM Contributions and Ionized Gas Fraction

Carbon has an ionization potential of $11.26\text{ eV}$, which is lower than that of hydrogen ($13.6\text{ eV}$). Consequently, the [[c-ii-158-m-line|[C II] 158 μm line]] can originate from multiple phases of the interstellar medium (ISM), including Photodissociation Regions (PDRs), the cold neutral medium (CNM), the diffuse ionized medium (DIM), and dense H II regions. Disentangling the fractional contributions of these phases is critical when using [C II] to compute molecular gas masses or as one of the primary [[star-formation-tracers|Star formation tracers]].

In the Milky Way and local [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], velocity-resolved spectroscopy and radiative transfer models indicate that PDRs and molecular regions dominate the integrated [C II] luminosity. In the Galaxy, approximately $47\%$ of the [C II] emission is estimated to arise from dense PDRs, $28\%$ from CO-dark molecular gas, $21\%$ from the cold atomic medium, and roughly $4\%$ from ionized gas [Pineda et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...554A.103P/abstract). However, relying on alternative treatments of the warm ionized medium, other estimates attribute up to $\sim 21\%$ of the Galactic [C II] emission to diffuse ionized gas [Velusamy et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...572A..45V/abstract). Extragalactic studies consistently find that the molecular fraction of [C II], $f_{\rm [CII],mol}$, typically ranges from $60\%$ to $80 \%$ [Accurso et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.3315A/abstract), though local H II regions can contribute $10\% - 30\%$ of the total line flux in specific environments [Mookerjea et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...532A.152M/abstract), [Parkin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...787...16P/abstract).

To quantitatively isolate the ionized gas fraction, observers frequently utilize the [[n-ii-emission-lines|[N II] emission lines]] at $122\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ and $205\text{ }\mu\text{m}$. Because the ionization potential of nitrogen is $14.53\text{ eV}$, $\text{N}^+$ exists almost exclusively in ionized regions. The empirical ratio of [C II] to [N II] $205\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ therefore serves as a robust diagnostic to anchor the ionized gas contribution to the total [C II] flux [Langer et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...590A..43L/abstract). This diagnostic reveals significant environmental variations: while typical starbursts maintain a [C II]/[N II] ratio of $\sim 20$, some high-redshift systems exhibit ratios exceeding $60$, suggesting extremely weak [N II] emission driven by low metallicities or low dust abundances [Pavesi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832..151P/abstract). Conversely, in specific high-redshift Lyman-$\alpha$ emitters, the ratio can drop as low as $\sim 2$, indicating that the bulk of the [C II] emission in these sources arises from H II regions rather than neutral PDRs [Decarli et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782L..17D/abstract). High-redshift detections of the [N II] 122 $\mu$m line further indicate large ionized gas fractions and demonstrate that some early starbursts do not exhibit the FIR line deficits typically seen in their local analogs [Ferkinhoff et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...740L..29F/abstract).

[C II] is also a crucial probe of the "CO-dark" molecular gas—regions where $\text{H}_2$ is self-shielded but carbon resides predominantly as $\text{C}^+$ due to the preferential photodissociation of CO. The CO-dark gas fraction is highly sensitive to the ISM metallicity and the ambient radiation field. In low-metallicity dwarf galaxies, reduced dust shielding permits far-ultraviolet photons to penetrate deeper into molecular clouds. In these environments, the ionized gas contribution to [C II] can drop below $30\%$, while the ISM becomes highly porous, dramatically increasing the mass fraction of the CO-dark phase relative to the CO-luminous phase [Cormier et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...578A..53C/abstract), [Cormier et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...626A..23C/abstract).

The literature reflects ongoing debates regarding the exact dependence of the [C II] phase distribution on global galaxy properties. Bayesian models suggest that the electron density ($n_e$) and specific star formation rate (sSFR) are the primary global drivers of $f_{\rm [CII],mol}$ [Accurso et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.3315A/abstract). However, variations in the physical geometry of the multiphase ISM—particularly whether [C II] emission traces compact clumps or extended, diffuse halos—complicate the translation of simple line ratios into universal mass conversion factors, a tension that is especially pronounced in high-redshift [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] and merging systems.

## Physical Drivers: Radiation Fields and Gas Density

The ratio of [[c-ii-158-m-line|[C II] 158 μm line]] emission to the far-infrared (FIR) continuum is fundamentally governed by the balance between gas heating and cooling in the interstellar medium (ISM). Because a substantial fraction of [C II] emission arises from photodissociation regions (PDRs) on the surfaces of molecular clouds, the physical conditions within these environments dictate the emergent line-to-continuum ratio. The two primary parameters controlling this balance are the intensity of the incident far-ultraviolet (FUV) radiation field, $G_0$ (typically measured in units of the Habing field), and the hydrogen gas density, $n_H$.

### PDR Conditions and the $G_0/n_H$ Ratio

Standard PDR models evaluate the thermal state of the gas as a function of $G_0$ and $n_H$. The ratio $G_0/n_H$ is a critical parameter that determines the dominant heating and cooling channels. In normal [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], $G_0/n_H$ remains relatively constant. For instance, in galaxies with an infrared surface brightness $\Sigma_{\rm IR} \lesssim 5 \times 10^{10} \, L_\odot \, \rm{kpc}^{-2}$, the typical ratio is $\simeq 0.32 \, G_0 \, \rm{cm}^3$ [Díaz-Santos et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...32D/abstract). In this regime, variations in the total infrared luminosity are primarily driven by the number of active star-forming regions rather than significant changes in the local PDR conditions.

However, in extreme environments such as [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] and ultraluminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs), the radiation field becomes much more intense. Above the critical surface brightness threshold of $\Sigma_{\rm IR} \approx 5 \times 10^{10} \, L_\odot \, \rm{kpc}^{-2}$, the $G_0/n_H$ ratio increases rapidly, reaching values up to $50 \, G_0 \, \rm{cm}^3$ [Díaz-Santos et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...32D/abstract). This shift signals a departure from normal star-forming conditions to highly compact, intensely irradiated geometries, which heavily modulates the resulting [C II]/FIR ratio. 

### Grain Charging and Photoelectric Efficiency

A leading explanation for the decline of the [C II]-to-FIR ratio in high-luminosity systems—often termed the "[C II] deficit"—is a reduction in the photoelectric heating efficiency. In PDRs, FUV photons heat the gas by ejecting photoelectrons from dust grains and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs). When the FUV radiation field $G_0$ is exceptionally high relative to the gas density $n_H$, dust grains become positively charged. This positive potential increases the work function required to eject subsequent electrons, rendering the photoelectric heating process highly inefficient [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract), [Ibar et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449.2498I/abstract). 

As a result, a smaller fraction of the absorbed FUV energy is transferred to the gas to power fine-structure cooling lines. Instead, the dust continues to absorb FUV photons and thermally re-radiates the energy as FIR continuum. This decoupling between gas heating and dust heating naturally suppresses the $L_{\rm [CII]}/L_{\rm FIR}$ ratio in the presence of intense radiation fields.

### Thermal Saturation of the [C II] Line

An alternative physical driver for the [C II] deficit is the thermal saturation of the C$^+$ fine-structure levels. The [C II] 158 $\mu$m transition arises from the $^2P_{3/2} \rightarrow {^2P}_{1/2}$ fine-structure states, which are separated by an energy equivalent to $\Delta E / k_B \approx 91$ K. In dense, intensely heated starburst environments where the gas kinetic temperature exceeds this $\sim 91$ K threshold, the population of the upper $^2P_{3/2}$ state becomes thermally saturated [Muñoz et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.2085M/abstract). 

Once saturated, the [C II] cooling rate approaches a theoretical maximum. Any further injection of energy into the gas—or an increase in the background radiation field—will not yield a proportional increase in [C II] luminosity. Instead, the excess energy must be radiated away by other coolants, such as the [O I] 63 $\mu$m line, while the FIR continuum continues to scale linearly with the radiation field. Models based on thermal saturation can successfully reproduce the amplitude of the [C II] deficit without requiring complex modifications to dust grain charging physics, provided that [C II] emission traces roughly 10% to 17% of the total gas in these systems [Muñoz et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.2085M/abstract). Recent simulations of dwarf galaxy mergers further demonstrate that intense starbursts driven by such interactions can induce a [C II] deficit simply through the thermal saturation of the line in the presence of strong FUV fields from massive star clusters [Bisbas et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934..115B/abstract).

### FIR Excess from Optically Thick Cores

Finally, other models argue that the deficit may not entirely reflect a physical suppression of [C II] emission, but rather a "FIR excess" driven by deeply embedded, optically thick star-forming cores. In these compact regions, young stars contribute heavily to the [[total-ir-luminosity|Total IR luminosity]], but their UV photons are absorbed by dust before they can escape to create the extended, [C II]-emitting PDRs that characterize normal galaxies [Pabst et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...651A.111P/abstract). 

Currently, the literature remains actively divided regarding which of these mechanisms is the primary physical cause of the [C II] deficit. It is highly plausible that they operate simultaneously in the most extreme astrophysical environments, compounding the suppression of [C II] emission relative to the underlying infrared continuum.

## [C II]-to-FIR as a Star Formation Rate Tracer

The [[c-ii-158-m-line|[C II] 158 μm line]] is one of the dominant cooling pathways for the neutral interstellar medium and is often the brightest fine-structure emission line in the far-infrared (FIR) spectra of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], typically contributing 0.1-1% of the bolometric luminosity [Gong et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...745...49G/abstract). Because its primary excitation mechanism is the photoelectric heating of gas by far-ultraviolet (FUV) photons from massive young stars in photodissociation regions (PDRs), the [C II] luminosity ($L_{\rm [C\,II]}$) scales closely with the star formation rate (SFR) in normal disk galaxies and low-metallicity dwarfs [De Looze et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2712D/abstract), [De Looze et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...568A..62D/abstract). Consequently, [C II] has become a key metric among [[star-formation-rate-indicators-and-metrics|Star Formation Rate Indicators and Metrics]], particularly for dust-obscured and high-redshift systems where rest-frame optical tracers suffer from severe attenuation.

However, the linear relationship between $L_{\rm [C\,II]}$ and the SFR breaks down in extreme environments. In systems with high infrared luminosity and high star formation rate surface densities ($\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$), such as [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] and ultraluminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs), the $L_{\rm [C\,II]}/L_{\rm FIR}$ ratio drops by over an order of magnitude [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract), [Díaz-Santos et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...32D/abstract). Because of this "[C II] deficit", applying local, normal-galaxy $L_{\rm [C\,II]}$-SFR calibrations to extreme starbursts will significantly underestimate their true SFRs. Calibrations for these systems therefore require luminosity-dependent or surface-brightness-dependent corrections.

The reliability of [C II] as an SFR tracer is further complicated by its multiphase origins. As discussed in previous sections, significant contributions from ionized gas and CO-dark molecular clouds mean that $L_{\rm [C\,II]}$ does not exclusively trace the PDRs associated with recent star formation. To isolate the star-formation-driven PDR component, observers frequently use the [[n-ii-emission-lines|[N II] emission lines]]—which arise exclusively in ionized gas—to estimate and subtract the H II region contribution from the total [C II] flux [Mookerjea et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...532A.152M/abstract), [Accurso et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.3315A/abstract). Furthermore, hydrodynamical simulations, such as SIMBA, indicate that [C II] luminosity can be a more robust tracer of molecular gas mass than CO $J=1-0$ at early cosmic epochs, particularly in low-metallicity environments where a large fraction of the gas is CO-dark [Vizgan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929...92V/abstract). To properly calibrate these gas masses, researchers have developed multivariate conversion functions that relate the [C II]/CO (1-0) ratio to the [[h2-conversion-factor|H2 conversion factor]] ($\alpha_{\rm CO}$), which explicitly account for both variations in [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]] and specific star formation rate [Accurso et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.4750A/abstract).

In the Epoch of Reionization ($z > 6$), Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) observations have revealed significant scatter in the $L_{\rm [C\,II]}$-SFR relation. Some highly star-forming, UV-bright galaxies are undetected in [C II], implying a high-redshift analogue to the [C II] deficit [Ota et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...792...34O/abstract), [Binggeli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646A..26B/abstract). This suppression of [C II] emission may result from extremely low [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]] ($Z < 0.1 Z_\odot$) leading to a dearth of carbon, or from high ionization parameters that favor higher ionization states like O$^{++}$ over C$^+$ [Arata et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5541A/abstract), [Fujimoto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..146F/abstract). Alternatively, recent high-resolution mapping has shown that [C II] emission in some high-$z$ galaxies extends into large halos up to $\sim 10$ kpc in size, physically offset from their compact UV-bright star-forming cores [Lambert et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.3183L/abstract), [Killi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.3222K/abstract). If such extended halos are common, unresolved or shallow observations may simply miss a substantial fraction of the total [C II] flux, artificially exacerbating the apparent deficit.

## Metallicity Effects and Dwarf Galaxies

In contrast to the pronounced [C II] deficit observed in dusty, high-luminosity starbursts, low-metallicity dwarf galaxies typically exhibit elevated $L_{\rm [C II]}/L_{\rm FIR}$ ratios. Observations from the Herschel Dwarf Galaxy Survey demonstrate that [[far-infrared-fine-structure-lines|Far-infrared fine structure lines]] collectively account for up to 3% of the [[total-ir-luminosity|Total IR luminosity]] ($L_{\rm TIR}$) in these metal-poor systems, with the [[c-ii-158-m-line|[C II] 158 μm line]] serving as the dominant cooling channel [Cormier et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...578A..53C/abstract). Within the massive star-forming regions of local dwarfs, the [C II]-to-FIR ratio routinely reaches values between $0.5\%$ and $0.8\%$ [Cormier et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518L..57C/abstract).

The primary physical driver for these enhanced ratios is the low [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]], which fundamentally alters the dust content and radiation transport within the interstellar medium (ISM). In metal-rich spiral galaxies, dust grains efficiently absorb far-ultraviolet (FUV) photons in the thin outer layers of molecular clouds, re-radiating the energy in the far-infrared. In metal-poor dwarf galaxies, the reduced dust opacity allows FUV radiation to penetrate significantly deeper into the clouds [Mochizuki et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...430L..37M/abstract). This deep FUV penetration readily photodissociates CO molecules while leaving carbon in its singly ionized state, generating extensive envelopes of "CO-dark" molecular gas. Because the [C II] emission traces this enlarged C$^+$ reservoir, the $L_{\rm [C II]}/L_{\rm CO}$ ratio in dwarf galaxies can reach $20,000 - 70,000$—more than an order of magnitude higher than the typical values found in galactic disks [Cormier et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518L..57C/abstract).

This deep radiation penetration is closely linked to a shift in the structural topology of the gas. Radiative transfer and photoionization models of Dwarf Galaxy Survey targets reveal a high degree of ISM porosity. The ISM in these galaxies is characterized by compact, dense gas clumps embedded within a much larger volume filling factor of diffuse gas [Cormier et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...578A..53C/abstract), [Cormier et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...626A..23C/abstract). This increased ISM porosity lowers the global photodissociation region (PDR) covering factor. As a result, global variations in the [C II]-to-FIR ratio in low-metallicity environments strongly reflect this porous geometry and the widespread leakage of ionizing photons, rather than the onset of the starburst-driven thermal saturation or grain charging effects that drive the [C II] deficit in ultra-luminous infrared galaxies [Cormier et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...626A..23C/abstract). To further probe these conditions, observers can utilize other fine-structure transitions; for instance, the ratio of $({\rm [O~III]}~52~\mu{\rm m} + {\rm [O~III]}~88~\mu{\rm m}) / {\rm [N~III]}~57~\mu{\rm m}$ serves as an excellent metallicity diagnostic that is relatively independent of dust extinction; however, its dependence on the ionization parameter must be explicitly accounted for using supplementary line ratios, such as the [N II] to [N III] ratio [Nagao et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...526A.149N/abstract). This technique proves especially powerful because these far-infrared fine-structure line ratios remain accurate and robust even in the presence of an active galactic nucleus (AGN) [Pereira-Santaella et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.1218P/abstract).

Despite the characteristically hard radiation fields and high specific star formation rates of dwarf galaxies, multiphase modeling confirms that the bulk of the [C II] emission still originates in neutral PDRs. In these low-metallicity environments, the ionized gas phase contributes less than $30\%$ of the total [C II] line flux [Cormier et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...626A..23C/abstract). Understanding this metallicity-driven porosity and the resultant high [C II]-to-FIR ratios in local dwarf galaxies is critical for interpreting high-redshift observations. In the early Universe, low dust and metal abundances similarly dictate ISM thermodynamics, although the most extreme metal-poor galaxies ($Z < 0.1 Z_\odot$) may eventually exhibit an overall lack of [C II] emission simply due to a severe underabundance of carbon atoms [Ota et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...792...34O/abstract), [Matthee et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881..124M/abstract), [Binggeli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646A..26B/abstract).

## Observations in the Epoch of Reionization

The advent of the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) has revolutionized [[far-infrared-astronomy|Far-infrared astronomy]] by enabling the detection of the [[c-ii-158-m-line|[C II] 158 μm line]] in the first billion years of cosmic time ($z > 6$). Initial detections at these redshifts were largely confined to hyper-luminous quasars, which exhibit extreme [C II] deficits. For example, the $z=6.42$ quasar SDSS J1148+5251 shows a $L_{\rm [CII]}/L_{\rm FIR}$ ratio of $2 \times 10^{-4}$, nearly an order of magnitude lower than local [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] [Maiolino et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...440L..51M/abstract). In such $z > 6.6$ quasar host galaxies, the strength of the [C II] and $158\ \mu{\rm m}$ continuum emission has been found to correlate with the quasar's overall bolometric luminosity [Venemans et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...816...37V/abstract). Conversely, low-luminosity quasars at $z > 6$ often exhibit $L_{\rm [CII]}/L_{\rm FIR}$ ratios similar to those found in normal local star-forming galaxies, lacking the severe deficit of their hyper-luminous counterparts [Izumi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PASJ...71..111I/abstract). More recently, large programs such as the [[rebels-survey|REBELS survey]] have targeted UV-selected star-forming galaxies at $z=6.4-7.7$, successfully mapping the infrared luminosity function and demonstrating that dust and [C II] are already abundant in the Epoch of Reionization [Barrufet et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.3926B/abstract). More broadly, infrared fine-structure lines have become a primary tool for measuring internal dynamics, black hole-host galaxy relations, and gas outflows in the early universe [Decarli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&ARv..33....4D/abstract).

Despite these successes, the literature reports a highly ambiguous relationship between [C II] emission and the rest-frame ultraviolet (UV) continuum at $z > 6$. Several UV-bright, actively star-forming Lyman-break galaxies and Lyman-alpha emitters (such as IOK-1 at $z=6.96$ and z7_GSD_3811 at $z=7.66$) remain undetected in [C II] [Ota et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...792...34O/abstract), [Binggeli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646A..26B/abstract). These non-detections imply a potential [C II] deficit in the early universe or extreme low [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]] ($Z < 0.1 Z_\odot$) where carbon is severely underabundant. Studies suggest that the global [C II]-to-UV ratio steepens at ${\rm SFR} < 30\ M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$ and anti-correlates with Ly$\alpha$ equivalent width [Matthee et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881..124M/abstract). For example, multi-line observations of the bright Lyman-break galaxy B14-65666 at $z=7.15$ exhibit high [C II]/CO luminosity ratios, suggesting lower gas densities in the photodissociation regions of bright high-redshift Lyman-break systems compared to local analogs [Hashimoto et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952...48H/abstract). Spectral stacking analyses of lensed $z \sim 6$ galaxies from the ALMA Lensing Cluster Survey further confirm that these early systems are often [C II] under-luminous relative to local star formation rate relations, which is consistent with low-metallicity environments [Jolly et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...652A.128J/abstract). Furthermore, observations of strongly lensed galaxies have enabled the detection of some of the lowest [C II] luminosities at $z > 6$, often falling significantly below local SFR--$L_{\rm [C II]}$ relations [Knudsen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462L...6K/abstract). Even more extreme deficits can arise from non-thermal continuum contamination; for instance, the $z=7$ blazar J0410-0139 exhibits the lowest [C II]-to-infrared ratio reported to date ($\sim 10^{-4}$), likely due to significant synchrotron contamination of the underlying infrared continuum [Bañados et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977L..46B/abstract).

A defining characteristic of many $z > 6$ galaxies is an unusually high luminosity ratio of the [[o-iii-88-m-line|[O III] 88 μm line]] to the [[c-ii-158-m-line|[C II] 158 μm line]]. ALMA observations frequently reveal $L_{\rm [O III]}/L_{\rm [C II]}$ ratios $>10$, which is roughly a factor of ten higher than ratios found in local starbursts [Arata et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5541A/abstract), [Ren et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...69R/abstract). This enhancement is typically attributed to the high ionization parameters, low metallicities, and low carbon-to-oxygen (C/O) abundance ratios prevalent in the early universe, which favor ${\rm O}^{++}$ over ${\rm C}^{+}$ [Fujimoto et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..146F/abstract). Complementing these probes, detections of the [O I] $145\ \mu{\rm m}$ line in high-redshift starbursts, such as BR 1202-0725 at $z = 4.7$, reveal high [O I]/[C II] ratios indicative of large reservoirs of warm, dense neutral gas [Lee et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913...41L/abstract). In the absence of full spectral energy distribution mapping, researchers have found that the [N II] 205 $\mu$m to CO(7-6) luminosity ratio can serve as an effective estimator for rest-frame far-infrared color—and by extension, dust temperature—in high-redshift systems [Lu et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...802L..11L/abstract).

Furthermore, high-resolution ALMA imaging has uncovered that [C II] emission at high redshift is often not co-spatial with the UV stellar continuum. Instead, [C II] frequently traces extended circumgalactic halos on $\sim 10$ kpc scales, with effective radii more than twice as large as the rest-frame UV emission [Lambert et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.3183L/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.4388Z/abstract). Significant spatial offsets between [C II] peaks and UV emission (sometimes by several kiloparsecs) have been observed in multiple systems [Carniani et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...605A..42C/abstract). The physical origin of these extended structures and offsets remains actively debated in the literature, with current theories proposing differential dust obscuration, merging clumps, or the cooling of hot ionized outflows into the circumgalactic medium [Fudamoto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961...71F/abstract), [Akins et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934...64A/abstract).

## Spatial Variations and Resolved Galactic Structure

The advent of resolved spectroscopy with *Herschel* and SOFIA has allowed the mapping of the [C II]-to-FIR ratio across sub-kiloparsec scales, revealing how local interstellar medium (ISM) conditions drive variations between galactic nuclei, spiral arms, and inter-arm regions.

Spatially resolved observations of local [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] reveal that the widely reported "[C II] deficit" is predominantly a nuclear phenomenon. While the central regions of these galaxies exhibit suppressed line-to-continuum ratios, their extra-nuclear extended disks maintain "normal" [C II]/FIR ratios of $\geq 4 \times 10^{-3}$, similar to the diffuse ISM of the Milky Way [Díaz-Santos et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...788L..17D/abstract). This nuclear suppression is similarly observed in the grand-design spiral M51, where the [C II] deficit is localized to the central black hole region and its companion M51b (associated with elevated dust temperatures), while the extended galactic disk exhibits a strong correlation between [C II] and star formation rate surface density [Pineda et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869L..30P/abstract). Detailed studies of extreme galactic centers also reveal complex multiphase environments; for instance, resolved spectroscopy of the nuclear region of the starburst galaxy NGC 253 identifies at least two distinct ionized gas components with different electron densities and solar-like metallicities [Beck et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...665A..85B/abstract).

Within galactic disks, the variation between spiral arms and inter-arm regions provides insight into the local multiphase ISM. In M51, detailed modeling of far-infrared fine-structure lines indicates a radial decrease in both the far-ultraviolet (FUV) radiation field intensity ($G_0$) and the hydrogen density ($n$). However, these properties are surprisingly similar when comparing arm and inter-arm regions at the same radius, suggesting that the fundamental physical properties of the molecular clouds remain consistent regardless of their location relative to the spiral pattern, yielding an average [C II]/[[total-ir-luminosity|Total IR luminosity]] ratio of $\approx 4 \times 10^{-3}$ [Parkin et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...776...65P/abstract). Velocity-resolved comparisons in nearby galaxies demonstrate that [C II] line profiles and spatial distributions more closely resemble those of CO than H I, suggesting that the CO-traced interstellar medium serves as the relevant dynamical tracer for [C II] emission [de Blok et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152...51D/abstract).

Radial gradients in the [C II]-to-FIR ratio have been observed in several nearby galaxies, though the trends are not universal. In M33, the photoelectric heating efficiency—often proxied by the [C II]/FIR ratio—remains constant at $\approx 0.8\%$ within the inner $4.5$ kpc, but rises to $\approx 3\%$ in the galactic outskirts due to declining volume densities and FUV fields [Kramer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...553A.114K/abstract). M31 likewise exhibits a large-scale trend of increasing [C II]/[[total-ir-luminosity|Total IR luminosity]] with galactocentric radius [Kapala et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...798...24K/abstract). However, multi-wavelength analysis suggests this variation in M31 is driven by the relative hardness of the absorbed stellar radiation field rather than intrinsic changes in photoelectric heating efficiency [Kapala et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...842..128K/abstract). Conversely, the disk of Centaurus A shows no significant radial trend in its ([C II] + [O I] $63\ \mu$m)/FIR heating efficiency proxy [Parkin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...787...16P/abstract).

High-resolution mapping also highlights a substantial contribution from diffuse, extra-nuclear gas. In M31, observations at $\sim 50$ pc resolution show that $20\%$--$90\%$ of the [C II] emission originates outside of discrete star-forming regions, likely excited by FUV photon leakage or older stellar populations [Kapala et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...798...24K/abstract). The fraction of [C II] arising from neutral versus ionized gas can also vary spatially; in NGC 7331, for instance, the neutral fraction of [C II] emission steadily increases with distance from the galactic center [Sutter et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926...82S/abstract).

Ultimately, these intra-galactic variations demonstrate that the relationship between [C II] emission, [[total-ir-luminosity|Total IR luminosity]], and star formation is highly sensitive to local ISM conditions. This introduces significant complexity when interpreting global, unresolved measurements, as galaxy-integrated fluxes blend heterogeneous and distinct physical environments [Kovačić et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...694A..87K/abstract).

## Historical Development of the [C II] Diagnostic

The historical evolution of the [C II] diagnostic is fundamentally tied to the development of far-infrared and submillimeter astronomical instrumentation. Early targeted observations, such as those toward the nuclei of gas-rich galaxies, established the [C II] transition as a dominant coolant of the neutral interstellar medium (ISM) and a key tracer of photon-dominated regions (PDRs) at the surfaces of molecular clouds [Crawford et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985ApJ...291..755C/abstract). Later airborne and balloon-borne experiments, including the Balloon-borne Infrared Carbon Explorer (BICE), allowed astronomers to successfully map the large-scale emission of the [[c-ii-158-m-line|[C II] 158 μm line]] across the Milky Way [Nakagawa et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...455L..35N/abstract) and the Large Magellanic Cloud [Mochizuki et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...430L..37M/abstract).

The first systematic extragalactic surveys were made possible by the ISO LWS aboard the Infrared Space Observatory. Observations of 60 local normal and starburst galaxies revealed that while the [C II] luminosity ($L_{\rm [C\,II]}$) generally correlates with the far-infrared continuum ($L_{\rm FIR}$), the $L_{\rm [C\,II]}/L_{\rm FIR}$ ratio drops precipitously in systems with warm dust colors (e.g., $F_{60}/F_{100}$) and high FIR-to-blue luminosity ratios [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract). This phenomenon, termed the "[C II] deficit," became a central focus of subsequent diagnostic modeling. The critical dependence of this deficit on infrared surface brightness ($\Sigma_{\rm IR}$) was firmly established by later high-resolution studies [Díaz-Santos et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...32D/abstract). Following the ISO era, ground-based millimeter facilities began pushing into the high-redshift universe, culminating in the first detection of [C II] at $z > 6$ in a hyper-luminous quasar host, which exhibited an extreme deficit of $L_{\rm [C\,II]}/L_{\rm FIR} \approx 2 \times 10^{-4}$ [Maiolino et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...440L..51M/abstract).

The launch of the *Herschel Space Observatory* drastically improved spatial and spectral resolution, cementing [C II] as a ubiquitous tool for extragalactic astrophysics. Instruments like Herschel/PACS allowed for detailed, galaxy-wide mapping of [C II] alongside other [[far-infrared-fine-structure-lines|Far-infrared fine structure lines]]. *Herschel* observations of [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] demonstrated that the [C II] deficit is primarily a nuclear phenomenon, while extra-nuclear emission often exhibits "normal" ratios of $\sim 4 \times 10^{-3}$ [Díaz-Santos et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...788L..17D/abstract). Furthermore, *Herschel* provided the robust statistical samples necessary to rigorously calibrate the [C II] line for use in [[star-formation-rate-indicators-and-metrics|Star Formation Rate Indicators and Metrics]] across diverse metallicities and galaxy types [De Looze et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2712D/abstract), [De Looze et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...568A..62D/abstract).

In the post-*Herschel* landscape, the Stratospheric Observatory for Infrared Astronomy (SOFIA) and the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) drove the next observational paradigm shifts. SOFIA/GREAT and FIFI-LS provided velocity-resolved sub-kiloparsec mapping of nearby galaxies, allowing researchers to dissect the multiphase origins of [C II] emission and separate it into contributions from molecular, atomic, and ionized components [Fahrion et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...599A...9F/abstract), [Pineda et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869L..30P/abstract). 

Concurrently, ALMA revolutionized the study of [C II] at high redshifts, allowing astronomers to map the gas dynamics of galaxies within 1.5 Gyr of the Big Bang [De Breuck et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Msngr.156...38B/abstract). ALMA's unprecedented resolution revealed that [C II] emission in the Epoch of Reionization is often spatially offset from rest-frame UV emission and frequently forms extended halos on $\sim 10$ kpc scales. This discovery is fundamentally altering the interpretation of [C II] as a simple, co-spatial proxy for nuclear star formation in unresolved observations [Lambert et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.3183L/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite decades of observations, the definitive physical driver of the [C II] deficit in [[luminous-infrared-galaxies-lirgs|Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs)]] and compact starbursts remains a subject of active debate. Whether the phenomenon is primarily driven by positive grain charging [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract), [Ibar et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449.2498I/abstract), thermal saturation of the C$^+$ fine-structure levels [Muñoz et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.2085M/abstract), dramatic shifts in PDR topology above critical surface brightness thresholds [Díaz-Santos et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...32D/abstract), or a "FIR excess" from embedded cores [Pabst et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...651A.111P/abstract), remains unresolved. Accurately capturing these sub-grid multiphase ISM processes in cosmological simulations is a persistent hurdle for theoretical models attempting to reproduce the high-luminosity end of the [C II] scaling relations [Popping et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.4906P/abstract). Additionally, interpreting gas mass diagnostics at high redshift faces systemic challenges; the warmer cosmic microwave background at these early epochs can significantly affect [C I] and CO line intensities, potentially biasing molecular gas mass estimates for galaxies with cold gas kinetic temperatures [Frias Castillo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...987..158F/abstract).

Moving to higher redshifts, ALMA observations have revealed that [C II] emission frequently extends far beyond the rest-frame UV continuum, forming halos up to $\sim 10$ kpc in radius [Lambert et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.3183L/abstract), [Fudamoto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961...71F/abstract), [Killi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.3222K/abstract). The physical origin of these halos—whether driven by multiphase outflows, gas recycling in the circumgalactic medium, or merging clumps—is currently unconstrained. The extended nature of this gas has profound implications for line intensity mapping (LIM), a technique designed to measure the aggregate emission of unresolved galaxies across cosmic time. Extended [C II] halos can boost the LIM power spectrum signal by factors of 20 to 100 compared to compact emission models [Zhang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.4388Z/abstract). Upcoming LIM experiments aim to use this boosted power spectrum to probe the total H I content, [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]], and star formation history of galaxies at $z > 6$ [Horlaville et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.2958H/abstract). To isolate the [C II] signal from lower-redshift interloper lines, cross-correlations with 21 cm emission have been proposed as a robust method to map the topology of reionization bubbles [Gong et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...745...49G/abstract). Observational frontiers will also be expanded by proposed future facilities like the Atacama Large Aperture Submillimeter Telescope (AtLAST), which has the potential to detect the "hidden" cold circumgalactic medium by imaging diffuse reservoirs of [C II], [O III], and [C I] extending far beyond galactic disks [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240300924L/abstract).

Finally, the advent of the James Webb Space Telescope has generated powerful new JWST synergy with ALMA. A pressing open question is why many UV-bright galaxies in the Epoch of Reionization exhibit unusually high ratios of the [[o-iii-88-m-line|[O III] 88 μm line]] to [C II] ($>10$) and frequent [C II] non-detections [Arata et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5541A/abstract), [Fujimoto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..146F/abstract). By combining ALMA's far-infrared mapping with JWST's rest-frame optical diagnostics (such as the [[o-iii-5007-line|[O III] 5007 Å line]] and [[n-ii-emission-lines|[N II] emission lines]]), researchers are investigating whether this [C II] deficiency is driven by low [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]] ($Z < 0.1 \ Z_\odot$), porous ISM geometries, or extreme ionization parameters. JWST/NIRSpec observations from the CEERS survey further complicate this picture by suggesting that the specific star formation rate (sSFR)—rather than the absolute star formation rate—is the primary parameter governing ISM ionization conditions across cosmic time [Gupta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RNAAS...8..266G/abstract). To guide future targeted observations, researchers have suggested using the empirical correlation between [[o-iii-88-m-line|[O III] 88 μm line]] and rest-frame $24\ \mu{\rm m}$ continuum emission in local analogs as a predictive tool for estimating line strengths in these high-redshift galaxies [Lambert-Huyghe et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...666A.112L/abstract). Recent modeling suggests that sub-solar carbon-to-oxygen (C/O) abundance ratios may be essential to fully explain the unusually high [O III]/[C II] luminosity ratios observed in early galaxies [Nyhagen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...702A.260N/abstract). Establishing whether the [O III]/[C II] ratio can serve as a robust, empirical proxy for the Lyman continuum escape fraction ($f_{\rm esc}$) is a major frontier in understanding how these early galaxies drove cosmic reionization [Ura et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...948....3U/abstract).
