The **[C II] 158 μm line** is a prominent [[fine-structure-emission-lines|fine-structure transition]] of singly ionized carbon and one of the brightest [[far-infrared-fine-structure-lines|Far-infrared fine structure lines]] in the universe. With an ionization potential slightly lower than that of hydrogen (13.6 eV), the C$^+$ ion is ubiquitous across multiple phases of the interstellar medium (ISM). It serves as the dominant [[astrophysical-cooling-processes|cooling mechanism]] for the cold neutral medium and photodissociation regions (PDRs), balancing the thermal energy inputted by far-ultraviolet (FUV) photoelectrons ejected from dust grains [Hollenbach et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ARA&A..35..179H/abstract). Because of this widespread distribution, integrated [C II] emission represents a complex composite signal originating from dense PDRs, diffuse atomic gas, ionized H II regions, and metal-poor "CO-dark" molecular gas, where carbon remains ionized but CO is completely photodissociated [Carilli et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ARA&A..51..105C/abstract). 

In normal [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], the luminosity of the [C II] line scales robustly with the global star formation rate (SFR), making it a cornerstone diagnostic for extragalactic astronomy. However, this scaling relation frequently breaks down in extreme starbursts and ultraluminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs). In these intense environments, observers note a pronounced drop in the [[c-ii-to-fir-ratio|[C II]-to-FIR ratio]], a phenomenon known as the "[C II] deficit" [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract), [De Looze et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2712D/abstract). The physical origin of this deficit remains a major open question, with competing theories attributing it to the thermal saturation of the C$^+$ fine-structure level, reduced photoelectric heating efficiency from dust grain charging, high dust opacity, or an excess of infrared continuum emitted from deeply embedded protostars. To disentangle these effects and isolate specific ISM phase contributions, astrophysicists frequently combine [C II] data with other [[fir-line-intensity-ratios|FIR line intensity ratios]], using transitions like [[n-ii-emission-lines|[N II] emission lines]] to anchor the ionized gas fraction.

Beyond local galaxies, [C II] 158 μm has emerged as a premier tool for spectroscopic redshift confirmation and kinematic modeling during the Epoch of Reionization (EoR). While standard textbook models historically predicted a slow, gradual build-up of cosmic metals as traced by optical [[c-iv-absorbers|C IV absorbers]], early interferometric detections of massive [C II]-emitting starbursts at $z > 6$ have revealed environments undergoing remarkably rapid chemical enrichment [Maiolino et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...440L..51M/abstract). Velocity-resolved [C II] observations now allow astronomers to distinguish between turbulent rotating disks and chaotic mergers, as well as to map massive, multiphase outflows extending into the circumgalactic medium. Current research heavily focuses on understanding the extreme ISM conditions of the first galaxies, which often exhibit highly elevated ratios of the [[o-iii-88-m-line|[O III] 88 μm line]] to [C II]. Future high-resolution imaging and large-scale intensity mapping aim to resolve the underlying causes of these elevated ratios, probe the hidden [[cold-halo-gas|cold gas in galactic halos]], and characterize the escape fraction of ionizing photons driving cosmic reionization.

## Physical Origins and ISM Phase Contributions

The [C II] 158 μm transition is typically the brightest of the [[far-infrared-fine-structure-lines|Far-infrared fine structure lines]] in [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] and acts as a primary [[astrophysical-cooling-processes|coolant]] of the interstellar medium (ISM). Because the ionization potential of neutral carbon is slightly lower than that of hydrogen ($13.6$ eV), singly ionized carbon (C$^+$) can persist in a variety of gas phases. Consequently, the integrated [C II] emission of a galaxy is a composite signal originating from photodissociation regions (PDRs), the "CO-dark" molecular medium, ionized gas (H II regions), and the cold neutral medium (CNM). Quantifying the fractional contribution of each phase remains a central challenge in extragalactic astrophysics.

**Photodissociation Regions and CO-Dark Gas**
In typical local star-forming systems, dense PDRs generally dominate the [C II] luminosity. In the Milky Way, for example, velocity-resolved surveys indicate that dense PDRs account for $\sim 47\%$ of the global [C II] emission [Pineda et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...554A.103P/abstract). When combining dense PDRs with more diffuse molecular gas, Bayesian radiative transfer models estimate that molecular regions collectively produce $75.8 \pm 5.9\%$ of the Milky Way's total [C II] luminosity, a fraction that scales between 60% and 80% for similar local galaxies depending on their specific star formation rate and gas density [Accurso et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.3315A/abstract). 

Furthermore, [C II] is a critical tracer of "CO-dark" molecular gas—regions shielded well enough to form H$_2$ but irradiated enough to completely photodissociate CO. In the Milky Way, CO-dark gas contributes $\sim 28\%$ to the [C II] luminosity [Pineda et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...554A.103P/abstract). This phase becomes increasingly dominant in low-metallicity environments where decreased dust shielding allows UV photons to penetrate deeper into clouds. In metal-poor dwarf galaxies, between 70% and 100% of the total H$_2$ mass can be CO-dark, making [C II] a far more accurate tracer of the molecular reservoir than traditional CO rotational lines [Madden et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.141M/abstract), [Ramambason et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...681A..14R/abstract). To quantify these varied molecular reservoirs across cosmic time, astronomers have developed multivariate $\alpha_{\rm CO}$ conversion functions that incorporate the $L_{\rm [C~II]}/L_{\rm CO}$ ratio, metallicity, and the galaxy's offset from the star-forming main sequence, allowing for robust molecular gas mass estimates up to $z \sim 2.5$ [Accurso et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.4750A/abstract). Alongside [C II], neutral carbon transitions such as [C I] (1-0) also serve as reliable molecular gas mass tracers relative to traditional CO (1-0) lines up to $z \sim 5$, though the warmer cosmic microwave background at high redshift can significantly affect the observed intensities of these transitions [Frias Castillo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...987..158F/abstract).

**Ionized Gas Fraction**
Because C$^+$ exists in ionized regions alongside H$^+$, the diffuse ionized medium and compact H II regions also contribute to [C II] emission. In typical star-forming disks, the ionized gas fraction is relatively minor, accounting for $\sim 4\%$ in the Milky Way globally [Pineda et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...554A.103P/abstract), 10-20% in Centaurus A [Parkin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...787...16P/abstract), and 10-25% in M33 [Mookerjea et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...586A..37M/abstract). To empirically isolate the ionized contribution, astronomers frequently compare [C II] fluxes with [[n-ii-emission-lines|[N II] emission lines]] (e.g., at 122 μm or 205 μm), as the higher ionization potential of nitrogen ensures N$^+$ originates exclusively in ionized gas [Langer et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...590A..43L/abstract). However, there is significant tension in the literature regarding the ionized gas contribution in extreme or dense environments. While disk-wide averages remain low, some numerical models and targeted observations suggest that the ionized contribution can dominate the [C II] budget in specific regimes; for instance, the H II regions in the dense nuclear starburst of IC 342 are estimated to contribute between 35% and 90% of the observed [C II] emission [Röllig et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...591A..33R/abstract). In the extreme environments of early quasars, the ionized contribution can be even more substantial; multi-line analysis of a $z \approx 6.4$ quasar host galaxy demonstrates that the ionized gas phase can account for approximately one-third of the total gaseous mass budget and up to 50% of the integrated [C II] emission [Decarli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...673A.157D/abstract).

**The Cold Neutral Medium**
[C II] serves as a primary coolant for the diffuse atomic gas, specifically the CNM. The atomic gas contribution is highly dependent on the local galactic environment and interstellar radiation field. In the Milky Way, the cold atomic gas is responsible for roughly 21% of the global [C II] emission [Pineda et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...554A.103P/abstract). Similar radial variations are observed in nearby galaxies like M33, where the CNM accounts for $\sim 15\%$ of the [C II] emission in the inner 2 kpc, but its contribution rises to $\sim 40\%$ in the outer disk [Kramer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...553A.114K/abstract). Fully disentangling these phases requires multi-line diagnostics (pairing [C II] with [O I], [N II], H I, and CO) combined with high spectral resolution to separate kinematic components [Mookerjea et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...586A..37M/abstract). Furthermore, in the host galaxies of Gamma-Ray Bursts such as GRB 980425, [C II], [O I], and CO lines reveal elevated ratios of [[o-iii-88-m-line|[O III] 88 μm line]]-to-FIR, which are likely caused by recent atomic gas inflows that trigger star formation before significant dust can accumulate [Michałowski et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...595A..72M/abstract).

**Quiescent and Elliptical Galaxies**
Beyond actively star-forming disks, [C II] emission is also observed in quiescent galaxies and giant ellipticals. In nearby giant elliptical galaxies, [C II] emission is often co-spatial with H$\alpha$ nebulae and low-entropy hot plasma, suggesting the cold gas originated from cooling instabilities within the hot galactic atmosphere [Werner et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.439.2291W/abstract).

## Thermal Balance and Star Formation Tracing

The thermal equilibrium of the neutral interstellar medium (ISM) is largely governed by the balance between photoelectric heating and [[astrophysical-cooling-processes|gas cooling]] via [[far-infrared-fine-structure-lines|Far-infrared fine structure lines]]. In this regime, the [C II] 158 μm transition serves as the primary coolant of the cold and warm neutral medium, as well as the outer layers of photon-dominated regions (PDRs) [Hollenbach et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ARA&A..35..179H/abstract), [De Looze et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2712D/abstract). The heating of these regions is primarily driven by the photoelectric effect: far-ultraviolet (FUV) photons ($6~{\rm eV} < h\nu < 13.6~{\rm eV}$) eject electrons from dust grains and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs). The excess kinetic energy of these photoelectrons thermalizes and heats the gas, which subsequently cools through the collisional excitation and radiative de-excitation of the C$^+$ ion.

The photoelectric heating efficiency, $\epsilon_{\rm PE}$, is conventionally approximated by the ratio of the dominant cooling lines to the total infrared continuum, typically evaluated as $(L_{\rm [C II]} + L_{\rm [O I]\,63\mu m}) / L_{\rm TIR}$ or simply $L_{\rm [C II]} / L_{\rm FIR}$ [Parkin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...787...16P/abstract). In normal [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], this efficiency typically ranges between $0.1\%$ and $3\%$ [Hughes et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...575A..17H/abstract), [Kramer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...553A.114K/abstract). Spatially resolved studies of nearby disks reveal environmental variations in this efficiency; for example, $\epsilon_{\rm PE}$ increases from the inner to the outer disk in galaxies like M33 [Kramer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...553A.114K/abstract) and NGC 891 [Hughes et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...575A..17H/abstract). However, recent analyses suggest that $L_{\rm TIR}$ traces all dust heating rather than solely the FUV photons capable of ejecting electrons. When normalized strictly by the attenuated FUV emission rather than the total infrared luminosity, the intrinsic heating efficiency appears remarkably constant at $\approx 1.85\%$ across diverse environments, as demonstrated in M31 [Kapala et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...842..128K/abstract).

Because its emission predominantly arises from PDRs heated by the FUV radiation of young, massive stars, the integrated [C II] luminosity, $L_{\rm [CII]}$, is tightly correlated with the global star formation rate (SFR) [De Looze et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2712D/abstract). Alternatively, this correlation might arise indirectly through the Schmidt law, linking star formation dynamically to the cold interstellar medium [De Looze et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2712D/abstract). Empirical calibrations demonstrate a linear or near-linear relationship between $L_{\rm [CII]}$ and SFR, establishing it as a reliable tracer of star formation with an uncertainty of approximately a factor of 2 for local starbursts and normal star-forming disks [De Looze et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...568A..62D/abstract). This robust correlation makes [C II] a highly sought-after diagnostic for mapping star formation in both local and high-redshift systems.

The applicability of local $L_{\rm [CII]}$-SFR relations to high-redshift ($z > 5$) and low-metallicity environments is complex. In metal-poor dwarf galaxies, the [C II]-SFR relation exhibits increased scatter as alternative coolants, such as the [[o-iii-88-m-line|[O III] 88 μm line]], begin to dominate the cooling budget [De Looze et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...568A..62D/abstract). Furthermore, the dominant heating mechanism can shift entirely. In extremely metal-poor galaxies, such as I Zw 18, X-ray heating from bright X-ray binaries can dominate over the traditional FUV photoelectric effect, causing [C II] to act as a tracer of purely atomic gas rather than CO-dark molecular gas [Lebouteiller et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...602A..45L/abstract). 

At high redshift, the literature shows some divergence regarding the evolution of the scaling relation. Some semi-analytical models predict that high-$z$ galaxies should sit below the local relations [Lagache et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...609A.130L/abstract). Conversely, recent observational surveys demonstrate that the fundamental $L_{\rm [CII]}$-SFR relation for many high-$z$ galaxies is in overall good agreement with local calibrations [Mordini et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A..36M/abstract). Despite this general agreement for main-sequence galaxies, deep observations of $z > 6$ systems occasionally yield [C II] luminosities far fainter than predicted by local relations [Knudsen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462L...6K/abstract). This implies a potential fundamental shift in certain populations, likely driven by lower gas-phase metallicities, evolving carbon-to-oxygen abundance ratios, or suppression of the emission contrast by the warmer cosmic microwave background [Lagache et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...609A.130L/abstract), [Arata et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5541A/abstract).

## The [C II] Deficit in Extreme Environments

While $L_{\rm [CII]}$ scales linearly with the star formation rate and infrared luminosity in normal [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], this relationship breaks down in systems with exceptionally high infrared surface brightness, such as luminous infrared galaxies (LIRGs), ultraluminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs), and extreme starbursts. In these intense environments, the [[c-ii-to-fir-ratio|[C II]-to-FIR ratio]] (or [C II] to total infrared luminosity) drops precipitously by more than an order of magnitude—a phenomenon known as the **[C II] deficit** [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract), [De Looze et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2712D/abstract), [De Looze et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...568A..62D/abstract). In typical star-forming systems, this ratio is roughly a few $\times 10^{-3}$, but in intense starbursts it frequently drops below $2 \times 10^{-4}$ [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract), [Maiolino et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...440L..51M/abstract). If standard [C II]-SFR calibrations are applied to ULIRGs, they can underpredict the true SFR by more than an order of magnitude [De Looze et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...568A..62D/abstract).

The magnitude of the [C II] deficit is strongly anti-correlated with the intensity of the far-ultraviolet (FUV) radiation field ($G_0$) and dust temperature. Despite extensive observational mapping, the exact physical origin of the deficit remains an active subject of debate, with several overlapping mechanisms proposed:

*   **Dust Grain Charging:** The primary heating mechanism for the neutral ISM is the photoelectric effect on dust grains. In environments with intense radiation fields, grains become positively charged. This increases the work function required to eject subsequent electrons, drastically reducing the photoelectric heating efficiency of the gas and consequently lowering [C II] emission relative to the TIR [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract), [Ibar et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449.2498I/abstract).
*   **Thermal Saturation:** The upper fine-structure level of the C$^+$ ion sits at an excitation temperature of $E/k \approx 91$ K. In highly concentrated starbursts where the gas temperature exceeds this threshold, the level populations saturate. Because thermal saturation limits further [[astrophysical-cooling-processes|Astrophysical Cooling Processes]] via this transition, any additional heating cannot increase the [C II] line luminosity. This leads to a relative drop in the ratio as the dust continues to radiate efficiently [Muñoz et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.2085M/abstract).
*   **FIR Excess and ISM Topology:** Rather than a strict deficit in [C II] emission, some models frame the phenomenon as a "FIR excess" driven by deeply embedded protostars [Pabst et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...651A.111P/abstract). In environments with deeply embedded protostars, dust absorbs a significant fraction of the ionizing photons before they can interact with the gas. Furthermore, variations in the ionization parameter and the hardness of the FUV spectrum can alter the partitioning of absorbed energy [Kapala et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...842..128K/abstract).
*   **Dust Opacity Effects:** Detailed studies of systems like the massive starburst galaxy GN20 at $z \sim 4$ suggest that the deficit may also be partially driven by dust opacity. High-opacity dust models for GN20 indicate dust temperatures of $\sim 52$ K, which is consistent with high [C I] excitation, implying that some of the apparent [C II] deficit may be caused by high infrared optical depth masking emission rather than just low intrinsic gas temperatures or reduced heating efficiency [Cortzen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...634L..14C/abstract).

Resolving whether the deficit is primarily driven by the intrinsic thermodynamics of the gas (e.g., thermal saturation), dust opacity effects, or the macroscopic topology of the ISM (e.g., deeply embedded protostars) remains a critical open question for interpreting [[fir-line-intensity-ratios|FIR line intensity ratios]] across cosmic time.

## [C II] as a Diagnostic of the Epoch of Reionization

As one of the most luminous [[far-infrared-fine-structure-lines|Far-infrared fine structure lines]], the [C II] 158 μm transition has become a foundational tool for the spectroscopic confirmation of high-redshift galaxies and the characterization of the interstellar medium (ISM) during the Epoch of Reionization (EoR). The line was first detected at $z = 6.42$ in the quasar host SDSS J1148+5251, revealing an extreme starburst with an estimated star formation rate of ${\sim}3000~M_{\odot}\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ [Maiolino et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...440L..51M/abstract). Furthermore, observations of multiple $z > 6.6$ quasar host galaxies reveal that both [C II] and the underlying 158 μm continuum emission correlate with the quasar's bolometric luminosity, establishing [C II] as a crucial tool for probing the host galaxies of the first supermassive black holes [Venemans et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...816...37V/abstract). Since then, deep millimeter-interferometry surveys have established [C II] as a primary probe of the earliest stages of galaxy assembly.

At $z > 6$, observations consistently reveal elevated luminosity ratios of the [[o-iii-88-m-line|[O III] 88 μm line]] to [C II] 158 μm, reaching up to $\sim 10$ [Arata et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5541A/abstract), [Fujimoto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..146F/abstract). This strong [O III] enhancement is attributed to the primitive conditions of the early ISM, which is characterized by hard radiation fields (high ionization parameters), low gas-phase metallicities, and suppressed carbon-to-oxygen (C/O) abundance ratios in early stellar populations [Arata et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5541A/abstract), [Nyhagen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...702A.260N/abstract). Because this high-ionization state can facilitate the escape of ionizing radiation, the $[{\rm O~III}]/[{\rm C~II}]$ ratio has been proposed as a diagnostic of the Lyman continuum escape fraction ($f_{\rm esc}$) in the early universe, helping to identify the specific sources that actively contributed to reionization [Ura et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...948....3U/abstract), [Fujimoto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..146F/abstract).

Despite its brightness in local [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], [C II] emission has proven unexpectedly faint or completely absent in several UV-bright Lyman-alpha emitters (LAEs) at $z > 6$, such as Himiko and z7_GSD_3811 [Walter et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...752...93W/abstract), [Binggeli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646A..26B/abstract). These non-detections indicate that the galaxies possess extremely low metallicities ($Z \lesssim 0.1 Z_{\odot}$) and low dust obscuration [Knudsen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462L...6K/abstract), [Binggeli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646A..26B/abstract). This phenomenon extends to even higher redshifts; in the remarkably bright galaxy JADES-GS-z14-0 at $z=14.179$, a massive pristine neutral gas reservoir ($\log N_{\rm H~I} \approx 22.3$) was identified via a damped Ly$\alpha$ profile, and its [C II] non-detection is fully consistent with appropriate scaling to this chemically pristine H I gas [Heintz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...987L...2H/abstract). 

While many early galaxies exhibit low metallicities, this is not universally the case. For instance, the possible detection of [[n-ii-emission-lines| [N II] 122 μm]] and [O III] 52 μm lines in the galaxy A1689-zD1 suggests near-solar metallicity in some galaxies even at $z=7.13$, challenging typical low-metallicity assumptions for the early universe [Killi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.2526K/abstract). This rapid enrichment is vividly illustrated in SPT0311-58, a system of two interacting galaxies at $z=6.9$. Wide variations in line ratios (e.g., $[{\rm O~III}]/[{\rm C~II}]$) and ionization parameters across this system demonstrate that nearly solar metallicities can be achieved just 800 Myr after the Big Bang [Litke et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...949...87L/abstract). Complementing far-infrared studies, rest-frame UV observations are pushing metal detection even further; the recent detection of the C III] doublet in the galaxy GS-z12 at $z=12.5$ represents the most distant metal line detection to date, providing crucial constraints on carbon enrichment just 350 Myr after the Big Bang [D'Eugenio et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.152D/abstract). Furthermore, the detection of [C II] at these extreme redshifts is fundamentally complicated by CMB suppression. Because the temperature of the Cosmic Microwave Background scales as $T_{\rm CMB} \propto (1+z)$, it provides a strong, warm background at $z > 6$ that severely diminishes the contrast of the 158 μm emission line, requiring careful correction when calibrating [C II] as a global star-formation tracer [Lagache et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...609A.130L/abstract).

When detected with high spatial and spectral resolution, [C II] serves as a powerful kinematic probe of the first galaxies. The [C II] line profile and velocity field allow astronomers to distinguish between turbulent, rotating disks and chaotic, merger-driven systems [De Breuck et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Msngr.156...38B/abstract), [Kohandel et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.3007K/abstract), [Fujimoto et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...911...99F/abstract). Dynamical mass estimates derived from high-redshift [C II] line widths are generally reliable to within a factor of two if the inclination angle is known, though inclination effects can push the peak flux below detection limits in edge-on systems [Kohandel et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.3007K/abstract). 

Deep interferometric mapping has additionally revealed that [C II] emission can extend significantly beyond the rest-frame UV continuum. In the $z=7.13$ galaxy A1689-zD1, [C II] traces a halo out to a radius of ${\sim}12$ kpc, interpreted as the cooling of hot, ionized outflows into the extended circumgalactic medium [Akins et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934...64A/abstract). Finally, in the metal-poor conditions of the EoR where CO molecules are largely photodissociated, [C II] remains a critical tracer of "CO-dark" molecular gas. Cosmological simulations of $z \simeq 6$ galaxies predict a sublinear relation between $L_{\rm [C~II]}$ and molecular gas mass ($M_{\rm mol}$), yielding a median conversion factor of $\alpha_{\rm [C~II]} \simeq 18~M_{\odot}/L_{\odot}$ [Vizgan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929...92V/abstract).

## Gas Kinematics and Galactic-Scale Outflows

Velocity-resolved spectroscopy of the [C II] 158 μm line is a uniquely powerful tool for probing the kinematics of both local and high-redshift [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]]. Because the [C II] line is optically thin in many environments and bright enough to map at kiloparsec scales with interferometers like ALMA, its emission profile can be used to distinguish between rotationally supported disks—such as the turbulent, rotating disk identified in the $z=4.8$ starburst ALESS73.1 [De Breuck et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Msngr.156...38B/abstract)—and chaotic, merger-driven kinematics, as seen in the $z=7.54$ quasar host ULAS J1342+0928 [Bañados et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881L..23B/abstract). 

[C II] kinematics also provide robust estimates of a galaxy's dynamical mass. Theoretical modeling indicates that high-redshift [C II] emitters follow a Tully-Fisher-like scaling relation, given by:
$$L_{\rm [C II]} \propto (\mathrm{FWHM})^{1.80 \pm 0.35}$$
When a galaxy's inclination is known, the [C II] line width yields a dynamical mass accurate to within a factor of two. However, unresolved edge-on systems can suffer from surface brightness dimming, as their large intrinsic line widths push the peak flux below detection limits, potentially leading to observational non-detections [Kohandel et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.3007K/abstract). Furthermore, [C II] emission can be significantly enhanced in collisional environments through turbulent dissipation and shocks, as observed in the 35 kpc intergalactic filament of Stephan's Quintet, highlighting its sensitivity to complex dynamical interactions [Appleton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777...66A/abstract).

Beyond bulk rotation, [C II] velocity maps routinely detect massive gas outflows driven by intense starbursts and AGN feedback. Because [C II] traces the dominant cold neutral phase of the interstellar medium, it provides critical constraints on the mass-loading factors of galactic winds. In the local starburst galaxy NGC 2146, a multi-phase superwind exhibits a velocity dispersion of $\sim 250 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ in [C II] and other [[far-infrared-fine-structure-lines|Far-infrared fine structure lines]], tracing shock-heated gas expanding up to 2.5 kpc above the disk [Kreckel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...790...26K/abstract). In active galaxies, such as the local AGN host HE 1353-1917, the presence of a global [C II] luminosity excess has been identified as a direct signature of a massive, multiphase outflow intersecting the cold galactic disk [Smirnova-Pinchukova et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...626L...3S/abstract).

At high redshift, deep [C II] observations frequently reveal massive, extended circumgalactic gas structures. Discoveries include 15-kpc-long [C II] gas plumes exhibiting velocity shifts up to $400 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Solimano et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.145S/abstract), and extended halos reaching radii of $\sim 12 \text{ kpc}$ in sub-$L^\star$ systems at $z > 7$ [Akins et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934...64A/abstract). Additionally, ALMA surveys of damped Ly$\alpha$ absorber (DLA) galaxies at $z \sim 4$ demonstrate a strong correlation between [C II] detectability and metallicity ($[{\rm M/H}] > -1.5$), effectively identifying metal-enriched neutral gas reservoirs within the circumgalactic medium at impact parameters up to 59 kpc [Neeleman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983...26N/abstract). However, interpreting these complex velocity fields requires caution. The literature shows significant tension in classifying high-redshift kinematics, as spectral and spatial offsets are frequently observed between neutral gas tracers like [C II] and highly ionized tracers such as the [[o-iii-88-m-line|[O III] 88 μm line]]. While such offsets can be misinterpreted as chaotic major mergers in low-resolution data, they often trace complex multi-phase outflows [Akins et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934...64A/abstract). Disentangling these phases necessitates high spatial resolution; for instance, multi-wavelength analysis of the $z \sim 5.5$ galaxy HZ4 demonstrates that its neutral outflow (traced by [C II]) and ionized outflow are roughly co-spatial, but the mass-loading factor of the neutral phase exceeds that of the ionized phase by two orders of magnitude [Parlanti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240719008P/abstract).

## Multi-line Diagnostics and PDR Modeling

The [C II] 158 $\mu$m line originates from a complex mixture of interstellar medium (ISM) phases, including photodissociation regions (PDRs), ionized H II regions, and CO-dark molecular gas. To break the degeneracies between gas density, radiation field hardness, and ISM phase contributions, [C II] must be modeled in conjunction with other [[far-infrared-fine-structure-lines|Far-infrared fine structure lines]]. 

In PDRs, combining [C II] with the [O I] 63 $\mu$m and 145 $\mu$m lines serves as a primary diagnostic of the hydrogen nucleus density $n$ and the incident far-ultraviolet (FUV) radiation field $G_0$. Because the [O I] 63 $\mu$m line has a higher excitation temperature and critical density than [C II], the $[\mathrm{O~I}] / [\mathrm{C~II}]$ ratio is highly sensitive to the gas density and temperature. Meanwhile, the ratio of the combined fine-structure line cooling to the total far-infrared (FIR) continuum traces the photoelectric heating efficiency of the gas:
$$ \epsilon_{\mathrm{PE}} \approx \frac{L_{\mathrm{[C~II]}} + L_{\mathrm{[O~I]}}}{L_{\mathrm{FIR}}} $$
By comparing these [[fir-line-intensity-ratios|FIR line intensity ratios]] against theoretical PDR models, typical star-forming environments are found to span densities of $n \sim 10^2$--$10^4 \ \mathrm{cm}^{-3}$ and radiation fields of $G_0 \sim 10^0$--$10^3$ [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract), [Kramer et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...639A..61K/abstract), [Parkin et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...776...65P/abstract). Spatially resolved mapping is crucial for interpreting these ratios in complex environments. For instance, in the resolved $z = 1.8$ starbursting quasar SDSS J1000, the $[{\rm C~II}]/{\rm FIR}$ ratio varies by a factor of 30 across the source. These extreme variations can be successfully explained by PDR models featuring high radiation fields (up to $5000 \ G_0$) interacting with highly fractured cloud structures with small characteristic radii ($\sim 3.5$ pc) [Rooney et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...987...61R/abstract).

A major challenge in interpreting [C II] emission is isolating the fraction of the line flux that arises from ionized gas. Because the ionization potential of carbon is slightly less than that of hydrogen, $\mathrm{C}^+$ exists in both neutral and ionized media. Nitrogen, however, requires a higher ionization potential than hydrogen, restricting $\mathrm{N}^+$ exclusively to H II regions. Consequently, the [[n-ii-emission-lines|[N II] emission lines]] can be used to anchor the ionized gas contribution to [C II] [Langer et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...590A..43L/abstract). The [N II] 205 μm line is particularly useful for measuring the electron density of this ionized phase, which typically ranges from $n_e \sim 5$--$21 \ \mathrm{cm}^{-3}$ in extended boundary layers [Langer et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...576A...1L/abstract). Observations of diverse galaxy samples demonstrate that the H II region contribution to [C II] can vary widely, from $\sim 10\%$ in some systems to greater than $50\%$ in dense nuclear starbursts [Decarli et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782L..17D/abstract), [Röllig et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...591A..33R/abstract). Additionally, astronomers have found a strong correlation between the [[o-iii-88-m-line|[O III] 88 μm line]] and the 24 μm continuum, which serves as a tool to disentangle ionized and PDR phases along the line of sight [Lambert-Huyghe et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...666A.112L/abstract).

Far-infrared line ratios are also increasingly utilized as dust-insensitive metallicity diagnostics. Ratios such as $[\mathrm{O~III}] \ 88 \ \mu\mathrm{m} / [\mathrm{N~II}] \ 122 \ \mu\mathrm{m}$ provide robust probes of the gas-phase metallicity, though they exhibit a secondary dependence on the ionization parameter [Pereira-Santaella et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.1218P/abstract), [Rigopoulou et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473...20R/abstract). Beyond carbon and oxygen, other fine-structure lines provide valuable context for evolving ISM conditions. The S32 ratio ($[\mathrm{S~III}] / [\mathrm{S~II}]$) has been introduced as a new diagnostic that is less sensitive to metallicity than the traditional $[\mathrm{O~III}] / [\mathrm{O~II}]$ ratio, providing a robust way to characterize evolving ISM conditions at $z \sim 1.5$ [Sanders et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888L..11S/abstract). To accurately map these parameter spaces, observers extensively employ Cloudy photoionization models coupled with Bayesian inference to disentangle the physical drivers of the line emission across different galaxy populations [Fernández-Ontiveros et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..226...19F/abstract), [Accurso et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.3315A/abstract), [Madden et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.141M/abstract).

At high redshifts ($z > 6$), multi-line diagnostics have revealed distinct ISM conditions in the early Universe. Galaxies in the Epoch of Reionization frequently exhibit extreme enhancements in the [[o-iii-88-m-line|[O III] 88 μm line]] relative to [C II], with $L_{\mathrm{[O~III]}} / L_{\mathrm{[C~II]}}$ ratios routinely reaching up to $\sim 10$ [Arata et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5541A/abstract). Photoionization modeling indicates that this enhancement cannot be explained by standard local ISM conditions; rather, it is driven by a combination of high ionization parameters, lower gas-phase metallicities, and suppressed carbon-to-oxygen (C/O) abundance ratios characteristic of young, rapidly enriching stellar populations [Nyhagen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...702A.260N/abstract), [Fujimoto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..146F/abstract).

## Historical Development and Observational Facilities

Because the rest-frame 158 $\mu$m transition lies in the far-infrared, it is heavily absorbed by Earth's atmospheric water vapor. Consequently, the historical development of [C II] observations in the local Universe has been closely tied to the advancement of airborne and space-based observatories. Following pioneering measurements with early balloon-borne experiments and the Kuiper Airborne Observatory (KAO)—which first demonstrated the tremendous luminosity of the line—subsequent space missions dramatically expanded the study of [[far-infrared-fine-structure-lines|Far-infrared fine structure lines]].

**Space-Based Infrared Observatories**
The Infrared Space Observatory (ISO) and its Long Wavelength Spectrometer (LWS) provided the first large-scale systematic surveys of [C II] emission in extragalactic sources. ISO observations of normal [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] and starbursts confirmed the [C II] line as a dominant pathway for [[astrophysical-cooling-processes|Astrophysical Cooling Processes]] in the neutral interstellar medium. These early datasets were crucial in quantifying the [[c-ii-to-fir-ratio|[C II]-to-FIR ratio]] and revealing its non-linear decline at high infrared luminosities, a phenomenon characterized as the [C II] deficit [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract), [Pierini et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003A&A...397..871P/abstract).

The Herschel Space Observatory represented a major leap in both sensitivity and spatial resolution. Its Photodetector Array Camera and Spectrometer (PACS) and Heterodyne Instrument for the Far-Infrared (HIFI) enabled detailed spatial and spectral mapping of [C II]. Velocity-resolved HIFI observations, such as those from the GOT C+ survey, were instrumental in tracing the different phases of the interstellar medium across the Galactic plane and mapping the distribution of "CO-dark" molecular gas [Pineda et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...554A.103P/abstract). Concurrently, PACS spectroscopy of low-metallicity systems in the Dwarf Galaxy Survey revealed highly porous interstellar structures where [C II] emission arises over extended physical scales rather than strictly from compact dense clouds [Cormier et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...578A..53C/abstract).

**Airborne High-Resolution Spectroscopy**
The Stratospheric Observatory for Infrared Astronomy (SOFIA) provided critical follow-up capabilities. Using the GREAT and FIFI-LS instruments, astronomers achieved extremely high spectral resolution, allowing them to disentangle the kinematic components of [C II] emission. Velocity-resolved SOFIA observations of nearby galaxies, such as IC 342 and NGC 4214, have been used to carefully separate the contributions of neutral atomic, molecular, and ionized gas phases to the integrated [C II] line flux [Röllig et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...591A..33R/abstract), [Fahrion et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...599A...9F/abstract).

**Ground-Based Interferometry at High Redshift**
For galaxies at redshifts $z \gtrsim 4$, cosmic expansion shifts the [C II] 158 $\mu$m line into the submillimeter atmospheric windows accessible from ground-based facilities. The first high-redshift detection of the line was achieved using the IRAM 30-m telescope in a quasar host galaxy at $z = 6.42$, revealing a massive starburst forming $\sim 3000$ $M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$ in the early Universe [Maiolino et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...440L..51M/abstract). 

The advent of the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) has revolutionized the study of [C II] at cosmic dawn. ALMA's unprecedented sensitivity and spatial resolution allow for the morphological and kinematic mapping of [C II] emission in galaxies during the Epoch of Reionization. These resolved observations have been used to identify turbulent, rotating gas disks in early starbursts [De Breuck et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Msngr.156...38B/abstract), differentiate between ordered rotation and chaotic major mergers [Kohandel et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.3007K/abstract), [Bañados et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881L..23B/abstract), and detect extended circumgalactic gas plumes and multiphase outflows [Akins et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934...64A/abstract). Today, ALMA utilizes the [C II] line as a workhorse diagnostic to constrain the dynamical masses, kinematics, and star formation rates of the earliest known galaxies. Comprehensive recent reviews underscore how far-infrared fine-structure lines have become foundational tools for tracing internal dynamics and the overarching baryon cycle in galaxies across the epoch of cosmic reionization [Decarli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&ARv..33....4D/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the [C II] 158 μm line is well established as a workhorse diagnostic for the interstellar medium (ISM), several fundamental questions regarding its origin and utility remain unresolved. A persistent debate centers on the physical driver of the "[C II] deficit"—the observed decrease in the [[c-ii-to-fir-ratio| $L_{\rm [C\,II]}/L_{\rm IR}$ heating efficiency]] in high-luminosity infrared systems. It remains unclear whether this deficit is primarily driven by the physical properties of the gas, such as the thermal saturation of the ${\rm C}^+$ fine-structure state at temperatures $>91$ K [Muñoz et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.2085M/abstract), a reduction in photoelectric heating efficiency due to dust grain charging in intense radiation fields [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...561..766M/abstract), [Ibar et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449.2498I/abstract), or the topology of the ISM, such as a "far-infrared excess" originating from deeply embedded protostars [Pabst et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...651A.111P/abstract). Disentangling these mechanisms will require unprecedented *spatial resolution* to map the multiphase ISM on sub-kiloparsec scales.

At high redshifts ($z > 6$), observers have noted unexpectedly high luminosity ratios between the [[o-iii-88-m-line|[O III] 88 μm line]] and [C II] 158 μm, often reaching up to $\sim 10$ [Arata et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5541A/abstract). The leading explanations invoke either low carbon-to-oxygen (C/O) abundance ratios in the primitive ISM [Nyhagen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...702A.260N/abstract) or the presence of extended, low-surface-brightness [C II] halos that dilute the central emission [Akins et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934...64A/abstract). Furthermore, the non-detection of [C II] in several UV-bright $z > 7$ galaxies remains puzzling. This absence could stem from extreme metal poverty ($Z \lesssim 0.1 Z_\odot$) [Binggeli et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646A..26B/abstract), unusually high gas densities that suppress emission [Kaasinen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...671A..29K/abstract), or the attenuating effects of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) at early epochs [Lagache et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...609A.130L/abstract). Addressing these mysteries relies heavily on *JWST synergies*, which allow astronomers to combine rest-frame optical data—such as the [[o-iii-5007-line|[O III] 5007 Å line]]—with ALMA far-infrared maps to accurately constrain ionization parameters, metallicities, and the escape fraction of ionizing photons [Fujimoto et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..146F/abstract).

Interpreting these high-redshift observations requires advanced *galaxy formation simulations* that couple non-equilibrium chemistry with radiative transfer. Recent numerical efforts (e.g., SIMBA, COLDSIM, MARIGOLD, and Althæa) are critical for calibrating the conversion of [C II] luminosity to molecular gas mass, particularly in metal-poor [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] where the majority of the ${\rm H}_2$ reservoir is "CO-dark" [Kohandel et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.3007K/abstract), [Vizgan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929...92V/abstract), [Casavecchia et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.106C/abstract), [Khatri et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241109755K/abstract). Simulations and high-resolution multi-wavelength observations also underscore that high spatial and spectral resolution is vital to accurately interpret galaxy kinematics; without it, low-resolution observations can cause observers to misclassify galaxy mergers as rotating disks [Parlanti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240719008P/abstract).

Finally, future observational strategies will pivot toward large-scale mappings of the circumgalactic and intergalactic media. Discoveries of extended 15 kpc [C II] gas plumes in high-redshift systems indicate the presence of massive cold gas reservoirs in the CGM, suggesting that [C II] can trace the [[cold-halo-gas|'hidden cold CGM']] associated with galactic outflows and accretion streams [Solimano et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240104919S/abstract), [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240300924L/abstract). Furthermore, [C II] provides a unique pathway to measure the neutral atomic gas in the early universe. By using [C II] to infer the H I gas mass ($M_{\rm HI}$) in individual galaxies out to $z \sim 6$, studies have revealed that H I mass begins to systematically exceed stellar mass at $z > 1$ [Heintz et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922..147H/abstract). On cosmic scales, [C II] *intensity mapping* will provide a statistical census of star formation during the Epoch of Reionization. Cross-correlating [C II] intensity maps with 21 cm H I emission will be a powerful probe of the topology of reionization, enabling measurements of ionized bubble sizes and the mean ionization fraction that are currently inaccessible via targeted galaxy surveys [Gong et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...745...49G/abstract), [Padmanabhan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488.3014P/abstract).
