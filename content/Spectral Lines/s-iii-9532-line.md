The **[S III] 9532 Å line** is a prominent near-infrared forbidden [[fine-structure-emission-lines|fine-structure transition]] of doubly ionized sulfur ($S^{2+}$). Emitted primarily by astrophysical plasmas in H II regions, planetary nebulae, and active galactic nuclei, it serves as a critical diagnostic for the ionization state and metallicity of the interstellar medium (ISM). While standard textbook treatments of extragalactic spectra frequently emphasize rest-optical and ultraviolet metal absorption lines (such as Mg II, C IV, or O VI) for tracing chemical enrichment, the [S III] 9532 Å line—along with its doublet companion, the [[9069-line|λ9069 line]]—offers a distinct observational advantage in emission-line studies. Because it is situated in the near-infrared, the 9532 Å line suffers significantly less attenuation from interstellar dust than classic optical tracers like the [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]] or the [[o-iii-5007-line|[O III] 5007 Å line]]. In environments where visual extinction exceeds $A_V \approx 3$ magnitudes, [S III] 9532 Å often becomes the superior, and sometimes only, viable probe of gas-phase conditions [Kistiakowsky et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993AJ....105.2199K/abstract).

A primary application of the [S III] 9532 Å line is its use in the $S_{32}$ diagnostic ratio, defined as the flux ratio of the [S III] $\lambda\lambda$9069, 9532 doublet to the lower-ionization [[s-ii-emission-lines|[S II] emission lines]] ($\lambda\lambda$6716, 6731). This ratio is a highly sensitive indicator of the ionization parameter ($U$) and the hardness of the incident ionizing radiation field. Unlike the analogous oxygen ratio ($O_{32}$), $S_{32}$ exhibits only a weak dependence on gas-phase metallicity, making it an exceptionally robust tool for [[nebular-emission-and-diagnostics|Nebular Emission and Diagnostics]] across a variety of environments. In local studies, these sulfur lines are used to anchor Galactic chemical evolution models, trace radial abundance gradients, and distinguish between stellar photoionization and shock-heating mechanisms in Low-Ionization Nuclear Emission-line Regions (LINERs) [Kirhakos et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989PASP..101..949K/abstract). 

Historically limited to the study of luminous, highly obscured local sources and distant radio galaxies [Rawlings et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991MNRAS.251P..17R/abstract), the diagnostic utility of the [S III] 9532 Å line has been drastically expanded by the advent of sensitive near-infrared spectrographs and the *James Webb Space Telescope* (JWST). The line is now a pillar of high-redshift astrophysics, routinely used to demonstrate that typical star-forming galaxies at "Cosmic Noon" ($z \sim 1.5$) possess significantly harder ionizing radiation fields than local H II regions [Sanders et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888L..11S/abstract). This article explores the atomic physics and diagnostic power of the [S III] 9532 Å line, traces its application in abundance gradients and active galaxy classification, and highlights ongoing observational frontiers.

## Physical Principles and Atomic Properties

As an $\alpha$-process element, sulfur is produced primarily following carbon and oxygen burning in massive stars via primary nucleosynthesis. Consequently, its nucleosynthetic yield depends very little on the initial stellar metallicity, making it a stable tracer of chemical enrichment [Rubin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0804.0828R/abstract). In ionized astrophysical plasmas, S$^{2+}$ is frequently the dominant ionization state of sulfur. Measurements of the near-infrared [S III] λλ9069, 9532 doublet therefore serve as highly accurate tracers of the ionic S$^{2+}$ abundance, directly improving upon optical-only studies that must rely heavily on uncertain ionization correction factors [Kwitter et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003PASP..115...80K/abstract). 

In addition to abundance measurements, the [[atomic-fine-structure-lines|fine-structure lines]] of S$^{2+}$ are sensitive probes of electron density. The ratio of the mid-infrared [S III] 18.71 μm to [S III] 33.48 μm transitions serves as a reliable density indicator, yielding typical values of 300-600 cm$^{-3}$ across various star-forming and active galactic nuclei environments [Dale et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...693.1821D/abstract). Comparisons between [O III] and [S III] density diagnostics in local dwarf galaxies like IC 10 suggest that these ions trace distinctly different gas components; notably, S$^{2+}$ is found to be less sensitive to radiation field fluctuations than O$^{2+}$ [Polles et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..117P/abstract).

While [S III] lines are a standard component of photoionization models, the exact physical driver of sulfur emission in some environments remains a subject of active debate. The absolute and relative strengths of intermediate-ionization sulfur lines compared to low-ionization species are highly sensitive to electron temperature and the hardness of the radiation field. This allows astronomers to model the thermodynamic state of the gas and differentiate between competing heating mechanisms, such as dilute power-law continuums from accreting black holes or collisional heating from shocks [Kirhakos et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989PASP..101..949K/abstract). Beyond emission, observations of absorption transitions provide vital clues to the multiphase interstellar medium. For example, comparisons between S III and [[al-iii-doublet|Al III absorption]] have provided compelling evidence for the presence of dust grains and constrained their relative destruction rates in the Galactic warm ionized medium .

## Diagnostic Utility in Dusty Environments

The core observational advantage of the [S III] 9532 Å line in [[nebular-emission-and-diagnostics|Nebular Emission and Diagnostics]] stems from its rest-frame wavelength. In regions heavily obscured by dust, the attenuation of shorter-wavelength transitions such as the [[o-iii-5007-line|[O III] 5007 Å line]] or the optical [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]] can be prohibitive. Photoionization models and observations demonstrate that in environments where the visual extinction exceeds $A_V > 3$ magnitudes, the observed intensity of the [S III] 9532 Å line surpasses that of [O III] 5007 Å. This reduced sensitivity to dust attenuation makes near-infrared spectroscopy of [S III] a critical diagnostic tool for discovering and analyzing distant Galactic planetary nebulae that lie hidden behind thick columns of the interstellar medium [Kistiakowsky et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993AJ....105.2199K/abstract). In such planetary nebulae, the peak [O III] 5007 Å luminosity is found to be surprisingly independent of galaxy type, a uniformity likely driven by an underlying relationship between central stellar luminosity and dust-to-gas ratios [Yao et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957...30Y/abstract).

The utility of the near-infrared line extends well beyond the Milky Way to extragalactic dusty starbursts and heavily obscured active galactic nuclei. It has been successfully employed to trace ionized gas and confirm redshifts in high-redshift ($z > 1$) radio galaxies, providing a window into the physical conditions of systems where rest-frame ultraviolet and optical lines are severely suppressed [Rawlings et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991MNRAS.251P..17R/abstract). In other dusty and edge-on systems, optical lines like Hα and [[s-ii-emission-lines|[S II]]] remain vital for studying the disk-halo interface and mapping 'chimneys' that facilitate the passage of ultraviolet photons from the galactic disk into the extended halo [Ferguson et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996AJ....112.2567F/abstract). Furthermore, mid-infrared [[neon-emission-lines|neon lines]] (such as [[ne-ii-emission-lines|[Ne II] 15.5 μm]] and [Ne III] 12.8 μm) provide a predominantly unobscured baseline that allows astronomers to rigorously calibrate dust attenuation corrections for highly extincted optical lines, such as H$\alpha$ and [O III], achieving accuracies within factors of 2-3 [Wild et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410.1593W/abstract).

However, while highly beneficial for targeted spectroscopy, the sheer strength of the [S III] 9532 Å line in dusty, intensely star-forming regions can act as a contaminant in broadband imaging. In young massive clusters ($\lesssim 5$ Myr) embedded in dusty starbursts, intense [S III] $\lambda\lambda$9069, 9532 emission—along with the associated nebular continuum—can severely contaminate near-infrared photometry, such as the widely used HST F814W $I$-band. If this nebular line emission is not properly accounted for, it can artificially inflate the apparent luminosity of young stellar populations, leading to skewed estimates of cluster mass, age, and local visual extinction [Reines et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...708...26R/abstract).

## The $S_{32}$ Ratio and High-Redshift Evolution

When quantifying the ionization state of astrophysical plasmas, the [S III] 9532 Å and [[9069-line|9069 Å]] fine-structure doublet components are frequently combined with the lower-ionization [[s-ii-emission-lines|[S II] emission lines]] (λλ6716, 6731) to form the sulfur ionization diagnostic, $S_{32}$:

$$S_{32} \equiv \frac{\mathrm{[S~III]} \lambda\lambda9069, 9532}{\mathrm{[S~II]} \lambda\lambda6716, 6731}$$

By comparing the relative populations of S$^{++}$ and S$^+$, the $S_{32}$ ratio provides a sensitive measure of the ionization parameter ($U$) and the hardness of the ionizing radiation field. Crucially, unlike the widely used $O_{32}$ ratio ([O III]/[O II]), $S_{32}$ exhibits a relatively shallow anti-correlation with gas-phase metallicity. This weak metallicity dependence isolates changes in the radiation field from chemical enrichment effects, establishing $S_{32}$ as a particularly robust tool for probing evolving ISM conditions across cosmic time [Sanders et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191013594S/abstract), [Sanders et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888L..11S/abstract).

Comparisons of the $S_{32}$ ratio reveal critical differences between local galaxies and their high-redshift counterparts. Observations from the MOSDEF survey at $z \sim 1.5$ demonstrate that, at a fixed stellar mass, high-redshift galaxies are systematically offset toward lower $S_{32}$ and higher [S II]/H$\alpha$ line ratios for a given [O III]/H$\beta$ value when compared to local H II regions. Detailed modeling indicates that these observed shifts cannot be explained merely by an elevated ionization parameter; instead, they are driven by a harder ionizing spectrum at fixed oxygen abundance (O/H) in high-redshift galaxies [Sanders et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888L..11S/abstract). Consequently, $S_{32}$ reveals that chemically young, $\alpha$-enhanced massive stars dictate the high-redshift ionizing field [Shapley et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..242S/abstract).

When utilizing $S_{32}$ to contrast distant galaxies with local baselines, proper accounting of the diffuse ionized gas (DIG) is required. The DIG is characterized by warmer temperatures and softer ionizing spectra, which strongly enhance [[low-excitation-emission-lines|Low-excitation emission lines]] such as [S II] relative to H$\alpha$ [Mathis et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...544..347M/abstract), [Madsen et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...652..401M/abstract). Consequently, integrated galaxy spectra at $z \sim 0$ can have artificially suppressed $S_{32}$ ratios. Accurate evolutionary studies therefore require baseline comparisons to isolated local H II regions where DIG contributions are negligible [Sanders et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191013594S/abstract).

The exact evolution of the ionizing spectrum at even earlier epochs has been a major focus of JWST. Recent JWST data reveal that ISM ionizing conditions at fixed gas-phase metallicity actually remain largely invariant at $z \gtrsim 3.5$, with the ionizing spectrum only beginning to soften as cosmic time proceeds toward $z \sim 2$ [Clarke et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260111092C/abstract). Regardless of the precise evolutionary timeline, the [S III] 9532 Å line remains an indispensable tool for breaking degeneracies between the ionization parameter and radiation field hardness in the distant universe. Recent advances with JWST's NIRSpec integral field spectroscopy have even enabled spatially resolved mapping of the [S III] 9532 Å transition at $z \sim 3.5$, facilitating detailed studies of outflows and chemical enrichment within the close environments of candidate protocluster members [Lamperti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A.153L/abstract).

## Galactic Chemical Evolution and Abundance Gradients

Because near-infrared sulfur transitions bypass the heavy dust obscuration found in the galactic plane, [S III] is uniquely suited for probing abundance gradients across the Milky Way and local spiral galaxies. Studies of compact H II regions have utilized sulfur emission to establish well-defined radial abundance gradients. By combining infrared fine-structure and near-infrared line data, the Galactic sulfur abundance gradient as a function of galactocentric distance $D_G$ (in kpc) has been robustly quantified as:

$$[\text{S}/\text{H}] = (-4.45 \pm 0.04) - (0.063 \pm 0.006) D_G$$
[Afflerbach et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...478..190A/abstract). This steady decline corresponds to an overall decrease in gas-phase metallicity from approximately $2 Z_\odot$ in the inner Galaxy to roughly $1 Z_\odot$ in the outer disk [Giveon et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...566..880G/abstract).

A critical diagnostic in galactic chemical evolution is the sulfur-to-oxygen ratio (S/O). Because both are $\alpha$-elements produced primarily through successive carbon and oxygen burning phases, the S/O ratio is expected to remain flat across different galactocentric radii. Observations confirm this theoretical prediction: the S/O ratio shows no significant dependence on $D_G$ in the Milky Way [Afflerbach et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...478..190A/abstract), with Galactic planetary nebulae yielding a consistent average value of $\text{S/O} = (1.1 \pm 1.1) \times 10^{-2}$ [Kwitter et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003PASP..115...80K/abstract). Stellar studies of extremely metal-poor stars further corroborate the nucleosynthetic origins of sulfur; [S/Fe] remains essentially constant below $\text{[Fe/H]} = -3$, confirming that sulfur behaves as a typical $\alpha$-element and providing a critical benchmark for the early chemical evolution of the Galaxy [Spite et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...528A...9S/abstract).

Extragalactic studies mirror these Galactic results. In the nearby spiral galaxy NGC 628, the relative abundances of $\alpha$-elements (including S/O, Ne/O, and Ar/O) remain constant across the galactic disk, exhibiting a small intrinsic dispersion ($\sigma \sim 0.1$ dex). This indicates that the interstellar medium is chemically well-mixed at any given radius and that the upper initial mass function remains uniformly consistent [Berg et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806...16B/abstract). Similarly, in the local group spiral M33, the sulfur abundance exhibits a steady radial decline of $d\log(\text{S/H})/dR_G = -0.052 \pm 0.021$ dex kpc$^{-1}$, while the relative $\alpha$-element ratios remain invariant throughout the disk [Rubin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0804.0828R/abstract). The behavior of sulfur relative to other elements also provides clues to macroscopic galaxy properties. For instance, observations reveal that the [[n-ii-emission-lines|[N II]]]/[[s-ii-emission-lines|[S II]]] ratio increases with galaxy luminosity, suggesting that nitrogen enrichment is more efficient in high-mass spiral galaxies compared to their lower-mass counterparts [Rubin et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApJ...281L..21R/abstract). Beyond emission-line studies, sulfur serves as a vital bridge to absorption-line diagnostics. Sulfur abundance measurements in the neutral interstellar medium of Damped Ly$\alpha$ (DLA) systems are found to be highly consistent with [[oxygen-spectral-lines|oxygen abundances]] derived from nearby H II regions, linking absorption and emission abundance scales [Bowen et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...635..880B/abstract).

However, localized deviations from these smooth abundance gradients can trace distinct kinematic events. For example, spatially resolved spectroscopy in the spiral galaxy NGC 99 has revealed anomalously low-metallicity H II regions with oxygen abundances $\sim 0.16$ dex lower than expected for their galactocentric radius [Olvera et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976..205O/abstract). Combined with distinct line-of-sight velocity offsets, these chemical anomalies are interpreted as indirect evidence of pristine gas inflows actively accreting onto the galactic disk.

## Diagnostics of Active Galactic Nuclei and LINERs

The near-infrared [S III] doublet serves as a critical diagnostic tool for characterizing the physical conditions within the narrow-line region (NLR) of active galactic nuclei (AGN), particularly in heavily obscured Seyfert galaxies and Low-Ionization Nuclear Emission-line Regions (LINERs).

A central problem in the study of LINERs is determining whether the primary excitation mechanism of the gas is driven by photoionization from a central accreting black hole or by shock heating from galactic outflows and mergers. To differentiate between these two competing scenarios, astronomers frequently employ a diagnostic sulfur line ratio, comparing the lower-ionization [[s-ii-emission-lines|[S II] emission lines]] to the intermediate-ionization [S III] lines:
$$ R_{\rm S} = \frac{I([\text{S II}]\ \lambda\lambda 6716, 6731)}{I([\text{S III}]\ \lambda\lambda 9069, 9532)} $$
This ratio is highly sensitive to the hardness of the radiation field and the electron temperature of the emitting gas [Kirhakos et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989PASP..101..949K/abstract). Furthermore, alternative diagnostic diagrams that utilize line ratios such as [O III]/[O I] have been developed to classify AGNs at intermediate redshifts where Hα may be inaccessible [Feuillet et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962..104F/abstract). 

In many classic LINERs, such as NGC 1052, the measured [S III] 9532 Å fluxes align well with theoretical photoionization models. In these systems, LINERs appear to form a continuous sequence with Seyfert nuclei, distinguished primarily by a lower dimensionless ionization parameter, $U$ [Diaz et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985MNRAS.214P..41D/abstract), [Kirhakos et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989PASP..101..949K/abstract). Under this paradigm, a dilute power-law continuum originating from the central engine is sufficient to reproduce the observed $R_{\rm S}$ ratio without requiring an additional contribution from collisional heating. Additionally, the [S III]/[S II] line ratio is effectively used to identify galaxy regions harboring excess populations of young massive stars, such as Wolf-Rayet stars, and to trace their association with actively accreting black holes [Kauffmann et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527..150K/abstract). Beyond the near-infrared, a combination of mid-infrared [[fine-structure-emission-lines|Fine-structure emission lines]] from sulfur and neon has been established as a robust tracer of the star formation rate in these heavily obscured galaxies [Mordini et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A..36M/abstract). When analyzing AGN, researchers have discovered a mid-infrared Baldwin effect—an anticorrelation between line equivalent width and continuum luminosity—specifically observed in the [S IV] 10.51 μm transition [Hönig et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...485L..21H/abstract). To further characterize the central engine, [[high-ionization-emission-lines|high-ionization infrared lines]] like [Ne V] and [O IV] correlate strongly with AGN bolometric indicators and help trace a sequence of Eddington ratios across different AGN types [Spinoglio et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...46S/abstract).

However, the literature demonstrates that the $R_{\rm S}$ ratio alone is often insufficient to unambiguously rule out shock models, and in several notable cases, the observational data explicitly contradict a pure photoionization origin. For instance, in the luminous infrared galaxy NGC 6240, the relative strengths of the [S II] and [S III] emission lines strongly favor shock heating as the dominant source of ionization [Kirhakos et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989PASP..101..949K/abstract). Similarly, near-infrared spectroscopy of the nuclear disk in the radio galaxy Centaurus A (NGC 5128) utilizes the [S III] 9532 Å line alongside [[fe-ii-emission-lines|[Fe II] emission lines]] to classify the source as a LINER; subsequent modeling of these specific line strengths indicates that the region is powered primarily by shocks rather than photoionization [Simpson et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...505L..99S/abstract). The extreme kinematics of shock heating are not restricted to galactic nuclei; in supernova remnants, infrared fine-structure lines including [O III] and [[c-ii-158-m-line|[C II]]] can effectively trace high-velocity ejecta and newly formed dust [Millard et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..257...36M/abstract).

While S$^{2+}$ primarily traces the broader narrow-line region, higher-ionization sulfur transitions—such as the near-infrared [S VIII] 0.991 μm and [S IX] 1.252 μm lines—serve as useful coronal tracers of immediate nuclear activity. These highly ionized lines are frequently observed to be blueshifted, indicating an association with energetic galactic winds or AGN-driven outflows [Rodríguez-Ardila et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...579..214R/abstract). The prevailing consensus suggests that both photoionization and shock heating likely coexist in many sources, with either process capable of dominating the [[low-excitation-emission-lines|Low-excitation emission lines]] depending on the specific dynamical and radiative environment of the host galaxy.

## Historical Observational Development

The observational history of the [S III] 9532 Å emission line is intimately tied to the technological advancement of near-infrared spectroscopy and space-based infrared astronomy. In the era of single-object, ground-based observations, the line was recognized as a powerful tool for discovering highly obscured Galactic planetary nebulae [Kistiakowsky et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993AJ....105.2199K/abstract). As near-infrared detectors became more sensitive, observers began targeting the transition in extragalactic sources, achieving a major milestone with the first detection of [S III] $\lambda$9532 at $z > 1$ in a sample of 3CR radio galaxies [Rawlings et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991MNRAS.251P..17R/abstract). The subsequent deployment of high-resolution instruments on 8-to-10-meter class telescopes, such as the NIRSPEC instrument on the Keck Observatory, routinely enabled detailed kinematic and excitation analyses of distant active galactic nuclei and starbursts [Larkin et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...533L..61L/abstract).

Simultaneously, the study of the $\text{S}^{++}$ ion was revolutionized by space-based infrared missions, which bypassed atmospheric absorption to observe its corresponding mid-infrared [[fine-structure-emission-lines|Fine-structure emission lines]] at 18.7 $\mu$m and 33.5 $\mu$m. The *Infrared Space Observatory*, utilizing the ISO-SWS instrument, provided foundational measurements of these lines in both Galactic and extragalactic H II regions [Nollenberg et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...581.1002N/abstract), [Giveon et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...566..880G/abstract). This multi-wavelength approach was drastically expanded by the *Spitzer Space Telescope*, which mapped mid-infrared [S III] emission across a wide array of low-metallicity dwarf galaxies, starbursts, and active nuclei [Rubin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0804.0828R/abstract), [Hao et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704.1159H/abstract). Later, the *Herschel* PACS spectrometer extended this legacy by cataloging [[far-infrared-fine-structure-lines|Far-infrared fine structure lines]] across large samples of local galaxies to classify excitation sources [Fernández-Ontiveros et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..226...19F/abstract).

Observations across the broader electromagnetic spectrum have similarly revealed extreme sulfur states in astrophysical environments; for example, high-resolution X-ray spectroscopy with the ASCA satellite discovered emission from H-like and He-like sulfur ions in the Seyfert galaxy NGC 1068, highlighting the significant contribution of obscured AGN to a galaxy's overall energy budget [Ueno et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994PASJ...46L..71U/abstract). In contemporary extragalactic astronomy, large-scale ground-based spectroscopic programs like the MOSDEF survey, and space-based observatories like JWST, have completed the transition of [S III] 9532 Å from a niche local diagnostic to a pillar of high-redshift astrophysics.

## Open Questions and Future Directions with JWST

The advent of JWST's NIRSpec instrument has opened a new frontier for utilizing the [S III] 9532 Å line as a probe of early galaxy formation. Because the [S III] doublet resides in the near-infrared for local objects, ground-based observations of high-redshift galaxies were historically limited by atmospheric absorption and thermal background. With JWST, these critical rest-optical diagnostics can now be measured out to $z \sim 5.5$, allowing for direct comparisons of sulfur abundances and ionization states between local H II regions and galaxies formed within the first billion years of the Universe [Sanders et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191013594S/abstract), [Sanders et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888L..11S/abstract). Already, early JWST observations of strongly lensed systems, such as the Sunburst Arc at $z = 2.37$, have provided some of the most rigorous chemical abundance analyses to date, utilizing sulfur and other species to directly compare the high-redshift interstellar medium to local H II regions [Welch et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...33W/abstract). Cosmological simulations predict that metal lines like [[o-iii-5007-line|[O III] 5007 Å line]] and [O II] 3727 Å will be detectable targets for spectroscopic observation with JWST and future 30-meter class telescopes out to redshifts of $z \sim 15$ [Shimizu et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461.3563S/abstract). These predictions are already being realized; recent MIRI detections of Hα and [O III] have spectroscopically confirmed a luminous and young galaxy at $z = 12.33$, indicating rapid metal enrichment in the very early universe [Zavala et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..155Z/abstract).

A critical future application for sulfur lines lies in characterizing the Epoch of Reionization. Recent JWST spectroscopy of galaxies at $z > 12$ has revealed surprisingly high (solar or super-solar) carbon-to-oxygen (C/O) ratios that contradict standard Type II supernova yield models, tentatively pointing to enrichment by Population III progenitor stars [D'Eugenio et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231109908D/abstract), [D'Eugenio et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.152D/abstract). To robustly test these specific chemical signatures, astronomers must utilize robust diagnostics capable of piercing deeply embedded regions, similar to how [[far-infrared-fine-structure-lines|Far-infrared fine structure lines]]—such as [N III] 57 μm and [O III] 52 μm—have been successfully developed as robust probes of the nitrogen-to-oxygen ratio in local galaxies to serve as templates for high-redshift studies [Peng et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908..166P/abstract). These far-infrared ratios, including [O III] 52, [[o-iii-88-m-line|88 μm]] to [N III] 57 μm, provide robust metallicity estimates for dusty ultraluminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs) and high-$z$ sources [Pereira-Santaella et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.1218P/abstract).

Future JWST campaigns will also seek to establish the universality of the neon-to-sulfur ratio across cosmic time. In local galaxies, the Ne/S ratio is largely invariant with galactocentric radius and metallicity [Rubin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0804.0828R/abstract), [Rubin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.1875R/abstract). Confirming this constant $\alpha$-element yield in the early Universe would validate the use of sulfur as an unwavering proxy for total metallicity in systems where traditional [[oxygen-spectral-lines|Oxygen Spectral Lines]] are compromised by extreme dust obscuration or complex ionization geometries.
