The **[S II] emission lines** are a prominent set of forbidden optical transitions arising from singly ionized sulfur, most notably the closely spaced doublet at $6716 \text{ \AA}$ and $6731 \text{ \AA}$. Because the ionization potential of neutral sulfur is lower than that of hydrogen, $\text{S}^+$ thrives in the partially ionized transition zones on the outer edges of [[line-emitting-gas|Line-emitting gas]] clouds. In [[forbidden-line-spectroscopy|Forbidden Line Spectroscopy]], the [S II] doublet serves as one of the most reliable and widely utilized probes of electron density ($n_e$) in astrophysical plasmas. The intensity ratio of the doublet is exquisitely sensitive to collisional de-excitation by free electrons within the range of $10 \lesssim n_e \lesssim 10^4 \text{ cm}^{-3}$, making it a cornerstone of [[optical-emission-line-diagnostics|Optical Emission-Line Diagnostics]] for H II regions, planetary nebulae, and other ionized environments [Kewley et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ARA&A..57..511K/abstract).

Beyond measuring local gas density, the intensity of the [S II] doublet relative to hydrogen [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]] (specifically $[\text{S II}]/\text{H}\alpha$) is a critical tool for determining the dominant [[excitation-mechanisms|Excitation mechanisms]] within a galaxy. In standard [[diagnostic-emission-line-ratios|Diagnostic emission line ratios]]—such as the BPT diagrams—an elevated $[\text{S II}]/\text{H}\alpha$ ratio distinguishes gas ionized by a hard radiation field or shock-heating from typical stellar photoionization. For example, an empirical threshold of $[\text{S II}]/\text{H}\alpha > 0.4$ is classically used to identify shock-heated supernova remnants. Similarly, the extended, partially ionized zones generated by active galactic nuclei produce strong [S II] emission, allowing astronomers to characterize [[narrow-line-regions-and-seyferts|Narrow-Line Regions and Seyferts]] and to distinguish [[liners|LINERs]] from normal star-forming galaxies [Filippenko et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApJ...285..458F/abstract). Diffuse, low-density environments like the galactic Warm Ionized Medium (WIM) also exhibit characteristically enhanced [S II] emission, pointing to complex thermodynamic conditions that require additional heating mechanisms beyond pure stellar photoionization [Mathis et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...544..347M/abstract).

Modern [[emission-line-spectroscopy|Emission Line Spectroscopy]] increasingly leverages sulfur transitions across multiple ionization states to map [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] and trace the evolution of the interstellar medium across cosmic time [Kaler et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981ApJ...244...54K/abstract). In contemporary extragalactic astronomy, a pronounced "[S II] deficiency"—where the lines are unusually weak relative to typical star-forming loci—has emerged as a powerful signature for identifying density-bounded H II regions that leak Lyman continuum radiation into the intergalactic medium. As next-generation observatories like the James Webb Space Telescope push [S II] observations into the epoch of reionization, researchers are actively investigating the precise physical drivers of this deficiency. Ongoing studies aim to resolve whether these weak sulfur lines are strictly the result of truncated gas distributions or if they reflect the extreme ionization parameters and harder stellar spectra characteristic of the early universe.

## Atomic Physics and Electron Density Diagnostics

The optical [S II] $\lambda\lambda$6716, 6731 emission lines are classic examples of [[forbidden-line-spectroscopy|forbidden transitions]] arising from singly ionized sulfur. These lines originate from metastable excited states decaying to the ground state, making them forbidden transitions. Consequently, in typical astrophysical plasmas, the upper energy levels are populated predominantly by free-electron collisional excitation rather than radiative processes, making them fundamental features in nebular physics.

The differing radiative lifetimes and collisional de-excitation rates of the two upper levels make the [S II] doublet one of the most widely used probes of electron density ($n_e$) in [[nebular-emission-and-diagnostics|Nebular Emission and Diagnostics]]. The diagnostic ratio is defined as:
$$R = \frac{I(\lambda6716)}{I(\lambda6731)}$$
At low electron densities, collisional de-excitation is negligible, and the relative line intensities are determined solely by the collision strengths, approaching a maximum asymptotic limit. Conversely, at high densities, collisional processes dominate, driving the ratio to its high-density limit. Between these two extremes, the ratio serves as a highly sensitive, monotonic diagnostic of $n_e$ . 

In typical extragalactic H II regions, densities generally fall in the lower half of this sensitive range; for example, integral-field spectroscopy of the nuclear starburst ring in NGC 7742 reveals very low gas densities of $n_e < 100 \text{ cm}^{-3}$ [Mazzuca et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...649L..79M/abstract). Spatially resolved mapping of the Trifid Nebula at $0.24\text{ pc}$ resolution reveals that [S II] electron densities directly trace internal variations at the ionization front [Sattler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251202802S/abstract). Furthermore, imaging surveys demonstrate that [S II] emission is often highly concentrated at the face-on 'bar' interface between H II regions and photodissociation regions (PDRs) [Hester et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991PASP..103..853H/abstract).

At gas densities significantly exceeding the critical density of these transitions, however, the [S II] doublet undergoes severe collisional quenching. This physical property naturally explains the absence of broad [S II] emission in the broad-line regions of active galactic nuclei, where $n_e$ is expected to reach $\sim 10^{10} \rm \, cm^{-3}$. As a result, [S II] lines are strictly confined to lower-density environments like [[narrow-line-regions-and-seyferts|Narrow-Line Regions and Seyferts]]. Spatially resolved high-resolution spectroscopy has utilized the [S II] ratio to detect steep density gradients in the inner narrow-line regions of active galaxies, effectively tracing the transition between circumnuclear gas and the unresolved nucleus [Barth et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...546..205B/abstract). Extreme collisional behavior is also observed in extraordinarily dense nuclear starbursts; for example, in the blue compact dwarf galaxy Mrk 996, central gas densities approach $10^6 \rm \, cm^{-3}$; here, the optical [S II] lines predominantly arise, while mid-infrared fine-structure lines are strongly suppressed by collisional de-excitation in the same dense central region [Thuan et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...689..897T/abstract).

Deriving a single, representative $n_e$ value from integrated galaxy spectra, however, requires caution. Real nebulae exhibit complex density gradients and temperature profiles that can complicate the interpretation of a single, flux-weighted emission-line ratio [Kewley et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ARA&A..57..511K/abstract). Complementing these density diagnostics, recent advancements include a physically motivated "charge-exchange method" that models the $[\text{O I}]/\text{H}\alpha$ ratio to solve for electron temperatures within H II regions based on the ionization fraction of oxygen [Kreckel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...667A..16K/abstract). Furthermore, diffuse, low-density gas can contribute significantly to the total [S II] emission of a galaxy [Hill et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...787..106H/abstract). If not properly modeled in [[emission-line-spectroscopy|Emission Line Spectroscopy]], the presence of this diffuse ionized gas can artificially skew the measured [S II] ratio toward the low-density limit, causing observers to systematically underestimate the density of the embedded discrete H II regions [Kewley et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ARA&A..57..511K/abstract).

While [S II] is invaluable in the optical, the near-infrared [[s-iii-9532-line|[S III] 9532 Å line]] serves as a superior tracer for detecting distant or obscured planetary nebulae compared to [O III] 5007 Å whenever visual extinction exceeds $3$ magnitudes [Kistiakowsky et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993AJ....105.2199K/abstract). At longer wavelengths, Spitzer SINGS data indicates that [S III] $18.7/33.5\ \mu\text{m}$ line ratios yield typical interstellar electron densities of $300\text{--}600\text{ cm}^{-3}$ across diverse galactic environments [Dale et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...693.1821D/abstract). In the context of active nuclei, high-resolution Spitzer IRS data use the [S III] 18.7/33.5 doublet to estimate similarly robust gas electron densities of $10^3\text{--}10^4\text{ cm}^{-3}$ for the highly ionized gas components typical of Seyfert nuclei [Tommasin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...676..836T/abstract). For even more extreme environments, forbidden transitions from highly ionized species such as [S VIII], [S IX], and [S XI] have been cataloged among infrared coronal lines, providing essential tools for analyzing exceptionally hot, highly ionized gas [Greenhouse et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJS...88...23G/abstract).

## Ionization Source Diagnostics and BPT Diagrams

The relative strength of the $[\rm S~II] \lambda\lambda6716, 6731$ doublet compared to the adjacent H$\alpha$ line is a cornerstone of [[optical-emission-line-diagnostics|Optical Emission-Line Diagnostics]] and the widely used [[diagnostic-emission-line-ratios|BPT]] (Baldwin, Phillips & Terlevich) classification diagrams. Because the ionization potential of neutral sulfur is lower than that of hydrogen, $\rm S^+$ is predominantly found in partially ionized transition zones. These zones are physically narrow in standard H II regions photoionized by massive OB stars, but they become highly extended in [[line-emitting-gas|Line-emitting gas]] that is subjected to shock heating or the hard radiation fields of active galactic nuclei (AGN). 

In extragalactic surveys, an empirical threshold of $[\rm S~II]/H\alpha > 0.4$ is the widely accepted criterion for distinguishing shock-heated supernova remnants (SNRs) from photoionized H II regions [Blair et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJS..108..261B/abstract), [Lee et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793..134L/abstract), [Long et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929..144L/abstract). This threshold effectively isolates SNRs because collisional excitation in the cooling post-shock gas strongly enhances sulfur forbidden lines relative to hydrogen [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]] [Moumen et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488..803M/abstract). However, application of the $[\text{S II}]/\text{H}\alpha$ BPT criteria to faint, infrared-selected galaxies reveals that many systems exhibit complex emission that eludes standard two-component (starburst versus AGN) models [Ashby et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992AJ....104..980A/abstract). Additionally, long-slit spectroscopy of nuclear starbursts demonstrates that $[\text{S II}]$ and $[\text{N II}]$ emission often extends over several hundred to $1000\text{ pc}$ from the center, further complicating standard classifications [De Robertis et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ApJ...329..629D/abstract). Environmental conditions also play a role; for instance, galaxies situated in the sparsely populated Bootes void exhibit unusually high $[\rm S~II]/H\alpha$ ratios compared to typical H II regions, potentially reflecting younger underlying stellar populations [Peimbert et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992A&A...253..349P/abstract). This distinction can additionally become blurred in the diffuse ionized gas (DIG) or warm ionized medium (WIM), where $[\rm S~II]/H\alpha$ ratios naturally rise above 0.4 as the local electron density and H$\alpha$ surface brightness decrease [Blair et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJS..108..261B/abstract), [Hill et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...787..106H/abstract). To circumvent this confusion, modern integral-field studies often supplement BPT classifications with kinematic indicators or additional line ratios, such as $[\rm S~III]/[\rm S~II]$, to cleanly separate true SNRs from the diffuse ionized background [Long et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929..144L/abstract).

For nuclear classifications, plotting the ratio of the [[o-iii-5007-line|[O III] 5007 Å line]] to H$\beta$ against $[\rm S~II]/H\alpha$ effectively separates star-forming galaxies from AGN [Law et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...915...35L/abstract). Complementing optical ratios, mid-infrared line ratios such as [Ne III]/[Ne II] and [O IV]/[Ne III] provide distinctive, extinction-resilient diagnostics for identifying hard X-ray selected AGNs compared to normal star-forming galaxies [Weaver et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...716.1151W/abstract). AGN generate harder ionizing spectra that penetrate deeper into neutral clouds, producing the extended partially ionized zones that yield strong $[\rm S~II]$ emission. To resolve ambiguities in [[excitation-mechanisms|Excitation mechanisms]], recent studies have coupled standard BPT diagnostics with spatially resolved gas dynamics. Integral-field spectroscopy from the MaNGA survey demonstrates that the gas-phase velocity dispersion ($\sigma_{\rm H\alpha}$) correlates strongly with $[\rm S~II]/H\alpha$ [Law et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...915...35L/abstract). Spaxels consistent with photoionization by star formation exhibit dynamically cold velocity distributions ($\sigma \sim 25 \text{ km s}^{-1}$), whereas gas situated in AGN and [[liners|LINERs]] exhibits significantly broader line-of-sight velocity dispersions (up to $200 \text{ km s}^{-1}$). To address overlapping AGN and LINER populations, recent studies have proposed improved Seyfert-LINER empirical classification lines within the $[\text{N II}]$ BPT diagram to better harmonize classifications across parallel $[\text{S II}]$ and $[\text{O I}]$ diagnostics [Cheng et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A.189C/abstract). This synthesis of BPT mapping and kinematics is increasingly necessary to distinguish genuine central engines from extended shock-heated environments.

## Probing the Warm Ionized Medium (WIM)

The Warm Ionized Medium (WIM), frequently referred to as diffuse ionized gas (DIG), constitutes a major, low-density phase of the interstellar medium in star-forming galaxies. Spectroscopically, the WIM is distinctly characterized by enhanced [[low-excitation-emission-lines|Low-excitation emission lines]] relative to the hydrogen [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]]. In particular, the ratio of the sulfur doublet to H$\alpha$, $$ \text{[S II]} \lambda\lambda 6716, 6731 / \text{H}\alpha $$, is observed to be significantly higher in the diffuse WIM than in discrete, classical H II regions [Lehnert et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...426L..27L/abstract), [Madsen et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...652..401M/abstract). Observations from the Wisconsin H$\alpha$ Mapper (WHAM) survey reveal a strong inverse power-law correlation between the [S II]/H$\alpha$ ratio and H$\alpha$ surface brightness. In the Milky Way, this ratio rises from $\sim 0.08$ in bright emission regions ($>100$ Rayleighs) to $\sim 1.0$ in the faintest diffuse gas ($<0.2$ Rayleighs) [Hill et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...787..106H/abstract). The widespread presence of this gas implies that integrated [N II]/H$\alpha$ and [S II]/H$\alpha$ ratios measured across entire disk galaxies are significantly enhanced relative to purely H II region-dominated fluxes [Moustakas et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...651..155M/abstract).

Mapping of these [[emission-line-regions|Emission-line regions]] provides quantitative constraints on the vertical distribution of the WIM into the galactic halo. In the Milky Way, measurements yield an electron scale height of $H \approx 1.0\text{ kpc}$ in the Perseus arm [Haffner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...523..223H/abstract), while the emission in the Scutum-Centaurus arm reveals an electron density squared scale height of $0.30\text{ kpc}$ [Hill et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...787..106H/abstract). Similar highly extended, extra-planar structures are observed in other edge-on spiral galaxies, such as NGC 891 and NGC 55, where both the [S II]/H$\alpha$ and [[n-ii-emission-lines|[N II] emission lines]] to H$\alpha$ ratios smoothly increase with distance $|z|$ from the galactic midplane [Ferguson et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996AJ....112.2567F/abstract), [Miller et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...592...79M/abstract), [Mathis et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...544..347M/abstract). More broadly, minor-axis spectroscopic databases for dozens of edge-on starbursts show systematic line ratio variations that trace the immense extent of this extraplanar ionized gas [Lehnert et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJS...97...89L/abstract).

The elevated [S II]/H$\alpha$ ratios in the WIM reflect profound differences in the physical conditions of the gas. The diffuse gas generally exhibits lower ionization parameters, is irradiated by a softer ionizing spectrum from distant OB stars, and maintains higher electron temperatures, ranging from $6,000$ to $10,000$ K [Haffner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...523..223H/abstract), [Madsen et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...652..401M/abstract). However, while photoionization by leaking Lyman continuum photons from the galactic disk can maintain the extensive ionization, it struggles to completely explain the observed thermal structure. The consistently high temperatures at large scale heights imply the existence of an "extra heating" mechanism beyond pure stellar photoionization [Mathis et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...544..347M/abstract), [Elwert et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...632..277E/abstract). 

Current literature debates the exact nature of this supplemental heating; proposed sources include photoelectric heating by dust grains, the dissipation of interstellar turbulence, and localized shocks or turbulent mixing layers [Mathis et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...544..347M/abstract), [Miller et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...592...79M/abstract), [Elwert et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...632..277E/abstract). The fact that the [S II]/[N II] ratio remains relatively constant throughout the WIM (typically $\sim 0.6-0.7$) indicates that the observed variations in [S II]/H$\alpha$ with height are primarily driven by temperature increases rather than drastic shifts in the ionization state of the gas [Haffner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...523..223H/abstract), [Mathis et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...544..347M/abstract). Thus, the [S II] doublet remains one of the most vital probes for tracing both the extent and the thermodynamic balance of the galactic halo.

## Extragalactic Supernova Remnant Surveys

Because shocks efficiently compress the interstellar medium and collisionally excite low-ionization species, the empirical threshold of $\text{[S II]} / \text{H}\alpha > 0.4$ has been widely adopted in [[emission-line-spectroscopy|Emission Line Spectroscopy]] to build extensive catalogs of extragalactic supernova remnants (SNRs) [Blair et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJS..108..261B/abstract), [Lee et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793..134L/abstract). 

Historically, extragalactic SNR catalogs were built using narrowband imaging filters centered on H$\alpha$ and [S II], followed by continuum subtraction. Comprehensive $[\text{S II}]$ and $\text{H}\alpha$ surveys of M31 have cataloged nearly $1000$ discrete nebulae, facilitating the largest statistical study of extragalactic H II regions and SNRs using these lines [Walterbos et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992A&AS...92..625W/abstract). In M33, the size distribution of 199 SNR candidates identified via this method exhibits a power-law slope of $\alpha = 2.38 \pm 0.05$, consistent with remnants evolving through the adiabatic Sedov-Taylor expansion phase [Lee et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793..134L/abstract). This technique also scales effectively; for instance, the MCELS survey of the Small Magellanic Cloud provided deep multi-wavelength inventories, expanding available catalogs of both SNRs and planetary nebulae using widespread [S II] and [O III] imaging [Winkler et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ASPC..491..343W/abstract). High-resolution space-based efforts, such as HST WFC3 mapping, have utilized [S II] emission to identify entirely new populations of SNRs and planetary nebulae in galaxies like NGC 4214 [Dopita et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Ap&SS.330..123D/abstract). Notably, the [S II]/H$\alpha$ line ratios of remnants in M31 and M33 exhibit a distinct bimodal distribution with concentration peaks at approximately $0.55$ and $0.8$ [Lee et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793..134L/abstract). Spectroscopic follow-up of specific remnants, such as the nitrogen-rich SNR WB92-26 in M31, reveals the extreme kinematics of these systems, showing broad [S II] velocity wings extending from $-2000$ to $+1500 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ due to shock interactions with the circumstellar medium [Caldwell et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954..107C/abstract). Similarly, [S II] emission highlights the intricate filamentary optical shells of large-scale synchrotron superbubbles, as observed in the galaxy IC 10 [Lozinskaya et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AstL...34..217L/abstract).

Beyond mature remnants, intermediate-width $[\text{S II}]$ emission lines can emerge in the late-time spectra of specific events (e.g., the extraordinary Type II supernova iPTF14hls), likely originating from dense, processed material in the core of the ejecta [Sollerman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...621A..30S/abstract). In addition to optical surveys, near- and far-infrared spectroscopy of SNRs often detects Doppler-broadened atomic lines from high-velocity ejecta, providing a multi-phase contrast to the traditional optical $[\text{S II}]$ emission [Millard et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..257...36M/abstract). For instance, near-infrared spectroscopy of remnants enables the simultaneous detection of sulfur, iron, and nickel lines, revealing signatures of significant nickel-to-iron enrichment synthesized in the explosion [Dennefeld et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986A&A...157..267D/abstract). Conversely, unusually faint $[\text{Fe II}]$ emission in certain actively star-forming dwarf galaxies, such as II Zw 40, can indicate an unexpectedly low number of supernova-driven shocks despite their active star formation [Vanzi et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...466..150V/abstract).

Recent advances in integral field spectroscopy, particularly with the Multi Unit Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE) on the Very Large Telescope, have revolutionized extragalactic SNR surveys. By providing simultaneous spatial and spectral coverage, MUSE allows for the direct measurement of [[excitation-mechanisms|Excitation mechanisms]] and kinematic broadening across entire galaxy disks. In the face-on spiral galaxy M83, MUSE observations have yielded 211 spectroscopically confirmed SNRs, marking the largest confirmed sample in any single external galaxy [Long et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929..144L/abstract). Beyond the traditional $\text{[S II]} / \text{H}\alpha$ diagnostic, MUSE spectra enable the use of the [S III]/[S II] ratio as a secondary diagnostic, which is found to be systematically lower in SNRs than in H II regions. Utilizing similar integral field techniques and principal component analysis on [S II] and [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]], astronomers have extended the optical detection of SNRs out to distances of $\sim 30 \text{ Mpc}$ in galaxies such as NGC 4030 [Cid Fernandes et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502.1386C/abstract).

Despite the robustness of the $\text{[S II]} / \text{H}\alpha$ threshold, the diagnostic can become ambiguous in the low-density diffuse ionized medium (DIM). In these environments, the [S II]/H$\alpha$ ratio naturally rises above 0.4 due to varying ionization parameters and gas temperatures driven by distant OB stars, blurring the boundary between ambient diffuse gas and extended supernova remnants, as has been noted in galaxies like NGC 7793 [Blair et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJS..108..261B/abstract). Careful kinematic analysis is therefore often required to definitively confirm the presence of shock-heated gas in low-surface-brightness regions.

## [S II] in Active Galactic Nuclei and LINERs

The [S II] $\lambda\lambda6716, 6731$ doublet is a critical component of [[optical-emission-line-diagnostics|Optical Emission-Line Diagnostics]] used to distinguish active galactic nuclei (AGNs) from star-forming galaxies. Because the harder non-stellar continuum of an AGN penetrates deeper into neutral gas than [[stellar-ionizing-radiation|Stellar ionizing radiation]], it generates an extensive partially ionized zone. This extended zone strongly enhances the emission of low-ionization species, pushing the [S II]/H$\alpha$ flux ratio to significantly higher values than those observed in typical H II regions. This enhancement scales steeply toward the central engine; bi-dimensional photometry reveals a dramatic increase in the gas-to-[[stellar-continuum|stellar continuum]] ratio toward the center of classic active galaxies like NGC 1068 based on prominent [S II] and [N II] structures [Alloin et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981A&AS...43..231A/abstract). In standard [[diagnostic-emission-line-ratios|Diagnostic emission line ratios]], an elevated [S II]/H$\alpha$ ratio serves as a primary empirical marker of nuclear activity.

In Seyfert galaxies, [S II] emission originates almost exclusively from the Narrow-Line Regions (NLRs), where electron densities are low enough ($n_e \sim 10^2 - 10^4 \text{ cm}^{-3}$) to avoid the collisional de-excitation that quenches forbidden lines in the dense ($n_e \sim 10^{10} \text{ cm}^{-3}$) broad-line region. Spatially resolved high-resolution spectroscopy has utilized the [S II] doublet ratio to directly measure steep density gradients in the inner NLRs of active galaxies, effectively tracing the transition between circumnuclear gas and the unresolved nucleus [Barth et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...546..205B/abstract). Often, localized high-excitation regions detected extended away from the nucleus in Seyferts can even exceed nuclear excitation levels due to sharp local increases in the ionization parameter [Haniff et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...368..167H/abstract). Kinematic studies in the infrared show that NLR line widths, including those of higher-ionization species like $[\text{S IV}]$, systematically increase with the ionization potential of the species [Dasyra et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...740...94D/abstract). Similarly, spatially resolved near-infrared spectroscopy of Seyfert 2 galaxies shows that $[\text{Fe II}]$ line profiles often differ significantly from recombination lines like Pa$\beta$, suggesting shock enhancement driven by radio outflows rather than pure photoionization [Knop et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....122..764K/abstract). 

When modeling these environments, multi-wavelength comparisons reveal that dust attenuation in AGN narrow-line regions is statistically similar to that of star-forming H II regions when comparing optical H$\alpha$ to infrared $[\text{Ne II}]$ [Wild et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410.1593W/abstract). Additionally, infrared observations of nearby Seyfert galaxies have led to the discovery of a strong mid-infrared Baldwin effect in the $[\text{S IV}]\ 10.51\ \mu\text{m}$ and $[\text{Ne II}]\ 12.81\ \mu\text{m}$ lines, offering further insight into the luminosity-dependent structure of AGN [Hönig et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...485L..21H/abstract). To complement sulfur diagnostics, extremely high-ionization species observed in the mid-infrared, such as [Ne V], are viewed as near-perfect signatures of energy production by an AGN, successfully differentiating both AGN type 1s and 2s from starburst-dominated systems [Tommasin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...709.1257T/abstract). Furthermore, high-ionization mid-IR transitions like [O IV] act as robust linear bolometric indicators of overall AGN power, remaining remarkably consistent across diverse Seyfert populations [Spinoglio et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...46S/abstract). Exploring extreme activity, near-infrared coronal lines such as [S IX] 1.252 $\mu$m and [S VIII] 0.991 $\mu$m serve as highly effective tracers of nuclear processes in Narrow-Line Seyfert 1 galaxies, frequently showing clear blueshifts indicative of fast outflows [Rodríguez-Ardila et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...579..214R/abstract). However, in the most deeply obscured galaxies, such as NGC 4945, extreme attenuation can suppress even [Ne V] and [O IV] emission, rendering them unreliable as strictly extinction-free tracers in such heavily buried nuclei [Pérez-Beaupuits et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...533A..56P/abstract).

The behavior of [S II] is central to the ongoing debate regarding the [[excitation-mechanisms|Excitation mechanisms]] powering [[liners|LINERs]] (Low-Ionization Nuclear Emission-line Regions). By definition, LINERs exhibit strong low-ionization forbidden lines, including elevated [S II]/H$\alpha$ and [O I]/H$\alpha$ ratios, which can be modeled through multiple distinct physical processes. Historically, LINERs were predominantly interpreted as being powered by photoionization from a low-luminosity AGN or a hard non-stellar continuum [Filippenko et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApJ...285..458F/abstract), [Bae et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753...10B/abstract). However, elevated [S II]/H$\alpha$ ratios are also a classical signature of shock-heated gas, leading to competing models where large-scale interstellar shocks—often driven by AGN feedback, galactic winds, or mergers—dominate the excitation [Gonzalez Delgado et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.281.1105G/abstract), [Simpson et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...505L..99S/abstract). For example, in NGC 1275, strong low-ionization forbidden lines are excited by widespread shocks rather than the nuclear continuum, mimicking classical LINER signatures [Rudy et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...414..527R/abstract). 

This ambiguity in the literature is compounded by spatially resolved observations revealing that LINER-like emission is frequently extended throughout quiescent galaxies (forming "Extended [[low-ionization-emission-regions|LIERs]]") rather than strictly confined to the nucleus. Separately, analysis of unresolved single-fiber spectra demonstrates that red sequence galaxies exhibiting LINER emission often harbor younger stellar populations, suggesting that photoionization by hot, evolved post-AGB stars provides a compelling alternative to nuclear photoionization in these systems [Graves et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...671..243G/abstract). Conversely, recent analyses of quiescent galaxies utilizing temperature-sensitive emission lines strongly favor interstellar shocks (with preshock densities of $n \sim 1 \text{ cm}^{-3}$) as the dominant ionization source for these extended regions [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240807952L/abstract). 

Efforts to break the degeneracy between shocks and photoionization have attempted to combine optical [S II] measurements with near-infrared [S III] transitions. However, studies show that neither mechanism can be universally ruled out using sulfur line ratios alone; the literature currently reflects a consensus that the LINER classification encompasses a heterogeneous population, where both low-luminosity AGNs and shocks are capable of dominating the observed emission-line spectrum depending on the specific galactic environment [Kirhakos et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989PASP..101..949K/abstract).

## [S II] Deficiency and Lyman Continuum Leakage

Identifying galaxies responsible for the reionization of the universe requires understanding the physical mechanisms that allow ionizing radiation—specifically Lyman continuum (LyC) photons—to escape into the intergalactic medium. While traditional [[emission-line-spectroscopy|Emission Line Spectroscopy]] has often relied on a high $[\text{O III}]/[\text{O II}]$ ratio (the $O_{32}$ index) or strong Lyman-$\alpha$ emission to identify LyC leakers, recent diagnostic efforts have highlighted the utility of a pronounced deficit in $[\text{S II}]$ emission [Wang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916....3W/abstract). 

The physical basis for this diagnostic lies in the ionization structure of [[line-emitting-gas|Line-emitting gas]]. In a typical, ionization-bounded H II region, hydrogen becomes fully neutral at the Strömgren radius, trapping all LyC photons. Because the ionization potential of neutral sulfur is lower than that of hydrogen, S$^+$ coexists with neutral hydrogen in the partially ionized outer transition zone of the nebula. In this region, collisional excitation yields strong $[\text{S II}]\lambda\lambda6716, 6731$ emission. However, in a "density-bounded" nebula, the gas cloud is physically truncated before the hydrogen ionizing photons are depleted. Consequently, the nebula lacks the extended, low-ionization outer shell where S$^+$ typically resides. This geometry permits LyC photons to leak into the intergalactic medium and simultaneously suppresses the $[\text{S II}]$ emission relative to hydrogen recombination lines such as H$\alpha$ [Wang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916....3W/abstract), [Mondal et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988..171M/abstract). For instance, the identification of an ionization cone in the dwarf starburst galaxy NGC 5253 using $[\text{S III}]/[\text{S II}]$ ratios provides direct evidence for LyC escape through optically thin channels [Zastrow et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741L..17Z/abstract). While useful, ionization-parameter mapping using combinations like $[\text{O III}]/[\text{S II}]$ to detect optically thin paths can be limited at higher metallicities, where $[\text{O III}]$ strength becomes increasingly dependent on shifts in nebular temperature [Sawant et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923...78S/abstract).

The predictive power of $[\text{S II}]$ deficiency was empirically validated by the Low-redshift Lyman Continuum Survey, which observed a sample of 66 star-forming galaxies using the Cosmic Origins Spectrograph on the Hubble Space Telescope. The survey detected flux below the Lyman edge in 35 of these galaxies, revealing that confirmed LyC leakers are systematically deficient in $[\text{S II}]$ compared to standard star-forming loci. Furthermore, the escape fraction of LyC photons was found to increase as the $[\text{S II}]$ deficiency became more prominent, establishing it as a robust complement to other [[nebular-emission-and-diagnostics|Nebular Emission and Diagnostics]] [Wang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916....3W/abstract).

This methodology has recently been extended to high-redshift targets observed by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). A notable example is GNHeII J1236+6215 at $z = 2.9803$, a heavily ionized starburst galaxy exhibiting strong [[he-ii-1640-line|He II 1640 line]] emission. Spectroscopic analysis revealed a severe $[\text{S II}]$ deficiency, with an observed ratio of $[\text{S II}]\lambda\lambda6716, 6731 / \text{H}\alpha = 0.08 \pm 0.02$. Combined with low dust extinction and a high ionization state ($\log U \approx -2.0$), this $[\text{S II}]$ deficiency strongly indicates the presence of density-bounded, optically thin H II regions leaking LyC photons [Mondal et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250606831M/abstract), [Mondal et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988..171M/abstract). 

Despite its emerging status as a prime diagnostic for cosmic reionization sources, there remains active debate in the literature regarding the exact physical drivers of the $[\text{S II}]$ deficit. While density-bounded geometry naturally explains the missing low-ionization emission, an extreme ionization parameter ($U$) driven by a hard ionizing spectrum can also drastically alter the volume of the S$^+$ zone. Current research aims to disentangle these degenerate effects to determine whether $[\text{S II}]$ deficiency is strictly the signature of truncated gas distributions or the byproduct of the extreme radiation fields characteristic of early-universe starbursts.

## Chemical Abundances and the Infrared Evolution of Sulfur Spectroscopy

Sulfur is an $\alpha$-process element synthesized primarily during oxygen and carbon burning in massive stars prior to Type II supernova explosions. Because of their shared nucleosynthetic origins, the abundance of sulfur tracks closely with other $\alpha$-elements such as oxygen, neon, and argon. Consequently, determinations of [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] in galactic disks show that while the overall metallicity (e.g., O/H or S/H) exhibits strong radial gradients, the S/O and Ar/O ratios remain relatively constant across both Galactic and extragalactic environments [Kaler et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981ApJ...244...54K/abstract), [Afflerbach et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...478..190A/abstract), [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591..801K/abstract). Historically, early observational surveys of Galactic H II regions established these foundational primary radial abundance gradients for sulfur, oxygen, and nitrogen across the Milky Way disk [Hawley et al. (1978)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1978ApJ...224..417H/abstract). In modern surveys, tools like deep narrowband mapping of galaxy groups (such as the M101 group) meticulously trace oxygen abundance gradients across hundreds of discrete H II regions [Garner et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941..182G/abstract). For instance, far-infrared measurements of Milky Way compact H II regions reveal a steady sulfur gradient of $\Delta \log(\text{S/H}) / \Delta R_G = -0.063 \pm 0.006$ dex kpc$^{-1}$, but find no significant radial dependence for the S/O ratio [Afflerbach et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...478..190A/abstract). 

Complex enrichment histories can complicate these patterns. The local dwarf galaxy I Zw 18 exhibits extremely low oxygen abundance but N/O and C/O ratios comparable to Galactic H II regions [Dufour et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ApJ...332..752D/abstract). Rare systems like NGC 1222, a solar-metallicity starburst dominated by high-excitation [Ne III] emission, demonstrate the capacity to host exceptionally massive stars up to 100 $M_\odot$ despite high metallicities [Beck et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AJ....134.1237B/abstract). In more massive starburst systems like M82, stellar and gaseous abundances show distinct alpha-enhancement consistent with recursive starbursts and widespread Type II supernova enrichment [Origlia et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...606..862O/abstract).

Calculating accurate sulfur abundances requires capturing the majority of the element's gas-phase ions. In typical star-forming environments, sulfur predominantly exists as S$^{++}$ and S$^{3+}$. While optical [S II] lines trace the low-ionization gas, reliance on the optical [S II] $\lambda\lambda 6716, 6731$ doublet alone yields significant uncertainties in ionization correction factors. Additionally, individual giant extragalactic H II regions (such as those in NGC 1365) exhibit distinct pixel-to-pixel fluctuations in [N II]/[O II] and [S II]/[O II] line ratios, providing clear evidence of intricate, varying internal density distributions that further complicate singular abundance estimates [Roy et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989AJ.....97.1010R/abstract). 

The first major advancement in sulfur spectroscopy incorporated the near-infrared [[s-iii-9532-line|[S III] 9532 Å line]] (and its $\lambda 9069$ counterpart) to directly calculate the abundance of S$^{++}$ [Kwitter et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003PASP..115...80K/abstract). Incorporating these lines minimizes the need for large, uncertain ionization correction factors. However, refined sulfur abundance estimates in the inner Galaxy emphasize the need to carefully account for density variations, as the critical densities of the observed far-infrared fine-structure lines (including transitions from S$^{++}$, N$^{++}$, N$^+$, and O$^{++}$) range broadly from $40$ to over $9000\text{ cm}^{-3}$ depending on the specific transition [Simpson et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...611..338S/abstract). Validating these nebular measurements, direct comparisons show that sulfur abundances measured in the neutral ISM (via absorption lines) are in good agreement with oxygen abundances measured in adjacent H II regions, as seen in the damped Ly$\alpha$ galaxy SBS 1543+593 [Bowen et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...635..880B/abstract). Furthermore, these optical and near-infrared studies revealed a persistent tension: strong-line empirical indicators systematically overestimate oxygen and sulfur abundances by $0.2$ to $0.5$ dex compared to direct electron temperature ($T_e$) methods [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591..801K/abstract).

To bypass the limitations of dust extinction and temperature fluctuations inherent to optical [[emission-line-spectroscopy|Emission Line Spectroscopy]], the field transitioned to space-based infrared observatories. The Infrared Space Observatory (ISO) pioneered the use of [[far-infrared-fine-structure-lines|Far-infrared fine structure lines]] to map nebular conditions, directly measuring S$^{3+}$ and S$^{++}$ abundances in optically obscured Galactic and extragalactic H II regions [Nollenberg et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...581.1002N/abstract), [Giveon et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...566..880G/abstract). These infrared emission lines successfully penetrate deep dust to probe massive stars in major clusters, such as those within the Antennae galaxies, or the heavily obscured starburst regions of Arp 220 [van der Werf et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASSL..329..109V/abstract). In actively forming galaxies like NGC 253, infrared observations detail multiple ionized gas components displaying varying densities mapped distinctly via sensitive [O III] and [S III] line ratios [Beck et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...665A..85B/abstract). NGC 253 also exhibits near-solar gas-phase silicon abundances, strongly evidencing grain destruction within its dense starburst environment [Carral et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...423..223C/abstract). A comprehensive compendium of ISO galaxy observations shows systematic statistical trends in $[\text{O III}]\ 88/52\ \mu\text{m}$ and $[\text{N II}]\ 122\ \mu\text{m}$ ratios relative to far-infrared colors [Brauher et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJS..178..280B/abstract). These far-infrared tools remain potent at higher redshifts; detections of $[\text{N II}]\ 122\ \mu\text{m}$ and $[\text{O III}]\ 88\ \mu\text{m}$ at high redshift allow for the use of the $[\text{O III}]/[\text{N II}]$ ratio to effectively constrain the effective temperature of ionizing stars [Ferkinhoff et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...740L..29F/abstract). Detailed modeling of the Wolf-Rayet nebula N76 in the Small Magellanic Cloud confirms that $[\text{S III}]$ and $[\text{S IV}]$ lines successfully establish physical conditions even in highly ionized, low-metallicity environments [Tarantino et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969..101T/abstract).

The Spitzer Space Telescope dramatically expanded the diagnostic utility of these infrared features, mapping [S IV] $10.51 \mu\mathrm{m}$ and [S III] $18.71 \mu\mathrm{m}$ lines across nearby galaxies [Rubin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.387...45R/abstract), [Rubin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.1875R/abstract). These observations confirmed that the relationship between neon and sulfur—the [[ne-s-abundance-ratio|Ne/S abundance ratio]]—is nearly universal across varying metallicities. Spitzer studies of H II regions in galaxies like M83 indicate a correlation of higher ionization with galactocentric radius, while consistently revealing Ne/S ratios that exceed canonical solar values [Rubin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.377.1407R/abstract). Because the yields of both elements have little dependence on the initial stellar metallicity, the Ne/S ratio serves as a nearly universal constant. Observations utilizing Spitzer have found median Ne/S abundance ratios of $16.3$ in the spiral galaxy M33 [Rubin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.387...45R/abstract) and $11.6$ in the low-metallicity dwarf NGC 6822 [Rubin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.1875R/abstract). These values are significantly higher than the older, canonical solar value of $\sim 5$, indicating that the solar Ne/S ratio may be anomalously low or historically underestimated compared to typical nebular environments.

Mid-infrared sulfur lines were also investigated for classifying dominant excitation mechanisms in dusty environments. While early hopes suggested that the $[\text{S IV}]\ 10.51\ \mu\text{m} / [\text{S III}]\ 18.71\ \mu\text{m}$ ratio might differentiate between starburst galaxies and active galactic nuclei, studies explicitly demonstrated that this sulfur ratio is inefficient for such separation. Instead, the ratio of $[\text{O IV}]\ 25.89\ \mu\text{m} / [\text{S III}]\ 33.48\ \mu\text{m}$ has been proposed as a much more successful diagnostic diagram to distinguish AGN from starbursts [Hao et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704.1159H/abstract). Similarly, the [Fe II]/[Ne II] flux ratio serves as a powerful diagnostic for widespread supernova shocks and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) destruction in low-metallicity starburst galaxies [O'Halloran et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...641..795O/abstract). Furthermore, the combined ratio of neon to sulfur fine-structure lines, $([\text{Ne III}]\ \lambda 15.56\ \mu\text{m} + [\text{Ne II}]\ \lambda 12.81\ \mu\text{m}) / ([\text{S IV}]\ \lambda 10.51\ \mu\text{m} + [\text{S III}]\ \lambda 18.71\ \mu\text{m})$, has been established as a robust, extinction-free metallicity tracer specifically tailored for heavily obscured objects where optical [[diagnostic-emission-line-ratios|Diagnostic emission line ratios]] fail [Fernández-Ontiveros et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..226...19F/abstract).

Most recently, the combination of optical [S II] and near-infrared [S III] lines has found renewed purpose in high-redshift galaxy evolution studies. The $S_{32}$ index, defined as the ratio of [S III] $\lambda\lambda 9069, 9532$ to [S II] $\lambda\lambda 6716, 6731$, has been introduced as a novel probe of evolving interstellar medium conditions [Sanders et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888L..11S/abstract), [Sanders et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191013594S/abstract). Unlike the highly metallicity-dependent [O III]/[O II] ratio, $S_{32}$ possesses a very shallow dependence on metallicity, making it an excellent tracer of the ionization parameter. Observations using this index indicate that high-redshift ($z \sim 1.5$) galaxies exhibit harder ionizing radiation fields at a fixed oxygen abundance compared to their local counterparts [Sanders et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888L..11S/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The advent of next-generation infrared facilities, notably the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), has inaugurated a new era for [[emission-line-spectroscopy|Emission Line Spectroscopy]], extending traditional optical sulfur diagnostics into the rest-frame optical spectra of high-redshift galaxies. A primary frontier is understanding how interstellar medium (ISM) conditions and stellar ionizing spectra evolve from Cosmic Noon into the epoch of cosmic reionization. While historically, rest-frame $[\text{S II}]$ has been detected as early as $z=2.63$ in obscured high-redshift radio galaxies like MRC 2025-218 [Larkin et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...533L..61L/abstract), JWST is now routinely capturing these lines across normal galaxy populations. Recent JWST surveys, such as the AURORA program, have successfully detected auroral and fine-structure lines, including [S II] and [S III], at redshifts $z > 2$ [Sanders et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250810099S/abstract), [Shapley et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..242S/abstract). Remarkably, direct abundance patterns have already been measured in the Sunburst Arc at Cosmic Noon using weak auroral $[\text{S II}]\ \lambda\lambda 4070, 4076$ and $[\text{S III}]\ \lambda 6314$ lines [Welch et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...33W/abstract). For the most extreme nuclear sources, new theoretical diagnostics utilizing the Mappings V photoionization code model the powerful JWST/MIRI lines of highly ionized argon and neon, defining fresh avenues to derive AGN ionization parameters and gas pressures at previously inaccessible scales [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250801439Z/abstract).

A major open question centers on the physical drivers of the evolving ISM conditions at high redshift. Observations indicate that $z \gtrsim 2$ galaxies exhibit harder ionizing spectra and higher ionization parameters at fixed metallicity compared to their local counterparts. Recent JWST studies find evidence for a harder ionizing spectrum at $z \sim 3.5$ compared to $z \sim 2$ at a fixed metallicity, causing a noticeable shift in the loci of star-forming galaxies on $[\text{S II}]$ BPT diagrams [Clarke et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260111092C/abstract). Furthermore, JWST measurements suggest that the specific star formation rate (sSFR) is the primary driver of ionization conditions in star-forming galaxies up to $z=7$ [Gupta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RNAAS...8..266G/abstract). To probe these conditions, researchers have utilized the $S_{32}$ diagnostic, defined as $$S_{32} \equiv \frac{[\text{S III}]\,\lambda\lambda 9069, 9532}{[\text{S II}]\,\lambda\lambda 6716, 6731}$$ [Sanders et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888L..11S/abstract), [Sanders et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191013594S/abstract). Because $S_{32}$ incorporates both low- and [[high-ionization-emission-lines|High-ionization emission lines]], it is highly sensitive to the hardness of the stellar ionizing continuum. However, applying local empirical metallicity calibrations directly to high-$z$ JWST spectra can bias oxygen abundance estimates downward by up to $1$ dex [Hirschmann et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.3504H/abstract). Resolving the discrepancies between local and high-redshift [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] calibrations remains an active area of research. The extension of $[\text{Ne III}]/[\text{O II}]$ diagnostics to redshifts $z > 4$ has also proven invaluable for tracking the evolution of chemical abundances and the star-forming main sequence in the early universe [Shapley et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846L..30S/abstract). Furthermore, JWST's unprecedented high-sensitivity near-infrared spectroscopy has yielded the first direct determinations of Ne/O and C/O abundances in galaxies at $z > 7$, providing a nascent look at early chemical enrichment [Arellano-Córdova et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...940L..23A/abstract). Even further back, the JADES survey has reported the detection of $\text{C III}]$ emission at $z = 12.5$, marking the most distant metal transition observed to date and indicating the presence of significant neutral gas reservoirs shortly after the Big Bang [D'Eugenio et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.152D/abstract). To complement chemical studies, kinematic tracers are vital; for instance, the use of $[\text{O II}]\ \lambda3727$ emission line widths serves as a reliable kinematic tracer for measuring galaxy masses at high redshift [Kobulnicky et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph.11361K/abstract).

Another critical inquiry involves the escape of Lyman Continuum (LyC) photons, which drove cosmic reionization. Recent studies have identified a pronounced "[S II] deficiency" (abnormally low $[\text{S II}]/\text{H}\alpha$) in known LyC leaking galaxies at various redshifts [Wang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...916....3W/abstract), [Mondal et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250606831M/abstract), [Mondal et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988..171M/abstract). The precise physical mechanism underlying this deficiency is still debated. It is unclear whether the weak [S II] emission strictly indicates density-bounded H II regions—where the outer, low-ionization sulfur zone is truncated, allowing ionizing photons to escape—or if it is primarily the result of extreme ionization parameters within radiation-bounded nebulae.

Beyond targeted galaxy observations, [[line-intensity-mapping-lim|Line Intensity Mapping (LIM)]] represents a promising future direction for tracing the aggregate evolution of [[line-emitting-gas|Line-emitting gas]]. By measuring 3D spatio-spectral fluctuations without resolving individual sources, LIM can efficiently probe galaxy clustering and global star formation rates. While current millimeter and submillimeter LIM efforts largely focus on transitions like the [[o-iii-88-m-line|[O III] 88 μm line]] and [C II] 158 $\mu$m [Uzgil et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793..116U/abstract), [Roy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957...87R/abstract), integrating these macroscopic measurements with deep JWST sulfur-line demographics will provide a comprehensive, multi-phase view of [[nebular-emission-and-diagnostics|Nebular Emission and Diagnostics]] across cosmic time.

In the local Universe, enduring mysteries remain regarding the heating and excitation of diffuse and low-ionization gas. The literature remains conflicting on the precise power source for [[liners|LINERs]] and extended low-ionization emission regions; while some spatially resolved integral field studies strongly favor large-scale interstellar shocks, others point to photoionization by post-AGB stars or weak active galactic nuclei [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240807952L/abstract), [Gonzalez Delgado et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.281.1105G/abstract), [Bae et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753...10B/abstract). Similarly, the Diffuse Ionized Gas (DIG) requires an "extra heating" mechanism to explain elevated $[\text{S II}]/\text{H}\alpha$ and $[\text{N II}]/\text{H}\alpha$ ratios compared to discrete H II regions [Mathis et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...544..347M/abstract), [Hill et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...787..106H/abstract). Whether this supplemental heating arises from photoelectric heating by dust, the dissipation of turbulence, or shock mixing layers remains unresolved [Elwert et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...632..277E/abstract), [Miller et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...592...79M/abstract). Future high-resolution, multi-wavelength mapping will be essential to disentangle these competing energetic feedback mechanisms.
