## Article

The **H I mass function** (HIMF) is a fundamental statistical measure in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] that describes the comoving [[number-density|Number density]] of galaxies as a function of their neutral atomic hydrogen (H I) mass. Typically parameterized by a [[schechter-function|Schechter function]], the HIMF is characterized by a normalization constant defining the overall [[spatial-density|Spatial density]], a characteristic "knee" mass ($M^* \approx 10^{9.8} M_\odot$) that marks the exponential drop-off of gas-rich massive systems, and a faint-end slope ($\alpha$) that dictates the abundance of H I in [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]]. By integrating this mass function, astronomers can derive the [[cosmic-h-i-density|Cosmic H I density]] ($\Omega_{\rm HI}$), which represents the total cosmological mass fraction locked in cold, neutral gas—the primary fuel reservoir for star formation across cosmic time [Schneider et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...507L...9S/abstract), [Zwaan et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....125.2842Z/abstract).

Unlike optically selected galaxy samples described by the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]], [[h-i-selection|H I-selected]] populations are among the most weakly [[galaxy-clustering-and-redshift-estimation|clustered]] objects in the universe. This severe antibiasing on small scales provides unique and stringent constraints on the [[h-i-mass-to-halo-mass-relation|H I-Mass-to-Halo-Mass relation]] and modern halo occupation distribution models. The HIMF is fundamentally intertwined with robust [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]], most notably the exceptionally tight H I [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] [Wang et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.2143W/abstract), which links the physical diameter of a galaxy's neutral gas disk directly to its H I mass. Furthermore, the shape of the HIMF is highly sensitive to the physics of galaxy formation; the faint-end slope acts as a critical diagnostic for baryonic feedback mechanisms, including the suppression of dwarf galaxy formation by photoionization from the ultraviolet background and internal supernova-driven outflows. The function also exhibits distinct environmental dependencies, shifting in shape as gas is stripped from galaxies in [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]], producing [[h-i-deficient-galaxies|H I-deficient galaxies]].

This article outlines the theoretical framework and observational history of the HIMF, tracing the evolution of measurements from early targeted observations to massive [[blind-hi-surveys|Blind HI surveys]] such as HIPASS and ALFALFA. It details the sophisticated statistical tools—such as [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|maximum likelihood estimators]] and the continuous [[busy-function|Busy function]]—required to mitigate the Eddington bias, survey selection effects, and the profound impacts of cosmic variance. The article also explores the [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|cosmic evolution]] of neutral gas; while the global H I density remains remarkably stable compared to the dramatic decline in the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] since $z \sim 2$ [Lagos et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.1649L/abstract), the gas fractions of individual [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|high-redshift galaxies]] evolve significantly. Finally, it addresses major open questions in the field, including lingering tensions over the exact steepness of the faint-end slope, the physical origins of the tight H I scaling relations, and how next-generation facilities like the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) will use redshift stacking and intensity mapping to probe the HIMF in the distant universe.

## Theoretical Framework and Characterization

The theoretical characterization of the H I mass function (HIMF) relies heavily on the [[schechter-function|Schechter function]], an analytic formalism originally developed for the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] but universally applied to parameterize the space density of neutral atomic hydrogen in galaxies. The HIMF is typically expressed as the number density of galaxies $\Phi(M_{\rm HI})$ per unit H I mass interval $dM_{\rm HI}$:

$$ \Phi(M_{\rm HI}) \, dM_{\rm HI} = \Phi^* \left( \frac{M_{\rm HI}}{M^*} \right)^\alpha \exp \left( - \frac{M_{\rm HI}}{M^*} \right) \frac{dM_{\rm HI}}{M^*} $$

where $\Phi^*$ represents the normalization constant (defining the overall spatial density), $M^*$ is the characteristic mass (the "knee" of the function), and $\alpha$ is the faint-end slope governing the abundance of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]]. 

Even before the advent of modern large-scale surveys, early studies noted that the H I luminosity function for late-type spirals exhibits an abrupt cutoff at $\log M_{\rm HI} \approx 10.2$ [Bothun et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985AJ.....90.1982B/abstract), a finding that foreshadowed the characteristic knee mass established by modern 21-cm blind surveys. In the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]], large blind 21-cm surveys such as HIPASS and ALFALFA have established a firm consensus on the general shape of the HIMF. The characteristic knee mass is consistently measured at $M^* \approx 10^{9.8} M_\odot$ [Zwaan et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....125.2842Z/abstract), [Moorman et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.3559M/abstract). This mass scale marks the transition between the power-law behavior at low masses and the exponential decline at high masses, reflecting a severe suppression in the neutral gas content of the most [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]].

While $M^*$ is tightly constrained, the precise value of the faint-end slope $\alpha$ remains a subject of active debate in the literature. Standard measurements from major wide-field surveys typically find $\alpha \approx -1.3$ [Zwaan et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....125.2842Z/abstract), [Moorman et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.3559M/abstract). However, deeper or differently corrected samples have suggested a steeper low-mass slope of $\alpha \approx -1.5$ [Rosenberg et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...567..247R/abstract). Recent studies highlight that discrepancies between different surveys, as well as tensions between blind 21-cm detections and optically based H I mass estimators (such as those using SDSS data), are often driven by [[low-redshift-large-scale-structure|Low-redshift large-scale structure]] variations. Specifically, differences in the low-mass amplitude and slope are strongly influenced by cosmic variance, variations in survey sensitivity, and inherent biases in standard completeness corrections when applied to clustered environments [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...48L/abstract), [Brooks et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.4043B/abstract).

Extracting these Schechter parameters from survey data generally employs [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] techniques to account for complex, flux-limited selection functions. Precise parameterization requires careful modeling of the underlying 21-cm spectral profiles and corrections for large-scale structure clustering, which are handled by advanced statistical estimators discussed in subsequent sections. Moreover, accurately determining the mass budget of galaxies requires synthesizing a variety of reliable estimators for [[stellar-component|stellar]], gaseous, and dark matter components across different observational regimes, as systematically detailed in comprehensive physical reviews [Courteau et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86...47C/abstract).

## Historical Development of Blind 21-cm Surveys

Historically, measurements of the local neutral hydrogen content relied on targeted 21-cm emission observations of optically selected galaxies. Early aperture synthesis work established the general coincidence of the spatial H I distribution with the optical extent in nearby spiral galaxies [Shostak et al. (1973)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1973A&A....24..411S/abstract), but this targeted approach inherently biased results against optically faint, gas-rich systems. The development of [[blind-hi-surveys|Blind HI surveys]] revolutionized the field by uniformly scanning the sky for 21-cm emission independent of prior optical catalogs. Early blind surveys, such as the Arecibo Dual-Beam Survey (ADBS), demonstrated the viability of this technique and yielded initial constraints on the H I mass function (HIMF). Although limited in search volume, ADBS suggested a steep faint-end slope of $\alpha \approx -1.5$, while also noting that the HIMF shape appeared flatter in dense environments like the Virgo Cluster [Rosenberg et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...567..247R/abstract).

The first definitive, wide-area blind survey was the HIPASS (H I Parkes All Sky Survey). Utilizing the 64-m Parkes radio telescope, HIPASS provided an unprecedented sample of gas-rich galaxies in the southern hemisphere. Analysis of the 1,000 brightest HIPASS galaxies securely established that the local HIMF is well-described by a [[schechter-function|Schechter function]] [Zwaan et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....125.2842Z/abstract). This sample, spanning nearly four orders of magnitude in H I mass, yielded a faint-end slope of $\alpha = -1.30$. Crucially, HIPASS provided a robust anchor for the [[cosmic-h-i-density|Cosmic H I density]], measuring $\Omega_{\rm HI} = (3.8 \pm 0.6) \times 10^{-4} h_{75}^{-1}$ [Zwaan et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....125.2842Z/abstract).

The field advanced considerably with the ALFALFA (Arecibo Legacy Fast ALFA) survey, which offered greater sensitivity and spectral resolution. The 40% data release ($\alpha.40$) cataloged over 10,000 objects via [[h-i-selection|H I selection]] [Martin et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...750...38M/abstract), and the final $7,000 \, \mathrm{deg}^2$ release ultimately identified 21,827 sources [Oman et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.3268O/abstract). The massive statistical power of ALFALFA has allowed researchers to measure the HIMF across different cosmic environments, revealing, for example, that galaxies residing in large-scale voids exhibit slightly lower characteristic H I masses but maintain a faint-end slope similar to their counterparts in denser walls [Moorman et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.3559M/abstract). 

Complementing these wide-area efforts, targeted but highly complete volume-limited surveys and catalogs have provided critical physical context. For instance, the Updated [[nearby-galaxies-catalog|Nearby Galaxies Catalog]] (the Local Volume catalog) has supplied fundamental data on the H I mass and [[stellar-density|Stellar density]] in the immediate neighborhood of the Milky Way [Karachentsev et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AJ....145..101K/abstract). Similarly, the Local Volume H I Survey (LVHIS) atlas provided deep, resolved H I maps and kinematic data for a complete sample of 82 nearby gas-rich galaxies, offering an invaluable baseline for [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|high-resolution studies]].

Despite the success of these large [[blind-hi-surveys|Blind HI surveys]], extracting an unbiased HIMF requires complex corrections for survey sensitivity and local volume effects. A notable tension emerged in the final ALFALFA data, which showed a significant North-South asymmetry in the derived HIMF, particularly affecting the low-mass slope and characteristic mass. While initially considered a potential physical variance, subsequent mock survey analyses indicated that this offset is largely driven by systematic effects. Specifically, unaccounted variations in survey sensitivity between the two fields and biases in completeness corrections—exacerbated by foreground spatial clustering—skewed the distribution [Brooks et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.4043B/abstract). 

To overcome limits imposed by formal detection thresholds, recent methodological developments have refined how 21-cm emission is quantified. Modern analysis pipelines increasingly adopt advanced spectral modeling and [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Bayesian stacking techniques]] to statistically measure the HIMF well below nominal sensitivity limits, laying the groundwork for mapping its evolution at higher redshifts.

## Statistical Estimators and Systematic Uncertainties

Accurate determination of the H I mass function (HIMF) from radio surveys requires sophisticated statistical estimators to account for varying survey completeness, sensitivity limits, and the complex selection functions inherent in 21-cm observations.

A traditional approach to estimating the unconstrained HIMF is the [[v-vmax-test|V/Vmax test]] (often formulated as the $1/V_{\rm max}$ method), which constructs the number density $\Phi(M)$ by weighting each detected galaxy by the inverse of the maximum comoving volume $V_{{\rm max}, i}$ over which it could have been observed:
$$ \Phi(M) \, \Delta M = \sum_{i} \frac{1}{V_{{\rm max}, i}} $$
where the sum extends over all galaxies within the mass bin $[M, M + \Delta M]$. While straightforward, this method is highly susceptible to biases introduced by large-scale structure clustering and local overdensities. To mitigate this, surveys like ALFALFA utilized an evolved variant known as the $1/V_{\rm eff}$ algorithm; however, recent studies specifically analyzing this method have demonstrated that even advanced algorithms designed to correct for clustering fail to fully account for foreground spatial biases [Brooks et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.4043B/abstract). To better decouple the intrinsic mass distribution from spatial density variations, [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] (MLE) methods are frequently employed. Stepwise and bivariate MLE techniques evaluate the probability of observing a galaxy given its H I mass and distance, naturally accounting for the peculiar velocity field and rendering the resulting HIMF largely insensitive to cosmic variance [Zwaan et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....125.2842Z/abstract), [Rosenberg et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...567..247R/abstract).

The precision of these statistical methods relies heavily on the accurate extraction of flux from 21-cm emission spectra. The [[busy-function|Busy function]]—a continuous, differentiable analytic model combining an error function and a polynomial—has been widely adopted to fit the diverse shapes of H I spectra. It accurately captures everything from the broad, asymmetric double-horn profiles of massive rotating disks to the narrow Gaussian profiles of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], significantly reducing systematic errors in the integrated flux and velocity width measurements [Westmeier et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.1176W/abstract).

Near the sensitivity limits of [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]], statistical scatter due to instrumental noise interacts with the underlying mass distribution to produce the Eddington bias. Because low-mass systems vastly outnumber their high-mass counterparts, measurement uncertainties scatter more faint galaxies into higher mass bins than vice versa. If left uncorrected, the Eddington bias artificially elevates the number density at the high-mass end of the HIMF and distorts the transition around the characteristic knee mass ($M^*$) [Blecher et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3681B/abstract), [Dev et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.2693D/abstract). 

Resolving the true shape of the faint-end slope ($\alpha$) remains a persistent challenge due to systematic uncertainties. Discrepancies in $\alpha$, which is typically measured between $-1.3$ and $-1.5$, are often driven by unaccounted-for variations in survey sensitivity across different fields and the profound impact of cosmic variance in the local volume [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...48L/abstract), [Brooks et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.4043B/abstract). To investigate these observational systematics and conduct null tests of assembly bias, researchers increasingly rely on multiwavelength mock galaxy catalogs based on halo occupation distribution (HOD) models. These simulated catalogs are essential for validating statistical estimators and making robust predictions for both the HIMF and the H I [[width-function-astrophysics|velocity width function]] prior to the execution of large surveys [Paranjape et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.4147P/abstract). To mitigate these issues and probe below formal detection thresholds, modern approaches increasingly utilize [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]] and spectral stacking. By forward-modeling the HIMF and simulating H I emission lines within a Bayesian framework, it is possible to reconstruct the mass function down to $M_{\rm HI} \approx 10^{7.5} M_\odot$, well below the nominal $5\sigma$ limit of individual detections [Pan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.1227P/abstract).

## The H I-to-Halo Mass Relation

The relationship between the neutral atomic gas content of galaxies and the gravitational potential of their host dark matter halos—formalized as the [[h-i-mass-to-halo-mass-relation|H I-Mass-to-Halo-Mass relation]]—is a critical component of modern galaxy formation frameworks. Unlike the tight, monotonically increasing relationship observed between stellar mass and halo mass, the total H I mass ($M_{\rm HI}$) scales much more complexly with halo mass ($M_h$). H I-selected galaxies are observed to be among the most weakly [[galaxy-clustering-and-redshift-estimation|clustered]] populations in the local universe, characterized by a real-space correlation length of $r_0 \approx 3.3 - 3.5\ h^{-1}$ Mpc [Meyer et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...654..702M/abstract), [Martin et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...750...38M/abstract). Consequently, on small spatial scales, H I-rich galaxies are severely antibiased relative to the underlying dark matter distribution, a feature that provides primary constraints for halo occupation distribution (HOD) modeling [Martin et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...750...38M/abstract).

In HOD models, the average H I mass residing within a dark matter halo is often parameterized as a power law with an exponential low-mass cut-off:
$$ M_{\rm HI}(M_h) \propto M_h^\alpha \exp \left( - \frac{M_{\rm min}}{M_h} \right) $$
Observational constraints derived from cross-correlating large 21-cm surveys with optical group catalogs suggest a power-law index of $\alpha \approx 0.48$ and a minimum threshold halo mass of $M_{\rm min} \approx 10^{11.2}\ h^{-1} M_\odot$ [Obuljen et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.5124O/abstract). While the total $M_{\rm HI}$ within a galaxy group generally increases with $M_h$, the H I mass of central galaxies depends only weakly on halo mass above a critical threshold of $M_h \gtrsim 10^{12}\ M_\odot$ [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...48L/abstract). This flattening arises because central galaxies in massive halos become [[h-i-deficient-galaxies|H I-deficient galaxies]] due to environmental processing—such as ram-pressure stripping and starvation—which truncates the replenishment of neutral gas [Hess et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AJ....146..124H/abstract), [Dénes et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444..667D/abstract).

A notable tension between H I observations and standard optical-tracer expectations is the host environment of low-mass systems. While optically selected clustering implies that low-mass galaxies predominantly reside in low-mass halos or act as satellites in massive ones, H I clustering measurements indicate that low-$M_{\rm HI}$ galaxies ($< 10^8\ h^{-2} M_\odot$) can be hosted by halos exceeding $10^{12}\ M_\odot$ [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..111K/abstract), [Guo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...61G/abstract). This phenomenon unexpectedly increases the clustering amplitude for the lowest H I mass bins. 

Reproducing these [[observable-mass-scaling-relations|Observable-mass scaling relations]] remains a distinct challenge for semi-analytic and hydrodynamic simulations, which frequently struggle to simultaneously predict the correct H I selected halo occupation distribution for both high- and low-mass halos [Hess et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AJ....146..124H/abstract). At the low-mass end, predictions are highly sensitive to photoionization feedback from the UV background, requiring high-resolution gas dynamics to properly capture the self-shielding limit [Kim et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.2315K/abstract), [Crain et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4204C/abstract). 

Furthermore, the H I content is deeply linked to the specific angular momentum and assembly history of the halo. Cosmological simulations demonstrate that high-spin halos produce extended disks capable of retaining substantial gas reservoirs, whereas low-spin halos predominantly host H I-poor galaxies [Zoldan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2236Z/abstract). The specific angular momentum-[[mass-scaling-relations|mass relations]] for stellar, H I, and baryonic components are exceptionally tight, and the scatter in the stellar relation is driven largely by the galaxy's overall H I content [Elson et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NewA...9901964E/abstract). As such, the H I-to-halo mass relation acts as an essential diagnostic for evaluating feedback efficiency and angular momentum transport in galaxy formation models.

## Scaling Relations and Gas Distribution

The H I mass function (HIMF) is fundamentally connected to the internal structure, stellar properties, and kinematics of galaxies through several remarkably tight [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]]. These scaling laws not only constrain semi-analytic models and hydrodynamic simulations but also provide a physical context for the galaxy populations that define the shape of the HIMF.

A cornerstone of neutral gas scaling laws is the H I [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]]. Observations reveal an exceptionally tight, universal power-law correlation between the H I mass, $M_{\rm HI}$, and the diameter of the H I disk, $D_{\rm HI}$, defined at a characteristic surface density threshold (typically $1\ M_{\odot}\ {\rm pc}^{-2}$). The relation follows $D_{\rm HI} \propto M_{\rm HI}^{0.5}$ with an observed scatter of merely $\sim 0.06$ dex [Wang et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.2143W/abstract). Recent studies using early science data from the MIGHTEE survey have demonstrated that this relation, along with its intrinsic scatter of $0.054 \pm 0.003$ dex, remains stable over the last billion years of cosmic time (out to $z \sim 0.084$) and is invariant across morphological types [Rajohnson et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.2697R/abstract). Theoretical models indicate that the structural self-similarity of exponential H I disks makes this relation mathematically robust; processes that truncate the gas disk, such as ram-pressure stripping, shift galaxies along the relation rather than increasing the scatter in the $D_{\rm HI}-M_{\rm HI}$ plane [Stevens et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490...96S/abstract), [Rabyang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251204582R/abstract).

The HIMF is also intrinsically linked to the [[stellar-component|Stellar component]] of galaxies through the correlation between H I mass and stellar mass ($M_*$). The connection between neutral gas and star formation has deep historical roots; an early foundational discovery established that the number of [[h-ii-region-luminosity-function|H II regions]] in a galaxy is directly proportional to its total H I mass [Hodge et al. (1967)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1967AJ.....72..129H/abstract). The statistical properties of [[h-ii-galaxies|H II galaxies]], which often feature large gas fractions, have long been studied as potential local analogs for primeval, unevolved systems in the early universe [Terlevich et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987hrpg.work..281T/abstract). In the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]], there is a continuous relationship between the neutral gas content and the stellar disk mass spanning over three orders of magnitude [Evoli et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...743...45E/abstract). The [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]], $f_{\rm gas} = M_{\rm HI} / M_*$, systematically decreases with increasing stellar mass. Findings from the GASS survey demonstrate that for massive galaxies ($M_* > 10^{10} M_\odot$), the HIMF follows a Schechter function where parameters scale with stellar mass (the characteristic H I mass scales as $M^* \propto M_*^{0.39}$) and star formation rate (the faint-end slope scales as $\alpha \propto \text{SFR}^{0.95}$) [Lemonias et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...776...74L/abstract). Analyses of large local samples have shown that the scaling of the gas fraction with stellar mass and stellar surface density is primarily driven by the underlying specific star formation rate and the integrated [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]] [Brown et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.2479B/abstract). Furthermore, the galaxy gas content is deeply connected to the mass-[[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|metallicity relation]], which serves as an essential chemical record of cumulative gas accretion and outflows over a galaxy's lifetime [Erb et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..265..147E/abstract). While late-type galaxies are significantly more gas-rich than [[early-type-fraction|early-type systems]] at a given stellar mass, both populations exhibit characteristic scaling distributions that can be mapped directly into their respective H I mass functions [Calette et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018RMxAA..54..443C/abstract). 

Kinematically, the integrated H I emission profile provides a direct measurement of a galaxy's rotational velocity, linking the HIMF to the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] and the underlying dark matter halo mass. The distribution of these line widths—the H I [[width-function-astrophysics|Width function (astrophysics)]]—serves as an independent cosmological probe. Unlike the HIMF, which exhibits spatial variations due to environmental gas stripping that can produce [[h-i-deficient-galaxies|H I-deficient galaxies]], the H I velocity width function is found to be largely insensitive to environmental processing. This makes it a stable tracer of the dark matter [[halo-multiplicity-function|Halo multiplicity function]] [Oman et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.3268O/abstract). Multivariate analyses of H I-selected galaxies in low-density environments confirm that the best optical predictors of H I mass are the physical diameter of the stellar disk and the maximum rotation speed. In these systems, optical magnitude and rotation speed follow a tight [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] with a logarithmic slope of approximately $-8.2$ [Toribio et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732...93T/abstract). Recent analyses incorporating cosmic time evolution have demonstrated that the baryonic [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] remains a universal law extending from individual galaxies to galactic clusters, maintaining a fixed logarithmic slope of approximately 4 [Marongwe et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251120188M/abstract).

## Environmental Dependence and Clustering

The H I mass function (HIMF) is fundamentally shaped by the large-scale cosmic environment, reflecting the ongoing processing of neutral gas in different density regimes. Studies of the HIMF and spatial clustering across environments—from isolated cosmic voids to dense galaxy clusters—reveal that the gas content of galaxies is highly sensitive to their location within the cosmic web.

H I-selected galaxies exhibit some of the weakest spatial [[galaxy-clustering-and-redshift-estimation|clustering]] of any known galaxy population. The real-space two-point correlation function of local [[h-i-rich-galaxies|H I-rich galaxies]] yields a [[comoving-correlation-length|Comoving correlation length]] $r_0 \approx 3.3 - 3.5 \ h^{-1} \ \mathrm{Mpc}$ and a power-law slope $\gamma \approx 1.5$ [Meyer et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...654..702M/abstract), [Martin et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...750...38M/abstract). Unlike typical optically selected populations that roughly trace the underlying dark matter distribution, H I galaxies are found to be severely antibiased on small scales, reflecting their preferential location in lower-density environments [Martin et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...750...38M/abstract). Furthermore, the spatial distribution of H I depends strongly on galaxy color; H I clustering amplitudes are found to be approximately 1.5 times greater for red galaxies than for blue galaxies on large scales, reflecting the complex interplay between gas quenching and environmental density [Osinga et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531..450O/abstract).

As galaxies enter denser environments, such as galaxy groups and [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]], their neutral gas reservoirs are subjected to heavy environmental processing. Mechanisms such as ram-pressure stripping and starvation (strangulation) rapidly remove cold gas, transforming gas-rich spirals into [[h-i-deficient-galaxies|H I-deficient galaxies]] [Dénes et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444..667D/abstract), [Rafieferantsoa et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.3980R/abstract). Foundational datasets measuring kinematics, mass-to-light ratios, and rotation curves for spiral galaxies across different cluster and field environments have been instrumental in quantifying how location affects galaxy gas content [Vogt et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127.3273V/abstract). Low-mass cluster galaxies are among the most common structural types in these environments, and understanding their gas depletion provides critical clues to dark matter and large-scale structure formation [Conselice et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Ap&SS.284..631C/abstract). This environmental depletion is a key driver of the well-known [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]]. Studies matching H I detections to group catalogs reveal that only about 25% of H I-selected galaxies reside in groups or clusters, and that [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] are typically the first to lose their gas as they undergo pre-processing in group environments [Hess et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AJ....146..124H/abstract). The evolution of gas within these groups is complex. While individual satellite galaxies in gas-rich 'Choir' groups typically exhibit lower [[gas-fraction|gas-mass fractions]] than isolated counterparts due to tidal stripping during early group assembly [Džudžar et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.5409D/abstract), stacking analyses of low-mass groups indicate higher overall mean H I gas fractions compared to isolated galaxies. This suggests the presence of significant amounts of diffuse H I that is not associated with cataloged member galaxies [Roychowdhury et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927...20R/abstract). In massive halos ($M_h \gtrsim 10^{12} \ \mathrm{M}_\odot$), the distribution of the H I [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]] becomes strongly bimodal, with satellite galaxies near the cluster center becoming almost entirely gas-depleted [Rafieferantsoa et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.3980R/abstract).

At the opposite extreme, void galaxies residing in the most underdense regions of the universe show distinct variations in their H I mass function when compared to galaxies in large-scale walls or filaments. While the faint-end slope ($\alpha$) of the [[schechter-function|Schechter function]] remains largely invariant between voids and walls ($\alpha \approx -1.3$), the characteristic "knee" mass ($M^*$) is shifted significantly. In voids, $M^*$ is approximately $0.14 \ \mathrm{dex}$ lower than in denser wall regions (e.g., $\log(M^*/\mathrm{M}_\odot) \approx 9.86$ versus $10.00$ for a given Hubble parameter scaling) [Moorman et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.3559M/abstract). This shift to lower overall H I masses in voids is broadly consistent with the shift in the dark matter halo mass function in underdense environments.

There remains some tension in the literature regarding the exact relationship between H I mass and halo mass across environments. While standard models imply a direct mapping, clustering analyses indicate that the bias factor and clustering amplitude increase significantly for galaxies with $M_{\mathrm{HI}} > 10^9 \ \mathrm{M}_\odot$, but behave anomalously at lower masses [Guo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...61G/abstract). Some low-mass H I galaxies are found to be hosted by unexpectedly massive halos ($>10^{12} \ \mathrm{M}_\odot$), suggesting that the neutral gas content is strongly dependent on the assembly history and formation time of the halo, rather than strictly on its present-day mass [Guo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846...61G/abstract), [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..111K/abstract).

## Galaxy Formation Physics and Feedback Mechanisms

The shape of the faint-end slope of the H I mass function (HIMF) is tightly coupled to the thermodynamics of the intergalactic medium and the efficiency of baryonic feedback in shallow potential wells. Below an H I mass of $M_{\rm HI} \sim 10^8\,h^{-2}\,\text{M}_\odot$, the abundance of gas-rich [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] serves as a critical probe of photoionization feedback during and after reionization. The theoretical framework for the growth of structure, reionization, and the formation of the first galaxies provides the foundational context for understanding how these earliest potential wells accumulate and retain pristine gas [Loeb et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013fgu..book.....L/abstract). The ultraviolet background radiation heats the intergalactic medium, raising the Jeans mass and suppressing the condensation of neutral gas into small dark matter halos. Theoretical models indicate that to reproduce the observed low-mass end of the HIMF, the critical dark matter halo mass below which galaxy formation is suppressed must evolve strongly with redshift. This critical mass threshold corresponds to a halo circular velocity of $v_{\rm circ} \sim 12\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ at $z=6$, which increases to $\sim 30\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ at $z \sim 1$, and reaches $\sim 50\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ at $z=0$ [Kim et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.2315K/abstract).

Internal stellar processes, particularly supernova feedback, also dictate the retention of neutral gas. Supernova-driven outflows eject gas from the disks of dwarf galaxies, actively shaping the low-mass limit of the HIMF. Interestingly, hydrodynamic simulations reveal a complex interplay between feedback and gas retention: at fixed stellar mass, galaxies can actually acquire larger H I reservoirs in models with stronger supernova feedback. This occurs because stronger feedback requires the galaxy to form in a more massive dark matter halo to achieve a given stellar mass, and these larger halos experience systematically higher cosmic gas infall rates [Crain et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4204C/abstract). Consequently, the tuning of supernova feedback prescriptions is highly degenerate with the predicted [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]] of a galaxy. To balance this gas depletion, neutral reservoirs must be continuously replenished. Sensitive synthesis observations of nearby galaxies have provided direct observational evidence for the accretion of small amounts of gas, supporting models where ongoing gas infall sustains galactic disks and ultimately shapes the neutral reservoir [van der Hulst et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004IAUS..217..122V/abstract).

Beyond mass and feedback, [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]]—specifically the angular momentum of the host halo—significantly impacts a galaxy's neutral gas content. Semi-analytic models demonstrate a strong correlation between halo spin and H I disk sizes. Low-spin halos predominantly host H I-poor galaxies, whereas high-spin halos support extended gas disks capable of harboring a wide range of H I masses [Zoldan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2236Z/abstract). The retention of this atomic gas is also governed by the transition of H I to molecular hydrogen (H$_2$), which is heavily reliant on local metallicity and pressure [Popping et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442.2398P/abstract).

Despite successes in reproducing general trends, significant tensions remain in modeling the H I distribution across all mass scales. Current cosmological models frequently struggle to simultaneously predict the correct H I-selected halo occupation distribution for both low-mass and [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] [Hess et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AJ....146..124H/abstract). Furthermore, standard-resolution hydrodynamic simulations (such as the fiducial EAGLE runs) systematically underestimate H I column densities, leading to an artificial H I deficiency in galaxies with stellar masses $M_\star < 10^{10}\,\text{M}_\odot$. Reproducing the observed HIMF typically requires significantly higher mass and spatial resolution to properly resolve the self-shielding of neutral gas in dwarf systems before smooth accretion from the ionized intergalactic medium can be accurately tracked [Crain et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4204C/abstract).

## Cosmic Evolution and Redshift Stacking

Measuring the evolution of the H I mass function (HIMF) and the [[cosmic-h-i-density|Cosmic H I density]] ($\Omega_{\rm HI}$) across cosmic time is fundamentally limited by the intrinsic faintness of the 21-cm emission line. Direct blind detections of individual galaxies are largely restricted to the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]] ($z \lesssim 0.1$). Consequently, tracing the HIMF to higher redshifts increasingly relies on statistical methods such as redshift stacking, intensity mapping, and multi-wavelength scaling relations.

**Cosmic H I Density and Gas Fraction Evolution**
In the local universe, the cosmological mass density of neutral gas is firmly measured at $\Omega_{\rm HI} \approx 3.8 \times 10^{-4} h_{75}^{-1}$ [Zwaan et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....125.2842Z/abstract). Observations and semi-analytic models indicate that, unlike the [[global-star-formation-rate|cosmic star formation rate]] and molecular hydrogen (${\rm H}_2$) density—which peak around $z \sim 2$ and decline rapidly toward the present day—the cosmic atomic hydrogen density evolves only weakly over time [Lagos et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.1649L/abstract), [Popping et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..477P/abstract). 

Despite this relatively constant global density, the gas properties of individual galaxies evolve significantly. Photometric and scaling estimators applied to [[redshift-catalogs|Redshift catalogs]] such as the [[deep2-redshift-survey|DEEP2 Redshift Survey]] demonstrate that massive star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 1$ exhibit gas fractions $4$ to $12$ times higher than their local counterparts at fixed stellar mass ($M_* \approx 10^{10} M_\odot$) [Zhang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...648A..25Z/abstract). Furthermore, the tight correlation between H I mass and star formation rate observed locally appears to weaken or vanish by $z \sim 1$. This suggests that high-redshift H I is more strongly coupled to cosmological gas accretion processes than to immediate star formation [Zhang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...648A..25Z/abstract). While current statistical techniques reach $z \sim 1$, mapping H I into the so-called "redshift desert" ($1.4 < z < 3$)—the epoch where cosmic star formation and mass assembly culminate—remains exceptionally challenging but essential for completing our understanding of galaxy evolution [Renzini et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Msngr.137...41R/abstract).

**Redshift Stacking**
To empirically constrain the HIMF at intermediate redshifts ($0 < z \lesssim 0.5$), researchers employ redshift stacking. By co-adding the 21-cm spectra of optically selected galaxies with known redshifts, surveys can measure average H I masses well below nominal flux limits. Bayesian stacking frameworks can formally reconstruct the Schechter function parameters ($\alpha$, $M^*$, and $\Phi^*$) for populations that are individually undetectable. In simulations tuned for upcoming facilities, these techniques have been shown to recover the HIMF down to $M_{\rm HI} \approx 10^{7.5} M_\odot$ out to $z \sim 0.55$, providing a critical bridge between local direct detections and high-redshift statistical measures [Pan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.1227P/abstract). At higher redshifts, stacking 21-cm signals at $z \sim 1$ can constrain the HIMF by probing galaxies that reside within dark matter halos in the mass range of $10^{11.4} - 10^{12.5} h^{-1} M_\odot$, revealing the contribution of undetected satellite galaxies to the total parent halo H I signal [Khandai et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.2580K/abstract).

**Intensity Mapping**
At higher redshifts, 21-cm intensity mapping offers a powerful statistical probe of the neutral gas distribution. Because the auto-power spectrum of 21-cm emission suffers from severe foreground contamination, modern efforts focus on cross-correlating intensity maps with optical [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] data. The shot noise term of this cross-correlation scales directly with the average H I brightness temperature of the target galaxies. This allows quantitative constraints to be placed on the average H I mass per galaxy for populations too faint to directly observe [Wolz et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.3220W/abstract), [Wolz et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.1007W/abstract). Such [[multi-tracer-analysis|Multi-tracer analysis]] is expected to break degeneracies between the H I bias and $\Omega_{\rm HI}$, potentially enabling 10% level constraints on the standard Schechter function using shot noise alone [Chen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502.5259C/abstract). Furthermore, semi-analytical models of the post-reionization universe suggest that the H I bias increases with redshift, and that H I-poor satellite galaxies significantly impact the one-halo term of the H I power spectrum, complicating the extraction of the true cosmic density from intensity maps [Spinelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.5434S/abstract).

Beyond statistical techniques, gravitational lensing provides a rare pathway to detect individual high-redshift H I emitters. Bayesian formalisms applied to strongly lensed galaxies have yielded marginal detections as high as $z \approx 0.4$, representing an early step toward directly sampling the high-redshift HIMF [Blecher et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3681B/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Frontiers

Next-generation radio facilities and advanced statistical techniques are poised to resolve several outstanding tensions in the local H I mass function (HIMF) and push measurements into the high-redshift universe. 

One of the most persistent active debates concerns the exact value of the HIMF faint-end slope, $\alpha$. Measurements of the local universe have yielded values ranging from $\alpha \approx -1.3$ to $\alpha \approx -1.5$, and it remains unclear to what extent this discrepancy is driven by cosmic variance versus uncorrected survey sensitivity biases [Zwaan et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....125.2842Z/abstract), [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...48L/abstract). Resolving this tension is critical for testing theoretical models of galaxy formation, as the abundance of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] is highly sensitive to the critical halo mass at which photoionization feedback from the UV background suppresses gas condensation [Kim et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.2315K/abstract). The FAST telescope, through its CRAFTS extragalactic survey, is expected to detect nearly $4.8 \times 10^5$ galaxies, which will allow for the recovery of the faint-end slope down to $10^7 \, M_\odot$ and precisely measure the characteristic knee mass ($M^*$) out to $z = 0.1$ [Zhang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.1741Z/abstract).

Extending HIMF measurements to higher redshifts represents another major frontier, as the 21-cm signal becomes prohibitively faint for direct detection. At $z \sim 1$, observations indicate that the correlation between H I gas and the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] weakens significantly, suggesting that high-redshift H I is driven more by cosmological accretion than by immediate star formation [Zhang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...648A..25Z/abstract). To bridge the gap between local measurements and the distant universe, pathfinder facilities like MeerKAT are utilizing deep integrations. The MIGHTEE survey, for example, has blindly probed the H I [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] out to $z \sim 0.084$, confirming its exceptionally tight scatter ($\sim 0.06$ dex) and revealing no significant redshift evolution over the last billion years [Rajohnson et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.2697R/abstract). To probe even deeper, the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) and its precursors will rely heavily on Bayesian stacking techniques—capable of reconstructing the HIMF below nominal detection thresholds down to $10^{7.5} \, M_\odot$ at $z \sim 0.5$ [Pan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.1227P/abstract)—as well as 21-cm intensity mapping cross-correlations with optical catalogs [Wolz et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.3220W/abstract), [Chen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502.5259C/abstract). Looking even further back, observing the highly redshifted 21-cm line presents a profound opportunity to map the neutral intergalactic medium during the cosmic dark ages and the epoch of reionization, pushing our understanding of the primordial neutral gas distribution to its absolute limit [Furlanetto et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S..83F/abstract).

Finally, observational systematics and environmental effects continue to complicate the interpretation of the HIMF as a cosmological tracer. A notable example is the reported north-south asymmetry in the ALFALFA survey HIMF, which researchers are actively debating as either a physical manifestation of large-scale structure clustering or a systematic artifact related to completeness correction algorithms [Oman et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.3268O/abstract), [Brooks et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.4043B/abstract). Because the H I mass of a galaxy is highly susceptible to environmental stripping in dense environments, there is growing momentum to use the H I velocity width function—closely related to the [[circular-velocity-function|Circular velocity function]]—as a primary cosmological probe. The velocity width function is significantly more resistant to environmental gas loss, making it a more stable tracer of the underlying dark matter halo mass function and a more robust test of the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm [Oman et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.3268O/abstract), [Ristea et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240905081R/abstract).
