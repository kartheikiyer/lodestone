#
The **He II λ1640 line** is a prominent rest-ultraviolet recombination emission line produced by the $n=3 \rightarrow 2$ electronic transition of singly ionized helium (He$^+$). Serving as the Balmer-alpha equivalent for the He II ion, its production is strictly contingent upon a hard extreme-ultraviolet radiation field. Because stripping the second electron from a helium atom requires an ionization potential of 54.4 eV (corresponding to a photon wavelength of $\lambda < 228$ Å), the presence of [[he-ii-emission|He II emission]] is an unambiguous indicator of the most highly energetic astrophysical environments. Unlike resonant transitions such as hydrogen Lyman-$\alpha$, the He II λ1640 line is optically thin. This property makes it an exceptionally robust, scatter-free probe of intrinsic gas kinematics, accretion processes, and ionizing photon budgets in early galaxies [Yang et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...640..539Y/abstract).

In stellar astrophysics, the line is a vital tracer of young, massive stellar populations. In local starbursts and [[h-ii-regions|H II regions]], broad He II λ1640 emission is a hallmark of [[wolf-rayet-signatures|Wolf-Rayet signatures]] arising from dense, fast stellar winds. When combined with its optical cascade counterpart, the [[he-ii-4686-line|He II 4686 line]], the fixed quantum mechanical ratio of the two lines provides a purely stellar, IMF-independent diagnostic for measuring dust reddening [Conti et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990AJ.....99..898C/abstract). Beyond local galaxies, He II λ1640 is a primary target in the search for metal-free Population III stars in the early universe. Because these primordial stars are predicted to possess top-heavy mass distributions and extremely high effective temperatures, their unusually hard [[stellar-ionizing-radiation|Stellar ionizing radiation]] is expected to power strong [[he-ii-emission|He II emission]]. However, isolating pure Pop III clusters remains challenging, as enriched systems hosting high-mass X-ray binaries or direct collapse black holes can easily mimic these extreme [[diagnostic-emission-line-ratios|Diagnostic emission line ratios]].

The 54.4 eV ionization threshold also establishes He II λ1640 as a premier diagnostic for high-energy accretion phenomena. In the study of active galactic nuclei (AGN), the line traces high-velocity, highly ionized gas deep within the [[agn-broad-line-region|AGN Broad Line Region]]. It is exceptionally sensitive to the AGN continuum, and emission-line diagnostics utilizing its optical cascade counterpart, [[he-ii-4686-line|He II 4686 line]], have proven superior to the traditional [[bpt-diagram|BPT diagram]] for identifying hidden, low-luminosity AGN embedded in highly star-forming galaxies [Bär et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.2879B/abstract). On larger scales, spatially resolved He II spectroscopy is used to map the extended [[line-emitting-gas|Line-emitting gas]] in circumgalactic halos and Ly$\alpha$ blobs, differentiating between relatively quiescent cooling radiation and shock-ionized galactic outflows.

Despite its well-understood atomic physics, the interpretation of He II λ1640 remains the subject of ongoing debate. A major open question in the field is the "missing ionizing photons" problem: standard stellar population synthesis models consistently fail to reproduce the high equivalent widths of He II observed in high-redshift, metal-poor star-forming galaxies. It remains uncertain whether resolving this tension requires novel binary stellar evolution pathways, very massive stars, or alternative non-stellar [[excitation-mechanisms|Excitation mechanisms]] [Nanayakkara et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...624A..89N/abstract). Driven by recent advancements with the James Webb Space Telescope and modern integral field spectrographs, current research focuses on resolving these discrepancies and utilizing He II intensity mapping to definitively characterize the energetic sources that drove cosmic reionization.

## Atomic Physics and Emission Mechanisms

Following ionization by extreme-ultraviolet photons ($E > 54.4$ eV, $\lambda < 228$ Å) [Cai et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...736L..28C/abstract), [Kehrig et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908L..54K/abstract), the subsequent recombination of free electrons with He$^{++}$ ions dictates [[nebular-emission-and-diagnostics|Nebular Emission and Diagnostics]]. The $\lambda 1640$ line is produced during the recombination cascade as electrons transition downwards through the $n=3 \rightarrow 2$ energy levels. Unlike the He II Ly$\alpha$ transition at $304$ Å, which is strongly resonant, He II $\lambda 1640$ is optically thin. This property makes it a highly robust probe of gas accretion and ionization budgets, as it is immune to the resonant scattering effects and intergalactic UV background sensitivity that complicate the interpretation of [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]] or hydrogen Lyman-$\alpha$ [Yang et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...640..539Y/abstract).

Because He II $\lambda 1640$ and the optical [[he-ii-4686-line|He II 4686 line]] ($n=4 \rightarrow 3$) are both recombination lines originating from the same ion, their intrinsic flux ratio is tightly constrained by atomic physics. Under typical [[wolf-rayet-signatures|Wolf-Rayet]] stellar wind conditions, recombination theory fixes the He II 1640/4686 intensity ratio at $\approx 7.76$ [Leitherer et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AJ....158..192L/abstract). This rigidly defined theoretical value allows the line pair to serve as one of the most reliable [[diagnostic-emission-line-ratios|Diagnostic emission line ratios]] for measuring stellar dust reddening. This diagnostic is highly advantageous because it is independent of the underlying stellar initial mass function or star formation history [Conti et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990AJ.....99..898C/abstract), [Leitherer et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AJ....158..192L/abstract).

The $54.4$ eV requirement restricts He II $\lambda 1640$ emission to the most energetic astrophysical environments. Nebular recombination emission traces hard [[stellar-ionizing-radiation|Stellar ionizing radiation]] from sources such as Population III stars, metal-poor Very Massive Stars (VMS), and the accretion disks of AGN (e.g., deep within the [[agn-broad-line-region|AGN Broad Line Region]]). Additionally, the line can be driven by other [[excitation-mechanisms|Excitation mechanisms]] like collisional excitation in large-scale shocks, or appear as broad [[wolf-rayet-signatures|Wolf-Rayet signatures]] originating directly from stellar winds. 

Despite the well-understood atomic physics governing the transition, standard photoionization models frequently struggle to reproduce the high equivalent widths ($EW \approx 0.2-10$ Å) of He II $\lambda 1640$ observed in high-redshift star-forming galaxies. It remains an active area of debate whether resolving this tension requires novel binary stellar evolution pathways, an overabundance of massive stars, or alternative non-stellar ionizing sources such as high-mass X-ray binaries [Nanayakkara et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...624A..89N/abstract), [Senchyna et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.6112S/abstract), [Kehrig et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908L..54K/abstract).

## Diagnostics for Population III Stars and Reionization

The search for the first generation of metal-free stars relies heavily on the He II $\lambda 1640$ line as a primary observational tracer. Because Population III (Pop III) stars are predicted to form with top-heavy initial mass functions (IMFs) and possess extremely high effective temperatures, they produce a significantly harder [[stellar-ionizing-radiation|Stellar ionizing radiation]] spectrum than Population II stars. This hard spectrum provides the $E > 54.4$ eV photons required for He$^+$ ionization, resulting in strong [[he-ii-emission|He II emission]] in primordial galaxies [Cai et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...736L..28C/abstract), [Grisdale et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.5517G/abstract). Theoretical models indicate that the line-intensity ratio of He II $\lambda 1640$ to [[h-alpha-astronomy|Hα]] serves as a critical diagnostic: a ratio of $\mathrm{He\ II}/\mathrm{H}\alpha \gtrsim 0.1$ is a strong indicator of a top-heavy Pop III IMF featuring stars of several hundred solar masses, whereas ratios $\lesssim 0.01$ signal the end of the Pop III-dominated era [Parsons et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933..141P/abstract). Furthermore, composite spectra from $z \approx 4.5$ Ly$\alpha$-emitting galaxies have been used to place stringent upper limits on the He II/Ly$\alpha$ ratio, tightly constraining the overall contribution of Pop III stars at those epochs [Wang et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...706..762W/abstract).

Direct spectroscopic detection of these pristine systems during the reionization era remains exceedingly difficult. Deep narrowband imaging of the $z = 6.96$ galaxy IOK-1 yielded a He II $\lambda 1640$ flux measurement of $(1.2 \pm 1.0) \times 10^{-18} \mathrm{\ erg\ s}^{-1} \mathrm{\ cm}^{-2}$, corresponding to a $1\sigma$ upper limit on the Pop III star formation rate of $<0.5\ M_\odot \mathrm{\ yr}^{-1}$. Assuming a Salpeter IMF with stellar masses between $50$ and $1000\ M_\odot$, this represents less than $\sim 6\%$ of the galaxy's total star formation [Cai et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...736L..28C/abstract). Similarly, in the extraordinarily luminous $z=8.683$ galaxy EGSY8p7, the non-detection of He II $\lambda 1640$ was used alongside the detection of N V to characterize the hard ionizing spectrum and place strong constraints on the nature of Ly$\alpha$-emitting galaxies deep in the reionization era [Mainali et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.1180M/abstract). To circumvent the difficulty of resolving individual faint sources at high redshift, intensity mapping of the He II $\lambda 1640$ line—cross-correlated with 21 cm intergalactic medium emission or CO(1-0) lines—has been proposed to measure the large-scale spatial fluctuations of Pop III starbursts between $z=10$ and $z=20$ [Visbal et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.2506V/abstract), [Parsons et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933..141P/abstract). In the local universe, evidence from the northwest region of I Zw 18 (I Zw 18-NW) suggests a possible 'pocket' of pristine star formation responsible for nebular He II $\lambda 1640$ emission, identified through the absence of corresponding metal lines [Heap et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150402742H/abstract).

The interpretation of high-redshift He II $\lambda 1640$ emitters is often ambiguous in the literature, as exemplified by the $z \approx 6.6$ Ly$\alpha$ emitter CR7. Initially, CR7 was proposed as a viable candidate for a Pop III starburst due to its exceptionally bright He II $\lambda 1640$ emission and the apparent absence of metal lines [Visbal et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460L..59V/abstract). However, subsequent analyses have contested this origin. High-resolution spectroscopy revealed a $+160 \mathrm{\ km\ s}^{-1}$ velocity offset between the Ly$\alpha$ and He II peaks, a kinematic signature that radiation-hydrodynamics simulations suggest is more consistent with an outflow driven by a direct collapse black hole (DCBH) rather than a Pop III star cluster [Smith et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.3143S/abstract). Furthermore, theoretical limits on the stellar mass and Ly$\alpha$ luminosity of Pop III-dominated halos indicate that CR7 may simply be too luminous to be powered purely by metal-free stars [Yajima et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467L..51Y/abstract).

The advent of JWST has revolutionized the search for pristine gas, but significant diagnostic challenges remain. Cosmological simulations demonstrate that the absence of metal emission lines alone is an insufficient criterion for identifying Pop III populations; metal-enriched galaxies can maintain very weak metal lines that fall below observational sensitivity limits [Katz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524..351K/abstract). Additionally, metal-poor systems harboring [[wolf-rayet-signatures|Wolf-Rayet signatures]] or X-ray binaries can mimic the hard ionizing spectrum of metal-free stars. Because the massive Pop III stars responsible for the hard emission have lifetimes of only a few million years, the pristine He II signature fades rapidly [Katz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524..351K/abstract). Consequently, robust identification of Pop III galaxies in the early universe will require combining He II $\lambda 1640$ measurements with comprehensive [[diagnostic-emission-line-ratios|Diagnostic emission line ratios]] across the rest-frame [[ultraviolet-spectral-characteristics|ultraviolet and optical spectra]].

## Stellar Wind Signatures and Wolf-Rayet Stars

In the spectra of star-forming galaxies and [[h-ii-regions|H II regions]], the He II $\lambda 1640$ line is frequently observed as a broad emission feature originating in the fast, dense stellar winds of Wolf-Rayet (WR) stars and other evolved massive stars [Robert et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...418..749R/abstract). Historically, the early discovery of broad [[he-ii-4686-line|He II λ4686]] emission in the dwarf galaxy Tololo 3 first signaled the presence of [[wolf-rayet-signatures|Wolf-Rayet stars]] (primarily of the WN sequence) in such compact, star-forming systems [Kunth et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981A&A...101L...5K/abstract). Shortly thereafter, early space-based [[emission-line-spectroscopy|ultraviolet spectroscopy]] detected He II $\lambda 1640$ in the core of extragalactic H II regions like IC 132 in M33, attributing the emission to a massive star or multiple system with WR features [Dodorico et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983MNRAS.203..157D/abstract). Subsequent optical and ultraviolet studies uncovered further galaxies with WR emission features, noting a general correlation where the luminosity of the WR He II feature scales with the parent galaxy's mass and metallicity [Kunth et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986A&A...169...71K/abstract). Moreover, observations of WN-type WR stars in the Large Magellanic Cloud have shown that their He II $\lambda 1640$ emission can be significantly stronger than in their Galactic counterparts, reflecting metallicity-dependent wind properties [Smith et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983A&AS...54..229S/abstract). This connection remains robust across various environments; for example, the detection of WR emission features, including high-ionization He II $\lambda 4686$, in the blue compact dwarf galaxy Mrk 22 highlights the role of these massive stars in actively evolving systems [Paswan et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.4566P/abstract). This ultraviolet stellar wind emission, alongside its optical counterpart, the [[he-ii-4686-line|He II 4686 line]], forms the characteristic [[very-blue-optical-continuum-spectrum|blue bump]] that serves as a primary observational tracer of WR populations [Izotov et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...487L..37I/abstract). Because the formation of broad He II emission requires an extreme radiation field capable of ionizing He$^+$ ($E > 54.4$ eV) and massive stellar outflows, its presence is a strong indicator of stars with initial masses exceeding $30 M_{\odot}$ [Robert et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...418..749R/abstract). Consequently, the detection of these WR features is widely used to constrain the upper-mass cutoff of the initial mass function (IMF) and typically indicates starburst ages of 3 to 5 Myr, corresponding to the short-lived WR evolutionary phase [Gonzalez-Delgado et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...439..604G/abstract). Recent far-ultraviolet surveys of local galaxies continue to reinforce these connections; for example, in the H II regions of M101, broad He II $\lambda 1640$ emission is strongly correlated with nitrogen WR features and the presence of young, metal-enriched massive stars [Berg et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971...87B/abstract).

Despite its utility, standard single-star population synthesis models often struggle to consistently match observed He II strengths. In early observations, characterizing these WR populations in low-metallicity environments like the dwarf galaxy I Zw 18 relied primarily on the optical He II $\lambda 4686$ line [Izotov et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...487L..37I/abstract). More recently, far-ultraviolet observations have demonstrated that models frequently fail to reproduce the intense nebular and stellar He II strengths in metal-poor galaxies [Kehrig et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908L..54K/abstract). The literature reflects an active debate regarding the source of this discrepancy. To reconcile these tensions, recent models frequently invoke an overabundance of very massive stars (VMS, $>100 M_{\odot}$) or the products of binary mass transfer and stellar mergers, which can sustain the necessary hard ionizing flux and broad [[wolf-rayet-signatures|Wolf-Rayet signatures]] on timescales up to $\sim 10$ Myr [Senchyna et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.6112S/abstract), [Martins et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...698A.262M/abstract). In star-forming rings like AM 0644-741, binary stellar population models are often required to replicate observed high-ionization features and WR signatures [Gómez-González et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4369G/abstract). Furthermore, at high redshift, recent chemical abundance analyses of the highly magnified Sunburst Arc with JWST have detected localized nitrogen enhancement consistent with Wolf-Rayet stars alongside these high-ionization zones [Welch et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...33W/abstract). However, even state-of-the-art models including binary evolution sometimes fail to simultaneously match the strongest wind features and the optical nebular line constraints, leaving the exact ionizing mechanisms in extreme metal-poor starbursts an open question [Senchyna et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.6112S/abstract). Recent spatially resolved integral field observations offer new clues; in the low-metallicity galaxy J1044+0353, mapping with the Keck Cosmic Web Imager revealed He II nebulae photoionized by young star clusters, where the line profiles indicate slow stellar winds consistent with high stellar rotation at low metallicity [Martin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966..190M/abstract).

Beyond probing the stellar population itself, the combination of ultraviolet and optical He II recombination lines provides a powerful, purely stellar diagnostic for dust attenuation. Because He II $\lambda 1640$ and [[he-ii-4686-line|λ4686]] originate from cascades within the same ion, their intrinsic flux ratio is strictly governed by atomic physics. Under typical WR wind conditions, the intrinsic He II 1640/4686 ratio is fixed at $\approx 7.76$ [Leitherer et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AJ....158..192L/abstract). By comparing the observed ratio to this theoretical value, observers can directly measure the dust reddening affecting the massive stars. This technique was pioneered for individual WR stars in the Milky Way and Magellanic Clouds [Conti et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990AJ.....99..898C/abstract) and has since been calibrated for integrated galaxy spectra [Leitherer et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AJ....158..192L/abstract). Crucially, because this He II 1640/4686 ratio measures the attenuation of the stellar light itself, it is entirely independent of the assumed IMF or star formation history. It therefore provides a critical complement to [[diagnostic-emission-line-ratios|Diagnostic emission line ratios]] like the nebular [[steep-balmer-decrements|Balmer decrement]], allowing astronomers to disentangle the dust column obscuring the stars from that affecting the surrounding ionized gas.

## Active Galactic Nuclei and Quasars

The high extreme-ultraviolet sensitivity of He II $\lambda$1640 makes it a premier diagnostic of the hard radiation field in active galactic nuclei (AGN). It is an exceptionally valuable indicator of AGN activity in environments where traditional [[optical-emission-line-diagnostics|Optical Emission-Line Diagnostics]] are obscured or diluted by host galaxy star formation. 

In the [[narrow-line-regions-and-seyferts|Narrow-Line Regions and Seyferts]], He II emission can successfully identify AGN that are heavily masked by strong host-galaxy starbursts. For example, utilizing diagnostics based on the optical cascade counterpart, [[he-ii-4686-line|He II λ4686]], rather than standard [[bpt-diagram|BPT diagram]] line ratios increased the identified AGN census in local Sloan Digital Sky Survey samples by $\sim 22\%$ [Bär et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.2879B/abstract). Similarly, high-ionization features like He II are found in some [[liners|LINER]] galaxies such as NGC 7213, revealing a non-stellar continuum similar to Type 1 Seyferts and separating them from purely shock-excited populations [Filippenko et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApJ...285..458F/abstract). The ultraviolet $\lambda$1640 line is also frequently observed in [[extended-narrow-line-regions|Extended narrow-line regions]], where it traces multi-phase quiescent gas photoionized by the central engine out to scales exceeding $100$ kpc in high-redshift radio galaxies [Maxfield et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002AJ....123.2321M/abstract), [Falkendal et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...645A.120F/abstract).

Within the [[agn-broad-line-region|AGN Broad Line Region]] (BLR), the high ionization potential of He$^+$ dictates that broad He II emission originates deep in the gravitational potential well, much closer to the central supermassive black hole than the [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]]. This radial stratification has been mapped quantitatively through reverberation mapping of the optical cascade counterpart, [[he-ii-4686-line|He II 4686 line]]. In the Seyfert 1 galaxy Mrk 335, the He II line exhibits a rapid continuum lag of $2.7 \pm 0.6$ days, significantly shorter than the $13.9$-day lag of [[balmer-series-spectroscopy|Hβ]]. The He II velocity profile is correspondingly roughly twice as broad as H$\beta$ [Kassebaum et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...475..106K/abstract), confirming that the high-ionization gas is in virialized motion. Such short-lag measurements enable precise black hole mass estimates, yielding $M_{\rm BH} = (2.6 \pm 0.8) \times 10^7 M_\odot$ for Mrk 335 [Grier et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...744L...4G/abstract). Furthermore, the variability of He II in Seyfert galaxies like NGC 5548 suggests the presence of two distinct cloud populations and points to a highly variable extreme-ultraviolet continuum dominating the BLR [Kallman et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ApJ...328..523K/abstract).

The extreme sensitivity of He II to the ionizing continuum also makes it a powerful tracer of dynamic accretion states. Recent systematic surveys have identified a unique population of "changing-look" quasars identified by the dramatic appearance or disappearance of the broad optical He II $\lambda$4686 emission. A sample of 34 such He II changing-look quasars exhibits intense continuum variability and demonstrates a preference for systems with high Eddington ratios and minimal host-galaxy spectral contamination [Lu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...994..154L/abstract). Additionally, the ratio of N V relative to He II $\lambda$1640 is utilized as an indicator of chemical abundances and extreme nitrogen overabundance in highly enriched quasi-stellar objects (QSOs) such as Q0353-383 [Baldwin et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...583..649B/abstract). Historically, it has also been suggested that strong, narrow He II $\lambda$1640 emission may be correlated with the radio properties of QSOs and the presence of associated [[narrow-absorption-line-systems|absorption systems]] [Foltz et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988qsal.proc...53F/abstract).

At high redshifts, He II $\lambda$1640 is a critical rest-UV feature used to distinguish AGN from metal-poor stellar populations. However, the interpretation of [[high-ionization-emission-lines|High-ionization emission lines]] in the early universe remains an active area of debate. While strong He II theoretically signals a hard AGN spectrum, observations of $z > 4$ quasars and galaxies often reveal conflicting line ratios. For instance, anomalously high N V/He II ratios observed in some early galaxies are inconsistent with standard AGN photoionization models. The literature explicitly notes that this tension may imply localized nitrogen enhancement or resonant scattering effects rather than solely reflecting the shape of the ionizing continuum [Tang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991..217T/abstract). Thus, while He II $\lambda$1640 is an unambiguous indicator of highly energetic photons, employing it to accurately constrain the exact continuum shape requires careful treatment of the host system's chemical abundances and gas kinematics.

Beyond steady-state AGN accretion, He II $\lambda$1640 is frequently observed in extreme, high-energy transient phenomena. It has been detected in the early-stage spectra of Type II supernovae, such as SN 2023ixf, where it appears as a flash-ionization feature from nitrogen/helium-rich circumstellar material [Yamanaka et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASJ...75L..27Y/abstract). High-resolution spectroscopy of SN 2024ggi has also detected early-phase asymmetric He II emission, likely driven by radiative acceleration during the supernova explosion [Pessi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...688L..28P/abstract). Beyond the earliest explosive phases, high-ionization conditions in non-AGN environments can be sustained in young supernova remnants; for example, the detection of He II and coronal lines in the remnant S8 within the dwarf galaxy IC 1613 indicates a wide range of shock velocities [Fesen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902...19F/abstract). Similarly, prominent He II lines are characteristic of fast-rising tidal disruption events (TDEs) such as AT 2023clx in the nearby LINER galaxy NGC 3799, potentially indicating collisional excitation within a low-density photosphere [Charalampopoulos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.350C/abstract). This connects to earlier discoveries, where strong He II emission was observed as part of the 'light echo' from a huge X-ray flare in the galaxy SDSS J095209.56+214313.3, which was similarly attributed to a tidal disruption event [Komossa et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...678L..13K/abstract).

## Extended Nebulae and Lyα Blobs

Spatially resolved [[he-ii-emission|He II emission]] at $\lambda 1640$ is a critical diagnostic for the extended [[line-emitting-gas|Line-emitting gas]] found in the circumgalactic medium (CGM) and massive high-redshift galaxy overdensities. While hydrogen Lyman-$\alpha$ is typically the brightest emission line in these structures—often forming giant Ly$\alpha$ blobs—its resonant scattering complicates kinematic and physical interpretations. In contrast, He II $\lambda 1640$ is optically thin, ensuring that its surface brightness and line profiles trace the intrinsic distribution and kinematics of the highly ionized gas without the spatial diffusion effects that plague Ly$\alpha$ [Yang et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...640..539Y/abstract), [Prescott et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799...62P/abstract).

A primary application of spatially resolved spectroscopy in the far-ultraviolet is distinguishing between the [[excitation-mechanisms|Excitation mechanisms]] powering extended halos. Specifically, He II $\lambda 1640$ is used to differentiate gravitational cooling radiation from galactic outflows and AGN photoionization. Theoretical models propose that pristine gas accreting onto young galaxies should produce He II cooling radiation characterized by narrow line widths ($\sigma < 400$ km s$^{-1}$), allowing it to be kinematically separated from the high-velocity signatures of starburst-driven superwinds [Yang et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...640..539Y/abstract).

Observations have successfully mapped He II kinematics to infer the dynamical state of the CGM, though distinguishing between power sources remains a challenge in the literature. In a $\sim 80$ kpc nebula at $z \approx 1.67$, low kinematic offsets ($100-200$ km s$^{-1}$) between Ly$\alpha$ and He II confirm that the photons are produced *in situ*, with a coherent velocity shear of $\approx 500$ km s$^{-1}$ indicating the large-scale rotation of accreting material [Prescott et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799...62P/abstract). Similarly, diffuse He II emission on 45 kpc scales around a $z=2.65$ quasar exhibits kinematics and metallicities that provide evidence for gas recycling rather than a purely disruptive outflow [Zhang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952..124Z/abstract). Spatially resolved spectroscopy also probes chemical variations; for instance, extended He II $\lambda 1640$ emission in the high-redshift radio galaxy MRC 2104-242 reveals metallicity gradients within its giant emission-line halo [Overzier et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002RMxAC..13..203O/abstract).

In massive galaxy overdensities, however, the literature reflects ongoing tension regarding the dominant ionization mechanism. Enormous Ly$\alpha$ nebulae (ELANe) at $z=2-3$ exhibit He II and C IV emission extending over $\gtrsim 30$ kpc with broad, double-peaked profiles ($\text{FWHM} \approx 700-1000$ km s$^{-1}$). These extreme kinematics suggest shock-induced ionization driven by AGN outflows, though strong photoionization by a heavily obscured central source cannot be fully ruled out [Cai et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837...71C/abstract). Conversely, extended He II halos reaching up to 150 kpc around the $z \simeq 3.6$ radio galaxy 4C 19.71 exhibit narrow line widths ($\text{FWHM} \sim 180$ km s$^{-1}$) and low velocity offsets. This traces a relatively quiescent, multi-phase CGM where the He II emission is best explained by diffuse AGN photoionization rather than shocks [Falkendal et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...645A.120F/abstract).

At lower redshifts, spatially resolved He II $\lambda 1640$ serves as a probe of the interface between different thermal phases in cluster environments. In the central cluster galaxy M87, He II emission traces intermediate-temperature ($\sim 10^5$ K) gas that is spatially associated with $10^4$ K optical filaments embedded in the $\sim 10^7$ K X-ray coronal halo. The observed He II fluxes are quantitatively consistent with models where thermal conduction regulates the interaction between the hot halo and the cold gas, demonstrating the line's utility in constraining transport processes and cooling flows in massive galaxies [Sparks et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...750L...5S/abstract).

## Spectral Modeling and Line Ratios

The high ionization potential of He II ($E > 54.4$ eV, or 4 Rydbergs) makes the He II $\lambda 1640$ line a sensitive anchor for [[diagnostic-emission-line-ratios|Diagnostic emission line ratios]] in the rest-ultraviolet. As high-redshift observations increasingly rely on UV spectra where the traditional optical [[bpt-diagram|BPT diagram]] is redshifted out of range, He II $\lambda 1640$ is frequently combined with collisionally excited metal lines—such as C IV $\lambda 1550$, O III] $\lambda 1666$, and C III] $\lambda 1909$—to distinguish between AGN photoionization, shock heating, and extreme stellar populations.

### Photoionization Models and the Ionizing Continuum
Photoionization codes, such as Cloudy, demonstrate that the ratios of C IV or O III] to He II are exquisitely sensitive to the shape of the incident ionizing continuum. The production of C IV and O III] requires photons with energies of 2.5-3.5 Rydbergs, whereas [[he-ii-emission|He II emission]] strictly requires photons above 4 Rydbergs. For instance, observations of high-redshift lensed galaxies revealing strong C IV and [[Oxygen Spectral Lines|O III] λ1666] but weak or absent He II $\lambda 1640$ indicate a hard ionizing spectrum with a steep drop-off at 4 Rydbergs. This spectral shape is characteristic of metal-poor starbursts, as AGN typically maintain a harder spectrum beyond 4 Ryd, which would yield stronger He II emission [Mainali et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...836L..14M/abstract). Furthermore, models have shown that the combination of C III] $\lambda 1909$ and nebular He II $\lambda 1640$ can effectively separate typical star-forming regions from shock-ionized gas [Jaskot et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..136J/abstract). In the JWST era, advanced methods for identifying AGN in the early universe highlight the He II $\lambda 1640$ equivalent width and line ratios like (C III] + C IV)/He II as superior diagnostics [Arevalo-Gonzalez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.544.2737A/abstract). Beyond standard AGN and starbursts, photoionization modeling of He II extends to transient host environments. For example, detailed modeling of optical and infrared line ratios, including the He II/H$\beta$ ratio, has been utilized to calculate metallicities and constrain the extreme physical conditions within long gamma-ray burst host galaxies at $z < 0.5$ [Contini et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.2787C/abstract).

### Dust Reddening and Recombination Ratios
Because He II $\lambda 1640$ and [[he-ii-4686-line|He II λ4686]] are both recombination lines of the same ion, their intrinsic flux ratio is largely fixed by atomic physics. Under typical Wolf-Rayet stellar wind conditions, this ratio is:
$$ \frac{F(\lambda 1640)}{F(\lambda 4686)} \approx 7.76 $$
This theoretical ratio allows the He II lines to serve as a robust probe for stellar dust reddening, acting as a UV-to-optical analog to the decrement observed in [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]]. Because the ratio depends only on atomic physics, it measures reddening independently of the initial mass function (IMF) or star formation history [Conti et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990AJ.....99..898C/abstract), [Leitherer et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AJ....158..192L/abstract).

### Population III Diagnostics
In theoretical modeling of the earliest galaxies, He II $\lambda 1640$ is a primary diagnostic for metal-free Population III stars. Due to their exceptionally hard ionizing spectra, Population III stellar populations are predicted to drive the He II/H$\alpha$ intensity ratio above 0.1, particularly if the IMF is top-heavy and populated by stars of several hundred solar masses [Parsons et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933..141P/abstract). However, relying solely on He II $\lambda 1640$ is complicated by the fact that metal-enriched systems hosting [[wolf-rayet-signatures|Wolf-Rayet signatures]] or high-mass X-ray binaries can mimic these properties, necessitating the simultaneous modeling of metal lines to securely identify primordial gas [Katz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524..351K/abstract).

### Tensions in Equivalent Widths
Despite the utility of these UV diagnostics, reproducing the observed He II $\lambda 1640$ equivalent widths in low-metallicity galaxies remains an active area of tension in the literature. Standard stellar population models struggle to produce enough ionizing photons to match the high equivalent widths ($0.2-10$ Å) observed in many high-redshift star-forming galaxies. While incorporating binary stellar evolution and very massive stars into models increases the hard photon budget, current photoionization models still frequently underpredict the He II $\lambda 1640$ strength. This discrepancy explicitly highlights that our understanding of the dominant ionizing sources or the gas geometry in these environments remains incomplete and occasionally conflicting [Nanayakkara et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...624A..89N/abstract). These advanced capabilities have also unveiled sources like GNHeII J1236+6215 at $z=2.9803$, where strong He II emission is attributed to young Population III stars or metal-poor very massive stars, potentially linked to Lyman continuum leakage [Mondal et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988..171M/abstract).

## Historical Development of UV Spectroscopy

The observational study of the He II $\lambda 1640$ line has evolved in tandem with advancements in space-based ultraviolet observatories and large ground-based optical/infrared facilities. Because this transition lies in the far-ultraviolet, early investigations were restricted to low-redshift targets accessible only from space.

The IUE satellite established the foundation for He II $\lambda 1640$ studies by providing the first systematic measurements of [[high-ionization-emission-lines|High-ionization emission lines]] in local active galactic nuclei. Early IUE observations of LMC stars, such as HD 269546, showed He II $\lambda 1640$ as one of the few prominent UV emission features [Grewing et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980ESASP.157..357G/abstract). IUE tracking of objects like Fairall 9 demonstrated differential continuum and emission-line variability [Zheng et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...392...74Z/abstract), while comparisons of He II $\lambda 1640$ to the optical $\lambda 4686$ line allowed astronomers to constrain dust reddening in [[narrow-line-regions-and-seyferts|Narrow-Line Regions and Seyferts]] [MacAlpine et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985ApJ...294..147M/abstract). During this era, libraries of high-dispersion IUE spectra of hot stars also yielded the first synthetic templates used to interpret He II profiles in starburst galaxies [Robert et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...418..749R/abstract). Subsequent missions, such as the Hopkins Ultraviolet Telescope (HUT), continued to refine the ultraviolet characterization of active and star-forming galaxies. The Far Ultraviolet Spectroscopic Explorer (FUSE) further advanced these efforts, providing the spectral resolution needed to detect weak He II emission alongside complex absorption lines in systems like the narrow-line Seyfert 1 galaxy NGC 4051 [Kaspi et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127.2631K/abstract).

For extragalactic targets, the early detection of He II $\lambda 1640$ in the distant radio galaxy 3C 256 represented one of the first times this line was observed in a high-redshift galaxy spectrum [Spinrad et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApJ...285L..49S/abstract). Both 3C 239 and 3C 256 were identified early on as distant radio galaxies showing prominent UV emission lines, including He II $\lambda 1640$ alongside C IV and Ly$\alpha$ [Spinrad et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985ApJ...299L...7S/abstract). A later statistical survey of 165 high-redshift radio galaxies firmly established that the He II $\lambda 1640$ line luminosity is strongly correlated with radio power and other UV emission line luminosities [De Breuck et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000A&A...362..519D/abstract). Pushing to even earlier epochs, observations of the $z=5.19$ radio galaxy TN J0924-2201 utilized an upper limit on He II emission, alongside the detection of C IV, to place early constraints on the carbon-to-oxygen ratio in one of the most distant radio galaxies then known [Matsuoka et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...532L..10M/abstract).

The deployment of the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) drastically improved the sensitivity and spectral resolution available to probe [[line-emitting-gas|Line-emitting gas]]. Long-term monitoring campaigns using the HST Faint Object Spectrograph (FOS) provided detailed reverberation constraints, showing that broad He II $\lambda 1640$ emission in Seyfert galaxies could vary by factors of $\sim 2$ on timescales of months [Goad et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...524..707G/abstract). More recently, the Cosmic Origins Spectrograph (COS) has served as a critical tool for establishing local benchmarks. Deep COS spectroscopy of nearby, young, low-metallicity starbursts has captured prominent [[wolf-rayet-signatures|Wolf-Rayet]] stellar wind profiles that challenge standard single-star population synthesis models [Senchyna et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.6112S/abstract). For high-redshift targets, the HST Wide Field Camera 3 (WFC3) enabled pioneering narrowband searches, placing strict limits on the He II flux of $z \approx 7$ sources [Cai et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...736L..28C/abstract).

Because the He II $\lambda 1640$ line redshifts into the optical window at $z \gtrsim 2$, modern ground-based integral field spectrographs have transformed its use as a cosmological probe. The VLT/MUSE instrument has routinely detected He II emitters in deep fields, reaching $3\sigma$ flux limits of $3.1 \times 10^{-19}$ erg s$^{-1}$ cm$^{-2}$ [Nanayakkara et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...624A..89N/abstract). These observations often uncover rest-frame equivalent widths between $0.2$ and $10$ \AA\ that cannot be fully reproduced by standard photoionization models, even when accounting for binary stellar evolution [Nanayakkara et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...624A..89N/abstract). Furthermore, MUSE has proven exceptionally adept at mapping spatially extended He II emission. It has mapped $\sim 150$ kpc multi-phase halos surrounding radio galaxies [Falkendal et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...645A.120F/abstract) and resolved kinematically distinct He II regions powered by massive submillimeter galaxies at $z=4.5$ [Jiménez-Andrade et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.2326J/abstract).

In the current era, the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has pushed high-resolution He II spectroscopy deep into the epoch of reionization. Using the NIRSpec instrument, astronomers can simultaneously measure rest-UV lines like He II $\lambda 1640$ alongside optical [[balmer-lines|Balmer lines]] for galaxies at $z > 4$ [Tang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991..217T/abstract). JWST has successfully detected prominent [[he-ii-emission|He II emission]] in a highly ionized galaxy at $z = 12.3$ [Castellano et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251208490C/abstract), and found tentative evidence for the line in a source as distant as $z = 11.28$ [Scholtz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250717809S/abstract). These capabilities are driving the search for pristine Population III stellar populations, which are expected to produce exceptionally hard [[stellar-ionizing-radiation|Stellar ionizing radiation]] capable of powering strong He II $\lambda 1640$ emission. However, the literature remains explicit about the ambiguities of this approach: metal-enriched systems hosting active black holes, Wolf-Rayet stars, or X-ray binaries can easily mimic these signatures, making the definitive identification of metal-free stars using [[diagnostic-emission-line-ratios|Diagnostic emission line ratios]] an ongoing and debated challenge [Katz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524..351K/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

**The Origin of the "Missing Ionizing Photons"**
One of the most persistent open questions surrounding [[he-ii-emission|He II emission]] is the identification of the dominant ionization mechanism in extremely metal-poor star-forming galaxies. Producing He II recombination lines requires a significant flux of hard photons with energies $E > 54.4$ eV. In local analogs of primordial galaxies, such as I Zw 18, standard [[stellar-ionizing-radiation|Stellar ionizing radiation]] models consistently underpredict the necessary ionizing budget [Kehrig et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908L..54K/abstract). While high-mass X-ray binaries (HMXBs) have been proposed as the source of this hard radiation, detailed spatial and spectral analyses reveal that X-ray sources are often spatially offset from the He II emission peaks and are insufficiently luminous to account for the total recombination flux [Kehrig et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908L..54K/abstract). Consequently, there remains a substantial deficit of "missing ionizing photons" in these systems.

Similarly, at higher redshifts ($z \sim 2-4$), state-of-the-art photoionization models that include binary evolution and very massive stars (VMS) still struggle to reproduce the high equivalent widths ($0.2-10$ \AA) of He II $\lambda 1640$ observed in star-forming galaxies [Nanayakkara et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...624A..89N/abstract). While an overabundance of massive stars resulting from binary mergers or VMS formation may alleviate some of this tension [Senchyna et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.6112S/abstract), it remains unclear whether stellar winds, radiative shocks, or low-luminosity active galactic nuclei (AGN) provide the bulk of the missing high-energy photons.

**Disentangling Population III Signatures**
The He II $\lambda 1640$ line is theoretically established as a premier diagnostic for identifying metal-free Population III (Pop III) stars. Theoretical models suggest that a high He II/H$\alpha$ ratio serves as a strong indicator of a top-heavy Pop III initial mass function (IMF) [Parsons et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933..141P/abstract). However, confirming a pure Pop III population remains observationally and theoretically challenging. Metal-enriched systems exhibiting [[wolf-rayet-signatures|Wolf-Rayet signatures]], X-ray binaries, or direct collapse black holes (DCBHs) can mimic the hard ionizing spectrum expected from primordial stars [Smith et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.3143S/abstract). Furthermore, cosmological simulations caution that the mere absence of metal emission lines is an insufficient diagnostic for Pop III, as sensitivity limits may obscure weak metal lines in enriched but low-metallicity galaxies [Katz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524..351K/abstract). Determining robust [[diagnostic-emission-line-ratios|Diagnostic emission line ratios]] to unambiguously separate Pop III starbursts from DCBHs or extreme Pop II populations remains an active area of research.

**Intensity Mapping and Future Surveys**
Because individual Pop III host galaxies are expected to be extremely faint, future observational strategies will increasingly rely on intensity mapping. By measuring large-scale spatial fluctuations in the cumulative line emission of unresolved galaxies, intensity mapping bypasses the need to detect individual faint sources. Theoretical models demonstrate that the mean He II/H$\alpha$ ratio derived from intensity mapping can effectively constrain the Pop III IMF; a ratio $\gtrsim 0.1$ would strongly favor a top-heavy IMF dominated by stars of several hundred solar masses [Parsons et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933..141P/abstract). Cross-correlating the He II $\lambda 1640$ signal with 21 cm emission from the intergalactic medium or molecular CO lines could further isolate the high-redshift signal from foreground interlopers [Visbal et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.2506V/abstract).

For direct detection, next-generation facilities are expected to push the boundaries of [[emission-line-spectroscopy|Emission Line Spectroscopy]]. Spectrographs on 30-meter class telescopes, such as ELT-HARMONI, are predicted to be capable of detecting He II emission from Pop III stars out to $z \sim 10$, provided these stars form with a top-heavy IMF [Grisdale et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.5517G/abstract). In the absence of a top-heavy IMF, detecting the line will require exceptionally massive Pop III stellar clusters ($\gtrsim 10^7 M_{\odot}$) or extremely deep integrations [Grisdale et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.5517G/abstract). Together with JWST, these future surveys hold the potential to definitively resolve the sources of extreme excitation in the early Universe.
