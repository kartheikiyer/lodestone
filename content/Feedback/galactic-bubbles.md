**Galactic bubbles** are expansive, low-density cavities of hot, ionized gas, relativistic particles, and magnetic fields driven into the interstellar medium and [[extra-planar-and-circumgalactic-gas|Extra-planar and Circumgalactic Gas]] by highly energetic, localized feedback. These structures span a vast range of physical scales and are primarily classified by their driving mechanisms. On parsec to kiloparsec scales, the collective winds and subsequent supernovae of massive OB stellar associations inflate [[interstellar-bubbles|Interstellar Bubbles]] and larger [[superbubbles|Superbubbles]] that regulate the multiphase structure of the galactic disk [McCray et al. (1977)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977ASSL...70...35M/abstract). On macroscopic scales extending up to hundreds of kiloparsecs, powerful relativistic jets and winds launched by [[central-engines|Central engines]] and active galactic nuclei ([[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|AGN]]) excavate giant cavities within the dense environments of galaxy groups and clusters [Gull et al. (1973)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1973Natur.244...80G/abstract). Regardless of their specific origin, these bubbles act as fundamental agents of astrophysical feedback, serving as primary conduits for the transport of mechanical energy, heavy elements, and cosmic rays from galactic cores into the broader halo.

The dynamical lifecycle of a galactic bubble typically begins with a phase of supersonic expansion, driving shock waves into the ambient medium. As the expansion decelerates to the local sound speed, the structures frequently transition into buoyantly rising plumes. These rising cavities uplift cool, metal-rich gas [Churazov et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...554..261C/abstract) while remaining largely intact against hydrodynamic instabilities due to the stabilizing effects of draped magnetic fields and plasma viscosity [Guo et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...756..182G/abstract), [Ehlert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.2878E/abstract). This mechanical displacement and subsequent energy dissipation are crucial for "maintenance mode" feedback, effectively quenching runaway cooling flows in the [[hot-astrophysical-plasma|Hot Astrophysical Plasma]] of cluster environments [Fabian et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..455F/abstract). Observationally, galactic and cluster-scale bubbles are profoundly multiphase. They are typically identified as surface brightness depressions or rims in thermal X-rays, luminous non-thermal synchrotron lobes at radio frequencies (which eventually cool into radio-quiet [[ghost-cavities|Ghost cavities]]), and can be directly probed via the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect to measure their internal pressure dynamics. 

A paramount local laboratory for studying these phenomena is the Milky Way's own Fermi and eROSITA bubbles—giant, highly symmetric $\gamma$-ray and X-ray lobes extending more than 10 kiloparsecs above and below the Galactic plane [Su et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724.1044S/abstract), [Predehl et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Natur.588..227P/abstract). Despite extensive multiwavelength mapping, the exact nature of these structures and larger extragalactic cavities remains the subject of significant debate. For instance, researchers remain divided on whether the Milky Way's bubbles were inflated by a recent, episodic AGN jet outburst or by a sustained nuclear starburst over gigayear timescales, as well as whether their $\gamma$-ray emission is primarily leptonic or hadronic in origin. Furthermore, modern observations increasingly challenge classical kinematic models of bubble expansion. Discrepancies such as the detection of "isothermal shocks" in the [[perseus-cluster|Perseus Cluster]] that lack expected textbook temperature jumps, the ambiguous ratio of thermal to non-thermal pressure support within AGN cavities, and the complex disruptions of [[galaxy-outflows|Galaxy outflows]] by dense disk gas continue to drive the development of next-generation 3D magnetohydrodynamic simulations and panchromatic surveys.

## Physical Nature and Classification

Galactic bubbles are ubiquitous structural features manifesting as expanding, overpressured, **low-density cavities** within the interstellar medium and the [[extra-planar-and-circumgalactic-gas|Extra-planar and Circumgalactic Gas]]. They are primarily classified by the physical mechanisms driving their expansion, spanning scales from parsecs to hundreds of kiloparsecs.

On smaller, galactic-disk scales, the collective winds and supernovae from OB associations inflate [[superbubbles|Superbubbles]] filled with **[[hot-gas|hot ionized gas]]** [Townsley et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..213....1T/abstract). Numerical models of the multiphase interstellar medium suggest that this hot ($T \ge 3 \times 10^5$ K) gas can achieve a midplane **filling factor** of approximately 50% [Rosen et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...470..839R/abstract). Conversely, on macroscopic cluster scales, powerful jets originating from [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] inflate giant cavities within the [[hot-astrophysical-plasma|Hot Astrophysical Plasma]] of galaxy clusters. These AGN-driven bubbles play a critical role in "maintenance mode" feedback by providing mechanical heating that offsets radiative losses and prevents catastrophic cooling flows [Sijacki et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.366..397S/abstract), [Fabian et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..455F/abstract).

The classical kinematic theory models the initial supersonic bubble expansion into a uniform medium of density $\rho$ with an expansion velocity given by:
$$v \sim \left(\frac{L}{\rho}\right)^{1/3} R^{-2/3}$$
where $L$ is the mechanical luminosity of the central source and $R$ is the bubble radius. During this phase, feedback energy is transferred to the surrounding gas via shocks. Once the expansion velocity drops to the local sound speed, the structures typically transition into buoyantly rising cavities that can uplift cool gas from galactic cores into the broader halo [Churazov et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000A&A...356..788C/abstract). However, idealized kinematic models do not universally apply; recent hydrodynamic simulations of relativistic jets demonstrate that complex interactions can sometimes result in large, confined cocoons rather than standard buoyant bubbles [Perucho et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...743...42P/abstract). Conversely, detailed modeling of nearby systems like the Circinus galaxy provides a prime example of young, kpc-scale elliptical bubbles being actively driven by AGN jets [Xie et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260104317X/abstract). Additionally, red giant wind bubbles entering blazar jets in elliptical galaxies can lead to detectable transient or persistent gamma-ray emission, highlighting the complexity of such multi-scale interactions [Torres-Albà et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...623A..91T/abstract).

The internal composition and primary pressure support of AGN-inflated bubbles remain a subject of active tension in the literature. While some models favor support by extremely diffuse, high-temperature thermal plasma, others propose that the cavities are dominated by non-thermal relativistic particles (**cosmic rays**) and draped magnetic fields [Pfrommer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...430..799P/abstract), [Ehlert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.2878E/abstract). Observations of the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect provide a direct probe of this internal pressure. SZ measurements of the massive cavities in the cluster MS 0735.6+7421 reveal a distinct signal deficit, implying that the bubbles are either supported non-thermally or by a highly diffuse thermal plasma with temperatures exceeding hundreds of keV [Abdulla et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...871..195A/abstract). While actively jet-driven bubbles are often radio-bright, older, detached cavities whose relativistic electron populations have cooled frequently fade into radio-quiet [[ghost-cavities|Ghost cavities]] [Randall et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...700.1404R/abstract), [Machacek et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...743...15M/abstract). Interestingly, some mysterious structures like Odd Radio Circles (ORCs) have recently been proposed as end-on views of oblate, jet-inflated bubbles situated in low-mass halos [Lin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..269L/abstract).

A paramount example of large-scale bubble phenomena resides in the Milky Way, where the Fermi ($\gamma$-ray) and eROSITA (X-ray) bubbles extend 10-15 kpc above and below the Galactic Center [Su et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724.1044S/abstract), [Predehl et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Natur.588..227P/abstract). These structures exhibit strong magnetic fields ($\sim 15\ \mu$G) and contain a vast reservoir of cosmic rays [Crocker et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106j1102C/abstract), [Carretti et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.493...66C/abstract). The physical origin of these Milky Way bubbles remains highly contested between recent episodic AGN jet outbursts and sustained starburst-driven winds (see the Case Study section below).

## Driving Mechanisms: Stellar Feedback and AGN Activity

Galactic bubbles are large-scale structures primarily inflated by two distinct sources of mechanical energy: the collective kinetic output of stellar populations (stellar feedback) and the relativistic jets or winds launched by accreting supermassive black holes ([[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]]).

**Stellar Feedback**
On parsec to kiloparsec scales, bubbles are driven by the intense mechanical feedback of localized OB associations. The combined effect of massive stellar winds and subsequent supernova remnants drives expanding shells of swept-up interstellar material, carving out cavities known as [[superbubbles|Superbubbles]] [Oey et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996PASP..108..547O/abstract). In the Milky Way, the distribution of giant superbubbles is primarily driven by massive-star clusters located within the spiral arms [Higdon et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...775..110H/abstract). As defined by classical kinematic scaling ($v \propto L^{1/3}\rho^{-1/3}R^{-2/3}$), once the supersonic expansion velocity drops to the local sound speed, the bounding shock weakens and the bubble expands subsonically, transferring energy into the surrounding medium primarily via $P {\rm d}V$ work. In the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]], intense centralized star formation can launch massive starburst winds that vent hot gas, cosmic rays, and metals into the halo via galactic chimneys [Lacki et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444L..39L/abstract), [Ponti et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.567..347P/abstract).

**AGN Feedback**
On larger scales, such as within galaxy groups and clusters, buoyant cavities are predominantly inflated by AGN jets. This AGN feedback provides critical "maintenance mode" heating that offsets catastrophic radiative cooling in the intracluster medium [Sijacki et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.366..397S/abstract), [Fabian et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..455F/abstract). Jets with mechanical powers often exceeding $10^{44}\text{ erg s}^{-1}$ displace the [[hot-astrophysical-plasma|Hot Astrophysical Plasma]] to form cavities filled with relativistic plasma [Churazov et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000A&A...356..788C/abstract), [Liu et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3376L/abstract). The morphology of these structures is intimately tied to the dynamics of the central engine; for instance, precessing AGN jets can explain the presence of multiple, misaligned pairs of X-ray cavities observed in galaxy clusters such as the [[perseus-cluster|Perseus Cluster]] [Falceta-Gonçalves et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...713L..74F/abstract). Furthermore, observations of red quasars have revealed spectacular 20-kpc "superbubbles", which represent a violent, short-lived "break-out" phase of quasar winds expanding into the galactic halo [Shen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023SciA....9G8287S/abstract). However, idealized analytical scaling relations often fail to capture the complexity of these environments. For instance, recent three-dimensional simulations demonstrate that relativistic jets interacting with a dense, [[gas-clumping|clumpy]] galactic disk may be disrupted into "failed jets," completely altering their expected expansion dynamics as the resulting plasma buoyantly rises to form large symmetric lobes [Tseng et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..146T/abstract).

**The Milky Way Dichotomy**
The tension between these two driving mechanisms is perfectly exemplified by the Milky Way's Fermi and eROSITA bubbles. Their exact physical origins remain actively debated between models invoking a brief, powerful AGN jet outburst and those driven by sustained nuclear starburst winds (see *Case Study* below for a detailed discussion).

## Observational Signatures Across the Electromagnetic Spectrum

Galactic and cluster-scale bubbles are inherently multiphase structures. Their detection and physical characterization require a panchromatic approach, as the thermal gas, relativistic particles, and magnetic fields emit via distinct mechanisms across the electromagnetic spectrum. 

**X-ray Cavities and Shocks**
In the intracluster and circumgalactic medium, bubbles are primarily identified as distinct depressions in the surface brightness of the [[hot-astrophysical-plasma|Hot Astrophysical Plasma]]. These "X-ray cavities" form when expanding jets or outflows displace the ambient thermal gas [Churazov et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000A&A...356..788C/abstract). The cavities are frequently bounded by bright X-ray rims composed of cooler, low-entropy gas that has been buoyantly uplifted from the galactic core, alongside alterations in local metallicity [Su et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...847...94S/abstract), [Gendron-Marsolais et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848...26G/abstract). While simple fluid models predict shock-heated gas at the expanding bubble edges, observations of the [[perseus-cluster|Perseus Cluster]] and similar systems sometimes reveal "isothermal shocks," where highly efficient thermal conduction or mediating cosmic rays suppress the expected temperature jumps [Fabian et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.366..417F/abstract). On galactic scales, the Milky Way's eROSITA bubbles manifest as X-ray shells tracing denser circumgalactic gas containing both virial ($T \sim 0.3$ keV) and [[super-virial-temperature-gas|Super-virial temperature gas]] components. However, the lack of a strong temperature jump across the boundary challenges classical adiabatic shock-heating models [Predehl et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Natur.588..227P/abstract), [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7..799G/abstract).

**Radio Synchrotron Emission**
The interiors of newly inflated bubbles are typically filled with relativistic leptons and magnetic fields, producing luminous non-thermal radio synchrotron emission. These structures initially manifest as extended lobes aligned with the outflow axis. Over time, as the central engine enters a quiescent phase and the bubble buoyantly rises, the high-energy electron population loses energy via synchrotron and inverse-Compton cooling. This cooling steepens the radio spectrum, resulting in [[ghost-cavities|Ghost cavities]] that are detectable primarily at low radio frequencies (e.g., $<200$ MHz) [Brienza et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatAs...5.1261B/abstract). In the Milky Way, giant, linearly polarized radio lobes coincide spatially with the high-energy bubbles, exhibiting ordered magnetic field strengths of up to $\sim 15\ \mu\mathrm{G}$ [Carretti et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.493...66C/abstract). Furthermore, recent polarization surveys have identified kpc-scale magnetized structures in the Galactic halo that spatially coincide with the eROSITA bubbles, confirming the magnetized nature of these outflows [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1416Z/abstract). Additionally, magnetized galactic outflows contribute significantly to the residual Faraday rotation measure observed in extragalactic radio sources, particularly at high redshifts [Arámburo-García et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4030A/abstract).

**Gamma-Ray Lobes**
At GeV to TeV energies, structures such as the Milky Way's Fermi bubbles exhibit a hard gamma-ray spectrum, roughly following $dN/dE \propto E^{-2}$, with a remarkably uniform surface brightness [Su et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724.1044S/abstract). The dominant emission mechanism populating this [[galactic-cosmic-ray-halo|Galactic cosmic-ray halo]] remains a subject of active debate in the literature. Leptonic models attribute the gamma rays to the inverse-Compton scattering of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) and interstellar radiation fields by relativistic electrons [Su et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724.1044S/abstract), [Chernyshov et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014NuPhS.256..179C/abstract). Conversely, hadronic models suggest the emission arises from the decay of neutral pions ($\pi^0$) produced in inelastic $p-p$ collisions between cosmic-ray protons and the diffuse halo gas [Crocker et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106j1102C/abstract). A population of millisecond pulsars in the Galactic bulge has also been proposed as a significant source of the GeV gamma-ray excess observed in the inner Galaxy [Calore et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827..143C/abstract). In extragalactic systems, however, a lack of gamma-ray detections currently excludes models involving full mixing between hadronic cosmic rays and the ambient medium, though protons may still serve as a viable pressure support agent in cluster cavities [H. E. S. S. Collaboration et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...545A.103H/abstract).

**The Sunyaev-Zel'dovich Effect**
The thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect provides a direct measure of the integrated electron pressure along the line of sight, $\int P_e dl$. If an X-ray cavity is supported purely by standard thermal plasma, the SZ signal should remain relatively undisturbed. However, millimeter-wave observations of highly energetic outbursts (e.g., in MS 0735.6+7421) reveal a distinct deficit in the SZ signal coincident with the X-ray cavities [Abdulla et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...871..195A/abstract). This SZ decrement confirms that the cavities are largely devoid of standard thermal electrons, implying that they are supported predominantly by non-thermal relativistic particles (cosmic rays and magnetic fields) or by an ultra-diffuse, extremely hot ($T > 100$ keV) thermal plasma [Abdulla et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...871..195A/abstract). Additionally, the kinetic SZ effect has the potential to constrain internal velocities and jet inclinations due to the entrainment and mixing of the ambient medium with low-density jet material [Ehlert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872L...8E/abstract).

## Dynamics and Evolutionary Phases

The lifecycle of a galactic bubble begins with the rapid injection of mechanical energy from [[central-engines|Central engines]] or massive stellar winds. In the standard analytical framework, the bubble undergoes an initial period of supersonic expansion into the ambient medium. The expansion velocity $v$ scales according to the relation $v \sim (L/\rho)^{1/3} R^{-2/3}$, where $L$ is the mechanical luminosity of the central source, $\rho$ is the ambient gas density, and $R$ is the bubble radius. During this early phase, feedback energy is transferred to the surrounding gas via shock fronts as increasing amounts of material are swept up. Observationally, integral field spectroscopy allows for the precise measurement of superbubble expansion velocities and kinematic ages in nearby galaxies like IC 1613 [Smirnov-Pinchukov et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AstBu..76..367S/abstract). In the Milky Way, observations and simulations provide precise numerical age estimates for specific stellar-blown bubbles, such as the GLIMPSE bubble N107, which is estimated to be highly energetic yet remarkably young at less than 2.25 Myr old [Sidorin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...565A...6S/abstract). Observations of these expanding cavities often reveal weak shocks with a Mach number of $M \sim 1.2 - 1.4$ [Million et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407.2046M/abstract), [Liu et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3376L/abstract). However, the literature presents some tension with standard shock-heating models; for example, deep X-ray observations of the [[perseus-cluster|Perseus Cluster]] reveal "isothermal" shocks that lack the expected temperature jumps. This anomaly suggests that efficient thermal conduction or the presence of relativistic plasma may significantly alter classical shock dynamics [Fabian et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.366..417F/abstract). Further complicating the dynamical picture, high-resolution spectroscopy by the Hitomi satellite revealed a surprisingly quiescent intracluster atmosphere in the Perseus core, characterized by a low velocity dispersion despite the active and continuous inflation of large-scale bubbles [Hitomi Collaboration et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.535..117H/abstract). Furthermore, complex central environments can disrupt the standard expansion scaling. Dense, clumpy gas can fragment collimated outflows into "failed jets" that subsequently inflate hot bubbles, whose overall geometry is later aligned by buoyancy rather than their initial ejection vectors [Tseng et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..146T/abstract).

The supersonic phase concludes when the expansion velocity decelerates to the local sound speed of the medium. For a typical bubble expanding into a [[hot-astrophysical-plasma|Hot Astrophysical Plasma]] with a temperature of $T = 1$ keV and a density of $n = 1$ cm$^{-3}$, driven by a characteristic outburst of $L = 10^{45}$ erg s$^{-1}$, the sonic radius is reached at approximately $50$ kpc. Once the expansion becomes subsonic, the bubble continues to grow through adiabatic expansion, and the mechanism of energy transfer to the environment shifts primarily to $P \text{d}V$ work. In the Perseus Cluster, statistical analysis of fluctuations shows that $\sim 80\%$ of gas perturbations on 8-70 kpc scales are isobaric, suggesting energy is primarily injected into bubble enthalpy rather than through strong shocks [Zhuravleva et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.458.2902Z/abstract).

In a stratified gravitational potential, the lower density of the bubble interior relative to the ambient medium causes the structure to undergo a subsonic buoyant rise. Rising bubbles typically reach terminal velocities roughly half the local sound speed [Churazov et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...554..261C/abstract). Without stabilizing forces, simple hydrodynamic models predict that buoyancy will deform initially spherical bubbles into toroidal "mushroom cloud" structures due to the onset of Rayleigh-Taylor and Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities [Churazov et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...554..261C/abstract), [Brienza et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatAs...5.1261B/abstract). 

Despite these hydrodynamic predictions, observations indicate that many bubbles—including older, radio-quiet [[ghost-cavities|Ghost cavities]]—remain remarkably intact over long timescales. This unexpected longevity is widely attributed to the stabilizing effects of shear viscosity and the "draping" of ambient magnetic fields over the bubble's leading edge, which successfully suppress surface instabilities [Guo et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...756..182G/abstract), [Ehlert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.2878E/abstract). As the intact bubbles rise, buoyancy generates well-developed eddies and wakes in their path. This fluid motion acts as an elevator, physically uplifting low-entropy gas from the galactic core out into the [[extra-planar-and-circumgalactic-gas|Extra-planar and Circumgalactic Gas]] [Gendron-Marsolais et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848...26G/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517..616Z/abstract). This uplifted gas is prone to becoming thermally unstable and condensing into cold clouds if its local cooling time is shorter than the time required for the gas to fall back to its equilibrium position in the cluster potential, thereby stimulating further AGN feedback [McNamara et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...79M/abstract). Eventually, these stabilizing mechanisms break down and the bubble undergoes dissipation. A turbulent cascade ensues, mixing the bubble's relativistic and thermal contents into the surrounding medium, distributing heat, and ultimately preventing catastrophic cooling in the host system [Scannapieco et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...686..927S/abstract). Additionally, cluster merger-driven sloshing can transport bubble-resident cosmic rays to large radii, stretching them into filamentary shapes that resemble [[giant-radio-relics|radio relics]] [ZuHone et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...914...73Z/abstract). However, the formation and exact appearance of these filamentary structures in sloshing models are highly sensitive to the underlying cosmic ray physics, specifically the rates of diffusion and Alfvén wave energy losses [ZuHone et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Galax...9...91Z/abstract).

## Plasma Microphysics and Transport Processes

The survival and macroscopic evolution of buoyant galactic and cluster bubbles depend critically on the plasma microphysics governing their boundaries. Without stabilizing mechanisms, bubbles rising through a stratified medium are expected to be rapidly shredded by fluid instabilities, such as the Kelvin-Helmholtz (KH) and Rayleigh-Taylor (RT) instabilities. The integrity of these structures is primarily maintained by the complex interplay of magnetic fields and plasma viscosity.

As a bubble buoyantly ascends through the ambient [[hot-astrophysical-plasma|Hot Astrophysical Plasma]], it sweeps up the surrounding magnetic field. This process, known as magnetic draping, forms a protective magnetic layer at the bubble's leading interface, amplifying the field to strengths sufficient to balance the ram pressure of the external medium [Ehlert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.2878E/abstract). Observationally, this draping effect has been directly measured in systems like NGC 628, where magnetic fields are seen visibly curving around the shells of kpc-scale expanding bubbles, following their expansion [Zhao et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967...18Z/abstract). Together with internal toroidal or helical magnetic fields [Dong et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704.1309D/abstract), [Ehlert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.2878E/abstract), magnetic draping effectively confines the relativistic plasma and suppresses the growth of disruptive instabilities. The internal magnetic topology is strongly dependent on the helicity injected by the central engine; outflows with high magnetic helicity can relax into global equilibria via reconnection, converting magnetic energy into heat while ensuring the bubble remains intact over large distances .

Viscous forces provide a secondary, yet vital, stabilizing mechanism. Theoretical models demonstrate that even a relatively low level of viscosity—approximately $0.1\%$ to $1\%$ of the classical Braginskii viscosity, or $\mu_{\rm visc} \gtrsim 3$ g cm$^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$—is sufficient to damp KH instabilities at the bubble surface [Guo et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...756..182G/abstract). Because the intracluster and circumgalactic media are weakly collisional and magnetized, momentum transport is highly directional. Simulations utilizing anisotropic viscosity show that viscous transport along magnetic field lines further suppresses instabilities parallel to the field [Dong et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704.1309D/abstract). However, recent measurements of turbulent kinematics in cold filaments indicate that isotropic viscosity is substantially suppressed in these environments, allowing turbulence to cascade down to scales comparable to the mean free path [Li et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889L...1L/abstract).

The microphysics of the bubble boundary also regulates cosmic ray (CR) diffusion. The draped magnetic fields severely inhibit particle transport perpendicular to the bubble surface, resulting in highly anisotropic CR diffusion [Guo et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...756..182G/abstract), [Yang et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761..185Y/abstract). This suppression prevents the internal CR population from mixing extensively with the external thermal gas, thereby maintaining sharp structural edges. This anisotropic diffusion naturally yields an edge-brightened (or flat-projected) CR distribution, which successfully reproduces the uniform gamma-ray surface brightness and sharp boundaries observed in the Milky Way's Fermi bubbles [Guo et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...756..182G/abstract), [Yang et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761..185Y/abstract).

Finally, the transfer of mechanical energy from expanding bubbles into the surrounding thermal bath is mediated by both viscous and conductive dissipation [Brüggen et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...630..740B/abstract). Thermal conduction is frequently invoked as a mechanism to distribute heat and offset radiative cooling in cluster cores. However, its true efficiency remains ambiguous, as models often require finely tuned suppression factors relative to the classical Spitzer conductivity to match observed heating rates [Brüggen et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...630..740B/abstract), [Pope et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.367.1121P/abstract). The standard shock-heating paradigm is further challenged by observations of the [[perseus-cluster|Perseus Cluster]], where bubble-driven shocks appear "isothermal" and lack the expected temperature jumps [Fabian et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.366..417F/abstract). This anomaly suggests either that thermal conduction operates with extreme efficiency across the shock front, or that the shock dynamics are fundamentally mediated by a co-existing non-thermal relativistic plasma [Fabian et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.366..417F/abstract).

## Environmental Impact: Feedback and Enrichment

Galactic bubbles are central to the thermodynamic and chemical regulation of galaxies and their surrounding environments. Operating across diverse physical scales, these structures mediate "maintenance mode" feedback, effectively coupling the mechanical energy of central engines and massive stellar populations to the broader interstellar and intracluster media.

**Quenching Cooling Flows**
In the dense cores of galaxy clusters, radiative cooling timescales are typically much shorter than the Hubble time, which theoretically should result in massive, runaway cooling flows. However, this catastrophic cooling is averted by periodic outbursts from active galactic nuclei ([[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]]). The AGN jets inflate cavities of relativistic plasma that expand supersonically before transitioning to a subsonic, buoyant rise. As the expansion becomes subsonic, the bubbles transfer energy to the surrounding [[hot-astrophysical-plasma|Hot Astrophysical Plasma]] via $P\text{d}V$ work and the dissipation of weak shocks and sound waves. There is a robust observational and theoretical consensus that the mechanical power of these cavities—often exceeding $10^{44} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$ in massive clusters—is sufficient to offset radiative cooling and quench star formation in central dominant galaxies [Fabian et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..455F/abstract), [Panagoulia et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.1236P/abstract).

**Gas Uplift and Metallicity Enhancement**
As AGN-inflated bubbles rise buoyantly through the gravitational potential well, they act as mechanical elevators. Observations of cluster cores, such as those in M87 and NGC 4472, demonstrate that rising bubbles physically transport cool, low-entropy gas from the galactic center outward into the hotter outer halo [Million et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407.2046M/abstract), [Gendron-Marsolais et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848...26G/abstract). This gas uplift process frequently entrains dense molecular filaments [Russell et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.4024R/abstract) and redistributes heavy elements synthesized in the galactic core. Consequently, pronounced metallicity structures—manifesting as either enhancements (e.g., M87) or deficits (e.g., NGC 4472), reflecting the native abundance gradient of the core—are routinely observed along bubble wakes and cool rims, fundamentally altering the chemical makeup of the host environment [Million et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407.2046M/abstract), [Gendron-Marsolais et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848...26G/abstract).

**Triggered Star Formation**
On galactic scales, [[superbubbles|Superbubbles]] driven by the collective stellar winds and supernovae of OB associations dictate the structure of the interstellar medium. Observations of $^{26}$Al emission suggest that the Galactic distribution of this isotope is closely tied to superbubbles generated by massive stars along spiral arms [Fujimoto et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497.2442F/abstract). The supersonic expansion of these bubbles sweeps up surrounding molecular clouds into dense shells. When these shells accumulate sufficient mass, they can become gravitationally unstable. This "collect-and-collapse" process frequently leads to [[bubble-induced-star-formation|triggered star formation]] at the bubble peripheries, occasionally precipitating sequential generations of massive stars [Yan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..117Y/abstract). Recent quantitative studies of these processes, such as the PHANGS-ALMA survey, show that molecular superbubbles accurately trace the overall efficiency of stellar feedback, revealing a supernova energy coupling to the interstellar medium of approximately 10% [Watkins et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...676A..67W/abstract). Recent high-resolution multiwavelength studies of nearby galaxies have vividly highlighted the macroscopic effects of such processes; for example, James Webb Space Telescope observations of NGC 628 revealed the 'Phantom Void,' a massive 1-kpc diameter bubble driven by multiple generations of stellar feedback and supernovae [Barnes et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944L..22B/abstract). Within the hot, metal-rich cores of these superbubbles, the continuous injection of energy and material provides an ideal environment for particle acceleration; indeed, theories suggest that a significant fraction of galactic cosmic rays are primarily accelerated directly from supernova ejecta-enriched matter within these expansive cavities [Higdon et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...509L..33H/abstract). Conversely, in regions subject to intense, fast AGN-driven winds, high ram pressures can ablate clouds and suppress overall star formation, demonstrating a complex duality in bubble feedback depending on the host environment [Dugan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839..103D/abstract).

**Halo Enrichment and Chimneys**
When expanding bubbles reach the scale height of the galactic disk, they can succumb to Rayleigh-Taylor instabilities or undergo "blow-out," creating vertical chimneys. These conduits vent hot, metal-enriched plasma directly into the [[extra-planar-and-circumgalactic-gas|Extra-planar and Circumgalactic Gas]]. In the Milky Way, prominent X-ray chimneys appear to connect the Galactic Center to the base of the massive Fermi and eROSITA bubbles [Ponti et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.567..347P/abstract), [Ponti et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646A..66P/abstract). Whether powered by recent episodic AGN jet activity or a nuclear starburst, these [[galaxy-outflows|Galaxy outflows]] illustrate how bubble expansion efficiently transports mechanical energy, cosmic rays, and metals out of the disk, profoundly altering the circumgalactic medium and regulating the long-term evolution of the host galaxy. On even larger cosmological scales, supernova-driven outflows are pivotal for injecting turbulence and kinetic energy deep into the intergalactic medium (IGM), with simulations indicating that these turbulent regions can reach volume filling factors of $\sim 10\%$ by $z < 2$ [Evoli et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.413.2721E/abstract).

## Case Study: The Milky Way's Fermi and eROSITA Bubbles

The Milky Way hosts two of the most spectacular examples of large-scale [[galaxy-outflows|Galaxy outflows]], collectively known as the Fermi and eROSITA bubbles. Discovered in $\gamma$-rays, the Fermi bubbles are highly symmetric lobes extending $\sim 50^\circ$ (10-14 kpc) above and below the Galactic plane with a hard emission spectrum ($dN/dE \sim E^{-2}$), and are spatially correlated with a hard-spectrum microwave excess known as the WMAP haze [Su et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724.1044S/abstract). Subsequent X-ray observations revealed the even larger eROSITA bubbles, which encompass the Fermi bubbles and extend up to $\sim 80^\circ$ into the Galactic halo [Predehl et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Natur.588..227P/abstract), [Yang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6..584Y/abstract). The Milky Way is not entirely unique in this regard; extragalactic analogs such as M106 (NGC 4258) exhibit strikingly similar large-scale bipolar radio and X-ray-emitting bubble-like structures tracing energetic nuclear outflows [Zeng et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526..483Z/abstract). Similarly, observations of the Andromeda galaxy (M31) have revealed a 6-7.5 kpc gamma-ray halo that closely mirrors the morphology of the Milky Way's Fermi bubbles, strongly suggesting that M31 also experienced significant past nuclear activity [Pshirkov et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459L..76P/abstract). Furthermore, a Chandra stacking analysis of 93 nearby galaxies detects soft diffuse X-ray circumgalactic emission with significant minor-axis anisotropies in high-star-formation-rate systems, highlighting that such outflows are common [Sacchi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250118681S/abstract). Observations of the starburst galaxy NGC 6286 reveal giant, X-shaped bubbles of 0.7 keV gas, consistent with a superwind scenario driven by tidal interactions and star formation [He et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250906470H/abstract).

Connecting the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|central few parsecs]] of the Milky Way to the base of these giant lobes are the Galactic Center chimneys. These prominent X-ray and radio structures extend for hundreds of parsecs perpendicular to the Galactic plane, acting as exhaust channels for the mass and energy injected by episodic or continuous central activity [Ponti et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.567..347P/abstract), [Ponti et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646A..66P/abstract). 

The total energy required to inflate the eROSITA bubbles is estimated to be $\sim 10^{56}$ erg [Predehl et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Natur.588..227P/abstract). The internal plasma is highly magnetized, with field strengths reaching up to $\sim 15\ \mu\mathrm{G}$ [Carretti et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.493...66C/abstract). While standard models often assume such bubbles are purely adiabatic shocks, Suzaku observations of the eROSITA bubble shells suggest they are brighter primarily due to density enhancements rather than strictly elevated temperatures. The shells contain both a virial temperature component ($\sim 0.3$ keV) and a [[super-virial-gas|Super-virial gas]] component that are thermally similar to the ambient medium. The shells also exhibit non-solar Ne/O and Mg/O abundance ratios, which provide strong support for stellar feedback models [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7..799G/abstract). The observed asymmetry of the eROSITA bubbles may be evidence of a $\sim 200$ km/s circumgalactic medium wind blowing across the northern halo [Mou et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatCo..14..781M/abstract).

Despite this, the primary power source of the bubbles remains a subject of active debate in the literature:
*   **AGN Jet/TDE Scenario:** One paradigm attributes the bubbles to a recent (1-6 Myr ago) episodic outburst from the central supermassive black hole, Sagittarius A*, either via [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|active galactic nucleus (AGN) jets]] [Guo et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...756..181G/abstract), [Yang et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761..185Y/abstract), [Yang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6..584Y/abstract) or a tidal disruption event (TDE). Numerical simulations suggest that a short burst of activity from Sgr A* approximately 6 Myr ago could reproduce the current bubble morphology and energetics [Zubovas et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASPC..460..268Z/abstract). Supporting this timeline, faint H-alpha emission along the Magellanic Stream suggests a 'Seyfert flare' from the Galactic Center occurred approximately 1-3 million years ago [Bland-Hawthorn et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...778...58B/abstract). Under the TDE hypothesis, periodic disruption events occurring every 10-100 kyr could have injected sufficient aggregate energy to inflate the Galactic bubbles [Scheffler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A..34S/abstract). Even obliquely oriented or "failed" jets that are disrupted by the Galactic disk can produce highly symmetric bubbles perpendicular to the plane via buoyancy effects [Tseng et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..146T/abstract). Furthermore, recent simulations suggest the eROSITA and Fermi bubbles were created by two distinct AGN jet episodes launched 15 Myr and 5 Myr ago, respectively [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250713665Z/abstract). Intriguingly, spectral features in the cosmic-ray positron fraction near 12 GeV may be counterpart signals from the episodic burst that formed the Fermi bubbles [Cholis et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...950..120C/abstract).
*   **Nuclear Starburst Scenario:** An opposing paradigm argues for a large-scale wind driven by a nuclear starburst, which may have operated persistently over much longer timescales ($\sim 1$ Gyr) [Crocker et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.3512C/abstract), [Carretti et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.493...66C/abstract). A portion of this wind may fall back onto the disk as [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]], dynamically sustaining the bubbles over time [Shimoda et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...973...78S/abstract). Models of winds driven by ring-like starburst geometries at inner Lindblad resonances can also reproduce the large-scale multiphase morphology seen in Galactic bubbles [Nguyen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935L..24N/abstract). Additionally, recent polarization surveys suggest that some of the extensive magnetized structures within the Galactic halo are sustained not solely by the Galactic Center itself, but by continuous outflows driven by star-forming regions located 3-5 kpc away in the inner Galaxy disk [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240806312Z/abstract).

Beyond their origin, the exact emission mechanisms and physical composition of the bubbles are conflicting. It is currently unresolved whether the $\gamma$-ray emission within the Fermi bubbles is predominantly leptonic (driven by inverse Compton scattering of cosmic microwave background photons) or hadronic (driven by proton-proton collisions and subsequent pion decay) [Su et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724.1044S/abstract), [Chernyshov et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014NuPhS.256..179C/abstract). Furthermore, interpretations of specific spatial substructures are disputed. For instance, a bright $\gamma$-ray enhancement within the Fermi bubbles, known as the "cocoon", has been attributed to a collimated Galactic outflow [Su et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724.1044S/abstract). Some authors have alternatively proposed that the cocoon is a projection effect caused by millisecond pulsars in the Sagittarius dwarf spheroidal galaxy [Crocker et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6.1317C/abstract), though subsequent spatial and spectral analyses have found no significant evidence to support this claim [Eckner et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.110l3006E/abstract). The true nature of the cocoon, like the overarching mechanism powering the bubbles, remains ambiguous.

## Historical Development of Theoretical Models

The theoretical framework for galactic bubbles originally relied on 1D analytical blast-wave models, most notably the Weaver model and the self-similar expansion solutions of Ostriker and McKee [Rowe et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412..905R/abstract). In these classical models, a central energy source with luminosity $L$ drives a supersonic shock into a uniform ambient medium of density $\rho$. During this phase, the expansion velocity scales as:
$$v \sim \left(\frac{L}{\rho}\right)^{1/3} R^{-2/3}$$
where $R$ is the bubble radius. Mechanical energy is efficiently transferred to the ambient [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] via shocks. As the bubble sweeps up mass, the expansion eventually slows. When the expansion velocity drops to the ambient sound speed, the supersonic phase ends. For a typical active galactic nucleus injection of $L = 10^{45}$ erg s$^{-1}$ into a $1$ keV plasma with density $n = 1$ cm$^{-3}$, this sonic transition occurs at a radius of $\sim 50$ kpc. Subsequent expansion becomes subsonic, and energy is deposited via $P\,dV$ work as the bubble rises buoyantly in the stratified host potential [Churazov et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000A&A...356..788C/abstract).

While foundational, these 1D analytical treatments cannot capture the complex fluid dynamics of bubbles, particularly the development of Rayleigh-Taylor and Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities that threaten to shred rising cavities. To address this, theoretical efforts advanced to multidimensional numerical techniques using adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) and moving-mesh codes. For example, cosmological frameworks like the TNG50 simulation have demonstrated that such overpressurized circumgalactic bubbles are common, expected features in disc-like galaxies prior to quenching, serving as prominent signposts of supermassive black hole activity [Pillepich et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508.4667P/abstract). High-resolution hydrodynamic simulations further revealed that proper subgrid modeling of turbulence is necessary to follow the turbulent cascade and mixing of the bubble with the intracluster medium [Scannapieco et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...686..927S/abstract). 

Modern theoretical models increasingly rely on 3D magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulations that incorporate both thermal plasma and non-thermal cosmic rays (CRs). Early purely hydrodynamic models struggled to explain the observed longevity of X-ray cavities and radio lobes. By incorporating magnetic fields, MHD simulations demonstrate that magnetic field lines drape around the leading edge of the bubble, amplifying to strengths that balance ram pressure and stabilizing the fluid interface against instabilities [Ehlert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.2878E/abstract). Furthermore, plasma shear viscosity has been shown to effectively suppress Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities at the bubble surface, ensuring the smooth edges observed in nature [Guo et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...756..182G/abstract). Simulated models that self-consistently couple thermal gas, magnetic fields, and anisotropic CR diffusion successfully reproduce the sharp boundaries and flat surface brightness profiles observed in the Fermi bubbles, demonstrating that CR diffusion must be strongly suppressed across the bubble edge [Yang et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761..185Y/abstract). Simulations incorporating a non-thermal particle population of relativistic protons into AGN bubbles show they provide significant pressure support even at low thermal temperatures, regulating cluster cooling flows [Sijacki et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.387.1403S/abstract).

Despite advances in numerical techniques, significant tensions remain between classical analytical theory and contemporary simulations. While the standard shock-heating model predicts bubbles should be hotter than their surroundings, recent X-ray observations of the eROSITA shells indicate they are brighter due to density enhancements rather than supervirial temperatures, challenging purely adiabatic assumptions [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7..799G/abstract). Furthermore, standard buoyancy arguments are complicated by highly relativistic simulations, where powerful jets may not inflate standard buoyant bubbles at all, but instead produce large, shock-confined structures analogous to a [[cocoon-astrophysics|Cocoon (astrophysics)]] [Perucho et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...743...42P/abstract). In other regimes, "failed jets" interacting with dense galactic disk gas can dramatically alter the early expansion dynamics, producing the symmetric morphologies of large-scale [[galaxy-outflows|Galaxy outflows]] through complex shock interactions rather than simple self-similar expansion [Tseng et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..146T/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite extensive multi-wavelength mapping, the driving mechanisms, internal composition, and longevity of large-scale galactic and cluster bubbles remain active areas of debate. Current literature highlights several unresolved tensions that future observational campaigns aim to address.

### Origin and Energetics of Milky Way Bubbles
The primary power source of the Milky Way's Fermi and eROSITA bubbles is still undetermined. Researchers are divided on whether these structures trace a recent ($1-6$ Myr) energetic outburst from the [[central-engines|central supermassive black hole]] [Guo et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...756..181G/abstract), [Predehl et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Natur.588..227P/abstract) or if they represent a steady-state wind driven by intense star formation in the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]] over timescales exceeding a gigayear [Carretti et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.493...66C/abstract), [Shimoda et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...973...78S/abstract). 

Furthermore, the exact particle acceleration mechanisms responsible for the observed $\gamma$-ray emission are heavily debated. Conflicting models favor either hadronic processes (involving $p-p$ collisions) or leptonic processes (inverse Compton scattering) [Thoudam et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...778L..20T/abstract). Adding to this complexity, recent studies disagree on the nature of the $\gamma$-ray [[cocoon-astrophysics|cocoon]] substructure; while some interpret it as a collimated galactic outflow, others argue it is merely projected emission from millisecond pulsars in the Sagittarius dwarf spheroidal galaxy, though this latter claim has been recently challenged [Crocker et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6.1317C/abstract), [Eckner et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.110l3006E/abstract).

### Bubble Composition and Plasma Physics
On cluster scales, the physical composition and pressure support within AGN-inflated bubbles remain difficult to constrain. While these bubbles displace X-ray emitting [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] to form cavities, it is highly uncertain whether they are supported by a diffuse, ultra-hot thermal plasma or if non-thermal pressure from relativistic cosmic rays and magnetic fields dominates [Abdulla et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...871..195A/abstract). Theoretical models suggest that the ratio of cosmic-ray pressure to thermal gas pressure can be bounded by $P_c/P_g \lesssim 0.1$ to successfully suppress catastrophic cooling [Guo et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.384..251G/abstract), yet direct observational consensus is lacking.

The standard model of shock-heating during bubble expansion is also challenged by observations in the [[perseus-cluster|Perseus Cluster]] and similar environments of "isothermal shocks," where expected temperature jumps are absent, potentially indicating efficient thermal conduction [Fabian et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.366..417F/abstract). Additionally, the long-term evolution of the magnetic fields draped around these structures—which suppress cosmic-ray diffusion and stabilize the bubbles against hydrodynamical instabilities—remains an open theoretical question , [Guo et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...756..182G/abstract), [Prokhorov et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.3388P/abstract).

### Next-Generation Observatories
Resolving these tensions requires deeper multi-wavelength data from upcoming facilities. The Cherenkov Telescope Array (CTA) will map the Galactic Center and the base of the Fermi bubbles with unprecedented sensitivity at TeV energies, providing crucial tests for particle acceleration models and tracing the origin of the [[galactic-cosmic-ray-halo|Galactic cosmic-ray halo]] [Viana et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ICRC...36..817V/abstract). 

In the radio and millimeter regimes, the next-generation Very Large Array (ngVLA) and the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) will trace older, fading bubble populations (such as [[ghost-cavities|Ghost cavities]]) [Owen et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.5834O/abstract), [Brienza et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...696A.239B/abstract), and directly measure the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect. The SZ decrement will allow astronomers to definitively separate thermal and non-thermal pressure contributions in both quasar and starburst winds [Lacy et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181108752L/abstract). Finally, future high-resolution X-ray spectroscopy missions will measure chemical abundances within the bubbles, offering a definitive diagnostic to distinguish between metal-rich supernova ejecta and AGN-driven [[galaxy-outflows|Galaxy outflows]] [Inoue et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PASJ...67...56I/abstract).
