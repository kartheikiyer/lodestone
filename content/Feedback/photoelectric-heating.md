## Article

Photoelectric heating is a dominant thermal process regulating the temperature of the neutral interstellar medium (ISM) and [[photodissociation-chemistry|photodissociation regions (PDRs)]] in star-forming galaxies. The mechanism is driven by far-ultraviolet (FUV) photons ($6\ {\rm eV} < h\nu < 13.6\ {\rm eV}$) that eject electrons from the surfaces of dust particles, most notably polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and [[very-small-grains|Very Small Grains]] [Wolfire et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...443..152W/abstract). Upon ejection, these photoelectrons transfer their excess kinetic energy to the ambient gas through elastic collisions, effectively converting stellar radiation into thermal energy. Balanced primarily by fine-structure [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]], photoelectric heating establishes the multiphase structure of the neutral ISM, maintaining the equilibrium temperatures of both the Cold Neutral Medium (CNM) and the Warm Neutral Medium (WNM) [Draine et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011piim.book.....D/abstract).

Beyond its microphysical origins, photoelectric heating acts as a critical, long-range stellar feedback mechanism that heavily influences global galaxy evolution. The efficiency of this heating—defined as the fraction of absorbed FUV energy converted into gas heating—is not a universal constant. It scales with the local radiation field and the electrical charge of the grains, typically increasing from dense inner galactic regions to diffuse outskirts. By elevating the temperature and pressure of the diffuse gas, photoelectric heating suppresses violent disk instabilities and reduces the fraction of the ISM capable of undergoing gravitational collapse. In contrast to the highly localized and episodic mechanical feedback from supernovae, FUV-driven photoelectric heating, operating alongside more dominant radiative feedback channels like [[photoionization-and-radiative-heating|photoionization]] and radiation pressure, provides a broadly distributed energy input that smooths a galaxy's star formation history [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.3702H/abstract). Furthermore, while this FUV flux is traditionally associated with young massive stars, recent multi-wavelength diagnostics reveal that evolved stellar populations also contribute substantially to the heating of diffuse dust in galactic bulges and large-scale disks.

The study of photoelectric heating is complicated by its strong environmental dependencies and its competition with alternative [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|Heating mechanisms (astrophysics)]]. As the dust-to-gas ratio declines in low-metallicity environments or early-universe dwarf galaxies, the photoelectric effect loses its dominance, eventually giving way to X-ray or cosmic ray [[photoionization-and-radiative-heating|Photoionization and Radiative Heating]]. Current theoretical models and 3D radiative transfer simulations must carefully integrate these shifting thermal drivers alongside non-equilibrium [[photodissociation-chemistry|Photodissociation chemistry]]. Despite the general success of standard thermochemical models, several prominent open questions remain. Resolving these tensions relies heavily on next-generation observatories mapping infrared dust features, which aim to connect small-scale grain physics to the global thermodynamic regulation of star formation.

## Physical Mechanism and Photoelectric Efficiency

The photoelectric heating of the interstellar medium is primarily driven by the absorption of FUV photons by PAHs and [[very-small-grains|Very Small Grains]] [Wolfire et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...443..152W/abstract). Upon absorbing an FUV photon, a dust grain or PAH molecule may eject a photoelectron. The kinetic energy of these ejected photoelectrons is subsequently transferred to the ambient gas through elastic collisions. This thermal energy conversion is the dominant heating mechanism for the CNM and WNM in typical star-forming galaxies [Wolfire et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...443..152W/abstract).

The photoelectric efficiency, denoted $\epsilon$, is defined as the fraction of the absorbed FUV radiation energy that is ultimately converted into gas heating. In galactic environments, $\epsilon$ is not a universal constant; observations and models indicate it typically ranges from approximately $0.8\%$ in the dense, inner regions of galaxies to $\sim 3\%$ in galactic outskirts where the radiation field is weaker [Kramer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...553A.114K/abstract).

The primary microphysical regulator of photoelectric efficiency is the grain charge. As grains and PAHs undergo continuous photoionization, they acquire a net positive charge, which increases the work function required to eject subsequent electrons. Consequently, efficiency is strongly parameterized by the local radiation and gas properties, scaling inversely with the ionization parameter. In environments with intense FUV radiation fields or low electron densities, grains become highly positively charged, and the efficiency of thermal energy conversion drops significantly [Kramer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...553A.114K/abstract). 

Efficiency is further constrained by the physical survival of the dust grains. In H II regions featuring extreme ionization parameters, the hydrogen-ionizing UV radiation physically destroys PAH molecules, sharply curtailing their ability to heat the gas [Stephens et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...784..147S/abstract), [Egorov et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944L..16E/abstract). Furthermore, photoelectric heating exhibits a strong metallicity dependence. As the dust-to-gas ratio drops in low-metallicity environments ($Z \lesssim 10^{-1}\ Z_\odot$), the photoelectric effect loses its dominance. Under these conditions, alternative [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|Heating mechanisms (astrophysics)]] take over the primary heating budget [Nakatani et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...857...57N/abstract), [Varese et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.147V/abstract).

## Thermal Balance in Photodissociation Regions (PDRs)

[[photodissociation-chemistry|Photodissociation regions (PDRs)]] are neutral interfaces where FUV radiation regulates the thermal and chemical state of the gas. In these environments, photoelectric heating driven by electrons ejected from PAHs and [[very-small-grains|Very Small Grains]] serves as the dominant heat source. In a steady state, the volumetric photoelectric heating rate, $\Gamma_{\rm PE}$, is balanced by the total cooling rate, $\Lambda$, yielding the equilibrium condition:

$$ \Gamma_{\rm PE} = \Lambda $$

This thermal balance is primarily maintained by [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]] through fine-structure emission—such as [C II] 158 $\mu$m and [O I] 63 $\mu$m—alongside molecular rotational lines like those of CO and H$_2$ [Wolfire et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...443..152W/abstract). Alongside fine structure lines, observations of specific molecular tracers such as HOC$^+$ and CO$^+$ in the starburst galaxy NGC 253 confirm the presence of a significant UV-irradiated PDR component in the starburst nucleus, even though the overall chemistry and thermal balance of the ISM in such environments are dominated by shock heating [Martín et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...706.1323M/abstract).

Standard PDR models attempt to simulate this thermal balance alongside [[photodissociation-chemistry|Photodissociation chemistry]] to predict the detailed structure of the transition from atomic to molecular gas. However, high-resolution observations of prototypical PDRs, such as the Orion Bar, reveal persistent discrepancies between theoretical models and empirical data. Specifically, spatial mapping of pure rotational H$_2$ lines at high resolution (e.g., $0.002$ pc) indicates that PDRs contain unexpectedly large volumes of very warm ($400 - 700$ K) molecular gas [Allers et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...630..368A/abstract). Current PDR models frequently under-predict the spatial extent and mass of this warm H$_2$ component. Resolving this tension surrounding H$_2$ excitation likely requires significant adjustments to the microphysics, such as modifying the assumed UV attenuation cross-sections of dust, updating the formation rate of H$_2$, or adopting enhanced photoelectric heating rates in the dense, highly irradiated transition layers [Allers et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...630..368A/abstract). Furthermore, macroscopic structural formations within these regions, such as prominent gaseous pillars, form at the boundary of hot ionized gas and cold molecular clouds through a complex interplay of [[photoionization-and-radiative-heating|photoionization]], ablation, and heating [Pound et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Ap&SS.307..187P/abstract).

## Global Regulation of Star Formation and Galaxy Evolution

Photoelectric heating acts as a fundamental stellar feedback mechanism that regulates the star formation rate (SFR) across galactic scales. Historically, early theoretical frameworks proposed that the heating from ionizing UV radiation associated with young stars acts as a negative feedback loop that essentially quenches runaway star formation [Cox et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...265L..61C/abstract). Modern understanding confirms that by supplying the necessary energy to maintain the thermal pressure of the diffuse neutral ISM, photoelectric heating operates as a long-range, continuous feedback loop. Unlike supernova (SN) feedback, which is episodic and highly localized, photoelectric heating from young stellar populations, operating alongside dominant channels like [[photoionization-and-radiative-heating|photoionization]] and radiation pressure, provides a broadly distributed energy input that acts to "smooth" the star formation history of a galaxy, preventing runaway local collapse [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.3702H/abstract). 

At the macroscopic level, the balance between photoelectric heating and [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]] dictates the multiphase structure of the ISM. By elevating the temperature of the neutral gas, this heating limits the fraction of the ISM that can transition into the cold, dense phase required for gravitational collapse, thereby significantly extending the global gas depletion time. Theoretical models suggest that the empirical Schmidt law, which dictates the observed power-law relationship between the star formation rate surface density ($\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$) and the gas surface density ($\Sigma_{\rm gas}$), is a natural result of global thermohydrodynamical balance driven by a combination of turbulent heating, wave pressure, and radiative feedback [Struck et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...527..673S/abstract). At the microscale, turbulence replenishment and thermal instability continuously compete to segregate the ISM into its distinct diffuse and dense phases [Vazquez-Semadeni et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1202.4498V/abstract). Furthermore, in interarm regions, element mixing and cloud dispersal are enhanced by increased ionization and heating, which contributes to mixing at the atomic level before gas re-enters spiral arms [Elmegreen et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..147..278E/abstract).

The excess thermal pressure generated by photoelectric heating also plays a critical role in maintaining large-scale disk stability. By reducing the overall ISM density, photoelectric heating suppresses violent disk instabilities and inhibits the formation of massive, bound star-forming clumps. In gas-rich disk models, the suppression of disk clumping directly limits the growth of the galactic bulge, as fewer massive clumps are available to migrate to the galactic center [Osman et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.2075O/abstract). Furthermore, because SN rates drop significantly in the low-density outer regions of galactic disks, FUV-driven photoelectric heating often becomes the dominant regulatory mechanism in these extended environments [Benincasa et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.2028B/abstract). 

The specific efficacy of photoelectric heating as a standalone feedback mechanism is a subject of active debate, particularly in the context of dwarf galaxies where shallow gravitational potential wells make the ISM highly sensitive to thermal energy injection. High-resolution simulations by Forbes et al. found that photoelectric heating is the dominant regulator in dwarf galaxies, suppressing star formation rates by more than an order of magnitude relative to SN-only models and natively reproducing the long gas depletion times observed in local dwarfs [Forbes et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.535..523F/abstract). Additionally, photoionization from the cosmic UV background can suppress the formation of low-mass galaxies (with circular velocities below $\sim 30\ {\rm km\ s^{-1}}$) by heating the gas before it can collapse entirely [Thoul et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...465..608T/abstract). 

At larger scales, UV diagnostics of starburst galaxies connect intense local star formation to the global heating and metal enrichment of the intergalactic medium (IGM) via large-scale galactic winds and escaping ionizing radiation [Martin et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..164...66M/abstract). Conversely, other models argue that while FUV heating alone can produce realistic disk scale heights, it is insufficient to regulate star formation globally without a tight coupling to mechanical SN feedback [Benincasa et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.2028B/abstract). This tension is further complicated by environmental dependencies, as ionizing and Lyman-Werner radiation eventually dominate the feedback budget over the photoelectric effect at the lowest metallicities [Emerick et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200703702E/abstract). Finally, in massive galaxies, maintaining the red sequence requires continuous thermal energy injection into hot gaseous haloes to prevent catastrophic cooling and the re-ignition of star formation [Gabor et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.417.2676G/abstract).

## Environmental Variations and Metallicity Dependence

The effectiveness of photoelectric heating is strongly coupled to galactic metallicity. As the metallicity and the corresponding dust-to-gas ratio decline, the abundance of [[very-small-grains|Very Small Grains]] and PAHs drops precipitously. In [[photodissociation-chemistry|PDRs]], metallicity fundamentally governs the chemical structure and dust composition, directly impacting the corresponding heating and cooling efficiencies [Röllig et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008EAS....31..129R/abstract). In the low-metallicity ISM (typically below $\sim 10\%$ of the solar value), the photoelectric effect ceases to be the dominant heating mechanism for the neutral atomic gas. Instead, other [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|Heating mechanisms (astrophysics)]] take over. Models of local dwarf galaxies reaching down to $3\%$ of solar metallicity indicate that [[photoionization-and-radiative-heating|Photoionization and Radiative Heating]] from X-ray sources and low-energy cosmic rays become the predominant sources of thermal energy in these environments, compensating for the lack of dust-driven heating [Varese et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241103912V/abstract), [Varese et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.147V/abstract). For instance, the local starbursting dwarf galaxy NGC 4214 serves as a crucial case study for modeling these transitions, demonstrating the necessity of separating PDR emission from diffuse dust emission components to accurately capture the thermal budget [Lisenfeld et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012IAUS..284..152L/abstract). Additionally, in early stellar clusters, partial ionization from X-rays can boost the escape fractions of He II ionizing radiation, significantly affecting the broader thermal history of the early IGM [Benson et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770...76B/abstract).

In extreme low-metallicity environments, exotic mechanisms may also contribute; for example, energy injection from primordial black holes has been proposed as a potential alternative heating source for the ISM in ultra-faint dwarfs like Leo T. Combining this with models of dark photons places even tighter constraints on dark matter fractions based on limits imposed by observed gas heating [Takhistov et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...03..017T/abstract), [Takeshita et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240903981T/abstract). Furthermore, the observed dust temperatures within our Galaxy can be utilized to set robust constraints on the fraction of dark matter composed of primordial black holes due to their continuous radiative heating effects [Melikhov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.107f3535M/abstract).

Metallicity variations also govern the thermodynamics of circumstellar environments, particularly concerning the [[disk-irradiation|Disk irradiation]] and photoevaporation of protoplanetary disks. In environments with moderately sub-solar metallicities ($Z \sim 0.1-1\ Z_\odot$), reduced dust shielding allows FUV photons to penetrate deeper into the disk, enhancing photoelectric heating and leading to shorter disk lifetimes [Nakatani et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...857...57N/abstract). However, in extremely metal-poor environments ($Z \lesssim 10^{-2}\ Z_\odot$), photoelectric heating becomes less efficient than gas-dust collisional [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]]. In this regime, the primary driver of photoevaporative flows shifts entirely to H I photoionization heating, resulting in a roughly constant mass-loss rate that is largely independent of further metallicity decreases [Nakatani et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...857...57N/abstract).

## Synergies and Competition with Alternative Heating Processes

While photoelectric heating (PEH) is the primary thermal driver in the diffuse neutral atomic and molecular phases of solar-metallicity star-forming galaxies, it competes with and is often superseded by alternative [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|Heating mechanisms (astrophysics)]] in extreme environments. The relative dominance of PEH depends heavily on the local dust-to-gas ratio, radiation field, and the presence of energetic mechanical or non-thermal sources. In particularly hot, tenuous gas ($10^6 - 10^7$ K) where the local radiation field is exceptionally weak ($G_0 < 0.1$), electron collisional heating can even dominate over photon absorption by dust grains [Bocchio et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...556A...6B/abstract).

**Low-Metallicity Environments and X-ray Photoionization**
Because PEH relies on the abundance of [[very-small-grains|Very Small Grains]] and PAHs, its efficiency drops precipitously in low-metallicity environments. In dwarf galaxies with metallicities below $\sim 1/18$ of the Solar value, the reduced dust abundance renders PEH largely ineffective [Varese et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.147V/abstract), [Varese et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241103912V/abstract). In these regimes, [[photoionization-and-radiative-heating|Photoionization and Radiative Heating]] from X-rays, along with cosmic ray (CR) heating, take over as the predominant thermal drivers of the neutral gas. On cosmological scales, cosmic reionization represents a major phase transition where escaping UV and X-ray radiation from early galaxies heated and ionized the surrounding IGM [Wise et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190706653W/abstract). During this epoch, X-ray heating is expected to have played a critical role in the thermal history of the early universe, where it likely produced a more uniform reionization morphology and suppressed small-scale power [Mesinger et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431..621M/abstract). However, the X-ray opacity of host galaxies at high redshift ($z \sim 8-15$) significantly reduces the escape fraction of soft X-rays into the IGM, modulating the inhomogeneous epoch of heating [Das et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.1166D/abstract). Theoretical models also suggest that dark matter self-annihilation could provide a steady background heating source that modulates galaxy formation alongside typical star formation and AGN feedback [Natarajan et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.388.1652N/abstract).

**Cosmic Ray Heating**
Cosmic rays maintain an energy density in the ISM comparable to magnetic fields and radiation fields, playing a leading role in heating the dense cores of molecular clouds [Yang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Innov...300260Y/abstract). Cosmic rays provide a highly penetrating heating source that competes with PEH in both diffuse and dense environments. In the Warm Ionized Medium (WIM), low-energy cosmic rays (LECRs) are significantly more effective at heating plasma than neutral gas, and they likely dominate the WIM thermal balance over photo-processes [Walker et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...818...23W/abstract). In the early Universe, blast waves from the first cosmic explosions at $z \sim 20$ likely generated the very first cosmic rays, directly contributing to early IGM heating and the generation of primordial magnetic fields [Ohira et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvD.100f1301O/abstract). In these early epochs, cosmic ray energy deposition was tightly regulated by the ongoing evolution and amplification of galactic magnetic fields [Owen et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ICRC...35..289O/abstract). Furthermore, in the dense central giant molecular clouds of modern starburst galaxies, CR ionization rates can reach $\zeta \sim 10^{-12} \ {\rm s}^{-1}$—orders of magnitude above the Galactic average. In systems like NGC 253, this intense CR flux dominates the total heating budget, overriding both PEH and mechanical heating in the dense molecular gas [Behrens et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...939..119B/abstract).

**Mechanical Heating and Shocks**
In galactic nuclei and the central molecular zones (CMZ) of barred or interacting galaxies, mechanical heating via turbulence and shocks frequently overtakes radiative heating. Observations of shock tracers, such as the SiO and HNCO emission ratios in the Milky Way's CMZ, demonstrate that strong shocks are the most efficient heating agents of the local molecular gas [Ott et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..303..104O/abstract). Multi-transition $^{12}$CO analyses in the composite starburst/AGN galaxy NGC 4945 reveal that mechanical heating dominates the interstellar medium thermal structure on scales greater than 100 parsecs, driven by the barred potential and X-ray outflows rather than stellar ultraviolet radiation [Bellocchi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...642A.166B/abstract). Mechanical heating from jets and cosmic rays can also drive an order-of-magnitude increase in internal cloud pressure, as explicitly observed in outflows from systems like IC 5063 [Dasyra et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6.1077D/abstract). Even in low-luminosity active galactic nuclei (LLAGN), high-resolution observations with JWST in the core of M58 reveal that H$_2$ rotational lines are primarily excited by low-velocity shocks, highlighting the profound impact of mechanical feedback in low-luminosity environments [López et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250925483L/abstract). In hot halos surrounding galaxies, energy dissipation from cool fragments infalling through the multiphase halo can act as an additional internal energy source for the background gas [Murray et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...615..586M/abstract). Additionally, the emergence of non-thermal proton tails in shock-heated plasma is often accompanied by excessive plasma heating, effectively constraining non-thermal bremsstrahlung models used to explain galactic X-ray emission [Petrosian et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...813....5P/abstract). Furthermore, stationary equilibrium states in hot coronal gas ($T > 10^6$ K) are dictated by steady heating sources and electron thermal conduction at the interfaces with cooler gas [McCray et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987soap.conf..255M/abstract).

**AGN Feedback and Cluster-Scale Heating**
On the largest macroscopic scales, such as massive early-type galaxies and the intracluster medium (ICM), PEH is negligible. Instead, the thermal state of the hot gaseous halos is regulated by [[non-stellar-emission|AGN feedback]]. Radio-mechanical feedback injects vast amounts of energy that offset X-ray [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]] and prevent catastrophic cooling flows [Gaspari et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.411..349G/abstract), [Werner et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019SSRv..215....5W/abstract). Cooling-induced gravitational collapse can provide a fueling source for AGN feedback in these systems, where the accretion rate is intimately linked to the global properties of the cooling flow [Pope et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.419...50P/abstract). The transfer of this AGN-driven energy into the thermal plasma is mediated by turbulent dissipation [Zhuravleva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.515...85Z/abstract), with recent simulations suggesting that kinetic-scale processes—such as magnetic pumping via mirror and firehose instabilities—play a crucial role in converting large-scale turbulent fluctuations into plasma heating [Ley et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...947...89L/abstract). Furthermore, radiative feedback from central black holes can undergo relaxation oscillations, cyclically regulating both black hole growth and the host galaxy's global thermal state [Ostriker et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RSPTA.363..667O/abstract). In rotating early-type galaxies, angular momentum strongly alters the heating and cooling balance, leading to shallower entropy profiles resulting from compressional or outflow-driven heating [Juráňová et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.5163J/abstract). On smaller, sub-parsec nuclear scales, viscous dissipation acts as a competing heat source alongside X-rays, creating the high-temperature environments necessary to pump water masers in circumnuclear accretion disks [Desch et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...496..775D/abstract). Finally, [[non-stellar-emission|AGN radiation]] can also directly participate in dust heating on galactic scales; high-resolution radiative transfer modeling of NGC 1068 demonstrates that AGN radiation can significantly heat diffuse dust well beyond the central torus, contributing measurably to the global disc heating budget [Viaene et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...638A.150V/abstract).

## Numerical Modeling and Radiative Transfer Simulations

Evaluating photoelectric heating alongside other [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|Heating mechanisms (astrophysics)]] requires accurately determining the local FUV radiation field. Modern 3D radiative transfer codes, such as SKIRT, utilize Monte Carlo methods to self-consistently simulate the absorption, scattering, and non-local thermal equilibrium (NLTE) emission of dust [Nersesian et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...637A..25N/abstract). Detailed modeling of the Galactic interstellar radiation field (ISRF) has become crucial in calculating the infrared radiation field through self-consistent dust heating codes [Porter et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ICRC....4...77P/abstract). These models have been instrumental in resolving the spatial distribution of the interstellar radiation field in nearby galaxies, revealing that evolved stellar populations can contribute significantly to diffuse dust heating, challenging the assumption that infrared emission strictly traces young star-forming regions [De Looze et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...571A..69D/abstract), [Verstocken et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...637A..24V/abstract). Furthermore, models of interacting systems like M51 indicate that radiation from a companion galaxy can locally contribute up to 38% of the absorbed energy heating the diffuse dust [Nersesian et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A..90N/abstract). On sub-galactic scales, empirical dust emission models like DustEM are used to fit spectral energy distributions (SEDs) and track the local abundance of heating agents [Stephens et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...784..147S/abstract).

Accurately computing the photoelectric heating rate and its interaction with [[photodissociation-chemistry|Photodissociation chemistry]] requires coupling multifrequency radiative transfer with non-equilibrium chemistry. In the low-density interstellar medium ($\rho < 10^5 \text{ cm}^{-3}$), the electron fraction dictates the recombination rate of dust grains, thereby controlling the grain charge parameter and the net photoelectric efficiency. Advanced frameworks, such as the PRISM model implemented in the RAMSES-RTZ code, track over 100 chemical species alongside on-the-fly radiation transport [Katz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221104626K/abstract). Such models demonstrate that the non-equilibrium electron fraction can deviate from equilibrium values by a factor of three or more, significantly altering the balance between [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]] and heating. Furthermore, recent algorithmic improvements, such as the piecewise power-law spectral reconstruction used in Gasoline2 and TREVR2, allow simulations to capture the evolving slopes of radiation spectra rather than relying on broad, constant-intensity bands. This reduces errors in [[photoionization-and-radiative-heating|Photoionization and Radiative Heating]] rates by up to two orders of magnitude, providing a much more rigorous treatment of the FUV flux driving the photoelectric effect [Baumschlager et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A.219B/abstract), [Baumschlager et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231016902B/abstract). For a comprehensive summary of the foundational equations and numerical schemes needed to solve this coupled heating and cooling balance—spanning from the diffuse ISM down to dense protoplanetary disks—readers are referred to standard modern texts on ISM thermodynamics [Kamp et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024asmo.book..283K/abstract). Beyond the ISM, advanced two-temperature general relativistic magnetohydrodynamic (GRMHD) simulations of magnetically arrested disks (MAD) demonstrate that magnetic turbulent cascades can also effectively heat electrons, driving the complex thermodynamics near supermassive black holes [Mościbrodzka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...981..145M/abstract).

## Observational Diagnostics and Spectral Energy Distributions

The efficiency of photoelectric heating in the ISM is observationally constrained by comparing the energy injected into the gas against the total energy absorbed by dust grains. Because the gas thermal balance is maintained by [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]], the intensity of far-infrared fine-structure lines—most notably the [CII] 158μm transition—serves as a primary calorimeter for the heating rate in the neutral atomic and molecular phases [Wolfire et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...443..152W/abstract). 

The photoelectric heating efficiency, $\epsilon$, is typically approximated observationally as the ratio of the cooling line intensity to the total far-infrared continuum:
$$ \epsilon \approx \frac{I_{\rm [CII]}}{I_{\rm FIR}} $$
Spatially resolved observations of nearby galaxies, such as M33, demonstrate that this efficiency is highly sensitive to the local environment, rising in galactic outskirts where volume densities and FUV fields drop [Kramer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...553A.114K/abstract).

To understand the specific radiation fields driving this process, astrophysicists rely on the broadband infrared SED. Mid-infrared features, particularly at 8 μm and 24 μm, are utilized to trace the abundance and excitation of PAHs and [[very-small-grains|Very Small Grains]], respectively [Lu et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...797..129L/abstract). Furthermore, the 20-42 μm continuum has been identified as a highly robust tracer of dust heating originating from current star formation [Dale et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...549..215D/abstract). AGN broadband IR SEDs and their variability are also key diagnostics for characterizing the circumnuclear dusty structures bridging the galaxy to the central black hole [Lyu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Univ....8..304L/abstract).

However, the interpretation of the infrared SED as a strict proxy for recent star formation has undergone a paradigm shift, driven largely by *Herschel* observations and sophisticated 3D radiative transfer modeling. Historically, far-infrared dust emission was assumed to be powered almost exclusively by young, massive stars (ages $< 100$ Myr). Contemporary models of local galaxies such as M31, M51, and M81 reveal that evolved stellar populations contribute substantially to the heating of diffuse dust. In galactic bulges and the diffuse ISM, older stars can power anywhere from 30% to over 90% of the total dust heating [De Looze et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...571A..69D/abstract), [Viaene et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...599A..64V/abstract), [Verstocken et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...637A..24V/abstract). Consequently, the observed dust temperature distribution reflects a complex combination of local FUV fields from young stars and non-local heating from older populations [Tailor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A..74T/abstract). Moreover, observations of interarm regions often reveal significant millimeter dust emission, highlighting the vital role of the general interstellar radiation field in heating cold dust far away from active star-forming centers [Ejlali et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022EPJWC.25700016E/abstract).

The relationship between these observational tracers remains an area of active tension at high redshift. Observations of star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 2$ reveal a pronounced "PAH/[CII] deficit" relative to the total infrared luminosity when compared to local analogues. It is currently ambiguous whether this deficit reflects a fundamental reduction in photoelectric heating efficiency in early-universe environments, hotter intrinsic dust temperatures, or a transition in the dominant ISM cooling channel from [CII] 158μm to [OI] 63μm [McKinney et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...892..119M/abstract).

## Evolution of Theoretical Models

The historical development of photoelectric heating models traces a path from simplified assumptions of uniform dust populations to highly complex treatments of grain microphysics, non-equilibrium chemistry, and global radiative transfer. 

Early models of [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|Heating mechanisms (astrophysics)]] recognized the role of the interstellar radiation field but often underestimated the efficiency of the photoelectric effect by omitting the smallest grain populations. A major paradigm shift occurred with the foundational Wolfire models [Wolfire et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...443..152W/abstract). By incorporating a continuous grain size distribution extending from a radius of $100$ Å down to $3$ Å, these models explicitly included PAHs and [[very-small-grains|Very Small Grains]]. Wolfire et al. established that photoelectric emission from these macromolecular structures overwhelmingly dominates the heating of the neutral atomic phases. Their models successfully predicted a stable two-phase medium and yielded global [C II] $158\ \mu\text{m}$ luminosities consistent with contemporary COBE observations.

As models grew more sophisticated, accurately calculating dust spectral energy distributions required the inclusion of stochastic heating. Because PAHs and VSGs have exceedingly small heat capacities, the absorption of a single FUV photon significantly spikes their temperature, necessitating a probabilistic treatment of their thermal state. However, applying stochastic heating models across diverse environments has revealed tensions. For instance, standard stochastic heating models driven by the typical interstellar radiation field fail to reproduce the high mid-infrared fluxes observed at the centers of dense protostellar cores; matching these observations requires artificially enhancing the ambient radiation field by a factor of $\sim 70$, which then conflicts with the lack of observed ring-like peripheral emission [Pavlyuchenkov et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421.2430P/abstract). 

A further layer of complexity in theoretical evolution is the dynamic survival of the heating agents themselves. With modern observational constraints demonstrating that PAHs are efficiently destroyed by hydrogen-ionizing UV radiation, modern theoretical frameworks no longer treat photoelectric heating efficiency as a universal constant, but instead as a variable parameterized by the local radiation field and metallicity. 

Finally, contemporary theory has integrated photoelectric heating into global, 3D radiative transfer simulations. These modern approaches have overturned the historical assumption that only young, massive stars drive dust heating. However, it is important to distinguish between the heating of the gas and the heating of the dust; modern models demonstrate that evolved stellar populations contribute significantly to the overall thermal [[photoionization-and-radiative-heating|infrared dust heating]] budget, up to a vast majority in certain galaxies [De Looze et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...571A..69D/abstract), [Viaene et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...599A..64V/abstract). Thus, relying solely on total infrared emission can obscure the specific FUV radiation field that drives the photoelectric heating of the gas. Coupled with advanced treatments of [[photoionization-and-radiative-heating|Photoionization and Radiative Heating]] and non-equilibrium [[photodissociation-chemistry|Photodissociation chemistry]] [Kim et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..264...10K/abstract), the modeling of photoelectric heating has evolved into a fully coupled chemodynamical problem essential for understanding the multiphase interstellar medium.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite its fundamental role in regulating the thermal state of the interstellar medium, the exact physical parameters governing photoelectric heating are subject to significant uncertainty. Standard models of [[photodissociation-chemistry|Photodissociation chemistry]] continue to struggle with reproducing high-resolution PDR observations, indicating potential errors in adopted [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]] and heating functions, or the need to substantially revise the assumed ultraviolet attenuation cross-sections for dust. 

Extending these models to the high-redshift ISM and extreme low-metallicity environments introduces further complications. It remains unclear precisely what physical mechanisms drive the "PAH/[C II] deficit" observed in early-universe star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 2$ [McKinney et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...892..119M/abstract). Furthermore, the exact transition threshold where photoelectric heating yields its dominance to [[photoionization-and-radiative-heating|Photoionization and Radiative Heating]] from X-rays and cosmic rays in metal-poor galaxies is still being actively parameterized by grid-based models [Nakatani et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...857...57N/abstract), [Varese et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.147V/abstract).

On macroscopic scales, the proper inclusion of photoelectric heating as sub-grid physics in global radiation-hydrodynamic simulations remains a subject of active debate. While it is clear that photoelectric heating acts as a long-range feedback mechanism that smooths out bursty supernova feedback, literature is highly conflicting regarding its absolute regulatory power in low-mass halos. 

Resolving these tensions requires a better understanding of small-scale dust physics, particularly the lifecycle of the grain populations responsible for the heating. JWST is poised to address many of these open questions by mapping the destruction and survival of PAHs and [[very-small-grains|Very Small Grains]] across diverse environments. Recent JWST observations have already demonstrated a strong anticorrelation between the PAH fraction and the local ionization parameter in H II regions, confirming that hydrogen-ionizing UV radiation drives PAH destruction [Egorov et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944L..16E/abstract). Future observations and coupled thermochemical modeling will be critical in determining how these microphysical destruction mechanisms scale up to alter the global energy budget of galaxies.
