## Article

## Introduction
In the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological paradigm, the condensation of gas into dark matter halos naturally leads to rapid radiative cooling and the catastrophic overproduction of stars. Cosmological simulations demonstrate that without energetic regulatory mechanisms, theoretical models severely overpredict both the cosmic [[star-formation-processes|star formation history]] and the number density of galaxies at the low- and high-mass ends of the galaxy stellar mass function [[bibcode-2009mnras-398-53b|bibcode:2009MNRAS.398...53B]], [[bibcode-2014mnras-445-581h|bibcode:2014MNRAS.445..581H]]. To reconcile theory with observation, astrophysicists invoke **stellar and AGN feedback**—the injection of energy, momentum, and mass into the [[galactic-gas-dynamics-and-flows|interstellar and circumgalactic media]] from stars and supermassive black holes. These [[multi-scale-feedback-processes|Multi-scale feedback processes]] fundamentally drive the cosmic baryon cycle, governing the continuous accretion, ejection, and recycling of gas.

The relative dominance of these feedback mechanisms is strongly mass-dependent. In halos below $M \approx 10^{12} M_\odot$, [[stellar-feedback-and-winds|Stellar Feedback and Winds]]—encompassing core-collapse supernovae, stellar winds, radiation pressure, and photoionization—act as the primary regulators of star formation [[bibcode-2018apj-855l-20m|bibcode:2018ApJ...855L..20M]], [[bibcode-2018apj-866-70l|bibcode:2018ApJ...866...70L]]. These processes disperse dense giant molecular clouds and drive multiphase galactic winds characterized by a high [[mass-loading-factor|Mass loading factor]]. By establishing [[self-regulated-feedback-mechanisms|Self-regulated feedback mechanisms]], stellar feedback reproduces the observed low efficiency of star formation in dwarf and Milky Way-mass galaxies [[bibcode-2014mnras-445-581h|bibcode:2014MNRAS.445..581H]].

In [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], groups, and clusters ($M \gtrsim 10^{12} M_\odot$), stellar energetic input is insufficient to offset rapid cooling. Here, [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] dominates via two primary channels. The "quasar" or radiative mode operates during high-accretion phases, utilizing radiation pressure to drive galaxy-wide winds that deplete central molecular gas reservoirs [[bibcode-2006apj-650-693n|bibcode:2006ApJ...650..693N]], [[bibcode-2021mnras-505l-46e|bibcode:2021MNRAS.505L..46E]]. Conversely, the "radio" or kinetic mode operates during low-accretion phases, providing continuous [[mechanical-feedback|Mechanical feedback]] via relativistic jets to maintain a long-lived heating-cooling balance in the intracluster medium [[bibcode-2012ara-a-50-455f|bibcode:2012ARA&A..50..455F]], [[bibcode-2015ara-a-53-115k|bibcode:2015ARA&A..53..115K]]. These powerful [[galactic-outflows-and-inflows|Galactic Outflows and Inflows]] redistribute baryons so profoundly that the total baryonic mass of a halo is often only recovered at a "closure radius" of $R_c \sim 1.5-2.5 R_{200c}$ [[bibcode-2023mnras-524-5391a|bibcode:2023MNRAS.524.5391A]]. A global inventory of feedback energy over cosmic time reveals distinct epochal peaks for these processes: while the energy injected by stellar feedback and AGN winds peaks at "cosmic noon" ($z \sim 2$), the kinetic energy from radio jets peaks significantly later, at $z \sim 1$ [[bibcode-2023galax-11-21h|bibcode:2023Galax..11...21H]].

While historically modeled as independent sources of [[negative-feedback|Negative feedback]] designed to instantaneously quench star formation, modern theoretical and observational research reveals a far more complex picture. High-resolution cosmological simulations demonstrate that stellar and AGN feedback are non-linearly coupled and can sometimes mutually weaken each other's global efficiency by prematurely disrupting the gas supply [[bibcode-2013natsr-3-1738b|bibcode:2013NatSR...3.1738B]]. Furthermore, observational studies often report a flat relationship between AGN bolometric luminosity and star formation rate, suggesting that powerful outflows can coexist with star-forming gas without immediately quenching the host galaxy globally [[bibcode-2017apj-839-120w|bibcode:2017ApJ...839..120W]], [[bibcode-2019mnras-486-4320r|bibcode:2019MNRAS.486.4320R]], [[bibcode-2020apj-901-42y|bibcode:2020ApJ...901...42Y]]. In addition, rather than strictly destroying molecular clouds, localized shocks from AGN jets and stellar outflows can compress the [[circumnuclear-environment|Circumnuclear Environment]] and trigger massive starbursts, a phenomenon known as [[positive-feedback-astrophysics|Positive feedback (astrophysics)]] [[bibcode-2013mnras-433-3079z|bibcode:2013MNRAS.433.3079Z]], [[bibcode-2016mnras-455-4166b|bibcode:2016MNRAS.455.4166B]], [[bibcode-2021scia-7-9511l|bibcode:2021SciA....7.9511L]]. Disentangling the varying timescales, kinetic coupling efficiencies, and net impacts of these competing feedback loops remains a central challenge in contemporary astrophysics.

## Historical Development of Feedback Theory

The theoretical necessity of feedback in [[star-formation-processes|galaxy formation]] emerged from the failure of early models relying purely on gravity and radiative cooling. In both early monolithic collapse models and subsequent hierarchical structure formation within the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm, simulations suffered from a severe "overcooling" problem. Without regulatory mechanisms, gas cooled too efficiently, overpredicting the cosmic star formation rate and yielding galaxy number densities that vastly exceeded observations at both the low- and high-mass extremes. Furthermore, early models predicted that [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] would be far too blue and actively star-forming compared to the observed universe.

To resolve the abundance of low-mass galaxies—often linked to the 'missing satellites' problem—and to reproduce the observed mass-metallicity relation, early theories invoked [[stellar-feedback-and-winds|Stellar Feedback and Winds]], primarily driven by supernovae (SNe). The theoretical necessity of this feedback is deeply rooted in the broader understanding of stellar structure and evolution, which defines the complete stellar life cycle and its foundational role in the chemical enrichment of the galaxy [[bibcode-2023sse-book-p|bibcode:2023sse..book.....P]]. However, it became apparent that [[sn-feedback-model|SNe feedback]] could only efficiently regulate star formation in halos below $M \sim 10^{12} M_\odot$ [[bibcode-2014mnras-445-581h|bibcode:2014MNRAS.445..581H]]. In more massive early-type galaxies and galaxy clusters, SNe energy was insufficient to offset rapid radiative losses. This theoretical deficit, famously manifested as the cooling flow problem in cluster cores, necessitated the introduction of [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] as a secondary feedback source. By injecting mechanical and radiative energy into the surrounding medium, AGN feedback prevents catastrophic overcooling in massive halos and maintains massive galaxies on the red sequence. Historically, models have contrasted 'ejective' feedback (which physically expels gas from halos) with '[[preventative-feedback|Preventative feedback]]' (which inhibits fresh gas from accreting). Theoretical work suggests that preventative models often offer more flexibility in matching the observed properties of the [[galactic-gas-dynamics-and-flows|interstellar and circumgalactic media]], as they avoid the extreme energetic requirements of unbinding gas deep within potential wells [[bibcode-2015arxiv150402109l|bibcode:2015arXiv150402109L]].

The implementation of these physical processes has undergone significant subgrid modeling evolution. In early hydrodynamical simulations, stellar and AGN feedback were typically modeled as simple thermal energy dumps into the interstellar medium (ISM). This approach proved highly ineffective; feedback energy was deposited into dense regions where the cooling time was exceptionally short, causing the energy to radiate away before it could perform mechanical work. To bypass this numerical overcooling, theorists developed ad hoc phenomenological prescriptions. These included temporarily disabling radiative cooling, artificially reducing the local gas density, or imparting direct kinetic kicks to gas particles to simulate momentum-driven winds.

Modern cosmological simulations have shifted away from single-mode thermal dumps toward explicit, multi-channel feedback models. Contemporary [[sn-feedback-model|SN feedback model]]s now explicitly resolve the multiphase ISM and account for early radiative feedback from [[massive-star-physics|Massive Star Physics]]—such as radiation pressure and photoionization—which pre-process and disperse dense giant molecular clouds before SNe explode [[bibcode-2014mnras-445-581h|bibcode:2014MNRAS.445..581H]]. 

This evolution in modeling has also revealed a significant "resolution bias" in feedback efficiency. Low-resolution simulations tend to artificially overestimate the destructive power of feedback because high-density, feedback-resistant clumps are smoothed out by the numerical grid [[bibcode-2015mnras-453-1829b|bibcode:2015MNRAS.453.1829B]]. Furthermore, while early semi-analytic models treated stellar and AGN feedback as independent components, high-resolution models demonstrate that they are non-linearly coupled. Rather than simply acting additively, SNe and AGN feedback can mutually weaken each other's effects by up to an order of magnitude; for instance, SNe-driven winds can deplete the cold gas reservoir necessary for black hole accretion, thereby delaying or reducing the efficacy of subsequent AGN feedback [[bibcode-2013natsr-3-1738b|bibcode:2013NatSR...3.1738B]].

## Stellar Feedback Mechanisms and Pre-Supernova Processes

Stellar feedback is the primary mechanism regulating [[star-formation-processes|star formation]] in galaxies residing in dark matter halos with masses $M < 10^{12} M_\odot$ [[bibcode-2018mnras-479-3189z|bibcode:2018MNRAS.479.3189Z]]. Historically, numerical simulations implementing a [[sn-feedback-model|SN feedback model]] struggled with the "overcooling" problem: injecting supernova (SN) energy purely as thermal energy into a dense, unresolved [[galactic-gas-dynamics-and-flows|interstellar medium (ISM)]] resulted in rapid radiative cooling before the energy could drive a macroscopic hydrodynamical response. To circumvent this, early models employed ad hoc prescriptions such as temporarily disabling radiative cooling or injecting momentum directly. However, it is now recognized that capturing realistic [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]] requires resolving the multiphase ISM and explicitly treating multiple feedback channels [[bibcode-2014mnras-445-581h|bibcode:2014MNRAS.445..581H]]. Care must be taken with such models, as low-resolution simulations can suffer from a "resolution bias", artificially enhancing feedback efficiency by washing out the dense, feedback-resistant gas clumps that naturally exist in the ISM [[bibcode-2015mnras-453-1829b|bibcode:2015MNRAS.453.1829B]]. Indeed, successfully capturing the effects of [[stellar-feedback-and-winds|Stellar Feedback and Winds]] without relying on ad hoc sub-resolution assumptions explicitly requires simulations with spatial resolutions finer than 50 pc to properly resolve hot gas bubbles and escaping chimneys [[bibcode-2008iaus-245-33c|bibcode:2008IAUS..245...33C]].

The modern theoretical paradigm emphasizes that supernovae do not operate in isolation. Before the first core-collapse SNe occur, massive stars inject significant energy and momentum into their natal Giant Molecular Clouds (GMCs) through [[strong-pre-sn-feedback|early radiative feedback]] and stellar winds [[bibcode-2013apj-770-25a|bibcode:2013ApJ...770...25A]]. This "pre-SN feedback" includes photoionization heating, radiation pressure on dust, and the dissociation of molecular hydrogen by Lyman-Werner (LW) photons. In high-resolution simulations, $H_2$-dissociating and ionizing radiation are often the dominant physical mechanisms for dispersing GMCs and suppressing early star formation; by contrast, SNe-only models often fail to capture the correct thermodynamic state and spatial distribution of the ISM [[bibcode-2017apj-841-82b|bibcode:2017ApJ...841...82B]]. Furthermore, turbulent dissipation resulting from large-scale [[wind-mechanical-energy|mechanical energy injection]] can enhance H$_2$ line emission, making it a critical cooling agent in high-redshift galaxies [[bibcode-2015sf2a-conf-81g|bibcode:2015sf2a.conf...81G]]. In low-mass dwarf galaxies, ionizing and LW radiation appear to dominate the early [[massive-star-physics|feedback budget]] over photoelectric heating and direct radiation pressure [[bibcode-2020arxiv200703702e|bibcode:2020arXiv200703702E]]. In the early universe, radiative transfer and subgrid turbulent mixing driven by supernova-induced turbulence are shown to suppress Population III star formation by a factor of roughly four, underscoring the critical role of these [[multi-scale-feedback-processes|Multi-scale feedback processes]] from the dawn of galaxy formation [[bibcode-2022apj-935-174s|bibcode:2022ApJ...935..174S]].

Observations of local star-forming regions provide quantitative constraints on these early feedback modes. In Magellanic Cloud HII regions (on scales of $\sim 10-100$ pc), multi-wavelength measurements of the pressures exerted on expanding shells show that the pressure of the warm ionized gas typically dominates over other terms, whereas direct radiation pressure is found to be one to two orders of magnitude weaker [[bibcode-2014apj-795-121l|bibcode:2014ApJ...795..121L]]. Similarly, integral field kinematics of ionized gas in metal-poor dwarf galaxies reveal expanding superbubbles where pre-SN feedback plays a critical role in driving early gas blowouts [[bibcode-2022mnras-517-4968g|bibcode:2022MNRAS.517.4968G]]. Furthermore, multi-wavelength studies of nearby dwarf galaxies establish critical timescales for these processes, revealing that atomic gas (HI) turbulence correlates strongest with star formation activity that occurred 100-200 Myr ago, setting a clear timescale over which stellar feedback drives interstellar turbulence [[bibcode-2022aj-163-132h|bibcode:2022AJ....163..132H]].

The net effect of pre-SN feedback is highly synergistic with subsequent SN explosions. By pre-clearing dense gas and opening low-density channels, early radiative feedback reduces the ambient density encountered by expanding SN remnants. This lowers the cooling rate of the SN shocks, mitigating catastrophic thermal losses and allowing the SN energy to couple much more efficiently to the large-scale ISM [[bibcode-2020mnras-493-4700l|bibcode:2020MNRAS.493.4700L]]. Together, these processes establish [[self-regulated-feedback-mechanisms|Self-regulated feedback mechanisms]] that maintain the low global efficiency of star formation. This multi-channel approach naturally reproduces the observed Kennicutt-Schmidt relation across a variety of galaxy types, largely independent of arbitrary small-scale subgrid parameter tuning [[bibcode-2011mnras-417-950h|bibcode:2011MNRAS.417..950H]], [[bibcode-2014mnras-445-581h|bibcode:2014MNRAS.445..581H]].

## AGN Feedback: Radiative and Mechanical Modes

[[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) feedback]] operates through two primary physical regimes distinguished by the mass accretion rate onto the supermassive black hole: the "quasar mode" (radiative) and the "radio mode" (mechanical). Both modes act as vital sources of [[negative-feedback|Negative feedback]], which is required in cosmological simulations to prevent overcooling and successfully reproduce the observed high-mass end of the galaxy stellar mass function and the red sequence [[bibcode-2012ara-a-50-455f|bibcode:2012ARA&A..50..455F]], [[bibcode-2013mnras-436-3031v|bibcode:2013MNRAS.436.3031V]].

**Quasar Mode (Radiative Feedback)**
The quasar mode typically operates during phases of [[net-accretion-rates|high, near-Eddington accretion]], which are most prevalent at [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|high redshifts]]. In this radiatively efficient state, the accretion disk emits intense ultraviolet and X-ray radiation. This energy couples to the interstellar medium (ISM) via [[momentum-driven-feedback-mechanisms|radiation pressure]] on dust [[bibcode-2016mnras-463-1291i|bibcode:2016MNRAS.463.1291I]], Compton heating, and photoionization. In standard theoretical prescriptions, it is often assumed that a small fraction (e.g., $\sim 5\%$) of the AGN bolometric luminosity couples thermally or kinetically to the surrounding gas, driving massive, wide-angle accretion disk winds. These quasar-driven winds propagate on kiloparsec scales, efficiently sweeping up or heating the gas and depleting the central molecular reservoirs on timescales of $\lesssim 100$ Myr [[bibcode-2009apj-690-1672s|bibcode:2009ApJ...690.1672S]], [[bibcode-2021mnras-505l-46e|bibcode:2021MNRAS.505L..46E]]. Furthermore, observationally calibrated X-ray radiation fields emitted from the central black hole can heat gas at large radii, proving potentially twice as effective as local thermal feedback at globally suppressing star formation [[bibcode-2011apj-738-16h|bibcode:2011ApJ...738...16H]]. Consequently, the quasar mode is widely associated with the rapid, initial truncation of star formation in massive galaxies. 

**Radio Mode (Mechanical Feedback)**
Conversely, the radio mode dominates at late cosmic times in massive ellipticals, galaxy groups, and clusters. It occurs during low-accretion, radiatively inefficient phases. Instead of radiation, the black hole outputs energy through highly collimated relativistic jets. This [[mechanical-feedback|Mechanical feedback]] drives shocks into the circumgalactic and intracluster medium, inflating buoyant X-ray cavities (or bubbles) that are visible in local cool-core clusters [[bibcode-2012ara-a-50-455f|bibcode:2012ARA&A..50..455F]], [[bibcode-2021univ-7-142e|bibcode:2021Univ....7..142E]]. The radio mode is considered a "maintenance" mechanism: the mechanical energy dissipated by these jet-driven bubbles continuously offsets radiative cooling in the hot halo, preventing a cooling catastrophe and keeping the host galaxy quenched over billions of years. The efficiency of this heating is sensitive to jet composition and density; for instance, lighter jets tend to inflate more spherical bubbles and deposit energy more isotropically throughout the cluster core than heavier jets [[bibcode-2025arxiv251017518t|bibcode:2025arXiv251017518T]]. The morphology of these bubbles serves as a vital probe of the underlying jet physics; for instance, the radial elongation of X-ray cavities indicates whether a jet is dominated by kinetic or thermal energy, with kinetic-energy-dominated jets typically producing more elongated (Type II) cavities than their thermal counterparts [[bibcode-2020apj-903-3g|bibcode:2020ApJ...903....3G]].

**Implementation in Simulations and Unresolved Tensions**
Modern cosmological hydrodynamical simulations rely on subgrid implementations of these modes, but macro-scale effects remain highly sensitive to micro-scale physics. For instance, comparing the SIMBA and IllustrisTNG suites reveals that SIMBA’s inclusion of high-speed, collimated jet feedback produces long-range heating that accurately matches the column density distribution of the low-redshift Ly$\alpha$ forest. In contrast, isotropic kinetic wind models have a negligible impact on such large intergalactic scales [[bibcode-2023apj-945l-17t|bibcode:2023ApJ...945L..17T]]. 

Recent theoretical efforts are moving toward explicit [[multi-channel-agn-feedback|Multi-channel AGN feedback]] models. For instance, sophisticated hybrid models now account for three distinct accretion states (thick, thin, and slim disks) and continuously track the evolution of the black hole's spin, which self-consistently determines the jet direction and feedback efficiency [[bibcode-2025arxiv250905179h|bibcode:2025arXiv250905179H]]. For instance, semi-analytic models indicate that at $z < 2$ and low luminosities, the AGN luminosity function is dominated by advection-dominated accretion flows (ADAF), while thin discs dominate at higher redshifts and higher luminosities [[bibcode-2019mnras-487-198g|bibcode:2019MNRAS.487..198G]]. Advanced simulations that incorporate the spin of the supermassive black hole correctly capture the anisotropy of the emitted radiation and winds. High-spin black holes at near-Eddington accretion rates exhibit more isotropic energy injection, which can evacuate polar regions, self-consistently form torus-like geometries, and alter the [[mass-quenching-efficiency|quenching efficiency]] [[bibcode-2016mnras-458-816h|bibcode:2016MNRAS.458..816H]], [[bibcode-2024a-a-690a-194b|bibcode:2024A&A...690A.194B]]. Despite these advances, the literature remains conflicted on the exact coupling efficiency between AGN luminosity and galactic winds across different redshifts, and the relative importance of thermal versus kinetic energy injection in the hot accretion mode continues to be heavily debated [[bibcode-2019apj-885-16y|bibcode:2019ApJ...885...16Y]].

## Galactic Outflows and the Circumgalactic Medium (CGM)

Large-scale galactic winds are the primary mechanism for regulating the baryon cycle, redistributing gas and metals from the interstellar medium (ISM) to the circumgalactic medium (CGM) and beyond. The efficacy of these [[galactic-outflows-and-inflows|Galactic Outflows and Inflows]] is conventionally parameterized by the [[mass-loading-factor|Mass loading factor]], $\eta = \dot{M}_{\rm out} / \rm SFR$, which quantifies the rate of mass ejection relative to the star formation rate. 

In low-mass galaxies, [[stellar-feedback-and-winds|Stellar Feedback and Winds]] drive outflows where the mass loading factor scales roughly with the inverse of the halo circular velocity ($V_c^{-1}$), routinely reaching $\eta \sim 10-20$ [[bibcode-2012mnras-421-3522h|bibcode:2012MNRAS.421.3522H]]. As the halo mass increases, the energetic requirements for unbinding gas rise. In massive galaxies, outflows driven by [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|active galactic nuclei (AGN)]] exhibit systematically higher mass loading factors than starburst-driven winds [[bibcode-2017a-a-601a-143f|bibcode:2017A&A...601A.143F]]. To successfully reproduce observed scaling relations, the coupling efficiency and [[momentum-driven-feedback-mechanisms|momentum loading factor]] ($f_{p, \rm AGN}$) of these AGN-driven winds must be substantial [[bibcode-2018mnras-479-3189z|bibcode:2018MNRAS.479.3189Z]]. 

In high-resolution hydrodynamical models, galactic winds exhibit a highly complex multiphase structure containing cold molecular, cool atomic, warm ionized, and hot X-ray emitting gas [[bibcode-2012mnras-421-3522h|bibcode:2012MNRAS.421.3522H]]. Observationally, this multiphase nature is detected across all mass scales, including dwarf galaxies where AGN-driven outflows can reach median velocities of $\sim 240$ km s$^{-1}$ and line widths of $W_{80} \sim 1200$ km s$^{-1}$ [[bibcode-2020apj-905-166l|bibcode:2020ApJ...905..166L]]. However, there remains an active tension between observations and theoretical models regarding the phase balance of these winds. While empirical data frequently show massive reservoirs of [[galactic-gas-dynamics-and-flows|cold molecular gas]] being entrained in AGN outflows, some high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations predict that AGN-driven winds are overwhelmingly composed of hot, diffuse gas with negligible cold atomic or molecular components [[bibcode-2020mnras-497-5292t|bibcode:2020MNRAS.497.5292T]], [[bibcode-2024arxiv240215240s|bibcode:2024arXiv240215240S]]. In intermediate-mass and massive systems, recent semi-analytic and numerical models (such as FEGA25) incorporate a dedicated "hot gas ejection" mode that selectively expels hot gas beyond the virial radius to solve persistent issues involving the overestimation of hot gas fractions in these halos [[bibcode-2025apjs-279-18c|bibcode:2025ApJS..279...18C]].

A critical consequence of multiphase outflows is the chemical enrichment of the CGM and the intergalactic medium (IGM). In dwarf galaxies, stellar feedback drives non-equilibrium fluctuations and ejects material with a higher metallicity than the ambient ISM, leading to characteristically low metal retention fractions [[bibcode-2019mnras-482-1304e|bibcode:2019MNRAS.482.1304E]]. This enrichment process is subject to complex theoretical interactions, such as the "feedback on feedback" effect, where early photoionization feedback during cosmic reionization suppresses the formation of low-mass galaxies, which subsequently limits the total volume of the IGM that can be enriched by subsequent galactic outflows [[bibcode-2007apj-662l-7p|bibcode:2007ApJ...662L...7P]]. Over cosmic time, supernova feedback acts as a foundational driver of IGM enrichment [[bibcode-2024apj-975-183o|bibcode:2024ApJ...975..183O]]. However, significant tension remains between modern cosmological simulations (such as EAGLE, TNG, and SIMBA)—which generally predict strong negative metallicity gradients at early times due to smooth stellar feedback models—and observations that reveal flatter or even positive metallicity gradients at $z = 1-4$ [[bibcode-2025apj-989-147g|bibcode:2025ApJ...989..147G]]. In more massive systems, AGN feedback—particularly in the mechanical or "radio" mode—creates collimated, bipolar outflows that deposit metal-rich, virial-temperature plasma deep into the IGM, a mechanism that has been shown to successfully reproduce the column density distributions of the low-redshift Ly$\alpha$ forest [[bibcode-2023apj-945l-17t|bibcode:2023ApJ...945L..17T]], [[bibcode-2024apj-975-183o|bibcode:2024ApJ...975..183O]].

Ultimately, the cumulative impact of stellar and AGN feedback fundamentally reshapes the baryon distribution around galaxies. By excavating gas from the central ISM and depositing it into the halo outskirts, feedback processes establish a characteristic "closure radius." Cosmological simulations demonstrate that this closure radius occurs at $R_c \sim 1.5-2.5 R_{200c}$, defining the vast spatial scale within which the universal baryon fraction of a given halo is finally recovered [[bibcode-2023mnras-524-5391a|bibcode:2023MNRAS.524.5391A]].

## Feedback Signatures: Negative versus Positive Feedback

The canonical view of feedback in galaxy evolution primarily emphasizes [[negative-feedback|Negative feedback]], wherein energy and momentum injected by supernovae and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|active galactic nuclei (AGN)]] heat or expel cold gas, thereby suppressing or quenching star formation. In the absence of such a [[global-star-formation-suppression-mechanism|Global star formation suppression mechanism]], cosmological simulations suffer from the "overcooling" problem, overpredicting the number of massive, highly star-forming galaxies. Observational evidence for this suppression includes the rapid destruction of molecular gas reservoirs. Spatially resolved studies reveal that molecular gas depletion occurs efficiently in the central kiloparsecs of AGN host galaxies, where gas fractions can be a factor of two lower than in inactive star-forming regions [[bibcode-2021mnras-505l-46e|bibcode:2021MNRAS.505L..46E]], [[bibcode-2009apj-690-1672s|bibcode:2009ApJ...690.1672S]]. However, the assumption that AGN feedback causes instantaneous, galaxy-wide quenching is currently challenged by observations showing a flat trend between star formation rates and AGN X-ray luminosity [[bibcode-2019mnras-486-4320r|bibcode:2019MNRAS.486.4320R]], with many luminous AGN residing in gas-rich, star-forming hosts [[bibcode-2020apj-901-42y|bibcode:2020ApJ...901...42Y]]. This tension suggests that negative feedback may require dynamical timescales to fully propagate and impact global star formation [[bibcode-2017apj-839-120w|bibcode:2017ApJ...839..120W]].

Conversely, the same outflows and shocks that drive gas expulsion can also over-pressurize dense molecular clouds, triggering starbursts through [[positive-feedback-astrophysics|Positive feedback (astrophysics)]]. When an AGN wind or jet impacts the multiphase interstellar medium, it compresses cold gas, pushing it over the threshold for gravitational collapse. Theoretical models predict that this triggered star formation can scale quasi-linearly with AGN luminosity, following relations such as $L_* \propto L_{\rm AGN}^{5/6}$ [[bibcode-2013mnras-433-3079z|bibcode:2013MNRAS.433.3079Z]]. Simulations show that moderate overpressurization can boost global star formation rates by an order of magnitude, often producing expanding rings or clumps of young stars at the periphery of outflow cones [[bibcode-2016mnras-455-4166b|bibcode:2016MNRAS.455.4166B]], [[bibcode-2017apj-844-37d|bibcode:2017ApJ...844...37D]]. While advanced integral field unit (IFU) diagnostics reveal that star formation and AGN outflows can spatially coexist—such as a star-forming ring surrounding the AGN bicone in the Seyfert galaxy NGC 5728 [[bibcode-2025apj-988-261z|bibcode:2025ApJ...988..261Z]]—direct observational evidence for triggered star formation is explicitly identified in systems subjected to stellar and AGN outflows. For example, stellar-driven positive feedback is observed in the prototypical Galactic bubble RCW 120, where the radiative and mechanical feedback of massive stars compresses gas to trigger starbirth [[bibcode-2021scia-7-9511l|bibcode:2021SciA....7.9511L]]. Similarly, AGN-driven positive feedback is observed in the starburst dwarf galaxy Henize 2-10 [[bibcode-2025arxiv251020897s|bibcode:2025arXiv251020897S]]. In these environments, energetic outflows create compressed gas regions where efficient cooling preserves high densities while radiating away thermal energy, yielding ideal conditions for starbirth [[bibcode-2025arxiv251020897s|bibcode:2025arXiv251020897S]]. Supporting this, statistical surveys reveal that radio-selected AGNs with pronounced jets can exhibit significantly higher star formation rates than purely X-ray-selected ones, strongly suggesting that jet-induced star formation is a key positive feedback channel [[bibcode-2013apj-774-66z|bibcode:2013ApJ...774...66Z]]. The implementation of positive AGN feedback is increasingly recognized as critical for theoretical frameworks; for example, semi-analytic models such as FEGA demonstrate that including positive feedback improves the modeling of primary scaling relations without requiring drastic alterations to existing negative feedback recipes [[bibcode-2024apjs-274-41c|bibcode:2024ApJS..274...41C]].

The coexistence of positive and negative feedback within the same galaxy highlights the complexity of [[multi-scale-feedback-processes|Multi-scale feedback processes]]. The fate of a gas cloud subjected to an outflow depends highly on local conditions and the ram pressure of the wind. High-resolution hydrodynamic simulations indicate a critical ram pressure threshold—approximately $2 \times 10^{-8} \mathrm{\, dyn \, cm^{-2}}$—above which cloud fragments acquire internal velocities too high to undergo gravitational collapse, stifling star formation entirely [[bibcode-2017apj-839-103d|bibcode:2017ApJ...839..103D]]. Below this threshold, or at the edges of the [[circumnuclear-environment|Circumnuclear Environment]] where the wind momentum is spread over larger volumes, compression-induced star formation can dominate. Ultimately, while localized positive feedback can significantly alter the morphology and immediate star formation rate of a galaxy, whether it or negative feedback dominates the long-term stellar assembly of the universe remains an active area of debate.

## Feedback Across Mass Scales: From Dwarfs to Clusters

The regulation of the cosmic [[star-formation-processes|star formation history]] relies on a mass-dependent transition in the dominant feedback mechanism. Cosmological simulations and empirical models demonstrate that a critical halo mass threshold of $M_{\rm halo} \approx 10^{12} M_\odot$ delineates the regimes where different feedback physics dominate. Without this transition, models systematically overpredict the baryonic mass and star formation efficiencies at both the low- and high-mass ends of the galaxy stellar mass function [[bibcode-2013mnras-436-3031v|bibcode:2013MNRAS.436.3031V]], [[bibcode-2014mnras-445-581h|bibcode:2014MNRAS.445..581H]].

In low-mass halos, [[stellar-feedback-and-winds|Stellar Feedback and Winds]]—driven by supernovae, radiation pressure, and photoionization—act as the primary regulators of star formation. The rapid, episodic injection of energy and momentum from massive stars regulates the Kennicutt-Schmidt relation and generates multiphase galactic outflows [[bibcode-2014mnras-445-581h|bibcode:2014MNRAS.445..581H]]. In dwarf galaxies, this localized feedback can drive severe non-equilibrium fluctuations in the galactic gravitational potential, which complicates [[internal-stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|dynamical mass modeling]] and heavily influences dark matter density profiles [[bibcode-2017apj-835-193e|bibcode:2017ApJ...835..193E]]. Furthermore, feedback-driven dynamical disequilibrium in dwarf galaxies can lead to errors of $\ge 10\%$ in circular velocity estimates, severely complicating the use of rotation curves to reliably constrain dark matter profiles [[bibcode-2025arxiv251211033d|bibcode:2025arXiv251211033D]]. In addition to internal processes, low-mass dwarf galaxies exhibit a distinct susceptibility to reionization, which acts as a powerful external feedback mechanism that severely impacts their star formation histories and overall metal content [[bibcode-2022natas-6-647c|bibcode:2022NatAs...6..647C]]. To address persistent dwarf galaxy anomalies (such as the cusp-core problem and overabundance of satellites), recent theoretical and observational work has increasingly investigated the role of [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|active galactic nuclei (AGN)]] in this low-mass regime. Observations of dwarf galaxies now confirm the presence of fast, AGN-driven ionized outflows with median velocities up to $\sim 240 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ and $W_{80}$ line widths reaching $\sim 1200 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [[bibcode-2020apj-905-166l|bibcode:2020ApJ...905..166L]], [[bibcode-2023apj-950-33a|bibcode:2023ApJ...950...33A]]. Recent comprehensive samples, such as the MaNGA AGN dwarf galaxies (MAD) survey, confirm that dwarf galaxies exhibit AGN-driven outflows with kinematics (reaching $W_{80}$ up to $566 \text{ km s}^{-1}$) that are surprisingly similar to those found in more massive galaxies [[bibcode-2025a-a-697a-235r|bibcode:2025A&A...697A.235R]]. This suggests that below the traditional mass threshold, AGN may still provide a viable and potent source of negative feedback [[bibcode-2018mnras-473-5698d|bibcode:2018MNRAS.473.5698D]].

Above $10^{12} M_\odot$, in [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], groups, and galaxy clusters, stellar feedback and alternative mechanisms such as thermal conduction or magnetic fields become energetically insufficient to halt cooling flows [[bibcode-2019mnras-487-4393s|bibcode:2019MNRAS.487.4393S]]. Instead, AGN feedback assumes the dominant role. In these massive systems, [[mechanical-feedback|mechanical (or "radio mode") AGN feedback]] injects tremendous kinetic energy into the intracluster medium (ICM) via relativistic jets. Indeed, a global accounting of energy budgets in the local universe demonstrates that radio AGN kinetic feedback dominates the total energetics for galaxies with $\log M_* > 10.6$, particularly driven by compact radio sources [[bibcode-2025arxiv250400090i|bibcode:2025arXiv250400090I]]. These jets inflate buoyantly rising X-ray cavities and drive shocks that offset radiative cooling losses [[bibcode-2012ara-a-50-455f|bibcode:2012ARA&A..50..455F]], [[bibcode-2014apj-782l-19b|bibcode:2014ApJ...782L..19B]]. This establishes a long-lived heating-cooling balance, providing the [[preventative-feedback|Preventative feedback]] necessary to maintain massive early-type galaxies on the red sequence. While contemporary feedback models can successfully reproduce local X-ray cluster scaling laws, they predict distinct evolutionary behavior compared to simplistic preheating models, particularly at higher redshifts ($z > 0.5$) [[bibcode-2010mnras-408-2213s|bibcode:2010MNRAS.408.2213S]]. Furthermore, the influence of this powerful AGN feedback is not strictly confined to the host galaxy. It can also impact neighboring galaxies within the same large-scale environment out to distances of up to 2 Mpc, producing both quenching and triggering effects in companion galaxies depending on their specific gas richness and mass [[bibcode-2025arxiv250708790d|bibcode:2025arXiv250708790D]]. The efficiency of this feedback redistributes baryons outward to a characteristic closure radius of $R_c \sim 1.5 - 2.5 R_{200c}$, severely depleting the baryon fraction within the inner halo [[bibcode-2023mnras-524-5391a|bibcode:2023MNRAS.524.5391A]].

Importantly, near the halo mass threshold, stellar and AGN feedback cannot be treated as independent, isolated processes. Modern hydrodynamical simulations incorporating [[multi-scale-feedback-processes|Multi-scale feedback processes]] reveal that these mechanisms are coupled non-linearly and can mutually weaken one another's global effects by up to an order of magnitude [[bibcode-2013natsr-3-1738b|bibcode:2013NatSR...3.1738B]]. Supernova-driven winds can efficiently eject or heat the cold gas reservoir necessary to fuel black hole accretion, effectively starving the AGN. Conversely, AGN-driven outflows can sweep away the dense gas required for nuclear starbursts, suppressing the subsequent generation of supernovae [[bibcode-2016mnras-463-1291i|bibcode:2016MNRAS.463.1291I]], [[bibcode-2024mnras-tmp-540c|bibcode:2024MNRAS.tmp..540C]]. Understanding the interaction between these channels remains critical for resolving the transition between stellar-dominated and AGN-dominated self-regulation.

## Co-evolution and Scaling Relations

Observations reveal a tight correlation between the mass of a supermassive black hole (SMBH) and the large-scale properties of its host galaxy, most notably the [[internal-stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|stellar velocity dispersion]], $\sigma$, of its central bulge. This $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ relation, alongside the broader SMBH-bulge connection, strongly suggests a co-evolutionary pathway where the growth of the black hole and the assembly of the stellar mass are intimately linked. Theoretical models generally invoke [[self-regulated-feedback-mechanisms|Self-regulated feedback mechanisms]] driven by [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN)]] as the physical basis for this connection. As the SMBH accretes gas, it injects energy and momentum into the interstellar medium via radiation, winds, and jets. Once the black hole reaches a critical mass, these feedback processes become sufficiently powerful to expel the remaining cold gas reservoir, terminating both star formation and further black hole accretion, thereby locking the system onto the observed scaling relations [[bibcode-2010iaus-267-341f|bibcode:2010IAUS..267..341F]], [[bibcode-2015ara-a-53-115k|bibcode:2015ARA&A..53..115K]]. Recent high-resolution cosmological simulations featuring [[multi-channel-agn-feedback|Multi-channel AGN feedback]] successfully reproduce the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ relation, reinforcing this feedback-driven paradigm [[bibcode-2024apj-973-149b|bibcode:2024ApJ...973..149B]]. The global impact of these self-regulating mechanisms is so profound that the overall stellar velocity dispersion function (VDF) of galaxies is highly sensitive to the strength of AGN feedback, particularly at high dispersions ($\sigma > 200$ km/s) [[bibcode-2025arxiv251204661c|bibcode:2025arXiv251204661C]].

However, the exact evolutionary tracks of galaxies in the $M-\sigma$ plane can vary based on the dominant feedback modes. For instance, jetted and non-jetted AGN may evolve differently: non-jetted AGN tend to grow diagonally upward in the $M-\sigma$ plane as both mass and dispersion increase together, whereas massive jetted AGN initially grow more rapidly in black hole mass with weaker effects on stellar dispersion (evolving upwards in the $M-\sigma$ plane). Over time, the feedback shifts to dramatically increase velocity dispersion while black hole growth slows, resulting in an extended horizontal evolutionary track [[bibcode-2023ojap-6e-27g|bibcode:2023OJAp....6E..27G]]. Furthermore, while powerful outflows are essential to reproduce the AGN luminosity function, some models propose that the fundamental scaling relations might also be partially imprinted by angular momentum loss mechanisms during gas accretion, rather than solely by energetic self-regulation [[bibcode-2006mmsai-77-678m|bibcode:2006MmSAI..77..678M]].

Beyond the central bulge, AGN feedback is essential for explaining global galactic scaling relations, including the phenomenon of "down-sizing," wherein the most massive galaxies formed their stars the earliest and quenched the most rapidly. Models incorporating robust AGN feedback successfully reproduce the observed color-magnitude bimodality, the $\alpha$-element enhancement of early-type galaxies, and the sharp drop in the specific star formation rate (sSFR) of massive halos at late times [[bibcode-2015mnras-448-1835t|bibcode:2015MNRAS.448.1835T]]. Without this feedback, simulations suffer from an "overcooling" problem, drastically overpredicting the number density of massive, highly star-forming galaxies at $z=0$ [[bibcode-2013mnras-436-3031v|bibcode:2013MNRAS.436.3031V]].

Despite the theoretical success of AGN feedback in globally regulating stellar mass, the relationship between ongoing AGN activity and immediate star formation quenching remains observationally complex. Several observational samples reveal a flat trend between AGN X-ray luminosity and host galaxy sSFR, indicating that AGN often reside in gas-rich, star-forming galaxies without showing signs of instantaneous [[negative-feedback|Negative feedback]] [[bibcode-2019mnras-486-4320r|bibcode:2019MNRAS.486.4320R]], [[bibcode-2022mnras-514-2936w|bibcode:2022MNRAS.514.2936W]]. Furthermore, galaxies exhibiting the strongest AGN-driven outflows frequently possess sSFRs comparable to typical star-forming galaxies, whereas those with weak or absent outflows show significantly suppressed sSFRs [[bibcode-2017apj-839-120w|bibcode:2017ApJ...839..120W]]. This tension suggests that feedback may act as a [[delayed-feedback|Delayed feedback]] mechanism, requiring a dynamical timescale (often tens to hundreds of millions of years) to visibly impact star formation on galactic scales. The net evolutionary impact is further complicated by the non-linear interaction between AGN and [[stellar-feedback-and-winds|Stellar Feedback and Winds]], which can mutually weaken each other's efficiency by disrupting the multiphase gas distribution before global quenching is achieved [[bibcode-2013natsr-3-1738b|bibcode:2013NatSR...3.1738B]].

## Computational Challenges and Subgrid Physics

Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations face an extreme dynamic range problem: while they track large-scale structure over hundreds of megaparsecs, the fundamental engines of feedback—such as supernovae (SNe) and black hole accretion disks—operate on scales of parsecs to astronomical units. Because resolving the multiphase [[galactic-gas-dynamics-and-flows|interstellar medium (ISM)]] across a cosmological volume remains computationally intractable, simulations rely on subgrid physics to model [[self-regulated-feedback-mechanisms|Self-regulated feedback mechanisms]].

Historically, simulations struggled with the "overcooling problem," where injecting SN energy purely as thermal energy proved highly ineffective. Because SNe explode in dense environments, subgrid thermal dumps radiate away their energy before doing any mechanical work on the gas. Modern high-resolution zoom-in suites, such as the FIRE (Feedback In Realistic Environments) project, address this by replacing single-mode prescriptions with explicit [[multi-scale-feedback-processes|Multi-scale feedback processes]]. FIRE demonstrates that regulating star formation requires radiation pressure, photoionization, and stellar winds to clear out dense giant molecular clouds *before* SNe detonate, thereby allowing [[mechanical-feedback|Mechanical feedback]] to couple efficiently to a lower-density ISM [[bibcode-2014mnras-445-581h|bibcode:2014MNRAS.445..581H]]. Even with advanced hydrodynamics, matching global constraints like the Kennicutt-Schmidt relation requires tightly calibrated stochastic thermal and mechanical prescriptions [[bibcode-2022apjs-262-9o|bibcode:2022ApJS..262....9O]]. In addition to established baryon-driven channels, exploring entirely novel physical mechanisms remains a frontier. For instance, recent theoretical work highlights the potential for dark matter interactions to ignite Type Ia supernovae in white dwarfs, creating an additional 'dark matter-induced' baryonic feedback channel that could further complicate the subgrid landscape [[bibcode-2023arxiv230908661a|bibcode:2023arXiv230908661A]].

On the scale of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] and clusters, models depend heavily on [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]]. Large-scale simulation suites—including IllustrisTNG, EAGLE, and SIMBA—typically bifurcate AGN feedback into a high-accretion "quasar/radiative" mode and a low-accretion "radio/kinetic" mode [[bibcode-2013mnras-436-3031v|bibcode:2013MNRAS.436.3031V]]. Novel theoretical frameworks, such as the MISTRAL model, attempt to bridge these gaps by employing stochastic bipolar momentum deposition to efficiently suppress star formation in massive galaxies while simultaneously supporting large-scale, radiatively driven outflows [[bibcode-2025mnras-543-967f|bibcode:2025MNRAS.543..967F]]. Moreover, exploring non-thermal components is crucial; varying the injection and transport parameters of cosmic ray feedback from AGN can lead to order-of-magnitude differences in the predicted properties of the circumgalactic medium [[bibcode-2025arxiv251211062g|bibcode:2025arXiv251211062G]]. 

However, the phenomenological nature of these models leads to significant tensions in their cosmological predictions. For example, while the isotropic kinetic wind mode in IllustrisTNG effectively quenches galaxies, it overpredicts the number density of [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|low-redshift]] Ly$\alpha$ forest absorbers at column densities $N_{\rm HI} < 10^{14}$ cm$^{-2}$. In contrast, the highly collimated, high-velocity jet feedback model in SIMBA reproduces the Ly$\alpha$ absorber column density distribution across five orders of magnitude [[bibcode-2023apj-945l-17t|bibcode:2023ApJ...945L..17T]]. Validating these models requires comprehensive multi-wavelength comparisons; for example, thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (tSZ) measurements of the circumgalactic medium around massive galaxies have proven highly effective at distinguishing between different AGN feedback models, such as differentiating between variants of the SIMBA simulation with and without feedback [[bibcode-2023apj-957-17g|bibcode:2023ApJ...957...17G]]. Additionally, tSZ radial profiles of galaxy groups have proven vital for distinguishing between various feedback models, with recent observations strongly favoring specific calibrated AGN formulations (e.g., the 'AGN 8.0' model) in cosmological simulations [[bibcode-2022epjwc-25700045t|bibcode:2022EPJWC.25700045T]]. Furthermore, comparative studies reveal that all major simulation suites struggle to simultaneously reproduce the observed number densities of both highly luminous ($L_{\rm x} \sim 10^{43-45}$ erg s$^{-1}$) and faint AGN at [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|high redshift]], often over-quenching or under-producing specific populations [[bibcode-2022mnras-509-3015h|bibcode:2022MNRAS.509.3015H]].

A major computational hurdle in validating these models is "resolution bias." In lower-resolution simulations, high-density, feedback-resistant gas clumps are artificially smoothed out by the computational grid or coarse particle masses. As a result, low-resolution implementations can overestimate the mass of gas ejected by AGN feedback by more than a factor of 10, incorrectly amplifying the feedback efficiency and producing unphysical clearing of the nuclear regions [[bibcode-2015mnras-453-1829b|bibcode:2015MNRAS.453.1829B]]. To better quantify these large-scale effects, theorists are introducing novel topological metrics; for example, Minkowski functionals are now used to characterize the geometric and topological imprints of feedback on megaparsec-scale gas fields, revealing that AGN jets primarily shape large-scale temperature and metallicity distributions [[bibcode-2024a-a-689a-311s|bibcode:2024A&A...689A.311S]].

Finally, modern theoretical frameworks increasingly recognize that [[stellar-feedback-and-winds|Stellar Feedback and Winds]] and AGN feedback do not operate in isolation. Treating them simultaneously reveals a highly non-linear, and often competitive, coupling. SNe feedback can disrupt smooth gas inflows, starving the central black hole and suppressing AGN activity, while AGN-driven winds can evacuate the nuclear gas reservoirs necessary to form massive stars. Consequently, rather than compounding additively, SNe and AGN feedback can mutually weaken each other's global quenching effect by up to an order of magnitude in intermediate-mass halos ($M_{\rm halo} \sim 10^{12} M_\odot$) [[bibcode-2013natsr-3-1738b|bibcode:2013NatSR...3.1738B]]. Incorporating these non-linear cross-talk effects remains an active frontier in subgrid model development. For instance, studies utilizing the CAMELS simulation suite highlight that the efficiency parameters governing stellar feedback can significantly delay the activation of AGN feedback, demonstrating a deep nonlinear interrelation between subgrid feedback modes [[bibcode-2025apj-980-61m|bibcode:2025ApJ...980...61M]].

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite widespread consensus on the necessity of feedback in galaxy evolution, the exact physical mechanisms governing energy transfer to the surrounding medium remain a major open question. A persistent theoretical and observational challenge is determining the kinetic coupling efficiency—the fraction of AGN bolometric luminosity converted into outflow kinetic power. Deriving this efficiency from observations requires significant assumptions, and comparing observationally derived kinetic coupling efficiencies directly to the subgrid parameters of cosmological models is fraught with systematic uncertainties [[bibcode-2018natas-2-198h|bibcode:2018NatAs...2..198H]]. Empirical estimates suggest that [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|AGN]] feedback must be highly efficient or possess large momentum loading factors to reproduce observed scaling relations, yet the underlying physical drivers remain debated [[bibcode-2018mnras-479-3189z|bibcode:2018MNRAS.479.3189Z]]. The role of black hole spin and magnetic fields in modulating the angular distribution and anisotropy of these outflows is also a critical frontier [[bibcode-2024a-a-690a-194b|bibcode:2024A&A...690A.194B]].

The literature currently exhibits tension regarding the timescale and net effect of AGN feedback on galactic [[star-formation-processes|star formation]]. While some models predict rapid, galaxy-wide quenching, several observational samples find a flat trend between the star formation rate and AGN X-ray luminosity, indicating that powerful AGN often coexist with unquenched, gas-rich host galaxies [[bibcode-2019mnras-486-4320r|bibcode:2019MNRAS.486.4320R]], [[bibcode-2020apj-901-42y|bibcode:2020ApJ...901...42Y]]. Resolving this discrepancy requires a better understanding of [[multi-scale-feedback-processes|multi-phase interactions]], such as how Ultra-fast outflows (UFOs) propagate through an inhomogeneous interstellar medium to ablate dense clouds and drive energy-conserving bubbles on kiloparsec scales [[bibcode-2013apj-763l-18w|bibcode:2013ApJ...763L..18W]]. On larger scales, the efficiency of energy dissipation within the intracluster medium depends highly on jet density and whether the jets are dominated by thermal gas, kinetic energy, or cosmic rays [[bibcode-2025arxiv251017518t|bibcode:2025arXiv251017518T]], [[bibcode-2026mnras-545f2060s|bibcode:2026MNRAS.545f2060S]]. Furthermore, there are open questions regarding the nonlinear coupling between different feedback channels. For instance, some studies suggest that [[stellar-feedback-and-winds|Stellar Feedback and Winds]] and AGN feedback mutually weaken each other's effects by up to an order of magnitude, complicating the interpretation of isolated feedback models [[bibcode-2013natsr-3-1738b|bibcode:2013NatSR...3.1738B]]. 

Upcoming and next-generation observatories are poised to provide definitive constraints on [[multi-channel-agn-feedback|Multi-channel AGN feedback]] across cosmic time. High-resolution JWST observations are already probing the kinematics of ionized gas and detecting starburst-driven outflows in sub-$L^\star$ galaxies at high redshift ($z > 4$), revealing that these early galaxies possess outflow velocities and a [[mass-loading-factor|Mass loading factor]] that make them highly efficient at removing baryons compared to their low-redshift counterparts [[bibcode-2025arxiv250117145s|bibcode:2025arXiv250117145S]]. Furthermore, JWST observations are uniquely capable of disentangling complex [[circumnuclear-environment|Circumnuclear Environment]]s and identifying extended Ly$\alpha$ nebulae indicative of anisotropic feedback at early epochs [[bibcode-2025arxiv250809749l|bibcode:2025arXiv250809749L]]. 

In the X-ray regime, next-generation facilities will offer unprecedented spatial resolution and sensitivity. The proposed AXIS mission, in particular, is expected to trace the interactions of winds and jets with the circumgalactic medium back to cosmic noon, while simultaneously identifying faint, high-redshift AGN populations that currently elude detection [[bibcode-2024univ-10-273r|bibcode:2024Univ...10..273R]], [[bibcode-2022mnras-509-3015h|bibcode:2022MNRAS.509.3015H]]. Together, these multi-wavelength constraints will help clarify whether feedback primarily operates through instantaneous ejection, [[preventative-feedback|Preventative feedback]] via halo heating, or complex cycles of wind recycling.
