The Tully-Fisher relation (TFR) is a foundational empirical scaling law in extragalactic astrophysics that links the intrinsic luminosity or stellar mass of a rotationally supported galaxy to its characteristic [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]]. First established as a tool to measure extragalactic distances, the relation is typically parameterized as a power law, $L \propto V^\alpha$, where the rotational velocity is conventionally extracted from the Doppler broadening of the 21 cm neutral hydrogen (H I) emission line or from spatially resolved [[rotation-curves|Rotation curves]]. Because it connects a distance-independent kinematic observable to an absolute luminosity, the TFR has historically served as a critical secondary distance indicator. It has been instrumental in the calibration of the cosmic distance ladder, the determination of the Hubble constant, and the mapping of large-scale peculiar velocity fields in the local universe [Sandage et al. (1976)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1976ApJ...210....7S/abstract). 

Beyond its utility in cosmography, the TFR provides profound constraints on the physical link between a galaxy's baryonic content and its embedding dark matter halo. The observed slope ($\alpha$) and scatter of the relation are highly wavelength-dependent; near- and mid-infrared bands yield a steeper, much tighter correlation than optical bands, as they are less affected by dust extinction and more accurately trace the underlying stellar mass rather than recent star formation. This correlation between mass and kinematics is most fundamentally expressed in the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] (BTFR), which utilizes the total baryonic mass (the sum of stellar mass and cold [[gas-fraction|gas mass]]). By accounting for interstellar gas, the BTFR extends the scaling law to [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] and [[h-i-rich-galaxies|H I-rich galaxies]], converging on a continuous sequence of $M_b \propto V^4$ [Verheijen et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563..694V/abstract), [McGaugh et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....143...40M/abstract). Notably, this fundamental relation has been shown to be remarkably independent of surface brightness and galactic size [Zwaan et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.273L..35Z/abstract).

The exact physical origin of the TFR remains one of the most heavily debated topics in galactic dynamics and cosmology. Within the standard Cold Dark Matter ($\Lambda$CDM) paradigm, the exceptionally tight scatter of the relation must emerge from complex, finely tuned baryonic feedback processes and the self-similar density profiles of dark matter halos regulating the stellar-to-halo mass relation [Mo et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000MNRAS.318..163M/abstract), [Ferrero et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...648A.124F/abstract). Conversely, the exact slope and minimal intrinsic scatter of the BTFR are predicted *a priori* by Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), making the relation a primary empirical test for alternative gravity theories [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106l1303M/abstract). This article explores the physical basis and multi-wavelength calibrations of the TFR, its extension into the BTFR, and its connection to other [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]]. It also reviews current open questions, including the remarkable stability of the stellar mass TFR over [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]], the physical drivers of intrinsic scatter, and ongoing efforts to unify the kinematics of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] with the Fundamental Plane of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]].

## Fundamental Definition and Physical Basis

The Tully-Fisher relation (TFR) is a fundamental empirical scaling law for rotationally supported galaxies that links a measure of the galaxy's baryonic content—typically intrinsic luminosity ($L$) or stellar mass ($M_\star$)—to its kinematics, parameterized by a characteristic [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]] ($V$). In its classic formulation, the relation takes the form of a power law:
$$ L = A V^\alpha $$
where $A$ is the zero-point and $\alpha$ is the [[logarithmic-slope|Logarithmic slope]]. While optical bands yield shallower slopes ($2.5 \lesssim \alpha \lesssim 3.4$), near- and mid-infrared calibrations reveal a steeper dependence approaching $\alpha \approx 4$, reflecting a tighter correlation with the underlying stellar mass rather than recent star formation [Masters et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....135.1738M/abstract), [Sorce et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....144..133S/abstract).

The physical basis of the TFR is rooted in the virial theorem, which dictates that for a system in dynamical equilibrium, the [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] $M_{\rm dyn}$ enclosed within a radius $R$ scales as $M_{\rm dyn} \propto V^2 R$. To map this dynamical scaling onto the observable luminosity-velocity plane, the relationship must incorporate the galaxy's total mass-to-light ratio ($\Upsilon = M_{\rm dyn}/L$) and its surface brightness or size distribution. A purely virial scaling would yield $L \propto V^4 / (I \Upsilon^2)$, where $I$ is the surface brightness. The fact that the TFR exhibits extremely tight scatter—independent of surface brightness for high- and low-surface-brightness disks alike [Zwaan et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.273L..35Z/abstract)—implies a finely tuned regularity in galaxy formation. Specifically, it requires systematic variations in the total mass-to-light ratio to cancel out surface brightness dependencies across different galaxies [Zaritsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ISRAA2012E..12Z/abstract).

The fundamental nature of this scaling becomes most apparent when extending the relation to the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] (BTFR). By accounting for the total baryonic mass $M_b = M_\star + M_{\rm gas}$ (combining stars and cold interstellar gas), the correlation becomes significantly tighter, particularly for low-mass, gas-dominated dwarf galaxies [Verheijen et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563..694V/abstract). The BTFR robustly converges on a slope of $M_b \propto V^4$ when utilizing the velocity measured along the flat portion of the galaxy's [[rotation-curves|Rotation curves]] [Lelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3267L/abstract).

Dynamically, the TFR is a manifestation of the disk-halo interaction. A galaxy's characteristic rotation velocity is not determined by the baryons alone but is heavily influenced by the gravitational potential of the embedding dark matter halo. Rotation curve decomposition reveals a self-similar radial profile of the baryonic-to-dark matter ratio across bright spirals [Kassin et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...643..804K/abstract). Furthermore, the "baryonic scaling model" can reproduce observed rotation curves across a variety of galaxy types using only two parameters, implying a remarkably tight local coupling between baryons and dark matter [Swaters et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.2299S/abstract). Reproducing the zero-point, slope, and scatter of the TFR requires a tight coupling between the baryonic disk mass and the halo's structural parameters, implying that both components contribute significantly to the maximum rotation velocity [Mo et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000MNRAS.318..163M/abstract). In addition, the relation can also be formulated as a "radial" Tully-Fisher relation that holds at different galactocentric radii, reinforcing that the rotation velocity is a robust measure of the gravitational potential throughout the entire optical disk [Yegorova et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.377..507Y/abstract).

The exact origin of the relation remains a subject of active debate in modern astrophysics. In the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological paradigm, the $V^4$ scaling and low scatter do not emerge trivially; they must arise as a consequence of halo abundance matching, the self-similar profiles of dark matter halos, and complex baryonic feedback processes that regulate the stellar-to-halo mass relation [Desmond et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1204.1497D/abstract), [Ferrero et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...648A.124F/abstract). Conversely, the precise $M_b \propto V^4$ slope and its extremely low intrinsic scatter are predicted *a priori* by Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), where the BTFR represents a direct consequence of modified gravitational laws at low accelerations rather than the presence of dark matter [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106l1303M/abstract), [McGaugh et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....143...40M/abstract). This article explores the physical basis and multi-wavelength calibrations of the TFR, its extension into the BTFR, and its connection to other [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]]. The literature remains sharply divided on whether the TFR constitutes proof of modified gravity or is a natural, albeit highly constrained, outcome of dissipative baryon collapse within cold dark matter halos [Keller et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835L..17K/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102499L/abstract).

At its core, the TFR is fundamentally linked to other empirical scaling relations. For instance, when mapped against the Fall relation—which connects stellar mass to specific angular momentum—ellipticals and spirals are found to follow parallel tracks with different offsets, underscoring differences in their morphological assembly histories [Romanowsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJS..203...17R/abstract). This connection between mass and kinematics also extends inward to the central galactic engines; supermassive black hole mass correlates strongly with the maximum rotational velocity ($M_{\rm BH} \propto V_{\rm max}^{10.6}$), suggesting a deep link between large-scale disk kinematics and central supermassive black hole growth [Davis et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877...64D/abstract).

## Historical Development and Distance Calibration

First introduced in the late 1970s, the TFR was quickly recognized as a powerful tool for distance calibration. Early formulations of the relation relied heavily on the width of the 21 cm H I emission line, which serves as a proxy for the depth of the gravitational potential well and the total [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] enclosed within the galaxy's disk [Sandage et al. (1976)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1976ApJ...210....7S/abstract).

Because it connects a distance-independent kinematic observable ($V_{\rm max}$) to an absolute luminosity, the TFR essentially serves as a customized "wattage rating" for individual galaxies. Once the zero-point is calibrated using local [[standard-candles|Standard candles]] such as Cepheid variables, the relation yields redshift-independent distances for spiral galaxies out to roughly 100 Mpc. The typical scatter in these derived distances is approximately 15-20% per galaxy. Historically, the TFR was a central pillar in the cosmic distance ladder, proving instrumental in resolving early debates over the value of the Hubble constant. Measurements shifted from initial estimates near $H_0 \approx 50 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$ [Sandage et al. (1976)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1976ApJ...210....7S/abstract) to modern consensus values in the range of $72-76 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$ [Freedman et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..673F/abstract), [Masters et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...653..861M/abstract), [Kourkchi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...896....3K/abstract). Early comparisons with Cepheid and SN Ia distances sometimes suggested that TFR distance moduli might be systematically too short by $\sim 0.4$ mag, particularly for galaxies with lower linewidths [Shanks et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997MNRAS.290L..77S/abstract). However, subsequent observations in rich environments like the Coma cluster established a tight $I$-band TFR scatter that was remarkably consistent with measurement error alone, which confidently placed upper limits on the peculiar line-of-sight velocities of gas-rich spirals at approximately $300 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Bernstein et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994AJ....107.1962B/abstract).

Beyond measuring the universal expansion rate, the TFR remains critical for mapping large-scale peculiar velocity fields. By subtracting the uniform Hubble flow from a galaxy's total observed radial velocity, astronomers use TFR-derived distances to measure the "peculiar" motions of galaxies falling toward massive overdensities or away from voids. Landmark programs such as the SFI++ survey and the 2MASS Tully-Fisher (2MTF) survey have successfully leveraged the TFR to trace bulk flows across the local universe [Masters et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...653..861M/abstract), [Masters et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....135.1738M/abstract). More recently, massive spectroscopic campaigns like the DESI Peculiar Velocity survey have continued this legacy, utilizing targeted optical fibers to measure velocities at specific, single off-center radii (e.g., $0.33$ or $0.4 R_{26}$) rather than full spatially resolved rotation curves, to efficiently extract highly precise TFR zero-points for thousands of galaxies [Douglass et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250711765D/abstract), [Douglass et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251203227D/abstract).

The accuracy of the TFR as a distance indicator depends strongly on the wavelength used to measure luminosity. Early calibrations in the optical (e.g., $B$-band) suffered from significant scatter due to dust extinction and the disproportionate luminosity contribution of young, massive stars. Modern distance calibrations have largely shifted to the near-infrared (e.g., $K$-band) and mid-infrared (e.g., 3.6 $\mu$m), which are less sensitive to dust and trace the underlying stellar mass much more robustly. As the wavelength increases, the power-law slope steepens, approaching $L \propto V_{\rm max}^4$ in the $K$-band [Masters et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....135.1738M/abstract), [Sorce et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....144..133S/abstract). This tightening of the relation ultimately motivated the development of the mass-based [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]], which incorporates both stellar and gas mass to provide an even tighter, more fundamental link between a galaxy's baryonic content and its dark matter halo.

## Kinematic Tracers and Velocity Definitions

The empirical calibration of the Tully-Fisher relation relies fundamentally on the accurate measurement of a galaxy's characteristic [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]]. This velocity scales with the total mass of the encompassing dark matter halo, but its extraction depends heavily on the choice of kinematic tracer and the specific parameterization of the rotation curve.

Traditionally, 21-cm emission from neutral hydrogen (H I) is the preferred kinematic tracer. Because H I gas typically extends far beyond the optical stellar disk, it effectively probes the dark matter-dominated outer regions of the galactic potential. For unresolved or marginally resolved sources, global H I profiles are used to extract line widths, such as $W_{50}$ (the line width at 50% of the peak flux), which provide an integrated measure of the rotation velocity [Verheijen et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563..694V/abstract), [Topal et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.3319T/abstract). Alternatively, spatially resolved [[rotation-curves|Rotation curves]] are derived from optical emission lines, most notably via H-alpha spectroscopy. Optical tracers are particularly crucial for intermediate and high-redshift studies (e.g., $z \sim 1$) where H I emission is either too faint to detect or suffers from severe beam smearing due to limited spatial resolution [Fernández Lorenzo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...521A..27F/abstract), [Miller et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741..115M/abstract), [Di Teodoro et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...594A..77D/abstract). Furthermore, molecular gas traced by CO emission has been successfully demonstrated as a reliable alternative kinematic tracer for star-forming galaxies, establishing consistent scaling relations at $z \le 0.3$ [Topal et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.3319T/abstract). However, in interacting systems, tidal disturbances can cause H I line widths to be significantly broader than their CO counterparts, potentially biasing kinematic measurements and affecting distance estimates if not properly accounted for [Tutui et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997A&A...326..915T/abstract).

The exact definition of the rotational velocity parameter dictates the measured slope, zero-point, and scatter of the resulting scaling relation. Common velocity definitions include:
*   **[[maximum-rotational-velocity|Maximum rotational velocity]] ($V_{\rm max}$):** This is the peak velocity reached by the rotation curve. While historically common, using $V_{\rm max}$ can introduce a characteristic "kink" or change in slope at the high-mass end of the relation (for velocities $V \gtrsim 200 \text{ km s}^{-1}$). This occurs because massive, early-type [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] often exhibit rotation curves that peak and then decline at large radii [Verheijen et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563..694V/abstract), [Noordermeer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.381.1463N/abstract).
*   **[[asymptotic-circular-velocity|Asymptotic circular velocity]] ($V_{\rm flat}$):** Measuring the velocity along the outer, flat portion of the rotation curve largely eliminates the systematic offsets seen in galaxies with declining curves. Substituting $V_{\rm flat}$ for $V_{\rm max}$ restores the linearity of the relation across a wider mass range [Verheijen et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563..694V/abstract), [Noordermeer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.381.1463N/abstract). The tightest empirical constraints on the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]], with an intrinsic orthogonal scatter of only $\sim 6\%$, are achieved when utilizing the mean velocity along the flat segment of the curve [Lelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3267L/abstract).
*   **Optical Radii Definitions ($V_{80}$):** In optical studies where the rotation curve may not conclusively reach a flat asymptote, velocity metrics are often evaluated at specific isophotal radii. A frequently used benchmark is $V_{80}$, defined as the rotation velocity evaluated at the radius containing 80% of the integrated optical light (e.g., in the $i$-band), which provides a stable kinematic metric minimizing the impact of inner central components [Pizagno et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AJ....134..945P/abstract), [Reyes et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.417.2347R/abstract).

The derived slope of the Tully-Fisher relation is highly sensitive to these kinematic definitions. Studies utilizing unresolved H I line widths typically find shallower slopes for the baryonic relation (around $\sim 3.3$), whereas measurements restricted strictly to resolved asymptotic rotation velocities yield significantly steeper slopes ranging from $3.85$ to $\sim 4.0$ [Bradford et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...11B/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3267L/abstract). This introduces a systematic uncertainty of up to $0.25$ dex in the power-law slope solely due to methodological choices, underscoring the critical need for commensurable velocity definitions when comparing observational catalogs against cosmological simulations [Bradford et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...11B/abstract).

## Multi-wavelength Properties and Wavelength Dependence

The slope and scatter of the Tully-Fisher relation (TFR) exhibit a pronounced wavelength dependence across the electromagnetic spectrum. Expressed in the power-law form $L \propto V^\alpha$, the [[logarithmic-slope|Logarithmic slope]] $\alpha$ becomes progressively steeper at longer observation wavelengths. In the optical regime, the slope is relatively shallow, with $\alpha \approx 2.5 - 3.0$ in the $B$-band. Moving toward the near-infrared (NIR), the relation steepens significantly, reaching $\alpha \approx 3.6$ in the $J$ and $H$ bands, and converging to $\alpha \approx 4.0$ in the K-band [Masters et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....135.1738M/abstract). When expressed in terms of absolute magnitudes, this corresponds to the slope steepening from roughly $-5.5$ mag per dex in the $g$-band to $-6.6$ mag per dex in the $z$-band [Pizagno et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AJ....134..945P/abstract), and approaching $-10$ to $-11$ mag per dex in the NIR [Verheijen et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563..694V/abstract), [Baes et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...696A..52B/abstract).

This chromatic variation in the TFR slope is driven primarily by the varying contributions of different stellar populations to the integrated luminosity, coupled with the effects of dust attenuation. Optical passbands are highly sensitive to recent star formation; consequently, the blue luminosity of a galaxy is strongly modulated by its current star formation rate rather than its total stellar mass. Because more massive, faster-rotating [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] tend to be systematically redder and possess older stellar populations, their optical luminosities do not scale as steeply with dynamical mass as their NIR luminosities [Gavazzi et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...419..469G/abstract), [Masters et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....135.1738M/abstract).

As a result, the NIR and mid-infrared (MIR) bands provide a much tighter correlation with lower intrinsic scatter. Observations in the $K$-band and at $3.6 \mu\mathrm{m}$ utilizing the Spitzer IRAC instrument minimize the effects of dust extinction and preferentially trace the old, low-mass stars that dominate a galaxy's stellar mass [Sorce et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....144..133S/abstract). This reduced sensitivity to recent starbursts ensures that the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|mass-to-light ratio]] remains relatively constant across a wide range of galaxy masses. Furthermore, MIR observations benefit from highly favorable backgrounds and exceptional photometric consistency across the sky, allowing for precise surface photometry even at low surface brightness levels [Sorce et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....144..133S/abstract).

The residuals of the optical TFR are found to correlate strongly with galaxy color and H$\alpha$ equivalent width, reflecting the fact that the optical scatter is largely driven by variations in star formation history rather than pure observational error [Kannappan et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002AJ....123.2358K/abstract). While applying a color-correction term to optical TFRs can artificially reduce this scatter to levels comparable with measurement errors, the preferred approach in modern cosmological distance measurements and galaxy evolution studies is to utilize the inherently tighter NIR/MIR bands. The stability of the $K$-band relation—which shows virtually zero intrinsic scatter in some tightly constrained samples [Verheijen et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563..694V/abstract)—suggests that the TFR fundamentally scales the dark matter halo potential with the total baryonic mass, a concept formalized in the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]]. Modern cosmological simulations incorporating radiative transfer successfully reproduce this observed steepening and tightening of the TFR from the ultraviolet through the MIR, confirming that wavelength choice is critical when applying the TFR as a dynamical scaling law [Baes et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...696A..52B/abstract).

## The Baryonic Tully-Fisher Relation (BTFR)

The Baryonic Tully-Fisher Relation (BTFR) is a fundamental scaling law that relates the total baryonic mass of a galaxy—the sum of its stellar mass and its neutral and molecular [[gas-fraction|gas mass]] ($M_b = M_* + M_{\mathrm{gas}}$)—to its characteristic [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]]. While the classical, luminosity-based Tully-Fisher relation tightly constrains massive spiral galaxies, it exhibits increased scatter and a shallower slope for [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] and dwarf irregulars. In these low-mass regimes, galaxies are heavily gas-dominated, and optical luminosity is no longer a reliable proxy for total mass. By incorporating the mass of the interstellar gas, the BTFR restores a tight, single power-law sequence across all mass scales, demonstrating that baryonic mass is more fundamentally linked to the dark matter halo potential than stellar mass alone [Verheijen et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563..694V/abstract).

The BTFR is generally parameterized as $M_b \propto V^\alpha$. The exact value of the logarithmic slope $\alpha$ depends subtly on the definition of the rotation velocity and the sample selection. Measurements utilizing the mean velocity along the flat part of the rotation curve ($V_{\mathrm{flat}}$) yield the tightest relation, with an orthogonal intrinsic scatter of approximately $6\%$ and a slope of $\alpha = 3.85 \pm 0.09$, though systematic uncertainties in velocity definitions can drive the slope anywhere from $3.5$ to $4.0$ [Lelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3267L/abstract). The relation holds remarkably well even for low surface brightness galaxies, which exhibit larger mass-to-light ratios and lower surface densities but lie on the identical BTFR sequence as high surface brightness spirals [Zwaan et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.273L..35Z/abstract). Furthermore, the BTFR is not strictly limited to late-type spirals; [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] equipped with regular extended H I disks are found to follow the exact same baryonic scaling sequence when their stellar mass-to-light ratios are robustly constrained via dynamical models [den Heijer et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...581A..98D/abstract). Moreover, joint BTFR fits using massive integral-field datasets like MaNGA across both spiral and elliptical galaxies yield a unified slope of approximately 3.5, which is broadly consistent with both $\Lambda$CDM simulations and MOND predictions for galaxy masses above $10^9 M_\odot$ [Ravi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251218577R/abstract).

Systems such as [[h-i-rich-galaxies|H I-rich galaxies]] serve as an optimal laboratory for calibrating the BTFR. Because their baryonic mass budgets are overwhelmingly dominated by gas rather than stars, their total masses are insensitive to the systematic uncertainties inherent in stellar population synthesis modeling. Observations of these purely gas-dominated systems independently converge on a slope of $\alpha = 4$, with an intrinsic scatter that is arguably consistent with zero once observational errors are accounted for [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106l1303M/abstract), [McGaugh et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....143...40M/abstract).

The physical origin of the BTFR remains the subject of active debate, presenting a significant tension between the cold dark matter ($\Lambda$CDM) paradigm and modified gravity theories. In $\Lambda$CDM, the BTFR arises from the complex interplay of halo abundance matching, the self-similar profiles of dark matter halos, and mass-dependent feedback mechanisms [Desmond et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1204.1497D/abstract). However, reproducing the remarkably low scatter and exact $M_b \propto V^4$ scaling across all mass regimes requires specific fine-tuning of a "feedback efficacy" parameter $\mathcal{E}$ to regulate star formation consistently across diverse halos [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....143...40M/abstract). Conversely, Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) predicts the exact BTFR slope of 4 and its absolute normalization *a priori* as a direct consequence of a modified force law below a critical acceleration scale $a_0 = 1.3 \pm 0.3 \ \mathrm{\AA \ s^{-2}}$ [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106l1303M/abstract), [McGaugh et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....143...40M/abstract). Regardless of the underlying gravitational framework, the BTFR functions as the fundamental plane of galaxy disks [Lelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3267L/abstract) and has been shown to be mathematically intertwined with other universal scaling laws, such as the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] [Wheeler et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...882...46W/abstract).

## Sources of Scatter and Secondary Parameters

While the Tully-Fisher Relation (TFR) is a remarkably tight scaling law, the observed intrinsic scatter—typically $\sim 0.3-0.4$ mag in optical and near-infrared bands—exceeds what can be attributed to observational measurement errors alone [Pizagno et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AJ....134..945P/abstract). This intrinsic scatter is primarily driven by variations in stellar [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|mass-to-light ratios]] ($\Upsilon_\star$) and differing fractions of dark to luminous matter within galaxy disks [Pizagno et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AJ....134..945P/abstract). Notably, recent constraints from the [[sparc-dataset|SPARC dataset]] indicate that photometric stellar mass estimates are systematically compatible with dynamical mass estimates, meaning they introduce minimal additional impact on the derived TFR slope or scatter [Marasco et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695L..23M/abstract). Furthermore, non-axisymmetric disk shapes—or intrinsic ellipticity—have been shown to contribute significantly to the observed TFR scatter, particularly for early-type spirals [Ryden et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...641..773R/abstract).

Residuals from the luminosity-based TFR correlate strongly with parameters that trace a galaxy's star formation history, such as $B-R$ color and H$\alpha$ equivalent width [Kannappan et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002AJ....123.2358K/abstract). Galaxies with bluer colors or active star formation tend to be overluminous for a given rotation velocity. Applying color-dependent corrections can reduce the TFR scatter to near-observational limits, effectively connecting the TFR with the [[color-magnitude-relation|Color-magnitude relation]] [Kannappan et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002AJ....123.2358K/abstract). Multiparametric TFRs for edge-on galaxies can further reduce this scatter down to $\sim 0.32$ mag by including the H I-to-optical luminosity ratio alongside optical colors [Makarov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.3373M/abstract). Such stellar population effects are largely mitigated by utilizing the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] or observing in near-infrared bands, where $\Upsilon_\star$ variations are significantly minimized [Verheijen et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563..694V/abstract).

The impact of a galaxy's radial extent and surface brightness on the TFR remains a subject of active debate. It is well-established that Low Surface Brightness (LSB) galaxies and High Surface Brightness (HSB) galaxies fall on the same mean TFR [Zwaan et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.273L..35Z/abstract), [Mo et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000MNRAS.318..163M/abstract). However, attempts to construct a multi-parametric "Fundamental Manifold" or "Fundamental Plane" for spiral galaxies by incorporating a radial scale length ($R$) have yielded conflicting interpretations. Some studies find that including size to form a 3D [[lvr-relation|LVR relation]] (e.g., $L \propto V^2 R$) can reduce the scatter of the relation by up to 50% [Han et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001PASJ...53..853H/abstract), drawing a bridge to the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] and internal velocity dispersion metrics [Tonini et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.3332T/abstract). Conversely, other analyses argue that TFR residuals are virtually independent of the half-light radius [Pizagno et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AJ....134..945P/abstract), and that the Baryonic TFR already serves as the ultimate "Fundamental Plane" for disks, with the addition of a radial parameter providing no extra predictive value [Lelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3267L/abstract), [Zaritsky et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....147..134Z/abstract). Theoretically, an anti-correlation between dark matter halo spin and concentration may naturally explain why TFR residuals and mass-size residuals are fundamentally uncorrelated [Posti et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...644A..76P/abstract). In stark contrast, traditional abundance matching models often predict a strong anti-correlation between TFR and mass-size relation residuals; because this conflicts with observations, it suggests that a requisite tight correlation exists between stellar surface density and enclosed dark matter to correctly reconcile such models with empirical facts [Desmond et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454..322D/abstract).

Scatter in the TFR is also highly sensitive to the exact definition of rotation velocity. Velocities measured along the flat portion of the rotation curve ($V_{\rm flat}$) yield a tighter relationship and eliminate systematic offsets seen when using the maximum velocity ($V_{\rm max}$) or integrated line widths [Verheijen et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563..694V/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3267L/abstract). Furthermore, the dynamic coupling between gas and stars varies with radius; kinematic measurements at $2 R_e$ show greater equilibrium and lower scatter than those in the inner regions ($\le 1.3 R_e$), suggesting that scatter is influenced by the degree of dispersional support from internal feedback or external mergers [Ristea et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.7438R/abstract). Additionally, the zero-point of the scaling relation between disk maximum velocity and central bulge velocity dispersion has been found to vary significantly with morphology and light concentration, with late-type bulgeless galaxies departing the most from the mean sequence [Ho et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...668...94H/abstract).

Finally, the analysis of secondary parameters is useful for contextualizing our own galaxy. While the Milky Way's mass and rotation velocity place it firmly on the standard TFR [Licquia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..220L/abstract), it is exceptionally compact. When mapped onto the 3D LVR relation or [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]], the Milky Way is found to be a significant outlier (up to $9.5\sigma$) due to a substantial deficiency in disk radius and angular momentum for its given velocity [Licquia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..220L/abstract), [Puech et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..287P/abstract).

## Cosmic Evolution and High-Redshift Observations

Observations of disk galaxies at high redshift ($z > 0.5$) provide critical constraints on the hierarchical assembly of galaxies and the co-evolution of baryonic matter with dark matter halos. Studies of the Tully-Fisher relation (TFR) over cosmic time reveal a stark dichotomy between the evolution of the luminosity-based TFR and the stellar mass-based TFR, reflecting the underlying [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]].

Because galaxies in the early universe generally had higher gas fractions and specific star formation rates, they were systematically brighter in rest-frame optical bands than their local counterparts at a fixed rotational velocity. Observations indicate a substantial brightening in the rest-frame $B$-band TFR, with the zero-point shifting by $\Delta M_B \approx 0.85 \pm 0.28$ magnitudes by $z \sim 1$ [Miller et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741..115M/abstract), [Fernández Lorenzo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...521A..27F/abstract). Simulations and semi-analytic models successfully reproduce this photometric brightening, attributing it to the elevated star formation rates characteristic of the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] at high redshifts [Portinari et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375..913P/abstract). 

In contrast to the optical luminosity TFR, the stellar mass TFR exhibits remarkable stability over the last 8 billion years. Deep spectroscopic surveys have found little to no significant evolution in the zero-point or slope of the stellar mass TFR out to $z \approx 1.2$ [Conselice et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...628..160C/abstract), [Miller et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741..115M/abstract), [Tiley et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.2166T/abstract). This invariant zero-point suggests that the buildup of stellar mass in disk galaxies tracks the growth of their dark matter halos in tandem. Furthermore, while the stellar mass TFR may exhibit mild evolutionary traces depending on sample selection, the total [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] exhibits no significant zero-point evolution since at least $z=0.6$, suggesting that the gas eventually converted into stars was already gravitationally bound to the galaxies' halos at these earlier epochs [Puech et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..277..138P/abstract). Remarkably, this stability appears to extend to even earlier epochs; recent observations have identified a giant, kinematically confirmed disk galaxy at $z = 3.25$ ($M_* \approx 3.7 \times 10^{11} M_\odot$) whose [[rotation-curves|rotation curve]] aligns precisely with the local TFR [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240917956W/abstract). Statistical samples out to $z \sim 3$ similarly suggest that the logarithmic slope and normalization of the relation are broadly independent of redshift, albeit with a characteristic break at $M_* \sim 10^{10} M_\odot$ that may reflect a mass-dependent stellar-to-halo mass ratio [Christensen et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.2599C/abstract).

Despite the stability of the mean stellar mass TFR, high-redshift studies consistently report a larger intrinsic scatter than is observed locally. The literature is currently divided on whether this scatter is predominantly observational or physical in origin. Observationally, obtaining spatially resolved kinematics at high redshift is exceptionally challenging; beam smearing and limited spatial resolution can artificially depress measured maximum rotational velocities and inflate the apparent scatter [van Starkenburg et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...450...25V/abstract), [Di Teodoro et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...594A..77D/abstract). However, there is strong evidence that a significant portion of the scatter is physical, driven by the hierarchical assembly process. Both major and minor mergers, which are more frequent at high redshift, perturb the morpho-kinematic stability of young disks, converting ordered rotation into velocity dispersion and scattering galaxies off the fundamental relation [Atkinson et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0712.1316A/abstract), [Puech et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488..876P/abstract). 

Finally, the evolution of the TFR intersects with debates surrounding morphological downsizing. There is ongoing tension regarding whether low-mass galaxies evolve at a different rate compared to high-mass systems. Some authors report that less massive galaxies display stronger evolutionary trends in their star formation efficiencies and kinematics [Ziegler et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006EAS....20..303Z/abstract), while others maintain that an invariant slope implies a uniform evolutionary pathway across the mass spectrum out to $z \sim 3$ [Christensen et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.2599C/abstract). As observational capabilities improve, resolving the mass-dependence of the high-redshift TFR remains a crucial test for models of disk galaxy formation.

## The Milky Way and M31 in Global Context

Placing our Galaxy and the Andromeda Galaxy (M31) into the broader context of external galaxy samples provides a critical test of whether our local environment, the Local Group, is representative of galaxy evolution. The Tully-Fisher relation, alongside related multiparametric laws such as the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]], serves as a primary benchmark for this comparison.

Historically, the status of the Milky Way as a typical Tully-Fisher galaxy has been a subject of debate. Early comparative studies suggested that the Milky Way was offset from the mean relation by $1\sigma$ or more, exhibiting a systematic deficiency in stellar mass, specific angular momentum, and disk radius for its observed asymptotic circular velocity, $V_{\rm flat}$ [Hammer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...662..322H/abstract), [Puech et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..287P/abstract). Under this interpretation, the Milky Way appeared unusually "quiet," having escaped any major merger events over the past $\sim 10$ Gyr, leaving it under-massive and deficient in angular momentum compared to a typical spiral of its rotational velocity [Hammer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...662..322H/abstract).

However, improved modern constraints on the global properties of the Milky Way have partially revised this picture. When evaluated with updated, robust estimates of its stellar and total baryonic mass, the Milky Way is found to be in excellent agreement with the standard luminosity-velocity and mass-velocity forms of the Tully-Fisher relation [Licquia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..220L/abstract). Its position on the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] falls neatly along the sequence defined by external [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]].

Despite this agreement in the mass-velocity plane, the Milky Way remains a significant outlier in its spatial extent. When considering the three-dimensional luminosity-velocity-radius scaling relation, the Milky Way is found to be unusually compact. Based on its luminosity and rotational velocity, empirical [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]] predict a disk scale length of $R_d \approx 5$ kpc. The observed scale length is approximately half this value, making the Milky Way a $\sim 9.5\sigma$ outlier in compactness relative to the general spiral galaxy population [Licquia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..220L/abstract). This compactness is also reflected in the vertical structure, where the Galaxy's [[thick-disks|thick disk]] appears thinner and less extended than expected from cosmological zoom-in simulations of similar-mass halos [Guerrette et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24c5002G/abstract). Furthermore, this compact size translates to a measurable deficiency in the specific angular momentum of the stellar disk compared to the general Fall relation for spiral galaxies [Hammer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...662..322H/abstract).

In contrast to the Milky Way, the Andromeda Galaxy (M31) aligns closely with the mean scaling relations across all parameters. M31 exhibits typical values for its stellar mass, disk scale length, angular momentum, and outer disk metallicity at its measured $V_{\rm flat}$ [Hammer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...662..322H/abstract). Its adherence to both the Tully-Fisher relation and the broader structural scaling laws suggests that M31 has experienced a more typical, merger-driven evolutionary history, whereas the Milky Way represents a subset of disks shaped by a uniquely quiescent assembly history.

## Theoretical Implications: CDM vs. MOND

The tight coupling between baryonic mass and asymptotic rotation velocity observed in the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] (BTFR) represents a critical benchmark for theories of galaxy formation. The origin of this scaling relation remains one of the primary theoretical battlegrounds between the standard dark matter paradigm and alternative theories of gravity. 

In the context of Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), the BTFR is not an emergent phenomenon but a direct, *a priori* prediction. MOND explicitly dictates a strict scaling law of the form:
$$M_b \propto V^4$$
where $M_b$ is the total baryonic mass and $V$ is the flat rotation velocity. This analytical result, often referred to as the [[milgrom-relation|Milgrom relation]], correctly predicted the slope and normalization of the BTFR long before precise measurements of gas-rich dwarf galaxies were available [Sanders et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990A&ARv...2....1S/abstract). Proponents of modified gravity argue that observations of gas-dominated galaxies fall precisely on this predicted relation and exhibit an intrinsic scatter that is consistent with zero, implying a single effective force law rather than the stochastic processes typical of galaxy formation [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106l1303M/abstract). Under this interpretation, the empirical [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] (RAR)—which couples the total observed centripetal acceleration to the acceleration inferred from baryons alone—is viewed as a fundamental law of nature [Lelli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102499L/abstract).

Conversely, within the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological framework, the Tully-Fisher relation emerges from the complex interplay of hierarchical halo assembly, gas accretion, and baryonic feedback. Early semi-analytic models and $N$-body simulations often struggled to reproduce the exact slope and minimal scatter of the relation, leading to persistent tensions. However, contemporary high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations have shown that the BTFR arises naturally through a tight stellar-to-halo mass relation and the self-similar density profiles of cold dark matter halos [Ferrero et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...648A.124F/abstract), [Desmond et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1204.1497D/abstract). From the $\Lambda$CDM perspective, the RAR is not indicative of new gravitational physics but is instead a natural consequence of the BTFR combined with the dissipative collapse of baryons within dark matter potential wells [Wheeler et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...882...46W/abstract), [Keller et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835L..17K/abstract).

The literature remains explicitly divided on whether the BTFR favors modified gravity or is a triumph of modern $\Lambda$CDM feedback models. Critics of the MOND interpretation note that claims of zero intrinsic scatter often conflict with rigorous treatments of observational uncertainties, and that the supposed failure of $\Lambda$CDM to predict the BTFR oversimplifies the highly non-linear, scale-dependent physics of galactic feedback [Foreman et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvL.108n1302F/abstract). Nevertheless, to successfully reproduce the observed zero-point and tight scatter in $\Lambda$CDM, strict constraints are required: the concentration of galactic-scale dark matter halos must remain relatively low and roughly independent of the halo circular velocity [Mo et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000MNRAS.318..163M/abstract). Furthermore, feedback efficacy must tightly modulate the condensed baryon fraction across galaxies to prevent excessive scatter [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....143...40M/abstract).

Finally, the TFR serves as a stringent constraint on alternative dark matter candidates, such as Self-Interacting Dark Matter (SIDM). While velocity-dependent SIDM models are frequently invoked to resolve small-scale cosmological anomalies, they allow dark matter to thermalize and accumulate in the central regions of massive galaxies. This produces steeper inner density profiles than the adiabatic contraction expected in standard CDM, causing the predicted TFR slope to deviate significantly from observational data [Correa et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240309186C/abstract). Consequently, the remarkably tight slope of the TFR continues to act as one of the most restrictive empirical filters for evaluating both the properties of dark matter and proposed modifications to Newtonian dynamics.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite decades of extensive application and theoretical study, the physical origins of the Tully-Fisher relation (TFR) and its exact universality remain subject to active debate. Upcoming large-scale surveys and advanced simulations aim to resolve several outstanding tensions regarding the coupling of dark and baryonic matter across cosmic time.

A primary theoretical tension lies in explaining the physical driver of the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] (BTFR). The exceptionally tight correlation and the specific logarithmic slope of 
$$M_b \propto V^4$$ 
are direct, *a priori* predictions of Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106l1303M/abstract). In the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological paradigm, this precise scaling must instead emerge naturally from halo abundance matching and complex subgrid physics, particularly stellar feedback and baryon dissipation [Desmond et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1204.1497D/abstract), [Keller et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835L..17K/abstract). Using the [[sparc-dataset|SPARC dataset]], recent work has shown that the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] is intrinsically linked to the BTFR [Wheeler et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...882...46W/abstract), though it remains contested whether this tight coupling constitutes evidence for new dynamical laws or is simply an inevitable outcome of dissipative collapse in dark matter halos [Foreman et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvL.108n1302F/abstract), [Keller et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835L..17K/abstract). Additionally, recent work exploring the 'Bosma effect' suggests that the TFR may be intrinsically linked to a tight correlation between the underlying dark matter and gas distributions, allowing for the construction of a normalized 'Bosma TF' relation that holds purely empirically [Sylos Labini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.2697S/abstract). Furthermore, simulations suggest that alternative dark matter models, such as self-interacting dark matter (SIDM), could cause observable deviations in the TFR slope due to core thermalization and dark matter accumulation in the central regions of massive galaxies [Correa et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240309186C/abstract).

Observationally, the linearity of the relation across all mass scales has historically been a topic of scrutiny. Early studies reported a "kink" or shallowing in the optical TFR slope at the high-mass end ($V > 200 \text{ km s}^{-1}$). However, this break is now largely understood to be an artifact of kinematic and photometric definitions; the change in slope largely disappears when using the asymptotic flat rotation velocity ($V_{\rm flat}$) instead of maximum rotation velocity ($V_{\rm max}$), and any remaining deviations vanish completely when total baryonic mass is used in place of optical luminosity [Noordermeer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.381.1463N/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3267L/abstract).

The role of environment also requires tighter constraints. While the TFR is generally robust across different cosmic web environments, the exact impact of local environment is still debated. Some studies show that galaxies residing in the dense cores of Galaxy clusters can exhibit truncated H I rotation curves or line-width anomalies due to ram-pressure stripping, complicating their use as distance indicators [Stauffer et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986AJ.....91.1286S/abstract). In contrast, other extensive studies of cluster samples have found no systematic environmental dependence for TFR residuals, colors, or the shape and extent of rotation curves [Dale et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AJ....120..552D/abstract). Consequently, considerable theoretical effort is being directed toward unifying the TFR with the Fundamental Plane of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] to formulate a single set of [[astrophysical-mass-scaling-relations|Astrophysical Mass Scaling Relations]] capable of describing all stellar dynamical systems regardless of morphology [Zaritsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ISRAA2012E..12Z/abstract), [Ferrero et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...648A.124F/abstract). Broader efforts to unify these galaxy scaling laws include concepts like the 'Galaxy End Sequence', which argues for a fundamental continuity between early-type and late-type galaxies lying along a strongly curved main sequence [Eales et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.3125E/abstract).

Looking ahead, resolving these questions demands highly complete, unbiased kinematic samples. Next-generation radio facilities, notably the Square Kilometer Array and its pathfinders such as the WALLABY survey, are currently refining the local velocity field by extracting H I kinematics to produce thousands of new TFR distances [Mould et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533..925M/abstract). Future surveys will increasingly employ Bayesian stacking techniques to measure the BTFR for galaxies that fall well below nominal H I detection thresholds, thereby extending the empirical relation to the lowest mass regimes at progressively higher redshifts [Pan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508.1897P/abstract). Capitalizing on these vast datasets will require advanced statistical frameworks, such as inclination-free maximum likelihood estimators, which have demonstrated the ability to recover TFR parameters accurately even when inclination angles are entirely unknown [Obreschkow et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777..140O/abstract). Concurrently, deep integral-field spectroscopic surveys are probing [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] to answer why the stellar mass TFR has maintained a remarkably stable zero-point over the last $\sim 8$ Gyr, even as the disk galaxy population exhibits significant rest-frame optical luminosity evolution over the same epoch [Miller et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741..115M/abstract), [Tiley et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.2166T/abstract).
