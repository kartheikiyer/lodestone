In extragalactic astrophysics, galaxy scaling relations are empirical power-law correlations that link the fundamental macroscopic [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] of galaxies, such as their total luminosity, characteristic radius, kinematic velocity, and metallicity. Rather than being mere phenomenological coincidences, these [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] arise from the basic requirements of dynamical equilibrium—specifically the virial theorem—acting upon self-gravitating systems [White et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997gsr..proc....3W/abstract). The most prominent examples include the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] for rotationally supported late-type disks [Courteau et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.10440C/abstract) and the Fundamental Plane for dispersion-supported early-type galaxies. When expressed in observable photometric terms, the theoretically expected geometric power laws are often "tilted." These deviations are primarily driven by systematic variations in the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]], which reflect changing stellar population demographics, initial mass functions, and gas fractions as a function of total galaxy mass.

Within the broader context of cosmology and structure formation, these empirical laws provide critical benchmarks for [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]] and our understanding of [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|Galaxy and Universe evolution]]. In the standard hierarchical assembly paradigm, baryonic matter cools and condenses within the gravitational potential wells of dark matter halos. Consequently, [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]] and the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] intrinsically link the observable properties of stars and gas to the unobservable physical parameters of the host halo, such as its total mass, spin, and concentration. The universality of these underlying dynamics extends to the largest bound structures in the universe, driving [[galaxy-cluster-properties-and-scaling-relations|Galaxy Cluster Properties and Scaling Relations]] that connect total cluster mass to observable X-ray and thermal emission. Furthermore, the spatial distribution of the galaxies themselves follows well-defined statistical [[universal-clustering-law|clustering laws]], though the exact transition scale at which these fractal-like structural scaling laws give way to large-scale cosmic [[homogeneity-scale|homogeneity]] remains a foundational topic of analysis [Fall et al. (1979)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1979RvMP...51...21F/abstract), [Efstathiou et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983FCPh....9....1E/abstract), [Guzzo et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...382L...5G/abstract).

This article explores the physical mechanisms, historical development, and modern statistical methodologies utilized to study galaxy scaling relations. It covers the major kinematic, structural, and chemical scaling laws across the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]], tracing their redshift evolution from "cosmic noon" to the local Universe and examining how environmental factors modulate them via the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]]. Crucially, the text details how advanced statistical frameworks, such as [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]], are used to separate observational noise from true [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]], thereby isolating the astrophysical variance that theories must explain. Finally, the article outlines ongoing tensions and open questions in the field, including whether [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf]] and giant galaxies follow unified or divergent structural sequences, the precise role of active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback in breaking cluster self-similarity, and persistent local anomalies such as the Milky Way's extreme structural compactness relative to standard extragalactic scaling laws.

## Introduction and Physical Foundations

Galaxies exhibit a variety of tight, empirical power-law correlations that link their fundamental structural and kinematic [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]], such as total luminosity, effective radius, and characteristic velocity. These [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]]—most notably the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] for rotation-supported disks and the Fundamental Plane for pressure-supported ellipticals—serve as essential benchmarks for theories of galaxy formation and evolution [White et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997gsr..proc....3W/abstract). Rather than being mere phenomenological coincidences, these relations are the macroscopic imprint of the virial theorem acting on gravitationally bound systems in equilibrium, modulated by the hierarchical assembly of dark matter halos [Navarro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ASSP...56..103N/abstract).

The physical foundation of all [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|stellar dynamical scaling relations]] is the virial theorem. For a self-gravitating system in a relaxed dynamical state, the dynamical mass $M_{\rm dyn}$ is related to a characteristic radius $R$ and a characteristic velocity $V$ (which may represent rotational velocity or velocity dispersion) via:
$$M_{\rm dyn} \propto R V^2$$
To connect this underlying dynamical reality to observable photometric properties, one introduces the galaxy's luminosity $L$ and the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]], denoted $\Upsilon = M_{\rm dyn}/L$. Substituting mass for average surface brightness $I \propto L/R^2$ yields the general structural scaling:
$$R \propto V^2 I^{-1} \Upsilon^{-1}$$
This basic derivation demonstrates that all galaxies must populate a well-defined geometric plane in the three-dimensional parameter space of $R$, $V$, and $I$ [Zaritsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ISRAA2012E..12Z/abstract).

In practice, observed [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]] often exhibit a "tilt," meaning their empirical power-law exponents deviate from pure geometric virial predictions (for instance, observing $L \propto V^{3.5-4}$ instead of $L \propto V^3$). This tilt is generally attributed to systematic variations in the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]] across the galaxy population [Cappellari et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..311...20C/abstract). These variations arise from differences in [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|stellar population]] parameters—such as age, metallicity, and the initial mass function—as well as the changing fraction of dark matter within the probed effective radius [Zaritsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ISRAA2012E..12Z/abstract). When observational data are recast in terms of stellar or dynamical mass rather than luminosity, the relations tighten considerably. This suggests the existence of a unified mass plane that seamlessly connects dwarf galaxies, giant ellipticals, and massive star-forming disks into a single continuous framework [Zaritsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ISRAA2012E..12Z/abstract), [Bezanson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799..148B/abstract). 

In the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological model, these relations are intrinsically tied to the properties of their host dark matter halos. Dark matter halos assemble hierarchically and exhibit self-similar mass profiles alongside tight theoretical scaling relations between [[galaxy-halo-scales|halo mass, virial radius, and maximum circular velocity]] [Navarro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ASSP...56..103N/abstract). As baryons dissipate energy, cool, and settle into the centers of these halos, they retain a fraction of the halo's angular momentum, translating the halo's underlying structural scaling onto the visible galaxy [White et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997gsr..proc....3W/abstract). 

While modern cosmological hydrodynamical [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]] successfully reproduce the general slope and low [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] of these relations, the literature notes historical and ongoing tensions. For example, early pure cold dark matter simulations predicted excessively high central mass concentrations for halos, creating a tension with the observed zero-point of the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] [Steinmetz et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..197..165S/abstract). Furthermore, the degree to which secondary halo parameters, such as spin and concentration, drive the intrinsic scatter of these relations remains an area of active debate and modeling [Posti et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...644A..76P/abstract).

## Historical Development and the Hubble Sequence

The historical development of galaxy scaling relations traces a conceptual shift from qualitative morphological taxonomy to quantitative dynamical laws. Early extragalactic astronomy relied heavily on visual classifications, organizing galaxies along the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]]. During the formative years of extragalactic studies, there was even historical skepticism regarding the functional form of universal expansion laws; for instance, [[non-parametric-methods-in-astrophysics|non-parametric analyses]] of [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|complete galaxy samples]] once suggested that quadratic redshift-distance power laws might provide a better empirical fit than the strictly linear Hubble law [Segal et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986PNAS...83.7129S/abstract). While originally descriptive, it became apparent that fundamental physical [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]]—such as luminosity, size, total mass, gas content, and color—vary systematically along this sequence, a phenomenon often described as [[galactic-bimodality|Galactic Bimodality]]. Early theoretical frameworks mapped galaxies across a two-dimensional parametrization of [[galaxy-central-density|surface density]] and [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|velocity dispersion]], leading to the hypothesis that [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxies]] serve as fossil links to primordial density fluctuations [Silk et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983Natur.301..574S/abstract).

By the 1970s and 1980s, these trends were codified into the so-called Classical relations. For rotation-supported disks, the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] established a tight power-law linking luminosity $L$ and maximum rotational velocity $V$, yielding observed virial relations of approximately $V \propto L^{0.31}$ and a corresponding disk size scaling of $R \propto L^{0.33}$ [Courteau et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.10440C/abstract). For dispersion-supported early-type galaxies, the Faber-Jackson relation was generalized into the Fundamental Plane (sometimes related to the [[d-sigma-relation|D-sigma relation]]), which demonstrated that ellipticals occupy a tight two-dimensional manifold within the parameter space of effective radius $R_e$, central velocity dispersion $\sigma_0$, and mean surface brightness. 

As observational techniques advanced, the strict boundaries of the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] were supplemented by continuous [[galaxy-structure|structural parametrizations]], particularly through Sérsic fitting. The Sérsic index $n$ provides a quantitative measure of stellar [[galaxy-central-density|concentration]] that correlates strongly with galaxy luminosity and mass; faint dwarf ellipticals exhibit near-exponential profiles, while the most massive early-type galaxies achieve highly concentrated profiles, reflecting the profound structural and [[galaxy-morphology-and-alignment|morphological evolution]] that galaxies undergo over cosmic time [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). 

This structural quantification introduced new complexities into the interpretation of scaling laws. For example, differing linear slopes in the early-type [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]]—where $R_e \propto M_*^{0.54}$ for [[bright-galaxies|giant ellipticals]] but flattens to $R_e \propto M_*^{0.30}$ for dwarfs [Chiosi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1206.2532C/abstract)—sparked a longstanding debate over whether dwarf and giant galaxies form via fundamentally distinct physical channels. However, recent analyses suggest this apparent dichotomy is a mathematical artifact of fitting linear models to inherently curved parameter spaces, and that dwarfs and giants actually form a single continuous structural sequence [Graham et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....48..231G/abstract).

Today, these scaling relations serve as the primary observational benchmarks for [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]] [Dutton et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..463D/abstract). While hierarchical cosmological models naturally predict the power-law nature of these relations, reproducing their exact zero-points and minimizing scatter remains challenging. Resolving these tensions requires a synthesis of rapid [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|hierarchical assembly]] and secular evolution—where internal dynamical mechanisms slowly transform a galaxy's morphology and redistribute angular momentum over its lifetime [Zhang et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004IAUS..220..417Z/abstract). Ultimately, in these unified models, apparent "tilts" or deviations from pure virial expectations are driven by systematic variations in the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]] resulting from age, metallicity, and the initial mass function [Zaritsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ISRAA2012E..12Z/abstract).

## Scaling Relations of Late-Type Galaxies

Late-type (spiral and gas-rich) [[galaxy-populations|Galaxy populations]] exhibit remarkably tight correlations between their luminosity ($L$), rotation velocity ($V$), and characteristic radius ($R$). The most prominent of these is the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] (TFR), which links the total luminosity or stellar mass (see [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]]) of a rotation-supported disk galaxy to its maximum or [[asymptotic-circular-velocity|asymptotic circular velocity]]. Observationally, the relation takes a power-law form:
$$L = A V^\alpha$$
where the slope $\alpha$ typically ranges between 2.5 and 4 depending on the photometric band. In the near-infrared and optical (e.g., $I$-band), the kinematic and spatial scaling relations follow roughly $V \propto L^{0.31}$ and $R \propto L^{0.33}$ [Courteau et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.10440C/abstract). 

The size-luminosity relation traditionally relies on the exponential disk scale length, but using isophotal radii has been shown to yield significantly tighter correlations within the [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] framework [Saintonge et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...726...77S/abstract). The [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] in these scaling relations provides stringent constraints on [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]]. Notably, the residuals of the size-luminosity and Tully-Fisher relations are largely uncorrelated [Courteau et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...671..203C/abstract). This lack of correlation is theoretically challenging but may be explained if secondary parameters, such as [[galaxy-halo-scales|dark matter halo spin and concentration]], are anti-correlated [Posti et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...644A..76P/abstract). [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Bayesian frameworks]] analyzing non-linear error propagation further confirm that the intrinsic scatter of relations like stellar mass to velocity is fundamentally driven by underlying astrophysics. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that TFR parameters can be accurately recovered using maximum likelihood methods even without prior knowledge of [[galaxy-inclination|Galaxy inclination]] [Obreschkow et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777..140O/abstract).

Investigations into the dark matter scaling laws of late-type galaxies further reveal that less luminous galaxies tend to host dark matter halos with smaller [[core-radius|core radii]] and higher [[galaxy-central-density|central densities]] [Kormendy et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ASPC...10...33K/abstract). To account for the significant [[galaxy-mass-fractions|Galaxy mass fractions]] of cold gas in these systems, the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] (BTFR) replaces stellar luminosity with the total baryonic mass. Observations indicate that while the stellar mass TFR evolves in its zero-point and scatter, the BTFR exhibits virtually no zero-point evolution over the past 6 Gyr. This implies that the gas reservoirs later converted into stars—tracked via the [[stellar-luminosity-function|Stellar luminosity function]]—were already gravitationally bound to the galaxy potentials at $z \sim 0.6$ [Puech et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..277..138P/abstract).

Furthermore, the Milky Way itself presents a notable anomaly within the multi-dimensional [[lvr-relation|luminosity-velocity-radius]] parameter space. While it adheres to the standard TFR, the Milky Way is significantly more compact than extragalactic counterparts, appearing as a $\sim 9.5\sigma$ outlier [Licquia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..220L/abstract). Compounding these anomalies are the systematic difficulties in using kinematic distances to determine the Milky Way's own [[rotating-galaxy-models|spiral structure]] [Benjamin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..387..375B/abstract).

## The Fundamental Plane and Early-Type Galaxies

Early-type galaxies—comprising ellipticals and lenticulars, whose distribution is described by the [[early-type-fraction|Early-type fraction]]—exhibit remarkably tight correlations among their photometric, kinematic, and structural [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]]. The earliest recognized of these is the Faber-Jackson relation, which links a galaxy's total luminosity to its central velocity dispersion. 

The definitive structural manifold for early-type systems is the Fundamental Plane (FP). In the three-dimensional space defined by the [[galaxy-structure|effective half-light radius]] ($R_e$), central [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|velocity dispersion]] ($\sigma$), and mean surface brightness ($\langle I \rangle_e$), elliptical galaxies occupy a tightly constrained plane described by the equation:
$$ \log R_e = a \log \sigma + b \log \langle I \rangle_e + c $$
The observed deviation from these theoretical expectations is driven by systematic variations in the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]] as a function of galaxy mass [Cappellari et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..311...20C/abstract). 

Substituting stellar mass for luminosity yields the Mass Fundamental Plane. Observations reveal that both massive quiescent galaxies and star-forming galaxies lie on nearly the identical Mass FP, maintaining a tight [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] of $\sim 0.055$ dex [Bezanson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799..148B/abstract). The stability of this plane across cosmic time suggests that [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]] are highly robust against [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]]. Efforts to completely unify [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] scaling laws have demonstrated that all stellar dynamical systems—from giant ellipticals to [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]]—can be mapped onto a single universal scaling relation [Zaritsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ISRAA2012E..12Z/abstract).

Within the context of the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]], early-type galaxies exhibit strong mass-dependent scaling. Linear fits once suggested a physical dichotomy: [[bright-galaxies|giant ellipticals]] follow a steep scaling, while dwarf ellipticals exhibit a shallower slope [Chiosi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1206.2532C/abstract). However, recent literature leans toward a unified model where dwarf and bright ellipticals belong to a single continuous sequence, with the perceived discontinuity resulting from varying [[galaxy-central-density|stellar concentrations]] [Graham et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....48..231G/abstract). At the extreme compact end, it has been discovered that ultra-compact dwarf galaxies and nuclear clusters exhibit unique scaling properties likely resulting from their formation via [[merger-rate-density|multiple star cluster mergers]] [Bekki et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...610L..13B/abstract).

Despite this overarching uniformity, notable structural outliers test the boundaries of these relations. Early-type galaxies in the early universe are observed to be a factor of 2 to 5 times more compact than their local equivalents [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). Furthermore, ongoing local merger events can produce highly compact dwarf outliers that possess [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|Unusually high specific star formation rates]] and are actively evolving toward the canonical early-type scaling relations [Nidhi Chhatkuli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250409801N/abstract).

## Baryonic Cycling and Chemical Scaling Laws

The continuous interplay of gas accretion, star formation, and feedback—collectively known as baryonic cycling—drives the [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|chemical and structural evolution]] of galaxies. The efficiency of these processes is empirically captured by relations linking [[galaxy-properties|stellar mass]], star formation rate, gas mass, and metallicity. Globally, the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] tightly correlates [[global-star-formation-rate|SFR]] and stellar mass for rotationally supported disk galaxies. On local scales, star formation efficiency is governed by the [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]], which relates the surface density of star formation to the gas surface density. While high surface brightness spirals readily convert their gas into stars, [[low-luminosity-galaxies|low surface brightness galaxies]] deviate from these standard efficiencies; despite hosting massive reservoirs of [[h-i-mass-function|atomic hydrogen]], they exhibit exceptionally long gas depletion times [Christie et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250920080C/abstract).

The chemical enrichment resulting from baryonic cycling is encoded in the mass-metallicity relation, a subset of [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]]. Principal component analyses—often treated with [[non-parametric-methods-in-astrophysics|Non-parametric methods in astrophysics]]—indicate that this phase space is intrinsically two-dimensional [Ginolfi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...638A...4G/abstract). When SFR is explicitly included, galaxies lie on a tight "Fundamental Plane of Metallicity." Even extreme populations, such as [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|low-metallicity starbursts]], conform to this plane [Hunt et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.427..906H/abstract). There remains active debate regarding whether this fundamental plane undergoes [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|intrinsic evolution]] over cosmic time.

Advances in integral field spectroscopy have demonstrated that global chemical scaling relations often originate from localized, sub-kpc physical processes. At the centers of nearby galaxies, a strong correlation exists between the local [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|stellar velocity dispersion]] ($\sigma$) and stellar metallicity [da Silva et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...702A..94D/abstract). The detailed mechanisms of gas transport also leave localized chemical signatures in [[mass-weighted-stellar-populations|nuclear stellar populations]]. Beyond stellar and gas populations, baryonic cycling is linked to central supermassive black holes, where nuclear optical luminosity and black hole mass are linked through the [[galaxy-hlqso-cross-correlation-function|Galaxy-HLQSO cross-correlation function]]. Additionally, nuclear optical luminosity scales with [[x-ray-scaling-relations|X-ray and radio emission]] [Salvato et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...600L..31S/abstract). Finally, theoretical models predict robust scaling relations for galactic magnetic fields, linking field strength to [[radial-astrophysical-profiles-and-dynamics|local gas density and rotation curves]] [Chamandy et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966...78C/abstract).

## Galaxy Cluster and Group Scaling Relations

Galaxy clusters and groups are the most massive virialized structures in the universe, dictating the physical state of the hot intracluster medium and producing robust [[galaxy-cluster-properties-and-scaling-relations|Galaxy Cluster Properties and Scaling Relations]]. Under the assumption of purely gravity-driven spherical collapse, clusters are treated as self-similar, following basic [[scaling-relation-models|Scaling relation models]] based on gravity [Lovisari et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022hxga.book...65L/abstract). 

Empirical observations consistently show that real clusters deviate from simple self-similarity. Studies measure steeper slopes in the [[l-t-relation|L-T relation]] and observe a notable "break" in scaling laws at group scales, typically around [[galaxy-dm-velocity-dispersions|velocity dispersions]] of $\sigma \sim 200$ km/s [Davé et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..268..236D/abstract). Comprehensive efforts to quantify these relations, such as the [[large-galaxy-catalogs|ASCA cluster catalog]], have provided a robust baseline for measuring $L-T$ and $M-T$ scaling laws. The general consensus attributes these deviations to [[galaxy-cluster-dynamical-state|radiative cooling]] and non-gravitational heating, such as AGN feedback [Maughan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.437.1171M/abstract).

In the microwave regime, the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect provides an alternative mass proxy, which traces the [[global-observables-of-galaxies|total thermal energy]] of the ICM [Saliwanchik et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160105452S/abstract). The $Y_{\rm SZ}$-mass relation forms a cornerstone of [[astrophysical-mass-scaling-relations|Astrophysical Mass Scaling Relations]]. Nevertheless, when stacked SZ signals are derived from optically selected catalogs in a [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]], they are highly sensitive to [[sample-variance|systematic uncertainties]]. Effects such as [[optical-richness|optical miscentering and errors in mass-richness calibrations]] can systematically suppress the inferred signals [Biesiadzinski et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757....1B/abstract).

The [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] in [[x-ray-scaling-relations|X-ray scaling relations]] limits their standalone precision for constraining cosmological parameters. However, incorporating secondary dynamical parameters can significantly tighten these relations. For example, accounting for the cluster concentration-mass relation can reduce scatter, allowing [[galaxy-cluster-mass-profiles|strong-lensing clusters]] to be utilized in precision cosmology [Comerford et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...715..162C/abstract). On slightly smaller mass scales, galaxy groups present their own structural parameters and can be characterized by their decoupling from [[alcock-paczynski-test|cosmic expansion]] [Tully et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015AJ....149...54T/abstract). Efforts to bridge these regimes have led to the proposal of generalized [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]] that unify galaxy clusters and rotationally supported [[rotating-galaxy-models|disk galaxies]] [La Fortune et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv211002684L/abstract).

Carefully calibrated scaling relations remain stringent tools for testing the standard $\Lambda$CDM model, enabling the detection of large-scale [[galaxy-galaxy-radial-velocity|bulk flows]] and independent tests of cosmic [[homogeneity-scale|isotropy]] [Migkas et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240601752M/abstract).

## Evolution Over Cosmic Time

The [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]] of galaxy scaling relations provides a direct empirical window into the physical processes governing [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|mass assembly]], structural growth, and [[galaxy-populations|quenching]]. One of the most striking evolutionary signatures is found in the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]]. Observations reveal that at a fixed stellar mass, galaxies at $z \sim 2-3$ measure $2-5$ times smaller in effective radius than their local counterparts [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). These progenitors gradually assemble through mechanisms such as [[specific-merger-rate|minor dry mergers]] and adiabatic expansion within the broader context of [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|Galaxy and Universe evolution]], although relics occasionally survive as [[galaxy-subcomponents|ultradense bulges]] [Gao et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..262...54G/abstract). To theoretically capture these transitions, researchers utilize [[number-density-evolution|continuity equations]] to track the rates of star-formation quenching [Lilly et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..141L/abstract).

Tracing the evolution of kinematic scaling laws highlights the complex interplay between stellar mass assembly and baryonic cycling. While the stellar mass [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] exhibits significant evolution, the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] remains remarkably stable out to $z \sim 0.6$. This implies that the total baryonic reservoir was already gravitationally bound within the [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]] at intermediate redshifts [Puech et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..277..138P/abstract). To track this assembly, the 'beta' parameter in the luminosity-velocity dispersion relation (similar to the [[d-sigma-relation|D-sigma relation]]) serves as a statistical 'thermometer' for [[galaxy-cluster-dynamical-state|galaxy virialization]] [D'Onofrio et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...675A.186D/abstract).

When interpreting the evolution of scaling relations, models must account for progenitor bias, often managed through [[galaxy-control-samples|Galaxy control samples]]. Recent studies of the mass Fundamental Plane show that it is highly stable, suggesting that the underlying virial constraints anchoring these [[global-observables-of-galaxies|Global observables of galaxies]] are established early [Bezanson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799..148B/abstract). When considering spatial distributions, [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy clustering strength]] does not evolve in simple proportion to dark matter clustering; instead, the clustering amplitude is tied to the epoch of average density peak collapse [Bagla et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998lsst.conf..253B/abstract). Mass-dependent evolutionary rates are also evident in [[morphological-downsizing|Morphological downsizing]], wherein less massive galaxies undergo stronger recent structural evolution [Ziegler et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006EAS....20..303Z/abstract).

## Environmental Drivers and the Cosmic Web

The impact of local environment is historically codified in the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]], which demonstrates that spiral galaxy fractions decline significantly in moving from field environments to high-density [[galaxy-cluster-mass-profiles|cluster cores]], accompanied by a rise in the [[early-type-fraction|Early-type fraction]]. The spatial distribution of these populations is quantified by [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy clustering statistics]], primarily the two-point correlation function. In the quasi-linear regime, clustering is characterized by a [[comoving-correlation-length|Comoving correlation length]] ($r_0$) and a [[logarithmic-slope|Logarithmic slope]] ($\gamma$). [[higher-order-clustering-statistics|Higher-order statistics]] further map out this complex distribution, allowing researchers to detail the dynamics of large-scale structure formation [Fry et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982ApJ...262..424F/abstract).

Beyond simple local overdensity, the anisotropic geometry of the cosmic web—tracked by [[galaxy-density-fields|Galaxy density fields]]—actively shapes scaling relations. Studies show that the scaling relations connecting [[galaxy-color-distributions|$(u-r)$ color]], stellar mass, and metallicity differ significantly depending on whether the host structure consists of sheets, filaments, or [[galaxy-overdensities|clusters]] [Nandi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...02..012N/abstract). In denser environments, phenomena such as [[age-environment-relation|environmental quenching]] systematically suppress star formation and elevate stellar metallicities at a fixed mass, driving populations off the standard [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]]. 

Furthermore, the large-scale network of the cosmic web itself obeys structural scaling laws. For instance, cosmological voids form a cellular system that exhibits dynamical and geometric scaling relations [Aragon-Calvo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200900977A/abstract). However, the extent to which the distribution of galaxies remains scale-invariant is a subject of ongoing tension. The standard cosmological paradigm predicts a crossover to large-scale [[homogeneity-scale|homogeneity]] at scales beyond $30 \text{--} 100 \ h^{-1} \mathrm{Mpc}$ [Guzzo et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...382L...5G/abstract). Conversely, some researchers argue that the galaxy distribution exhibits fractal behavior up to much larger scales, challenging the FAIR sample hypothesis and the normalization of [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy correlation functions]].

## Statistical Methodology and Intrinsic Scatter

The observed dispersion in [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] represents a convolution of [[sample-variance|measurement uncertainties]] and true [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] driven by astrophysical variance. To robustly constrain [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|Galaxy and Universe evolution]], researchers compare [[scaling-law-based-mass-estimates|different mass estimators]], such as comparing emission line widths directly to luminosities to track the evolution of the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]] [Bershady et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph.12020B/abstract).

**Maximum Likelihood Estimation**
Maximum likelihood techniques are extensively employed to recover scaling parameters. For instance, distance-dependent noise in a [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] systematically biases size-luminosity relations; specialized approaches successfully correct these biases [Rossi et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.387..735R/abstract). In kinematic studies, MLE methods can reliably recover the zero-point of the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] by assuming a random distribution for [[galaxy-inclination|Galaxy inclination]] [Obreschkow et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777..140O/abstract). In cluster cosmology, models are developed to constrain the form of [[observable-mass-scaling-relations|Observable-mass scaling relations]] without the biases inherent to linear regression [Maughan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.437.1171M/abstract).

**Bayesian Inference**
[[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]] has increasingly superseded classical methods for quantifying [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]]. Bayesian analysis of [[rotating-galaxy-models|disk galaxies]] reveals that the residual variances in structural and dynamical planes are predominantly intrinsic rather than observational [Stone et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912...41S/abstract). Furthermore, for discrete scaling relations—such as the correlation between [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|globular cluster counts]] and stellar mass—Bayesian predictive comparisons show that negative binomial models accurately reproduce physical variance [Berek et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...972..104B/abstract).

**Principal Component Analysis**
When exploring multidimensional parameter spaces, Principal Component Analysis (often grouped with [[non-parametric-methods-in-astrophysics|Non-parametric methods in astrophysics]]) is used to identify physical drivers. In the study of local star-forming systems, PCA reveals that [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] and stellar mass define the plane of [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|metallicity]] [Hunt et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.427..906H/abstract). Similarly, PCA has been deployed to parameterize massive spectroscopic datasets in [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|spectroscopic surveys]] [Madgwick et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001misk.conf..331M/abstract).

**Weak Gravitational Lensing**
Weak gravitational lensing has emerged as a powerful statistical tool. [[galaxy-matter-cross-correlation|Lensing analyses]] independently confirm foundational scaling relations across various environments [McKay et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph..8013M/abstract). Furthermore, surveys rely on specialized scaling laws for systematic error mitigation, such as scaling relations between the two-point clustering of [[linear-alignment-models|intrinsic alignments]] and underlying matter fields [Meng et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...864....1M/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite the success of scaling laws in describing massive systems, extending them to [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] presents ongoing challenges [Graham et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....48..231G/abstract). Furthermore, [[low-luminosity-galaxies|low-surface-brightness galaxies]] and [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|metal-poor starbursts]] frequently deviate from standard scaling laws. For example, some systems host massive H I reservoirs but exhibit extremely inefficient star formation [Christie et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250920080C/abstract). 

In galaxy dynamics, empirical laws such as the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] (RAR) pose significant challenges. Crucially, [[galaxy-matter-cross-correlation|weak lensing data]] confirm that these dynamical laws extend outward to hundreds of kiloparsecs [Lelli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102499L/abstract). Alternative models have also been proposed to explain the extraordinarily tight correlations between luminosity and [[galactic-potential|dynamical mass]] without relying strictly on hierarchical merging [Disney et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170300363D/abstract).

At the high-mass end, interpreting [[galaxy-cluster-properties-and-scaling-relations|Galaxy Cluster Properties and Scaling Relations]] requires disentangling gravitational collapse from baryonic physics. Purely gravity-driven models fail to reproduce the observed [[logarithmic-slope|Logarithmic slope]] of cluster relations [Maughan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.437.1171M/abstract). The role of AGN feedback is central to resolving this tension, though alternative simulations suggest that [[galaxy-cluster-dynamical-state|radiative cooling]] might sufficiently alter the hot gas fraction without ad hoc heating [Davé et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..268..236D/abstract).

Looking forward, upcoming surveys are poised to test relations from "cosmic noon" back to the epoch of cosmic dawn, both tracked through [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]] [Förster Schreiber et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ARA&A..58..661F/abstract). It remains debated whether relations like the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] undergo true physical evolution or if shifts reflect a [[morphological-downsizing|"downsizing"]] effect [Hunt et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.427..906H/abstract). Intriguingly, the baryonic [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] appears to show minimal evolution, implying that baryonic gas reservoirs were bound early in [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|Galaxy and Universe evolution]] [Puech et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..277..138P/abstract). Reducing systematic uncertainties is crucial for [[galaxy-clustering-and-redshift-estimation|precision cosmology]]. Galaxy and cluster scaling laws are increasingly used to constrain cosmological parameters, measure bulk flows, and test cosmic [[homogeneity-scale|isotropy]] [Migkas et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240601752M/abstract). Advanced statistical techniques, including [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]], will be paramount for leveraging these relations as exact cosmological probes, especially in understanding the secondary parameters driving [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] [Posti et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...644A..76P/abstract).
