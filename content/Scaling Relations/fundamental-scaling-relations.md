In extragalactic astrophysics, **fundamental scaling relations** are tight empirical correlations that link the global structural, kinematic, luminous, and chemical properties of galaxies and galaxy clusters. Despite the immense diversity in galaxy morphology and assembly history, populations of galaxies occupy well-defined, low-dimensional manifolds within higher-dimensional parameter spaces. These power-law dependencies are primarily manifestations of self-gravitating systems residing in virial equilibrium within their host [[dark-matter-halo-scales|dark matter halos]], as broadly governed by the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|Virial Theorem and Parameters]]. However, observed scaling relations systematically deviate from idealized, self-similar expectations. Tracing the physical origins of these deviations—such as variations in mass-to-light ratios, baryonic feedback, and angular momentum conservation—provides one of the most powerful observational constraints on the [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|galaxy assembly process]] and [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|cosmological models]] of structure formation.

The specific form a scaling relation takes depends fundamentally on the morphological and dynamical state of the system. In rotationally supported [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], the classical [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] links stellar luminosity to maximum rotation velocity, a correlation that becomes significantly tighter when accounting for cold gas to form the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]]. Similarly, these disk systems exhibit a remarkably tight [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] that couples the observed dynamical acceleration directly to the baryonic mass distribution. Conversely, dispersion-supported [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] populate a thin three-dimensional parameter space known as the Fundamental Plane, which connects their [[half-mass-radius|effective radius]], central [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|velocity dispersion]], and surface brightness. Beyond structural and kinematic properties, scaling laws also capture the steady-state interplay of baryon cycling and gas cooling, as seen in the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] and the [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Fundamental Metallicity Relation]]. At the centers of these potential wells, tight correlations such as the $M_{\text{BH}}-\sigma_*$ relation point to the symbiotic co-evolution of supermassive black holes and their host bulges.

On the macroscopic scales of the cosmic web, these equilibrium concepts extend to [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]], where [[x-ray-scaling-relations|X-ray scaling relations]] between intracluster medium temperature, X-ray luminosity, and total mass serve as vital [[mass-proxy|Mass proxy]] tools for [[cluster-cosmology|Cluster cosmology]]. This article explores the physical foundations and historical development of these classical scaling laws, alongside their [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|redshift evolution]] as tracers of cosmic [[morphological-downsizing|Morphological downsizing]] and gas depletion. Finally, it reviews the critical open questions that continue to challenge the standard paradigm. These include ongoing debates over the physical origin of the Fundamental Plane's "tilt" (e.g., systematic variations in the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] versus structural non-homology), the breakdown of universal metallicity relations at $z > 7$ seen in recent JWST observations, the anomalously compact nature of the Milky Way compared to extragalactic [[galaxy-analogues|Galaxy analogues]], and whether the extremely low [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] in disk kinematics implies a "baryon-halo conspiracy" or necessitates alternative gravitational physics.

## Physical Foundations and the Virial Theorem

At the most basic level, the [[galaxy-scaling-relations|structural scaling relations]] of galaxies and [[clusters-of-galaxies|galaxy clusters]] are manifestations of the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|virial theorem]] governing self-gravitating systems in equilibrium. For a system with total mass $M$, characteristic velocity dispersion or rotation velocity $V$, and characteristic radius $R$, virial equilibrium dictates that $M \propto R V^2$. Beyond purely gravitational dynamics, the fundamental scales of galaxies are further governed by thermodynamic constraints. The classic Rees-Ostriker limit posits that galaxy masses and radii are fundamentally restricted by the requirement that the cooling time of pregalactic gas must be shorter than its free-fall gravitational collapse time. If the luminous matter faithfully traces the dynamical mass such that the mass-to-light ratio $M/L$ is constant, and if galaxies form a structurally homologous sequence, this principle naturally predicts a tight plane in the parameter space of size, velocity, and surface brightness $I \propto L/R^2$.

However, observed scaling relations systematically deviate from these idealized, self-similar predictions. In [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|early-type galaxies]], this deviation is known as the "tilt" of the Fundamental Plane. While virial equilibrium remains the primary driver of these relations, the tilt arises because the assumptions of constant $M/L$ and strict structural homology break down. The systematic variation in $M/L$ across the galaxy sequence is attributed to a combination of changing stellar population ages, [[chemical-abundances|metallicities]], and variations in the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. Notably, some studies argue that dark matter is unimportant within the half-light radius, where the total mass profile is close to isothermal, whereas others point to a varying central [[galaxy-mass-fractions|dark matter fraction]] as a contributing factor to the tilt. Furthermore, the remarkable thinness of the Fundamental Plane is intrinsically linked to the dynamical stability of the underlying [[galaxy-models|galaxy models]]; systems with extreme radial orbital anisotropy are unstable and naturally settle back onto the observed scaling plane.

Within the $\Lambda$CDM cosmological framework, the origin of these scaling laws is deeply tied to the assembly of [[dark-matter-halo-scales|dark matter halos]]. Numerical simulations demonstrate that the tight scaling relations observed in both disk and elliptical galaxies emerge from the self-similar mass profiles of cold dark matter halos and the strong correlation between galaxy [[stellar-component|stellar mass]] and [[mass-scaling-relations|halo mass]]. As baryons cool and settle into the centers of these halos, their final dynamical state is dictated by the halo's gravitational potential well. The resulting interplay between dark and baryonic matter gives rise to universal dynamical laws across varying morphologies, such as the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] and the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]]. Recent integral-field studies, such as the MaNGA DynPop survey, have further demonstrated the existence of a unified inclination-corrected mass plane for both early-type and late-type galaxies that strictly satisfies the virial theorem.

The depth of the gravitational potential, defined as $\Phi \propto M/R$, directly regulates several other fundamental properties of galaxies. For example, the specific scaling between a galaxy's structural parameters and its [[mass-weighted-stellar-populations|stellar population]] is driven by the potential's control over the gas escape velocity. Higher gravitational potentials allow galaxies to more effectively retain metals against the mechanical feedback of supernova-driven winds, leading to the tight [[z-l-relation|mass-metallicity scaling relations]] observed across the galaxy population. 

Self-similarity arguments also extend to the [[galaxy-cluster-properties-and-scaling-relations|scaling relations of galaxy clusters]], where pure gravitational collapse predicts a specific set of power-law relations between total mass, [[x-ray-luminosity-total-mass-and-gas-mass-relations|X-ray luminosity]], and [[temperature-luminosity-scaling-relations|gas temperature]] (e.g., $M \propto T^{3/2}$). While high-mass clusters generally align with these self-similar predictions, significant deviations occur at the group and low-mass cluster scales. These departures indicate that the intracluster medium is not purely governed by gravity; non-gravitational physics, such as radiative cooling and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback, plays a critical role in reshaping the gas density profiles and breaking self-similarity in the low-mass regime.

## Historical Development of Classical Scaling Laws

The historical development of galaxy scaling relations began with the identification of power-law correlations between global photometric and kinematic properties. For [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], the earliest such correlation was the Faber-Jackson relation, which demonstrated that a galaxy's total luminosity scales strongly with its central [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|velocity dispersion]], roughly following $L \propto \sigma_0^4$. Concurrently, for [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] established a tight link between luminosity and the maximal rotation velocity of the disk. This is typically parameterized as:
$$ L = A V_{\max}^\alpha $$
where the zero-point $A$ and the [[logarithmic-slope|logarithmic slope]] $\alpha$ depend on the photometric band, with observed values of $\alpha$ generally ranging between 2.5 and 4. These early kinematic relations provided robust extragalactic distance indicators and hinted at a fundamental physical connection between the luminous stellar mass and the depth of the host [[galactic-potential|dark matter potential]].

Early structural analyses also revealed purely photometric scaling laws, most notably the Kormendy relation, which connects the [[half-mass-radius|effective radius]] $R_e$ and the central or effective [[luminous-high-surface-brightness-galaxies|surface brightness]] of bulges and elliptical galaxies. However, these two-dimensional projections—the Faber-Jackson and Kormendy relations—exhibited significant [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]], suggesting they were merely cross-sections of a more fundamental, higher-dimensional parameter space.

In the late 1980s, researchers demonstrated that early-type galaxies actually populate a remarkably thin two-dimensional manifold within the three-dimensional space of size, velocity dispersion, and surface brightness. This structure, known as the Fundamental Plane, is conventionally expressed in the form:
$$ \log R_e = a \log \sigma_0 + b \log \langle I \rangle_e + c $$
The tightness of the Fundamental Plane pointed to highly regulated equilibrium processes during galaxy assembly. However, a major historical and theoretical focus has been the "tilt" of the Fundamental Plane. The empirically derived coefficients (typically $a \approx 1.2$ to $1.5$ and $b \approx -0.75$) deviate significantly from the purely homologous, constant mass-to-light ratio expectation of $a=2$ and $b=-1$ derived from the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|Virial Theorem and Parameters]]. Tracing the physical origin of this tilt drove decades of subsequent research. The consensus indicates that the tilt is driven by a combination of systematic variations in the stellar [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], changing mass-to-light ratios due to age and [[abundance-gradients|metallicity gradients]] along the mass sequence, and structural non-homology.

Simultaneously, the scaling relations for spiral galaxies underwent significant refinement. Large multi-band optical and near-infrared surveys demonstrated that while the Tully-Fisher relation is virtually independent of surface brightness, the scatter in the [[halo-mass-luminosity-relation|size-luminosity relation]] is mostly dominated by surface brightness, and both exhibit a notable dependence on galaxy color and [[galaxy-stellar-age|star formation history]]. As the role of cold gas became better quantified, it was realized that the inclusion of atomic and molecular gas masses tightened the rotational scaling laws considerably. This led to the formulation of the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]], which demonstrated that the total baryonic mass—rather than just the stellar luminosity—is the most fundamental quantity tracing rotation velocity. This realization successfully linearized the scaling relation down to the gas-dominated [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxy]] regime, unifying high-mass spirals and low-mass irregulars under a single dynamical law.

## Scaling Relations of Disk-Dominated Galaxies

The structural and kinematic properties of [[disk-galaxies|disk-dominated galaxies]] are governed by a tight network of scaling relations that link their baryonic content to their host [[dark-matter-halo-scales|dark matter halos]]. These scaling laws encapsulate the complex physics of [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|angular momentum conservation]], gas cooling, and [[galaxy-property-distributions|stellar feedback]], providing stringent benchmarks for cosmological models of galaxy formation. Furthermore, scaling relations also extend to non-baryonic observables, such as the predicted power-law dependencies between galactic magnetic field strengths and [[universal-rotation-curves|galaxy rotation curves]] or [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|gas surface densities]].

**The Baryonic Tully-Fisher Relation**
The classic [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] links the luminosity or stellar mass ($M_*$) of a disk galaxy to its characteristic rotation velocity ($V_{\rm rot}$). However, in low-mass and late-type spirals, [[h-i-rich-galaxies|neutral gas]] forms a substantial fraction of the total baryonic mass budget. By defining the total baryonic mass as $M_{\rm bar} = M_* + M_{\rm HI}$, researchers establish the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] (BTFR). The BTFR is fundamentally tighter and more linear than its purely stellar counterpart, extending reliably down to [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxy scales]] where gas dominates the mass budget. The tightness of the BTFR strongly suggests that global galactic dynamics are universally dictated by the total baryonic mass rather than the stellar component alone. Additionally, studies of the [[chemical-abundances|interstellar medium]] in galaxies reveal that [[h-i-mass-to-halo-mass-relation|atomic-to-stellar mass ratios]] systematically decrease with increasing stellar mass, while the global [[h-i-mass-function|HI abundance]] exhibits only weak evolution over cosmic time.

**The Radial Acceleration Relation**
Moving from global to spatially resolved kinematics, disk galaxies exhibit a remarkable correlation known as the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] (RAR). The RAR demonstrates that the observed total radial acceleration, $g_{\rm obs} = V^2(r)/r$, is tightly coupled to the Newtonian acceleration predicted solely from the baryonic mass distribution, $g_{\rm bar}$. This coupling persists across roughly 16 orders of magnitude in baryonic acceleration. Recent rigorous [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Bayesian]] treatments of observational errors place the intrinsic scatter of the stellar RAR at just $0.11 \pm 0.02$ dex. The physical origin of the RAR remains a subject of active debate; while it is routinely interpreted as a signature of Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), numerous studies argue that this "baryon-halo conspiracy" arises naturally within $\Lambda$CDM [[galaxy-models|galaxy models]] due to the self-similar mass profiles of dark matter halos and tightly constrained galaxy-halo mass relations.

**Angular Momentum and the Fall Relation**
Another fundamental axis of disk galaxy scaling is the relationship between [[galaxy-subcomponents|specific angular momentum]] ($j$) and mass ($M$), commonly referred to as the Fall relation. Observations reveal extremely tight power-law scaling relations between stellar specific angular momentum ($j_*$) and $M_*$. The residual scatter in the $j_*-M_*$ relation is driven largely by the galaxy's neutral gas content; at a fixed stellar mass, HI-rich galaxies exhibit systematically higher specific angular momentum than their HI-poor counterparts. Notably, inferring the spin parameter of galactic disks from these relations yields a distribution that is significantly narrower than the spin parameter distribution of dark matter halos predicted by cosmological simulations. Tracing these relations over cosmic time reveals an "angular momentum paradox"; scaling relations imply a massive growth factor of $20-50$ in disc angular momentum since $z=2$, requiring the massive accretion of high-vorticity gas.

**Structural Scalings and the Milky Way Outlier**
Scaling relations can be generalized into a three-dimensional [[lvr-relation|Luminosity-Velocity-Radius (LVR) parameter space]]. The structural sizing of disks plays a crucial role here; for instance, defining sizes via isophotal radii rather than exponential [[length-scale-astrophysics|scale lengths]] yields significantly tighter size-luminosity and size-velocity correlations. Additionally, the relative stability of cold gas and stars against gravitational collapse ([[structural-concentration-and-profiles|Toomre's Q]]) imposes a uniform set of constraints across a wide mass range, anchoring the size and gas fraction relations of disks. Furthermore, the relation between H I mass and size is remarkably robust and feedback-invariant in cosmological simulations, arising directly from the inherent self-similarity of exponential H I radial profiles.

Despite the robustness of these extragalactic relations, placing the Milky Way within this framework reveals unexpected tensions. While the Milky Way adheres well to the standard Tully-Fisher scaling, it appears as a severe outlier in the multi-dimensional LVR space. Based on extragalactic scaling laws, the expected disk scale length for a galaxy of the Milky Way's luminosity and rotation velocity is $\sim 5$ kpc. The actual scale length is approximately half that value, as modeled by systems like the [[besan-on-galaxy-model|Besançon Galaxy Model]], rendering the Milky Way a $\sim 9.5\sigma$ outlier based on Milky Way measurement errors alone; however, when the full variance and intrinsic scatter of the extragalactic relations are taken into account, this tension drops to a much more modest $\sim 1.4\sigma$.

## The Fundamental Plane and Spheroidal Systems

[[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] exhibit a tight correlation between their structural and kinematic properties, defining a two-dimensional surface within a three-dimensional parameter space. This relation, known as the Fundamental Plane (FP), links the [[half-mass-radius|effective radius]] $R_e$, the central [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|velocity dispersion]] $\sigma_0$, and the mean [[luminous-high-surface-brightness-galaxies|surface brightness]] $\langle I \rangle_e$, as detailed in studies of [[intrinsic-galaxy-and-stellar-properties|Intrinsic Galaxy and Stellar Properties]]. Under the assumption of structural homology and a constant mass-to-light ratio ($M/L$), the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|Virial Theorem and Parameters]] predicts coefficients of $a = 2$ and $b = -1$. Within this plane, giant ellipticals populate a more restricted region known as the "Fundamental Band", reflecting an underlying structural constraint between [[length-scale-astrophysics|scale length]] and total galaxy mass.

Observations consistently demonstrate that the empirical coefficients deviate from these virial expectations, a phenomenon referred to as the "tilt" of the FP. The physical origin of this tilt remains a subject of active debate. One framework attributes the tilt to systematic variations in the stellar mass-to-light ratio, potentially driven by changes in stellar population ages, metallicities, or a [[non-universal-initial-mass-function|Non-universal initial mass function]] that is bottom-heavy in massive galaxies, as opposed to the standard [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. An alternative explanation suggests that the tilt arises from structural non-homology and systematic variations in [[galaxy-mass-fractions|dark matter fractions]] or velocity dispersion anisotropy. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that the tilt can result primarily from the decrease of gas fraction with increasing progenitor mass during [[merger-rate-density|merger events]].

Despite this tilt, the FP is remarkably thin, exhibiting an [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] of just $\sim 0.055$ dex at $z \sim 0$. This thinness is closely linked to the dynamical stability of radially anisotropic galaxy models. N-body simulations indicate that the FP is preserved during equal-mass hierarchical merging but can be disrupted by the accretion of smaller systems. The FP is well-established out to at least $z \sim 0.7$, where both quiescent and [[low-redshift-star-forming-galaxies|massive star-forming galaxies]] lie on nearly the same mass Fundamental Plane with an intrinsic scatter of only $\sim 0.08$ dex.

While massive galaxies follow the classic FP, related [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]] exhibit curvature at lower masses. For instance, the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] scales as $\log R_e \propto 0.54 \log M_*$ for high-mass galaxies, but flattens to a slope of $\sim 0.3$ for [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf spheroidal systems]]. At the lowest luminosities, the Fundamental Plane exhibits a 'warping' or peculiar feature that structurally distinguishes dwarf cluster galaxies from their more massive counterparts.

Efforts to unify these diverse systems have led to the proposal of the Fundamental Manifold. This framework suggests that all stellar dynamical systems—ranging from giant ellipticals and dwarf spheroidals to [[globular-clusters|star clusters]] lacking dark matter—populate a unified scaling relation when accounting for the total mass-to-light ratio within the half-light radius. Interesting, when specifically examining globular clusters, they appear to deviate from a two-dimensional plane; instead, they may occupy a "Fundamental Line", implying a purely photometric scaling law between luminosity and effective radius.

## Co-evolution of Supermassive Black Holes and Host Galaxies

It is now firmly established that almost all [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] harbor a central supermassive black hole. One of the most striking results in extragalactic astrophysics is the discovery of tight empirical scaling relations connecting the central black hole mass ($M_{\mathrm{BH}}$) to the large-scale structural and kinematic properties of the host galaxy's [[galaxy-subcomponents|bulge]], as explored in studies of [[galaxy-nuclei-and-complex-morphologies|Galaxy Nuclei and Complex Morphologies]]. These relations strongly imply a symbiotic co-evolution between black holes and their host galaxies across cosmic time.

The most prominent of these correlations is the $M_{\mathrm{BH}}$-$\sigma$ relation, which links the black hole mass to the [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|stellar velocity dispersion]] ($\sigma$) of the host bulge. An equally important constraint is the relation between the black hole mass and the total [[galaxy-spheroid-mass|Galaxy spheroid mass]] ($M_{\mathrm{bulge}}$). In the standard paradigm, the central black hole typically accounts for approximately $0.1\%$ of the host bulge's stellar mass. The establishment of these relations is generally attributed to self-regulating feedback mechanisms: [[central-galaxy-properties-and-evolution|active galactic nucleus (AGN)]] feeding and feedback. As the black hole accretes gas, it drives powerful outflows and jets that heat or expel the surrounding [[chemical-abundances|interstellar medium]], thereby quenching [[global-star-formation-rate|star formation]] and choking off its own fuel supply.

Alongside supermassive black holes, compact nuclear star clusters are frequently found at the centers of galaxies across the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]]. Interestingly, these nuclear star clusters exhibit structural and kinematic properties that place them on scaling relations analogous to those of large [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], suggesting that their formation is similarly governed by the generic settling of baryons in deep potential wells. Furthermore, scaling relations linking velocity dispersion, [[galaxy-stellar-age|age]], and metallicity are not just macroscopic properties but persist down to nuclear and circumnuclear scales of tens to hundreds of parsecs. Beyond central cores, other compact stellar systems trace global halo properties; for instance, observations reveal that the combined mass of a galaxy's [[galactic-globular-cluster-system|globular cluster system]] is a remarkably constant fraction of the total galaxy mass across a vast mass range.

Beyond structural scaling laws, the physics of black hole accretion is described by the "Fundamental Plane of black hole accretion." This multi-wavelength scaling relation connects the [[x-ray-scaling-relations|X-ray luminosity]] ($L_X$), radio continuum luminosity ($L_R$), and $M_{\mathrm{BH}}$. Observational studies indicate that black hole accretion generally favors an advection-dominated flow.

Despite the remarkable homogeneity often described in textbook theory, recent observations have revealed significant and challenging deviations from these universal scaling relations. In particular, [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] frequently appear offset from the standard Fundamental Plane of black hole accretion. Even more striking is the discovery of "over-massive" black holes in compact lenticular galaxies, which drastically violate the canonical $0.1\%$ mass ratio. These systems represent either the extreme tail of a broader statistical distribution of merger-driven growth, or they indicate that disk-dominated and compact galaxies form a distinct evolutionary class that fundamentally diverges from standard [[astrophysical-mass-scaling-relations|Astrophysical Mass Scaling Relations]].

## Chemical and Star-Formation Scaling Relations

[[low-redshift-star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] exhibit strong correlations between their [[mass-distribution-and-functions|stellar mass]], [[global-star-formation-rate|star formation rate]] (SFR), and gas-phase [[chemical-abundances|metallicity]], which serve as empirical records of their integrated [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|evolutionary histories]]. The most prominent of these is the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] (SFMS), a tight sequence in the SFR-$M_\star$ plane. Similarly, the mass-metallicity relation (MZR) correlates a galaxy's total stellar mass with its gas-phase oxygen abundance. Observations of the MZR indicate a break in its logarithmic slope at low stellar masses. This transition is widely attributed to an escape velocity threshold; below this mass scale, supernova-driven winds can efficiently expel metal-enriched gas from the shallow potential wells of [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxies]], drastically lowering their retained metal fraction.

These two relations are not independent. The 3D parameter space of $M_\star$, SFR, and chemical abundances collapses onto a tight 2D surface known as the [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Fundamental Metallicity Relation]] (FMR). By accounting for the SFR, the FMR significantly reduces the [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] seen in the isolated MZR. The existence and stability of the FMR are heavily utilized to constrain [[bathtub-models|Bathtub models]] of the galactic baryon cycle. In these equilibrium models, galaxies exist in a slowly evolving steady state governed by a balance between cosmological gas inflow, [[stellar-birthrate|star formation]], and feedback-driven outflows. 

Complementary to the FMR, researchers have proposed the "Fundamental Formation Relation" (FFR), which links [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|star formation efficiency]] and the gas-to-stellar mass ratio into a universal framework for both active star formation and quenching. Furthermore, the effective nucleosynthetic yield serves as a sensitive tracer of the cumulative impact of both supernova and [[central-galaxy-properties-and-evolution|active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback]] over the galaxy's lifetime. In theoretical studies, replicating these complex scaling relations requires sophisticated modeling of baryon physics, often through [[semi-empirical-galaxy-evolution|semi-analytic models]] that incorporate positive AGN feedback.

While the FMR was historically considered to be universally redshift-invariant, recent JWST observations have revealed a breakdown of the relation in the early Universe. At $z > 7$ in the [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|reionization epoch]], galaxies exhibit metallicities approximately $0.6$ dex lower than FMR predictions. This offset suggests that galaxies have not yet achieved the equilibrium state, as they are subject to massive, continuous inflows of pristine intergalactic gas that dilute the interstellar medium faster than stellar nucleosynthesis can enrich it.

Finally, the transition from single-fiber spectroscopy to integral field spectroscopy has demonstrated that these [[global-observables-of-galaxies|Global observables of galaxies]] are emergent properties of highly localized phenomena. The FMR and MZR have spatially resolved local counterparts measured on kiloparsec scales, implying that the balance between star formation and metal enrichment is fundamentally regulated at local scales within [[galactic-disk-structure-and-dynamics|galactic disks]].

## Scaling Relations in Galaxy Clusters and Groups

As the largest virialized structures in the universe, [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] provide a crucial astrophysical laboratory for testing [[galaxy-models|structure formation]]. In a purely gravitational hierarchy, the thermodynamic properties of the intracluster medium (ICM) are expected to scale self-similarly with the total host halo mass. Under the assumption of [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|virial equilibrium]], simple power-law relations link the total mass, the [[x-ray-luminosity-total-mass-and-gas-mass-relations|X-ray luminosity]], and the [[temperature-luminosity-scaling-relations|gas temperature]]. 

Observations, however, reveal significant deviations from these self-similar predictions, particularly at the low-mass end of the cluster and group regime. The observed [[l-t-relation|L-T relation]] steepens considerably for systems with lower temperatures. This steepening is widely attributed to non-gravitational physics—specifically, energetic feedback from AGN and radiative cooling. Consequently, robust [[x-ray-scaling-relations|X-ray scaling relations]] at the group scale depart from strict scale-invariance. Furthermore, comparisons between observations and simulations indicate that significant baryons are 'missing' in central cluster scaling relations, having likely been redistributed as intracluster light, a property defined in [[galaxy-cluster-properties-and-scaling-relations|Galaxy Cluster Properties and Scaling Relations]].

To utilize clusters for precision [[cluster-cosmology|Cluster cosmology]], observers must identify a reliable [[mass-proxy|Mass proxy]] that minimizes intrinsic scatter. While foundational, the scatter in these relations is highly sensitive to the dynamical state of the cluster. However, incorporating additional structural parameters, such as the [[structural-concentration-and-profiles|cluster concentration-mass relation]], can significantly reduce this scatter, particularly for unrelaxed systems. To overcome these biases, researchers often rely on the thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect, which probes the integrated pressure of the ICM, often calibrated via [[tsz-wl-relation|weak gravitational lensing]]. 

## Redshift Evolution and Galaxy Assembly

The [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|redshift evolution]] of fundamental scaling relations provides crucial constraints on the [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|galaxy assembly process]], tracing the transition from chaotic early accretion to secular equilibrium. By tracking shifts in the zero-points and intrinsic scatter of these relations over cosmic time, researchers can isolate the effects of merging, gas accretion, and feedback.

**Structural Relations and Morphological Downsizing**
Observations of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] reveal a clear pattern of [[morphological-downsizing|Morphological downsizing]], wherein the most massive systems assemble their stellar mass and settle into virial equilibrium earlier than their low-mass counterparts. A key feature is the contrasting structural evolution observed across different parameters. For instance, [[passive-galaxies|quiescent galaxies]] exhibit significant size evolution, expanding considerably over time, which sharply contrasts with the nearly negligible evolution of their central [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|velocity dispersion]]. Theoretical models like [[semi-empirical-galaxy-evolution|semi-analytic models]] struggle to perfectly replicate this complex evolution. The structural and dynamical scaling relations for these massive galaxies are remarkably stable over the latter half of cosmic history. The preservation of the Fundamental Plane over time suggests that hierarchical assembly via equal-mass dry mergers maintains the structural scaling.

**Kinematic Scaling and Gas Reservoirs**
At intermediate redshifts, major mergers play a significant role in disrupting kinematic scaling relations. This physical disruption induces substantial scatter in high-redshift measurements of the purely stellar [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]]. However, the inclusion of cold gas fundamentally stabilizes the relation. While the stellar Tully-Fisher relation shows significant zero-point evolution, the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] remains largely invariant. This non-evolution implies that the vast reservoirs of gas were already gravitationally bound to their host halos at intermediate redshifts. Recent local surveys, such as the [[nearby-galaxies-catalog|WALLABY survey]], have further established the baseline for these relations.

**Chemical Evolution and Early Universe JWST Observations**
The interplay between star formation, gas accretion, and feedback is strongly constrained by the FMR. However, recent observations indicate a breakdown of this relation during the epoch of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|reionization]]. At redshifts $z > 7$, galaxies are observed to deviate significantly from the FMR, exhibiting chemical abundances lower than expected. This rapid dilution suggests that galaxies at $z > 7$ occupy a distinct evolutionary regime characterized by massive, continuous inflows of pristine gas.

## Intrinsic Scatter and Environmental Impact

The observed variance around fundamental scaling relations is a combination of observational measurement error and true [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]]. Accurately isolating the intrinsic component is critical, as the physical residuals encode the diverse assembly histories of galaxies. Estimating this scatter is statistically non-trivial; traditional methods struggle with covariant uncertainties. Additionally, validating mass proxies is essential; for instance, stellar mass estimates show consistency with dynamical masses, confirming the robustness of [[rest-frame-galaxy-properties|fundamental mass indicators]]. Modern analyses increasingly rely on [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]] to disentangle measurement noise from astrophysical variance, while accounting for [[probability-density-function-cosmology|selection effects]] that can mimic evolutionary trends.

For disk galaxies, the intrinsic scatter is largely driven by the stochasticity of baryon cycling. The interdependencies of these residuals also yield physical insights; dark matter halo properties have been shown to naturally remove correlations between the residuals of the Tully-Fisher and [[size-mass-relation|size-mass relations]]. At the higher mass scales of galaxy clusters, deviations from scaling relations are heavily influenced by the system's dynamical state. Clusters undergoing major mergers exhibit significant [[cluster-substructure|Cluster substructure]], which biases the inferred hydrostatic mass downwards and constitutes a dominant source of scatter.

In addition to internal feedback and merger histories, a galaxy's macroscopic [[age-environment-relation|environment]] plays a dominant role in driving systematic offsets. This impact is fundamentally linked to the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]], which demonstrates that the fraction of gas-rich spiral galaxies drops in cluster cores. These processes introduce severe residuals in gas-mass scaling relations. Galaxies in dense environments, such as the [[nearby-galaxies-catalog|Virgo cluster]], exhibit systematically lower gas ratios. Consequently, mapping the spatial residuals of scaling relations provides a powerful empirical tool for identifying localized regions of [[galaxy-property-distributions|environmental processing]].

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite the empirical robustness of fundamental scaling relations, their exact physical origins remain the subject of intense theoretical debate. A persistent unresolved issue is the "tilt" of the Fundamental Plane. If galaxies were perfectly homologous systems, structural parameters would scale simply. Observations, however, consistently yield different exponents. The literature is conflicted on the primary driver; one hypothesis attributes this to the stellar [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], while other models argue it is a consequence of [[galaxy-mass-fractions|dark matter fractions]]. 

A second major theoretical challenge is the "Baryon-Halo conspiracy," which describes the surprisingly tight coupling manifested in the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] and the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]]. The remarkably low intrinsic scatter has motivated alternative theories or exotic [[galaxy-models|dark matter physics]]. In contrast, cosmological simulations contend that these relations emerge naturally within the $\Lambda$CDM framework. 

The advent of high-redshift observations has challenged universality. For instance, the [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Fundamental Metallicity Relation]] is stable up to $z \simeq 3.7$, but potentially breaks down at $z > 7$. Finally, the application of galaxy clusters to cosmology requires a rigorous understanding of the scatter in [[x-ray-scaling-relations|X-ray scaling relations]] and [[temperature-luminosity-scaling-relations|Temperature-luminosity scaling relations]]. Properly accounting for this scatter remains a vital hurdle for the future of [[cluster-cosmology|Cluster cosmology]].
