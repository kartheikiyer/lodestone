The Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation (BTFR) is one of the most robust [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] in extragalactic astrophysics. It establishes a tight empirical correlation between a galaxy's total baryonic mass—defined as the sum of its stellar and cold [[gas-fraction|gas content]] ($M_b = M_* + M_{\rm gas}$)—and its characteristic [[asymptotic-circular-velocity|rotation velocity]], typically measured along the asymptotic flat portion of its [[universal-rotation-curves|rotation curve]] ($V_{\rm flat}$). The BTFR serves as a physically motivated generalization of the classical [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]]. While the classical relation relies on [[stellar-luminosity-function|stellar luminosity]] as a [[mass-proxy|Mass proxy]], it systematically breaks down for [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] and [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]], which possess a significantly higher cold [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]]. By accounting for these un-astrated neutral gas reservoirs, the BTFR absorbs extreme low surface brightness outliers and restores a continuous [[mass-scaling-relations|scaling law]] across the Hubble sequence [McGaugh et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...632..859M/abstract).

Modern empirical calibrations of the BTFR reveal a continuous power law of the form $M_b \propto V_{\rm flat}^\alpha$ that holds over at least five decades in baryonic mass. Utilizing high-quality, spatially resolved kinematic tracers and near-infrared photometry to minimize uncertainties in the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|stellar mass-to-light ratio]], observational studies typically measure a [[logarithmic-slope|Logarithmic slope]] $\alpha$ between 3.5 and 4.0 [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....143...40M/abstract), [Zaritsky et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....147..134Z/abstract). The relation is exceptionally precise, exhibiting an intrinsic scatter as low as $\sim 0.02$ to $0.1$ dex [Lelli et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...816L..14L/abstract), [Ponomareva et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.4366P/abstract). This minimal scatter indicates that integrated baryonic mass, rather than stellar light, is the fundamental parameter governing the kinematics of rotationally supported disks—a realization that has paved the way for more generalized empirical laws like the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]]. 

Theoretically, the BTFR provides a stringent testbed for [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|models of galaxy and universe evolution]] by probing the deep coupling between [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|visible matter]] and the [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] of dark matter halos. Within the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological paradigm, reproducing the BTFR's near-zero intrinsic scatter poses a significant challenge. It requires a finely tuned "disc-halo conspiracy" wherein complex baryonic feedback processes precisely offset natural cosmological variations in halo concentrations and spin parameters. Conversely, alternative theoretical frameworks such as Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) argue that the relation's exact slope of 4 and vanishing intrinsic scatter are fundamental *a priori* predictions of a modified gravitational force law at low accelerations [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106l1303M/abstract). Resolving this theoretical tension—alongside determining whether the BTFR exhibits a genuine "turndown" at extreme low masses ($M_b \lesssim 10^8 M_\odot$), and understanding how newly discovered [[deficient-dark-matter-galaxies|Deficient dark matter galaxies]] fit into this kinematic scaling—remains a central focus of contemporary extragalactic research.

## Introduction and Theoretical Foundations

Rooted in the fundamental principles of galactic dynamics, the Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation (BTFR) extends the classic luminosity-velocity correlation to encompass the entire [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|baryonic inventory]] of a galaxy. Specifically, it establishes that when a galaxy's stellar mass is combined with its un-astrated gas reservoirs, the resulting total baryonic mass ($M_b$) correlates remarkably well with its characteristic [[asymptotic-circular-velocity|rotation velocity]] ($V_{\rm rot}$). 

While the classical luminosity-based relation exhibits a prominent "kink" or breakdown at both the high-mass end and for [[h-i-rich-galaxies|gas-rich]] [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], the BTFR unifies these systems into a single, continuous power law [McGaugh et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...632..859M/abstract), [Noordermeer et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.381.1463N/abstract). Historically, some early studies argued that low surface brightness (LSB) galaxies conform to the classical optical TFR simply due to systematically different [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|mass-to-light ratios]] . However, by robustly accounting for the substantial cold gas fractions in dwarf and LSB galaxies, the broader BTFR framework ultimately demonstrated that baryonic mass, rather than stellar luminosity, is the true fundamental parameter governing the dynamics of rotationally supported disks [Geha et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...653..240G/abstract).

Mathematically, the BTFR is expressed as a simple power law:
$$ M_b = A V_{\rm rot}^\alpha $$
where $A$ is the zero-point normalization and $\alpha$ is the [[logarithmic-slope|Logarithmic slope]]. Some models suggest that the [[size-mass-relation|galaxy scale length]] acts as a necessary third parameter in the BTFR to minimize scatter, particularly in the B-band where color dependence is strongest [Avila-Reese et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....136.1340A/abstract). Observational calibrations utilizing high-quality, spatially resolved [[universal-rotation-curves|rotation curves]] and near-infrared photometry typically find a slope $\alpha$ between 3.5 and 4.0 [McGaugh et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...632..859M/abstract), [Zaritsky et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....147..134Z/abstract). The relation is exceptionally tight; when the rotation velocity is measured along the flat portion of the rotation curve ($V_{\rm flat}$), the intrinsic scatter can be as low as $\sim 0.02$ to $0.1$ dex [Lelli et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...816L..14L/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3267L/abstract). 

At its core, the BTFR reflects the deep theoretical underpinnings of the Virial theorem and provides a direct observational probe of the [[galaxy-halo-scales|galaxy-halo connection]]. In standard structure formation, galaxies reside within extended dark matter halos. Naive virial expectations dictate that a halo's [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] scales with its circular velocity as $M_{\rm halo} \propto V^3$. The systematically steeper slope observed in the BTFR ($\alpha \approx 3.5 - 4.0$) has led to varied interpretations. Some models suggest a mass-dependent [[total-baryon-fraction|baryon retention fraction]], wherein low-mass halos are less efficient at retaining their primordial gas due to shallower potential wells and efficient galactic outflows, resulting in a steeper power law [Dutton et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.3123D/abstract). In contrast, other studies argue that a slope of 3.5 actually implies a nearly constant baryon fraction—condensing approximately 40% of all expected baryons—that is fundamentally independent of the galaxy's circular velocity [Zaritsky et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....147..134Z/abstract). Further supporting this, in the innermost regions of disk galaxies, the [[galaxy-central-density|central dynamical mass density]] has been observed to scale linearly with the central baryonic density, suggesting that these inner regions may possess a constant baryonic fraction near unity [Lelli et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Galax...2..292L/abstract). In addition, the empirical "baryonic scaling model" suggests that the detailed shape of a galaxy's rotation curve is determined locally simply by scaling up the stellar and gas contributions. This implies an exceptionally tight local coupling between dark and baryonic mass, independent of global halo properties [Swaters et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.2299S/abstract).

Because of its precision, the physical origin of the BTFR remains a subject of intense debate and explicit tension in the literature. Within the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm, the scaling law is understood to emerge from the complex interplay of gravitational collapse, radiative cooling, and baryonic feedback. Specifically, the process involves baryonic dissipation and the resulting adiabatic contraction of dark matter halos, which pulls dark matter inward and significantly affects the final shape of the rotation curve [Blumenthal et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...301...27B/abstract). However, reproducing the near-zero intrinsic scatter of the BTFR poses a significant challenge, requiring a delicate "disc-halo conspiracy" where the distribution of baryons must perfectly offset variations in the [[density-profiles|structural parameters]] (like concentration and spin) of the host dark matter halos [Di Cintio et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456L.127D/abstract), [Desmond et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484..239D/abstract). Conversely, advocates of MOND argue that the exact slope of 4 and the vanishingly small scatter are not coincidental byproducts of galaxy formation physics, but were predicted *a priori* as the consequence of a fundamental modification to gravitational laws at low accelerations [de Blok et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...508..132D/abstract), [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106l1303M/abstract). Resolving whether the BTFR is a highly regulated outcome of baryonic feedback in dark matter halos or a signature of new fundamental physics remains a central question in modern astrophysics.

## Historical Development and Evolution from the Tully-Fisher Relation

The classical [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]], initially formulated as an empirical correlation between the optical luminosity $L$ and the [[maximum-rotational-velocity|Maximum rotational velocity]] $V_{\rm max}$ of spiral galaxies, takes the power-law form $L = A V_{\rm max}^\alpha$. For bright, high surface brightness disk galaxies, this luminosity-rotation relation is remarkably tight. It exhibits a typical intrinsic scatter of roughly 20% (or $\sim 0.08$ dex) in luminosity at a fixed velocity, with an observed slope $\alpha$ ranging between 2.5 and 4.0 depending on the photometric band. Historically, this relationship served as a critical secondary distance indicator and provided fundamental constraints on the [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] of dark matter halos. 

However, as observational surveys expanded to probe [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]], and low surface brightness (LSB) galaxies, significant deviations from the classical scaling law emerged. Faint, late-type dwarf galaxies systematically fell below the optical Tully-Fisher relation defined by massive spirals. Instead of following the established power law, these systems produced a visible "kink" or turndown at low luminosities and displayed a dramatically increased intrinsic scatter, reaching up to $\sim 1.6$ magnitudes in the $I$-band [Geha et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...653..240G/abstract), [Begum et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.386..138B/abstract).

The physical driver of this breakdown is the systematic variation in the [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]] across the Hubble sequence. While massive spiral galaxies have relatively low cold gas mass fractions ($f_{\rm gas} \sim 0.1$), gas-dominated systems such as LSBs and dwarf irregulars are significantly less efficient at converting their primordial gas reservoirs into stars. Consequently, their cold gas fractions routinely exceed 60%, and can reach upwards of 90% in extreme cases [Geha et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...653..240G/abstract). For these gas-rich systems, stellar luminosity ceases to be a reliable [[mass-proxy|Mass proxy]] for the total assembled baryonic mass.

The paradigm shift from a luminosity-based to a mass-based framework resolved this faint-end discrepancy. By substituting optical luminosity with the total baryonic mass—defined as the sum of the stellar mass ($M_\star$) and the cold gas mass ($M_{\rm gas}$, predominantly H I and Helium)—researchers established the Baryonic Tully-Fisher Relation (BTFR). The inclusion of the gaseous component successfully realigns the gas-rich dwarfs and LSB outliers onto a single, continuous power law that spans over five decades in mass [McGaugh et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...632..859M/abstract), [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....143...40M/abstract).

The modern BTFR is now recognized as far more fundamental than its luminosity-based predecessor. When calibrated using the [[asymptotic-circular-velocity|asymptotic flat rotation velocity]] ($V_{\rm flat}$) and robust [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|stellar mass-to-light ratios]], the intrinsic scatter of the BTFR drops to near-zero values [Lelli et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...816L..14L/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3267L/abstract). The measured slope $\alpha$ generally converges to between 3.5 and 4.0 [McGaugh et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...632..859M/abstract), [Zaritsky et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....147..134Z/abstract). This evolution from early photometric [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]] to a strictly mass-based dynamical law demonstrated that the coupling between baryons and dark matter is nearly universal in rotationally supported systems, eventually paving the way for more generalized frameworks like the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]].

## Kinematic Tracers and Measurement Methodologies

The calibration of the BTFR relies heavily on the adopted definition of the characteristic rotational velocity. The choice of kinematic tracer—ranging from spatially resolved H I rotation curves to integrated line widths—dictates the resulting slope, normalization, and intrinsic scatter of the relation.

**Resolved Rotation Curves**
For galaxies with resolved kinematics, the [[maximum-rotational-velocity|Maximum rotational velocity]] ($V_{\rm max}$) has historically been utilized. However, at the high-mass end ($V \gtrsim 200 \text{ km s}^{-1}$), massive early-type disc galaxies often exhibit declining rotation curves. Utilizing $V_{\rm max}$ for these systems introduces a characteristic "kink" or offset in the BTFR, displacing them by up to 1.5 magnitudes from the main relation defined by less massive spirals [Noordermeer et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.381.1463N/abstract). 

In contrast, the circular velocity measured along the flat, asymptotic outer part of the rotation curve ([[asymptotic-circular-velocity|$V_{\rm flat}$]]) systematically straightens this high-mass kink, restoring a single power law [McGaugh et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...632..859M/abstract), [Noordermeer et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.381.1463N/abstract). Using $V_{\rm flat}$ minimizes the intrinsic scatter of the BTFR to values as low as $\sim 0.02 - 0.1$ dex, yielding a fundamental [[fundamental-scaling-relations|scaling relation]] that is significantly tighter than when parameterized by $V_{\rm max}$ [Lelli et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...816L..14L/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3267L/abstract).

**Integrated Line Widths**
For unresolved or distant galaxies, researchers frequently rely on the widths of neutral hydrogen (H I) 21-cm global profiles, typically measured at 20% ($W_{20}$) or 50% ($W_{50}$) of the peak flux. While these single-dish measurements are highly efficient for large surveys, the exact definition of the line width introduces significant systematic uncertainties. BTFR slopes derived from unresolved H I line widths typically range between 2.6 and 3.5, whereas resolved asymptotic velocities ($V_{\rm flat}$) tend to yield steeper slopes near 3.9-4.0 [Bradford et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...11B/abstract), [Ponomareva et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.4366P/abstract). Large-scale automated studies of over 30,000 ALFALFA galaxies have derived a robust local BTFR slope of 3.30, demonstrating its utility as a redshift-independent distance indicator [Ball et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...950...87B/abstract).

Additionally, the exact relationship between the kinematics and the gas mass is highlighted by the "Bosma effect," which reveals that the behavior of the rotation curve at large radii is largely determined by a rescaled version of the cold gas component, acting as a primary driver for the observed BTFR itself [Sylos Labini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.2697S/abstract). Studies of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] reveal that BTFRs constructed using $V_{\rm flat}$, the velocity at the last measured H I point ($V_{\rm last}$), $W_{20}$, and $W_{50}$ increasingly diverge at low masses [Brook et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459..638B/abstract). Nevertheless, $W_{20}$ is often found to correlate more tightly with mass and yield lower scatter than $W_{50}$, making it a preferred integrated tracer for dwarf galaxies and high-redshift studies [Begum et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.386..138B/abstract), [Glowacki et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.3267G/abstract).

**Molecular, Ionized Gas, and Stellar Tracers**
While H I is the traditional tracer for the extended, dark matter-dominated regions of galaxies, extending the BTFR to higher redshifts or varying morphologies often requires alternative [[stellar-tracers|tracers]]. Carbon monoxide (CO) observations have proven to be reliable kinematic proxies for the molecular gas, demonstrating no significant evolution in the BTFR zero-point out to $z \approx 0.3$ [Topal et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.3319T/abstract). Furthermore, CO linewidths can provide more accurate distance measurements in interacting galaxies; extended H I reservoirs are highly susceptible to tidal perturbations that artificially broaden the H I linewidth, whereas the more centrally concentrated CO traces the potential more robustly during early interaction phases [Tutui et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997A&A...326..915T/abstract). 

Recent applications utilizing integral field spectroscopy, such as data from the SDSS MaNGA survey, have constructed an extended BTFR that seamlessly includes both spiral and elliptical galaxies by estimating effective rotational velocities directly from [[galaxy-dm-velocity-dispersions|stellar velocity dispersions]] [Ravi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251218577R/abstract). To synthesize these disparate datasets, statistical conversions mapping $H\alpha$ or CO velocities to $V_{\rm flat}$ have been formulated. These normalizations allow for proper cross-calibration between varied tracers, cementing the BTFR as one of the most fundamental [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]].

## The Stellar Mass-to-Light Ratio and Baryonic Accounting

The BTFR improves upon the classical [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] by replacing stellar luminosity with total baryonic mass: $M_b = M_\star + M_{\rm gas}$. This shift from luminosity to mass is critical because the classical luminosity-velocity relation breaks down for [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] and low surface brightness (LSB) systems, creating a characteristic "kink" at the low-mass end [Noordermeer et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.381.1463N/abstract). By properly accounting for the complete baryonic budget, the relationship remains linear well into the extreme gas-dominated dwarf regime.

The most significant systematic uncertainty in computing $M_b$ stems from the stellar mass-to-light ratio, $\Upsilon_\star$. To convert an observed luminosity $L$ into a stellar mass $M_\star$, researchers typically rely on stellar population synthesis models, SED fitting, or photometric colors to constrain $\Upsilon_\star$ [Ponomareva et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.4366P/abstract). Early techniques to derive M/L ratios from broadband colors allowed for the derivation of dark matter distributions by scaling up the H I gas contribution [de Jong et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004IAUS..220..281D/abstract). Because $\Upsilon_\star$ varies substantially with the age, metallicity, and initial mass function of the underlying stellar population, optical bands often exhibit large scatter. To minimize these variations, modern empirical calibrations of the BTFR strongly favor near-infrared photometry, particularly at $3.6 \mu\rm{m}$, where $\Upsilon_\star$ is expected to be nearly constant across diverse [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] [Lelli et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...816L..14L/abstract).

The exact slope and intrinsic scatter of the BTFR are highly sensitive to the assumed $\Upsilon_\star$ prescription. The literature exhibits an active debate regarding the precise BTFR slope, which generally ranges from $3.5$ to $4.0$; this ambiguity is largely driven by differing treatments of $\Upsilon_\star$ and the chosen velocity metric [Zaritsky et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....147..134Z/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...816L..14L/abstract). Studies testing various $\Upsilon_\star$ scalings find that the intrinsic scatter of the BTFR is minimized—reaching values as low as $\sim 0.1$ dex—when adopting $\Upsilon_\star$ values that correspond to nearly maximal disks, such as $\Upsilon_\star \gtrsim 0.5 M_\odot/L_\odot$ at $3.6 \mu\rm{m}$ [Lelli et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...816L..14L/abstract). Furthermore, while high surface brightness spirals typically attain $\Upsilon_\star$ values near $\sim 75\%$ of the maximum disk limit, LSB galaxies often attain only $\sim 25\%$ of this maximum [McGaugh et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...632..859M/abstract).

Translating these light profiles into mass introduces additional nuance. The assumption of a universal initial mass function (IMF) is increasingly challenged; implementing an environment-dependent, galaxy-wide stellar initial mass function (gwIMF) can significantly alter derived mass-to-light ratios, shifting the BTFR normalization particularly for high-mass galaxies [Zonoozi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250712521Z/abstract). Furthermore, investigations into stellar populations have revealed a strong positive correlation between [[galaxy-stellar-age|Galaxy stellar age]] and dark matter density or mass. This supports assembly bias models where older galaxies accumulate more dark matter over time, further complicating the conversion of stellar light into dynamic mass limits [Kottur et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251200823K/abstract).

The second critical component of baryonic accounting is the cold [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]], typically defined as $f_{\rm gas} = M_{\rm gas} / (M_{\rm gas} + M_\star)$. While massive spiral galaxies like the Milky Way exhibit low gas fractions ($f_{\rm gas} \approx 0.1$), the baryonic budget of dwarf and LSB galaxies is overwhelmingly dominated by cold atomic and molecular gas. The average neutral gas fraction for low-mass dwarfs is $\langle f_{\rm gas} \rangle \approx 0.6$, and in extreme cases, LSB systems can have gas fractions exceeding $90\%$ [Geha et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...653..240G/abstract). Because dwarf galaxies are comparatively inefficient at converting their cold gas reservoirs into stars, their stellar luminosities severely underestimate their total baryonic content. Accounting for this cold gas reservoir is precisely what restores the linearity of the BTFR down to baryonic masses of $M_b \sim 10^7 M_\odot$ [Geha et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...653..240G/abstract).

## Morphological Diversity and Environmental Effects

The classic luminosity-based Tully-Fisher relation exhibits a pronounced morphological dependence, with early-type galaxies (ETGs) generally offsetting from the spiral relation and displaying higher scatter when characterized solely by stellar light. However, the BTFR extends robustly across the Hubble sequence. When converting luminosity to total baryonic mass ($M_b$) and tracing kinematics using extended H I or CO discs, ETGs are found to fall on the same continuous relation as late-type galaxies [den Heijer et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...581A..98D/abstract). Assuming an appropriate stellar mass-to-light ratio for the stellar populations of spiral galaxies, the discrepancy between spirals and ellipticals vanishes [den Heijer et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...581A..98D/abstract). This unification implies that all stellar dynamical systems share a common underlying framework, a concept generalized globally by the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] [Zaritsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ISRAA2012E..12Z/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...836..152L/abstract).

This morphological unification is further supported by the formulation of a "generalized Baryonic Faber-Jackson relation," which brings both rotation-dominated disks and dispersion-dominated spheroids onto a single dynamical scaling relation by applying a concentration-based correction to [[galaxy-central-density|stellar velocity dispersions]] [Catinella et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.420.1959C/abstract). Remarkably, these continuous connections between mass and kinematics appear to extend beyond individual galaxies. Recent work highlights the discovery of universal power-law relations connecting total [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] and baryonic mass that hold consistently across both individual galaxies and much larger galaxy clusters [Chan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221201018C/abstract). This convergence supports a unified scenario where the distinct structural scaling laws for spirals (the BTFR) and ellipticals (the Fundamental Plane) naturally arise from a single, tight galaxy-halo mass relation coupled with the self-similar [[density-profiles|Density profiles]] of dark matter halos [Ferrero et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...648A.124F/abstract).

At the faint end of the Hubble sequence, [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] and low surface brightness galaxies provide stringent tests of the BTFR. Gas-rich [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] typically possess cold gas fractions exceeding 60% and rely on H I rather than stellar kinematics to map their potential [Geha et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...653..240G/abstract). Incorporating the atomic and molecular gas mass resolves the systematic "kink" observed in luminosity-based relations at low masses, bringing dwarfs onto a single power law spanning five decades in mass [Begum et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.386..138B/abstract). 

However, at the extreme low-mass end ($M_b \lesssim 10^8 M_\odot$), the literature reflects an active debate regarding a potential "turndown" in the BTFR. Some studies report that dwarf galaxies below this threshold exhibit [[asymptotic-circular-velocity|maximum rotation velocities]] systematically lower than expected from extrapolations of the high-mass BTFR, implying highly reduced [[total-baryon-fraction|baryon fractions]] due to inefficient gas retention [McQuinn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...940....8M/abstract), [Ruan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.541.2180R/abstract). Conversely, other observations suggest these deviations may be exacerbated by selection effects, limitations in H I sensitivity, or the difficulty of measuring asymptotic velocities [Brook et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459..638B/abstract), [Hu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...947L...9H/abstract). Anomalous dynamics are also observed in some H I-rich ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs), which can significantly deviate from the relation due to their unusually high gas fractions or diffuse matter distributions [Hu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...947L...9H/abstract). The discovery of pseudobulge-hosting UDGs like AGC721966 reinforces their distinct evolutionary path and confirms they are not misidentified nuclear star cluster dwarfs [Liu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250607457L/abstract).

Environmental processes also introduce secondary variations in both the zero-point and scatter of the BTFR, manifesting as a kinematic [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]]. The distinction between central and satellite galaxies is critical: satellites evolving in dense cluster or group environments are subject to mechanisms such as ram-pressure stripping, tidal stripping, and strangulation, which deplete their cold gas reservoirs. Consequently, while internal feedback processes may reduce gas fractions to $\sim 0.4$, external environmental quenching is required to fully strip dwarf galaxies of their gas [Geha et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...653..240G/abstract). In dense group environments at intermediate redshifts, studies report a significant offset in the TFR zero-point compared to isolated counterparts [Abril-Melgarejo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...647A.152A/abstract). This shift is interpreted as a combination of truncated star formation and the adiabatic contraction of the halo mass distribution induced by environmental interactions [Abril-Melgarejo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...647A.152A/abstract). Despite these environmental effects, the overall persistence of the BTFR indicates that the total gravitationally bound [[total-baryon-fraction|Total baryon fraction]] relative to the dark matter halo potential remains tightly regulated.

## Cosmological Implications: ΛCDM vs. Modified Dynamics

The Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation serves as a primary empirical benchmark for distinguishing between the Cold Dark Matter paradigm and alternative gravity theories like Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND). Because it tightly links a galaxy's baryonic mass to its [[asymptotic-circular-velocity|Asymptotic circular velocity]] (a proxy for total [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]]), the relation directly probes the fundamental coupling between baryons and dark matter. 

In MOND, the BTFR is interpreted not as an emergent property of galaxy formation, but as an analytical consequence of modifying the Newtonian force law below a fundamental acceleration scale [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106l1303M/abstract). Consequently, MOND makes two strict *a priori* predictions: a [[logarithmic-slope|Logarithmic slope]] of exactly 4 and an intrinsic scatter of essentially zero [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106l1303M/abstract), [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....143...40M/abstract). This behavior represents a specific manifestation of the broader [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]], which dictates a tight coupling between the observed dynamical acceleration and the gravitational acceleration implied by baryons alone across diverse galaxy morphologies [Lelli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...836..152L/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102499L/abstract).

Conversely, in the standard $\Lambda$CDM framework, rotation velocities are dominated by dark matter halos. The fact that the baryonic mass so cleanly traces the outer dynamical mass requires a finely tuned "disc-halo conspiracy" [Desmond et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484..239D/abstract). Historically, early semi-analytic models struggled to simultaneously reproduce the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and the BTFR zero-point without over-predicting the baryon fraction in low-mass halos. However, modern hydrodynamic $\Lambda$CDM simulations successfully reproduce both the slope and normalization of the observed BTFR [Torrey et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.1985T/abstract), [Glowacki et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.3687G/abstract), [Goddy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.3895G/abstract). In these models, the scaling relation emerges naturally from the dissipative collapse of gas coupled with mass-dependent stellar and AGN feedback [Dutton et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.3123D/abstract), [Keller et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835L..17K/abstract). Detailed multi-wavelength analyses further reveal that its intrinsic scatter can be further minimized by incorporating specific star formation rate (sSFR) as a secondary parameter [Baes et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...696A..52B/abstract).

Despite these successes, the literature exhibits significant ambiguity regarding the intrinsic tightness of the relation. High-quality observational studies report an intrinsic scatter as low as $\sim 0.02$ to $0.1$ dex [Lelli et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...816L..14L/abstract), [Ponomareva et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.4366P/abstract). This exceptionally low scatter presents a well-documented challenge for $\Lambda$CDM, which generally predicts an intrinsic scatter of $\sim 0.15$ dex due to the expected cosmological variance in halo concentrations and spin parameters [Dutton et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.3123D/abstract), [Desmond et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472L..35D/abstract). 

Some authors argue that this scatter discrepancy fundamentally falsifies $\Lambda$CDM in favor of MOND [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106l1303M/abstract). In another theoretical vein, frameworks utilizing derivative expansions within standard General Relativity have been proposed to capture both the BTFR and flat rotation curves [Haddad et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021GReGr..53...80H/abstract). Alternative analytic derivations suggest the BTFR may emerge as a consequence of Newtonian gravitation acting within a hierarchically distributed universe [Roscoe et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230710228R/abstract). However, other researchers demonstrate that mock observations of simulated galaxies can yield similarly tight relations when sample size effects and mass-dependent [[density-profiles|Density profiles]] are rigorously accounted for [Di Cintio et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456L.127D/abstract), [Sorce et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.458.2667S/abstract). 

## Redshift Evolution and Galaxy Assembly

The study of the BTFR across cosmic time provides a stringent constraint on [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]] and the fundamental processes governing [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|Galaxy and Universe evolution]]. By tracking how the relationship between baryonic mass and rotational velocity changes with redshift, astronomers can probe gas accretion, the efficiency of the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]], and the assembly of dark matter halos.

At low to intermediate redshifts ($z \lesssim 1$), observational evidence indicates a strong divergence between the evolution of the stellar-mass Tully-Fisher relation and the BTFR. Note that while the B-band TFR shows significant brightening with redshift, the stellar mass relation shows negligible evolution [Fernández Lorenzo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...521A..27F/abstract). Conversely, the zero-point of the BTFR shows virtually no evolution out to $z \approx 0.6$ [Puech et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..277..138P/abstract) and remains largely consistent out to $z \approx 1$ [Tiley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.2166T/abstract). This stability implies that the expansive cold gas reservoirs destined to be converted into stars over the past $\sim 8$ Gyr were already gravitationally bound within the galaxies' dark matter halos at earlier epochs [Jimenez et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356..495J/abstract).

Pushing these scaling relations to $z > 1$ relies heavily on high-redshift integral field spectroscopy to resolve the kinematics of distant, turbulent disks. Data from the KMOS3D survey, which mapped [[high-mass-galaxies|massive star-forming galaxies]] at $z \sim 0.9$ and $z \sim 2.3$, reveal a more complex picture of galaxy growth. Rather than remaining static, the BTFR zero-point exhibits a non-monotonic evolution [Übler et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...842..121U/abstract). At a fixed circular velocity, galaxies at $z \sim 2.3$ actually possess *higher* total baryonic masses than their counterparts at $z \sim 0.9$. Because the gas-to-stellar mass ratio declines strongly toward the present day, this positive zero-point evolution from $z \sim 0.9$ to $z \sim 2.3$ implies that the [[dark-matter-fraction|Dark matter fraction]] contributing to the dynamical mass on galactic scales increases toward lower redshifts [Übler et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...842..121U/abstract). Complementing these kinematic constraints, studies of the [[mass-distribution-and-functions|baryonic mass function]] indicate that the high-mass end has evolved remarkably little since $z \sim 2.2$. This stability signals a fundamental transition in galaxy assembly from an accretion-dominated growth phase to a depletion-dominated regime around $z \sim 1.5$ [Pan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...885L..14P/abstract).

It must be noted that the literature is currently conflicting regarding the precise nature of the BTFR's high-redshift evolution. While surveys like KMOS3D find a non-monotonic zero-point shift, other studies utilizing integrated [[galaxy-dm-velocity-dispersions|gas velocity dispersions]] report that both the slope and normalization of the relation are remarkably invariant out to $z \sim 3$ [Christensen et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.2599C/abstract). Recent observations utilizing the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) to study massive galaxies at cosmic dawn suggest a "fast and furious" speed-up in early galaxy formation [van Putten et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PDU....4301417V/abstract).

Upcoming deep radio observations, such as the LADUMA survey on the MeerKAT array, are expected to map the kinematic properties of high-redshift galaxies in neutral hydrogen directly. These surveys will be critical for resolving current tensions in the literature and determining precisely how the tight coupling between dark and baryonic matter scales over [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]].

## Connection to the Radial Acceleration Relation (RAR)

The global Baryonic Tully-Fisher Relation (BTFR) is intrinsically linked to a more generalized, localized dynamical scaling law known as the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] (RAR) [McGaugh et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Galax...2..601M/abstract), [Di Cintio et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456L.127D/abstract). While the BTFR connects the integrated baryonic mass of a galaxy to its asymptotic circular velocity, the RAR correlates the local, observed dynamical acceleration with the gravitational acceleration predicted solely by the spatial distribution of the baryons [Lelli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...836..152L/abstract).

Observationally, the RAR reveals a tight coupling between the total [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]] and the baryonic mass distribution across all morphological types [Lelli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...836..152L/abstract). Intriguingly, this fundamental acceleration scale is not restricted to rotationally supported disks; its apparent presence has also been identified in pressure-supported systems such as galaxy clusters [Edmonds et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200912915E/abstract). The utilization of resolved stellar mass measurements to characterize gravitational contributions, particularly in gas-rich galaxies, demonstrates that employing a spatially varying mass-to-light ratio minimizes intrinsic scatter [Vărăşteanu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.541.2366V/abstract). When placing our own galaxy in this context, it is noteworthy that while the Milky Way strictly obeys the global BTFR, it appears as a distinct outlier in the generalized 3D [[lvr-relation|LVR relation]], primarily due to its unusually compact disk scale length [Licquia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..220L/abstract).

The precise mathematical and physical connection between the global BTFR and the local RAR implies that they are essentially manifestations of the same underlying physics. The RAR effectively subsumes the BTFR alongside other [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]], serving as a unified dynamical law for late-type, early-type, and dwarf spheroidal galaxies [Lelli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...836..152L/abstract). Conversely, it has been demonstrated that the RAR slope and scatter can naturally emerge simply by requiring that galaxy rotation curves satisfy the global BTFR [Wheeler et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...882...46W/abstract).

The theoretical interpretation of this tight, universal coupling remains the subject of active debate. Proponents of Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) argue that the transition and the near-zero intrinsic scatter of the RAR and BTFR are fundamental *a priori* predictions of a modified gravitational force law [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106l1303M/abstract), [McGaugh et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Galax...2..601M/abstract). In contrast, within the standard $\Lambda$CDM framework, the tight RAR and BTFR are interpreted as the result of a "disc-halo conspiracy" where dissipative collapse and mass-dependent feedback adjust the dark matter [[density-profiles|Density profiles]] [Di Cintio et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456L.127D/abstract), [Keller et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835L..17K/abstract). 

## Open Questions and Future Directions

**The Low-Mass Turndown and Missing Baryons**
A major ongoing debate concerns the behavior of the Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation at the extremely low-mass end ($M_b \lesssim 10^8 M_\odot$). While gas-rich [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] and dwarf irregulars generally follow the same power-law relation as [[high-mass-galaxies|High-mass galaxies]] [Geha et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...653..240G/abstract), some observational and theoretical studies suggest a "turndown" or steepening of the relation at the lowest masses [McQuinn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...940....8M/abstract), [Ruan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.541.2180R/abstract). In the $\Lambda$CDM framework, this deviation is expected due to the reduced efficiency of low-mass dark matter halos in retaining their gas, which contributes to the phenomenon of missing baryons. In these shallow potential wells, supernova feedback and background UV heating can easily expel gas [McQuinn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...940....8M/abstract). Hypothetical solutions to the "missing baryon" problem include the presence of ionized gas reservoirs that remain undetected in standard H I surveys [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1109.1599M/abstract). Such feedback models are currently challenged by the discovery of "baryon-deficient dwarfs" (BDDs) [Mancera Piña et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522727M/abstract). Alternatively, to address these low-mass discrepancies, the Tully-Fisher relation has been explicitly derived from first principles within warm dark matter scenarios [Hoeneisen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IJAA...13...25H/abstract). 

**Ultra-Diffuse and Dark Matter-Free Galaxies**
The limits of the BTFR are also being tested by extreme morphological classes, such as ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs). While many H I-rich UDGs align with the standard BTFR [Hu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...947L...9H/abstract), recent discoveries of apparently [[dark-matter-free-galaxies|Dark matter-free galaxies]], such as NGC 1052-DF2 and NGC 1052-DF4, have sparked intense controversy. These galaxies exhibit unusually low velocity dispersions for their stellar masses, placing them significantly off the established BTFR. While some researchers argue these objects represent a new class of [[deficient-dark-matter-galaxies|Deficient dark matter galaxies]], others initially suggested that their anomalous kinematics might result from overestimated distances, though follow-up studies have defended their dark matter-deficient status [van Dokkum et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019RNAAS...3...29V/abstract). Resolving the dynamical status of these galaxies is crucial, as their existence challenges modified gravity theories like MOND, which predict that all galaxies must strictly obey the BTFR and the broader [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] without exception.

**Limits of Intrinsic Scatter and Future Surveys**
To answer these open questions, the next generation of radio telescopes will be transformative. Upcoming SKA/MeerKAT surveys, such as the LADUMA project, will dramatically expand the parameter space of BTFR studies [Glowacki et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.3267G/abstract). By providing high-sensitivity H I kinematics out to intermediate and high redshifts, these instruments will place stringent limits on the redshift evolution of the BTFR. Furthermore, SKA pathfinders like MeerKAT and ASKAP (e.g., the WALLABY survey [Holwerda et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250110563H/abstract)) will probe the low-mass dwarf regime with unprecedented surface brightness sensitivity. Pinning down the intrinsic scatter—currently debated to be anywhere from near zero to $\sim 0.15$ dex—will ultimately determine the extent to which fine-tuned baryonic feedback in $\Lambda$CDM is required to explain the tight coupling between dark and baryonic matter [Dutton et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.3123D/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...816L..14L/abstract).
