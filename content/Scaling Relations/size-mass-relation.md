**Size-mass relation**

The **size-mass relation** is a cornerstone empirical scaling law in extragalactic astrophysics that links the physical extent of a galaxy—typically parameterized by its effective radius ($R_e$)—to its total stellar mass ($M_*$). Forming a distinct projection within the broader [[galaxy-manifold|Galaxy Manifold]] of [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]], the relation generally follows a mass-dependent power law of the form $R_e \propto M_*^\alpha$. A defining feature of this correlation is a strong structural dichotomy that mirrors the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]]: at a fixed stellar mass, actively star-forming [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] populate a shallower, extended sequence, while quiescent, [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] form a steeper, systematically more compact locus [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract), [Chiosi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1206.2532C/abstract). This fundamental bimodality underscores the differing physical drivers of galaxy assembly. Disk sizes are primarily regulated by the [[specific-angular-momentum|Specific Angular Momentum]] of cooling gas and inside-out [[star-forming-main-sequence|star formation]], whereas spheroidal sizes are governed by radiative dissipation, violent relaxation, and the constraints of the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|Virial Theorem and Parameters]] [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract).

Tracking the size-mass relation across cosmic time provides a robust framework for tracing the structural evolution of galaxies. Observational studies demonstrate strong [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]], revealing that galaxies at $z > 2$ were roughly a factor of two to five more compact than their local counterparts. This profound expansion is driven by distinct mechanisms across the galaxy population: star-forming systems generally expand via angular-momentum-supported accretion, whereas massive quiescent systems and [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] assemble their extended outer envelopes through frequent minor mergers. Consequently, the stellar size-mass relation does not exist in isolation, but is deeply intertwined with other [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]]. It operates as a structural anchor for dynamical scaling laws, such as the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] for rotating disks [Said et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231016053S/abstract) and the Fundamental Plane for pressure-supported spheroids [Cappellari et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..311...20C/abstract).

This article explores the physical drivers, environmental dependencies, and cosmic evolution of the size-mass relation, detailing how feedback mechanisms quench star formation and drive the [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]] of galaxies. Furthermore, it examines recent methodological advances that challenge traditional empirical size metrics. While the canonical half-light radius reveals a strong size bimodality, this distinction nearly vanishes when comparing the 80% light radius. This dependence on definition has driven the development of physically motivated sizes defined by star-formation thresholds, which offer a more rigorous proxy with significantly reduced [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]]. Finally, the article outlines current open questions in the field, including the ongoing tension between observationally inferred sizes and the true [[half-mass-radius|Half-mass radius]] in simulations of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], as well as the frontier challenge of utilizing new data from JWST to determine if these structural scaling laws hold during the epoch of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]].

## Definition and the Fundamental Dichotomy

The **size-mass relation** is one of the most fundamental [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]], describing the empirical correlation between the physical extent of a galaxy and its total stellar mass. Typically, a galaxy's size is parameterized by the effective radius ($R_e$, or $R_{50}$), defined as the radius containing half of the total emitted light, though the [[half-mass-radius|Half-mass radius]] is also frequently used to trace the [[mass-distribution-and-functions|mass distribution]] directly. Observationally, $R_e$ is conventionally derived from fitting a radial surface brightness profile with a specified Sérsic index. The scaling relation is generally expressed as a power law of the form:
$$ R_e \propto M_*^\alpha $$
where $M_*$ is the stellar mass and $\alpha$ is the [[logarithmic-slope|Logarithmic slope]].

A defining characteristic of the size-mass relation is the strong structural bimodality it exhibits, reflecting the divergent evolutionary pathways of different galaxy populations. In the size-mass plane, galaxies bifurcate into two distinct sequences: star-forming [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] (which populate the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]]) and quiescent, [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. At a fixed stellar mass, quiescent galaxies are systematically more compact (having smaller $R_e$) than their star-forming counterparts [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). 

For early-type, [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]], the scaling relation is notably mass-dependent. [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|High-mass early-type galaxies]] exhibit a steep relation with a logarithmic slope of $\alpha \approx 0.54$, whereas the relation significantly flattens for [[low-mass-galaxies|low-mass]] [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf]] systems, yielding $\alpha \approx 0.3$ [Chiosi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1206.2532C/abstract). Historically, these dwarf galaxies have been viewed as fossilized links to primordial density fluctuations, providing crucial signposts of the gas-rich protogalactic phase that eventually seeded larger structures [Silk et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983Natur.301..574S/abstract). By contrast, star-forming galaxies generally follow a shallower, more continuous sequence over a wide mass range, driven by angular-momentum-supported gas accretion and inside-out star formation.

The precise nature of this fundamental dichotomy is sensitive to the metric used to define galaxy size. As explored in subsequent sections, while the canonical effective radius ($R_{50}$) reveals a clear separation between the two populations, utilizing different light fractions or physically motivated density thresholds can significantly alter this perspective.

## Physical Drivers: Angular Momentum and Dissipation

The physical size of a galaxy reflects its evolutionary history and internal dynamical state. For [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], which are rotationally supported, sizes are primarily a measure of the [[specific-angular-momentum|Specific Angular Momentum]] acquired by gas as it cools and settles within dark matter halos. Conversely, in pressure-supported [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], sizes reflect the degree of radiative dissipation and violent relaxation that occurred during their hierarchical assembly.

In the standard paradigm of disk formation, gas cools quasi-adiabatically, largely conserving its specific angular momentum. This mechanism results in exceptionally tight empirical scaling relations between a galaxy's optical size and its kinematic parameters. For instance, the correlation between the isophotal radius $R_{23.5}$ and stellar specific angular momentum $j_*$ is one of the tightest known dynamical relations, with its minimal scatter driven by intrinsic physical variations rather than observational error [Stone et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912...41S/abstract). Furthermore, the physical extent and stellar mass fractions of disks are heavily regulated by gravitational instability. Analytical models connecting the stellar mass fraction to specific angular momentum and [[galaxy-dm-velocity-dispersions|radial velocity dispersion]] indicate that disk instability maintains a nearly universal Toomre's $Q$ parameter, exhibiting a cosmic variance of only $\sim 0.2$ dex across morphological types ranging from lenticulars to blue compact dwarfs [Romeo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.5656R/abstract).

Fundamentally, the emergence of these scaling laws in a $\Lambda$CDM framework arises directly from the self-similar nature of dark matter halo profiles, combined with the complex, non-linear physical relationships that link a central galaxy's mass and radius to those of its host halo [Navarro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ASSP...56..103N/abstract). The structural evolution of early-type galaxies involves highly non-linear, non-adiabatic processes—such as major mergers—that redistribute or expel angular momentum. In these systems, the final effective radius $R_e$ is governed by the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|Virial Theorem and Parameters]], which links the system's total energy to its mass and velocity dispersion $\sigma$ via the equilibrium condition $E = W/2$. This virial equilibrium, combined with systematic variations in the stellar mass-to-light ratio, is the primary driver of the Fundamental Plane, coupling size, velocity dispersion, and surface brightness into a tight two-dimensional manifold [Cappellari et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..311...20C/abstract). Theoretical frameworks indicate that the observed distribution of early-type galaxies in the mass-radius plane results from the intersection of these local virial processes and global cosmological limits on halo mass assembly over cosmic time [Chiosi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1206.2532C/abstract), [D'Onofrio et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...661A.150D/abstract).

Reproducing the exact zero-points and intrinsic scatter of the size-mass relation remains a stringent test for hydrodynamical simulations. While hierarchical models naturally reproduce the slopes of dynamical scaling laws like the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] through the scale-free assembly of dark matter halos, accurately matching the observed size normalizations depends heavily on the implemented feedback prescriptions to prevent excessive central mass concentrations [Steinmetz et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..197..165S/abstract).

## Structural Evolution over Cosmic Time

The structural evolution of galaxies over cosmic time is a critical probe of their assembly histories. Observational evidence unequivocally demonstrates strong [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]] in the stellar size-mass relation. At a fixed stellar mass, galaxies at high redshift ($z \sim 2-6$) are observed to be highly compact systems, exhibiting effective radii a factor of $2$ to $5$ smaller than their local universe ($z \sim 0$) counterparts [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). This profound structural transformation over billions of years of look-back time is observed across the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]], albeit driven by distinct physical mechanisms depending on the galaxy population.

For [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], the observed size growth is generally consistent with inside-out growth. As these galaxies form stars intensely at intermediate redshifts, newly accreted gas settles into extended disks, preferentially adding mass at larger radii and gradually increasing the overall size of the system [Bell et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..389B/abstract). Studies utilizing resolved integral field spectroscopy, such as the SINFONI survey, have provided detailed views of the resolved kinematics and star formation properties of massive galaxies at $z \sim 2$, confirming that their early assembly is characterized by dynamically complex, extended, rotation-dominated disks [Förster Schreiber et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Msngr.145...39F/abstract).

In contrast, the size evolution of massive quiescent systems and [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] (BCGs) is far more rapid. Observations of BCGs indicate that their sizes scale with redshift as $r_e \propto (1+z)^{-1.58 \pm 0.13}$, translating to a size increase of a factor of $\sim 2.5$ between $z \sim 1.2$ and $z \sim 0.2$ [Yang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4006Y/abstract). This dramatic expansion is primarily attributed to frequent minor mergers, which efficiently deposit stripped stellar material into the outer envelopes of the [[descendant-galaxies|Descendant galaxies]], increasing the effective radius without significantly altering the total stellar mass. Furthermore, reproducing the correct size-mass normalization for these massive galaxies at $z \lesssim 1$ in cosmological simulations strictly requires active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback to prevent over-cooling and central mass concentration [Taylor et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.2465T/abstract).

While stellar mass is the most fundamental parameter governing this size evolution, environment plays a secondary role. At $z < 0.5$, mass-matched samples of quiescent and star-forming galaxies show negligible differences in the slope of the size-mass relation across different environments, indicating that mass, rather than large-scale density, dictates the overall scaling [Chen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..253C/abstract). Reproducing the broader chemical and structural evolution across these epochs, however, poses a challenge for theoretical frameworks; for instance, semi-analytical models explicitly require a redshift-dependent [[average-star-formation-efficiency|Average star formation efficiency]] to accurately reproduce the evolving [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|mass-metallicity relation]] [Sakstein et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410.2203S/abstract). 

## Environmental Influence and Cluster Physics

Although stellar mass is the primary determinant of a galaxy's size, the large-scale environment imposes secondary modulations on [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]]. This environmental dependence is intricately linked to the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]], where dense cluster environments are dominated by [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] and early-type systems with distinct assembly histories compared to field galaxies. However, the literature presents some ambiguity regarding the magnitude of environmental effects on the stellar size-mass relation. While some large surveys suggest that mass-matched samples of quiescent and star-forming galaxies show negligible differences in their slopes across field and cluster environments at $z < 0.5$ [Chen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..253C/abstract), other analyses indicate that extreme large-scale structures drive measurable deviations in [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]].

At the largest environmental scales, rich [[galaxy-cluster-properties-and-scaling-relations|galaxy clusters]] themselves occupy a fundamental plane remarkably similar to that of individual elliptical galaxies, tightly linking their cluster velocity dispersion, physical radius, and overall luminosity [Schaeffer et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993MNRAS.263L..21S/abstract). This structural regularity extends to the dark matter itself; galaxy cluster dark matter halos exhibit a remarkably tight scaling relation between their [[galaxy-central-density|central density]] ($\rho_c$) and [[core-radius|Core radius]] ($r_c$), following $\rho_c \propto r_c^{-1.46}$ [Chan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442L..14C/abstract). This macro-scale cluster physics introduces further scaling relations, such as the observed correlation between Sunyaev-Zeldovich temperature decrements and [[x-ray-luminosity-total-mass-and-gas-mass-relations|X-ray luminosity]], which serves as a vital probe of the cluster's [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]] [Cooray et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.307..841C/abstract). Crucially, multi-wavelength approaches combining X-ray and Sunyaev-Zeldovich observations are essential for accurately calibrating cluster mass scaling relations and understanding thermodynamic deviations from simple self-similarity [Giodini et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013SSRv..177..247G/abstract). Additionally, dark matter halos establish a tight halo mass-temperature ($M-T$) relation that follows a uniform power law ($M_{2500} \propto T^{1.65}$) spanning three orders of magnitude in temperature, holding from individual galaxies up to massive clusters [Babyk et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946...54B/abstract). Furthermore, macro-scale phenomena such as cluster mergers introduce significant systematic effects; merging events are known to induce asymmetric scatter and a redshift-dependent bias in the Sunyaev-Zeldovich-mass scaling relation [Krause et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.419.1766K/abstract). In the regime of massive lensing clusters, great care must also be taken when deriving scaling laws, as statistical biases and observational selection effects can artificially steepen the measured mass-concentration relation [Sereno et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449.2024S/abstract).

The most pronounced environmental deviations for individual galaxies are observed in [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] (BCGs). BCGs obey a distinct, steeper size-mass scaling relation than non-BCG galaxies, with a logarithmic slope of $\sim 0.73 - 1.00$ [Chen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..253C/abstract), [Yang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4006Y/abstract). As noted earlier, their accelerated size growth is predominantly driven by frequent minor mergers in the dense cluster core, which build up the extended outer stellar envelopes without significantly increasing the central mass. Interestingly, massive early-type galaxies exhibit similar [[galaxy-stellar-age|stellar ages]] regardless of their large-scale environment, although their size growth continues for longer periods in the field via extended merger histories [van Dokkum et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASPC..379..249V/abstract). Notably, this BCG size-mass relation shows no strong correlation with the total halo mass of the cluster, implying that the environmental dependence primarily affects the galaxy's extended stellar halo rather than its deep [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]] [Yang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4006Y/abstract).

At the opposite environmental extreme, galaxies residing in cosmic voids exhibit structural differences from their cluster counterparts. Furthermore, within these cluster environments, the distribution of low-mass systems is highly sensitive to local density. A pronounced [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf population-density relation]] indicates that dwarf galaxies are significantly more prevalent in the lower-density outer regions of clusters compared to the dense cores [Phillipps et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...498L.119P/abstract). In terms of chemical properties, while the slope of the [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|mass-metallicity relation]] remains generally independent of the large-scale environment, the turnover mass closely correlates with local density, reflecting subtle variations in the gas fractions of galaxies across different environmental regimes [Wu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.1881W/abstract). [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] in voids are, on average, 10-20% smaller than those in denser environments at a fixed mass, and the mass-size relation for massive void galaxies exhibits a shallower slope [Perez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250107345P/abstract). This shallower slope indicates a deficit of minor accretion events, which are the primary drivers of size growth for massive galaxies in denser environments. Conversely, satellite galaxies falling into clusters are subject to severe environmental interactions, including tidal stripping and ram-pressure stripping. Late-type quenched cluster galaxies with stellar masses of $10^9 - 10^{10.5} M_\odot$ are generally smaller and more concentrated than their counterparts in filaments and voids, a consequence of severe environmental interactions, with early infallers being most affected [Perez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250107345P/abstract). Despite these severe environmental interactions, recent models of Local Group satellites demonstrate that a small scatter in the mass-metallicity relation remains perfectly compatible with substantial tidal mass loss [Riley et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250906859R/abstract).

Interestingly, while the stellar size-mass relation is modulated by these environmental factors, the atomic hydrogen (H I) size-mass relation remains exceptionally robust. Analytical and hydrodynamical models show that external processes such as ram-pressure stripping scarcely affect the tightness of the $D_{\rm HI}-M_{\rm HI}$ relation. As long as the gaseous disk is not completely disrupted by the cluster environment, galaxies simply migrate along the relation rather than scattering off of it, preserving a universal scaling law across environments [Stevens et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490...96S/abstract). Indeed, the [[h-i-mass-function|H I size-mass relation]] is remarkably tight, exhibiting a scatter of just $\sim 0.06$ dex that is invariant across varying galaxy luminosities, H I richness, and morphological types [Wang et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.2143W/abstract). The remarkable robustness of this H I mass-size relation has been confirmed in the Simba cosmological simulations, which demonstrate that it remains stable against variations in feedback prescriptions due to the inherent self-similarity in the underlying gas profiles [Rabyang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251204582R/abstract). Recent blind surveys from the MIGHTEE-H I project confirm this extraordinary stability, finding an intrinsic scatter of only $\sim 10\%$ with no significant structural evolution over the past billion years [Rajohnson et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.2697R/abstract).

## Interplay with Other Scaling Relations

The size-mass relation does not exist in isolation; rather, it represents a two-dimensional projection of a broader, multi-dimensional manifold of [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] that connect the structural, kinematic, and chemical properties of galaxies. To explain the observed variance within these multifaceted relations, analytical toy models suggest that the intrinsic scatter in scaling relations primarily originates from stochastic accretion rates modulated by varying gas-loss timescales [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443..168F/abstract).

**Kinematics and the Fundamental Manifold**
For early-type galaxies, the size-mass relation is deeply intertwined with the Fundamental Plane, which links the effective radius ($R_e$), surface brightness, and central [[galaxy-dm-velocity-dispersions|velocity dispersion]] ($\sigma$) into a tight 3D parameter space [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract). Recent frameworks suggest the existence of a "Fundamental Manifold" that unifies the scaling behaviors of [[star-clusters|Star clusters]], [[spheroidal-galaxies|dwarf spheroidals]], and giant ellipticals into a single continuous sequence [Zaritsky et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...727..116Z/abstract), [Zaritsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ISRAA2012E..12Z/abstract). The formulation of a specific universal scaling equation by Zaritsky fundamentally links a galaxy's radius, velocity, surface brightness, and mass-to-light ratio with an extraordinarily tight intrinsic scatter of just $\sim 0.09$ dex across disparate galaxy types [Zaritsky et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..381Z/abstract). This structural continuity extends to [[globular-clusters|globular cluster]] populations: [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] exhibit a nearly linear scaling between the total number of globular clusters and the total galaxy mass, extending down to masses of $10^{8.75} M_\odot$ [Zaritsky et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513.2609Z/abstract). Furthermore, this linear scaling relation between the total globular cluster system mass and the host halo mass broadly holds across almost all observed galaxy masses; however, non-classical dwarfs, such as ultra-diffuse and extremely low-surface-brightness galaxies, exhibit systematically higher globular cluster specific frequencies that offset them from this relation and drive significant scatter at the low-mass end [Dornan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251208453D/abstract). In a similar unifying vein, utilizing a concentration-corrected stellar velocity dispersion allows both rotation-supported disks and pressure-supported spheroids to follow a single, generalized Faber-Jackson relation [Catinella et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.420.1959C/abstract). This structural-kinematic coupling further extends to central supermassive black holes, yielding a universal black hole-size-mass relation parameterized as $M_{\bullet} \propto (M_{\star}/R_e)^{2.9}$ [van den Bosch et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..134V/abstract). Additionally, by linking a galaxy's circular velocity ($v_c$) to its central velocity dispersion ($\sigma$), one can robustly estimate supermassive black hole masses across a wide variety of morphological types [Buyle et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004IAUS..220..317B/abstract). Evolutionary models further demonstrate the resiliency of the $M_{\bullet}-\sigma$ relation to variations in redshift-dependent merger progenitor properties, playing a key role in constraining the cosmic downsizing of supermassive black holes [Robertson et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...641...90R/abstract).

In rotation-supported systems, galaxy size and mass are intrinsically linked to the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]]. The combination of luminosity (or stellar mass), rotational velocity, and disk scale length forms a 3D [[lvr-relation|LVR relation]]. Interestingly, while the Milky Way is often considered a typical spiral, LVR analyses indicate at a $\sim 9.5\sigma$ confidence level that the Galactic disk is unusually compact for its mass and rotation velocity [Licquia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..220L/abstract). Furthermore, the inclusion of gas mass via the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] has been shown to unify fast-rotating early-type galaxies and late-type spirals onto a single scaling sequence, emphasizing that kinematics, rather than visual morphology, primarily drive these global correlations [den Heijer et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...581A..98D/abstract). Recent theoretical frameworks attempt to further extend this unification by modelling the cosmic time evolution required to bridge the offset between galaxy-scale and cluster-scale baryonic Tully-Fisher relations [Marongwe et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251120188M/abstract). For highly inclined, edge-on flat galaxies, incorporating optical colors and H I-to-optical luminosity ratios can significantly reduce the scatter in the Tully-Fisher relation, tightening the connection between mass and rotation [Makarov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.3373M/abstract).

**Dynamics and the Radial Acceleration Relation**
The structural parameters of the size-mass relation also map directly onto the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] (RAR), which correlates the observed dynamical acceleration ($V^2/R$) with the expected baryonic acceleration ($GM_{\rm bar}/R^2$). The RAR acts as a fundamental 1D correlation governing in-disk dynamics across many orders of magnitude in mass [Stiskalek et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.6130S/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102499L/abstract). Recent studies utilizing resolved stellar mass measurements from MIGHTEE-H I show that accounting for these details leads to an even tighter RAR, revealing a characteristic low-acceleration power-law slope of $\sim 0.5$ [Varasteanu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250420857V/abstract). Theoretical support for this dynamic scaling comes from cosmological simulations like MassiveBlack-II, which successfully reproduce the tight correlation between baryonic and total acceleration, even though the exact functional form may vary slightly from SPARC observational data [Tenneti et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.3125T/abstract). However, the universality of the RAR remains a subject of active debate. While it tightly holds for most disk galaxies, slow-rotating early-type galaxies display systematic offsets at the low-acceleration end, likely due to merger-driven disruptions of their spin and mass distributions [Rong et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477..230R/abstract). Additionally, the literature conflicts on whether the RAR holds at the scale of galaxy clusters, with some studies reporting significant scatter and functional deviations from the galaxy-scale relation [Chan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.105h3003C/abstract).

**Chemical Evolution and Surface Density**
The size-mass relation is also fundamentally coupled to the [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Mass-Metallicity relation]] and the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]]. Together, mass, star formation rate, and gas-phase metallicity form the Fundamental Metallicity Relation (FMR). Characterizing this surface is complex; a significant observational challenge lies in separating intrinsic chemical evolution from redshift-dependent sampling biases within the FMR [Niino et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761..126N/abstract). Nevertheless, by linking [[star-formation-history|star-formation histories]] from high-redshift observations to the $z \sim 0$ mass-metallicity-star formation rate relation, researchers can empirically predict the detailed stellar metallicity distributions of nearby galaxies [Peeples et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.1766P/abstract). In late-type disks, this chemical complexity manifests as a multi-parametric relationship linking oxygen abundance with both the surface brightness profile and the morphological T-type [Pilyugin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....148..134P/abstract). 

However, adding the stellar surface density ($\Sigma \propto M_{\star}/R^2$) as a fourth parameter forms a tighter 4D hypersurface that reduces the intrinsic metallicity scatter by 50% [Hashimoto et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.4424H/abstract). Studies of spatially resolved galaxies show that within this manifold, distinct physical mechanisms drive different axes: a galaxy's stellar age correlates most tightly with its surface mass density ($\Sigma$), while its metallicity is primarily governed by the gravitational potential ($\Phi \propto M_{\star}/R_e$), which regulates the local escape velocity of supernova-driven outflows [Barone et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...898...62B/abstract). Translating observational light profiles into intrinsic mass distributions remains highly dependent on assumptions about stellar populations; indeed, variations in the stellar [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] represent one of the largest systematic uncertainties in characterizing galaxy scaling laws [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...39H/abstract). Furthermore, a variable integrated stellar IMF, which is dynamically linked to a galaxy's star formation rate, has been proposed as a mechanism that could partially explain the emergence of the mass-metallicity relation itself [Köppen et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375..673K/abstract). In terms of local star formation relations, studies of luminous infrared galaxies (LIRGs) reveal a distinct scaling relation between the magnitude of the brightest super star cluster and the host galaxy's total [[integrated-star-formation|star formation rate]]. Intriguingly, analyses of local spiral galaxies demonstrate that the [[global-star-formation-rate|global star formation rate]]-mass relation is fundamentally driven by the structural size-mass relation, despite significant galaxy-by-galaxy variations in the spatially resolved $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$--$\Sigma_{\star}$ correlation [Vulcani et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488.1597V/abstract).

While these interconnected scaling relations are remarkably stable in the local Universe, recent JWST observations suggest that the unified chemical manifold breaks down at very high redshifts ($z > 7$). In this epoch, extreme dilutions from pristine gas infall result in metal abundances roughly four times lower than what the local FMR and size-mass sequences would predict [Heintz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7.1517H/abstract).

## The Role of Feedback and Quenching

The physical mechanisms that dictate a galaxy's trajectory through the size-mass plane are heavily dependent on the regulation of its star formation and cold gas reservoir. Theoretical models and cosmological simulations require both stellar and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback to accurately reproduce the observed size-mass relations across cosmic time [Taylor et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.2465T/abstract). 

In the regime of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], supernova feedback plays a dominant role. Below a critical stellar mass of approximately $10^9 M_\odot$, the shallow potential wells of dwarf galaxies make them highly susceptible to supernova-driven gas loss [Scholte et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240803996S/abstract). This mass scale corresponds to an escape velocity threshold that regulates the atomic [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]], directly imprinting a slope change onto the atomic gas sequence and associated scaling relations. By ejecting gas and heating the interstellar medium, supernova feedback prevents excessive central mass accumulation, thereby modulating the physical sizes and stellar densities of these low-mass systems.

Conversely, in [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], stellar feedback becomes gravitationally inefficient, and AGN feedback is required to reproduce empirical scaling relations. Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate that without the inclusion of AGN feedback, models fail to place massive galaxies at $z \lesssim 1$ on the correct locus of both the size-mass relation and the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] [Taylor et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.2465T/abstract). Energy and momentum injected by central supermassive black holes heat the surrounding circumgalactic medium, suppressing the further accretion of cold gas and halting star formation. 

This suppression is intimately linked to the overarching process of quenching and the resulting [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]] of the galaxy. "Mass quenching" is observationally correlated with changes in morphological composition; as galaxies build massive central bulges, their [[global-star-formation-rate|global star formation rates]] drop, driving a flattening of the main sequence at high stellar masses ($> 10^{10} M_\odot$) [Leslie et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...58L/abstract). The tight coupling between bulge growth, [[galaxy-dm-velocity-dispersions|stellar velocity dispersion]], and the cessation of star formation indicates that quenching fundamentally reshapes the galaxy's mass profile [Cappellari et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..311...20C/abstract). 

Furthermore, early-universe conditions, which are characterized by high gas fractions, enable intense, dissipation-driven starbursts that form stars in highly compact configurations [Barone et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...898...62B/abstract). Once these compact progenitors undergo quenching—often aided by AGN feedback and environmental interactions—they evolve passively, establishing the compact locus of quiescent [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. Over the last 8 billion years ($z < 1$), the relative stability of scaling relations for extended star-forming disks contrasts sharply with the rapid build-up of this non-star-forming, spheroid-dominated population [Bell et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..4407B/abstract), [Bell et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..389B/abstract). Ultimately, the distinct size-mass scaling laws observed for star-forming versus quiescent galaxies are a direct consequence of these feedback-driven structural transformations, which permanently alter the spatial distribution of stellar mass following the depletion or ejection of the fuel for star formation.

## Methodological Advances in Size Measurement

Historically, galaxy sizes in scaling relations have been parameterized by the half-light radius ($r_{50}$) or the isophotal diameter. While observationally convenient, these metrics are largely empirical and lack a strict astrophysical boundary [Chamba et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020RNAAS...4..117C/abstract). Recent methodological shifts emphasize the structural information encoded in different enclosed light fractions, which can significantly alter the perceived scaling relations. For instance, the well-documented size bimodality between star-forming and quiescent populations at a fixed stellar mass is highly sensitive to the chosen radial definition. While quiescent galaxies are systematically more compact than star-forming systems when measured via $r_{50}$ or the core-sensitive $20\%$ light radius ($r_{20}$), this structural divergence nearly vanishes when utilizing the $80\%$ light radius ($r_{80}$). This suggests that different radii trace distinct physical phenomena: $r_{20}$ is closely linked to centralized star formation processes, whereas $r_{80}$ is more sensitive to extended accretion histories and interactions with the host dark matter halo [Miller et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872L..14M/abstract).

To move beyond arbitrary light fractions, recent literature advocates for physically motivated size definitions grounded in gas density and star-formation thresholds. One such metric is $R_1$, defined as the radius at which the [[stellar-surface-density|Stellar surface density]] drops to $1 \, M_\odot\,\text{pc}^{-2}$. By anchoring the size to a physical star-formation threshold, the $R_1$-based size-mass relation exhibits an [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] of just $\sim 0.06$ dex, approximately three times smaller than relations relying on the half-light radius [Chamba et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020RNAAS...4..117C/abstract). Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations confirm that the $R_1$-mass relation is exceptionally robust, remaining consistent across various feedback prescriptions, galaxy masses, and redshifts [Arjona-Galvez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250220398A/abstract). Furthermore, this physically motivated radius correlates strongly with the spatial extent of the host dark matter halo, allowing halo sizes to be inferred from deep imaging with significantly higher accuracy than when using traditional effective radii [Dalla Vecchia et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251019926D/abstract). Additionally, measuring scaling relations using large-scale surveys is often complicated by observational uncertainties. For instance, noise in [[galaxy-clustering-and-redshift-estimation|photometric redshift]] estimates can significantly bias inferred scaling relations, such as the size-luminosity and size-mass relations, requiring maximum-likelihood methods to correct for these statistical biases and recover the true, unbiased distributions [Rossi et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.387..735R/abstract).

The accurate measurement of the size-mass relation is further complicated by the distinction between observational proxies and intrinsic physical quantities. Because light is generally more centrally concentrated than mass, the observational half-light radius systematically underestimates the true [[half-mass-radius|Half-mass radius]]. This discrepancy is driven by radially increasing [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|mass-to-light ratio]] gradients; because the outer regions of these simulated low-mass galaxies have systematically higher mass-to-light ratios, assuming a constant ratio severely underpredicts the mass in the galaxy outskirts [Klein et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532..538K/abstract). Studies utilizing mock imaging of high-resolution simulations demonstrate that while mock observations successfully reproduce the observationally inferred size-mass relation, direct measurements of intrinsic star-particle distributions yield galaxies that are physically larger and obey a considerably tighter relation. Consequently, the discrepancy between true mass profiles and light profiles suggests that the intrinsic physical size-mass relation may possess even less scatter than current empirical estimates imply [Klein et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532..538K/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the stellar size-mass relation (SMR) has proven to be a fundamental tool for tracking structural evolution, several persistent theoretical tensions and observational ambiguities remain. A major open question involves reconciling apparent ΛCDM discrepancies in the sizes of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] and [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]]. Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations, such as the FIRE models, predict that the "true" physical sizes of simulated dwarf galaxies are systematically larger and exhibit less [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] than observationally inferred relations [Klein et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532..538K/abstract). Closer to home, a distinct tension exists within the [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|Local Group]] regarding the dynamical masses of the Milky Way and M31, which appear at odds with the expectations derived from standard stellar mass-halo mass abundance matching models [McGaugh et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230500858M/abstract). It remains an active debate whether the observed intrinsic scatter in these scaling relations reflects fundamental astrophysical variance—such as stochastic accretion histories and variations in dark matter halo concentrations—or is dominated by observational systematics, such as the mass-to-light gradients that obscure the true [[half-mass-radius|Half-mass radius]] [Stone et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912...41S/abstract), [Klein et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532..538K/abstract).

A related challenge is transitioning the community away from purely empirical metrics. As previously noted, adopting physically motivated definitions like $R_1$ significantly reduces the intrinsic scatter of the SMR [Chamba et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020RNAAS...4..117C/abstract), [Arjona-Galvez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250220398A/abstract). A major future direction involves determining whether such metrics can robustly replace the traditional $R_{50}$ in forthcoming large-scale deep-imaging surveys to provide tighter constraints on galaxy formation models.

Alongside JWST, large-scale wide-field surveys are actively resolving these tensions. Recent results from the Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1) have provided a first expansive view of the star-forming main sequence in the Euclid Deep Fields, rigorously characterizing its dependence on and relation to galaxy morphology at high masses [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315314E/abstract).

Finally, the frontier of size-mass scaling relations lies in the high-redshift universe ($z > 6$). With the advent of JWST, researchers are now poised to probe the size-mass relation during the epoch of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]]. While local and intermediate-redshift observations show a consistent, albeit evolving, SMR [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract), it is currently unknown whether galaxies assembling in the first billion years follow these established scaling laws. Recent JWST discoveries have already demonstrated that high-redshift galaxies at $z = 7-10$ significantly deviate from the local [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|mass-metallicity relation]] due to extreme dilution from pristine gas inflows [Heintz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7.1517H/abstract). Similarly, a long-standing challenge lies in reconciling discrepancies in the abundances and detailed merger histories of massive galaxies when comparing cosmological simulations to observational constraints [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract). Resolving these questions will be critical for achieving a unified, self-consistent theory of galaxy assembly across all of cosmic time.
