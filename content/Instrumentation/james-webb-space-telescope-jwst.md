The **James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)** is a flagship 6.5-meter space observatory positioned at the second Sun-Earth Lagrange point (L2), engineered to revolutionize [[near-infrared-astronomy|Near-Infrared Astronomy]] and [[mid-infrared-astronomy|Mid-Infrared Astronomy]]. Conceived to extend the scientific legacy of its predecessors, JWST overcomes the redshift and dust-extinction boundaries that historically constrained [[hubble-space-telescope-deep-surveys|Hubble Space Telescope Deep Surveys]] and the [[spitzer-space-telescope|Spitzer Space Telescope]]. By operating in a stable, cryogenic environment with a suite of highly sensitive imagers and spectrographs ([[nircam|NIRCam]], [[nirspec|NIRSpec]], [[miri-instrument|MIRI (instrument)]], and [[niriss|NIRISS]]), the observatory is uniquely optimized to detect the rest-frame optical and near-infrared emission of the universe's earliest structures [Windhorst et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006NewAR..50..113W/abstract), [Pontoppidan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...936L..14P/abstract). While textbooks often frame the rapid expansion of multi-wavelength observational astronomy as providing a flood of data to be interpreted via established physics, JWST's unprecedented sensitivity has yielded discoveries that actively challenge standard theoretical models of early galaxy formation and structural evolution. 

A defining achievement of JWST is its exploration of Cosmic Dawn and the Epoch of Reionization. [[deep-field-campaigns|Deep field campaigns]] have systematically shattered previous distance records, using [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|High-Redshift Galaxy Observations]] to spectroscopically confirm luminous galaxies at redshifts up to $z > 14$ [Carniani et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Natur.633..318C/abstract). These observations reveal an unexpectedly high number density of massive systems fully assembled within the first 500 million years of the universe. At intermediate redshifts during Cosmic Noon ($z \sim 1-3$) and extending into the Epoch of Reionization (up to $z \sim 8$), the telescope's extended mid-infrared coverage has unveiled a vast population of dust-obscured, "HST-dark" galaxies that contribute significantly to the cosmic star formation rate density. Furthermore, JWST has resolved the underlying morphology of these systems, demonstrating that many high-redshift star-forming galaxies possess mature, bulge-dominated stellar structures rather than the clumpy, irregular morphologies inferred from prior rest-frame ultraviolet imaging. 

Beyond the high-redshift universe, JWST provides transformative insights into the local interstellar medium. It utilizes [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] to map molecular hydrogen and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) in extremely metal-poor environments and energetic, active galactic nucleus (AGN) driven outflows. The observatory also capitalizes on massive galaxy clusters like [[smacs-j0723|SMACS J0723]] as gravitational lenses, amplifying the faint signatures of individual background stars and distant supernovae. As detailed in the following sections, the discoveries of JWST have introduced profound open questions into astrophysics. Chief among these is a potential tension with the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological model: the surprising abundance of early massive galaxies and the ubiquitous presence of compact, obscured AGNs—dubbed "Little Red Dots"—demand either significant revisions to astrophysical processes like bursty star formation or the introduction of novel dark matter and primordial black hole paradigms [Dolgov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231000671D/abstract). Concurrently, JWST's precise characterization of local distance indicators is providing critical new data to address the Hubble tension [Freedman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985..203F/abstract). Resolving these questions will rely heavily on ongoing multi-wavelength synergies with facilities like the [[atacama-large-millimeter-array-alma|Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA)]] and upcoming wide-field maps from the [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]].

## Mission Overview and Technical Capabilities

The James Webb Space Telescope is optimized to push the boundaries of [[near-infrared-astronomy|Near-Infrared Astronomy]] and [[mid-infrared-astronomy|Mid-Infrared Astronomy]]. Operating from a halo orbit around the Sun-Earth second Lagrange point (L2 point), the observatory utilizes this stable, cryogenic environment to minimize thermal noise and maintain extraordinary optical performance [Windhorst et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006NewAR..50..113W/abstract). The observatory features a 6.5-meter segmented primary mirror—a massive collecting aperture that provides unprecedented sensitivity and spatial resolution. To maintain this exceptional image quality, the telescope relies on a sophisticated wavefront sensing and control (WFS&C) system that routinely uses [[nircam|NIRCam]] imagery to phase and align its 18 primary mirror segments [Contos et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008SPIE.7010E..0SC/abstract).

The observatory achieves its broad scientific objectives through a highly complementary suite of four primary instruments:

**Near-Infrared Instruments**
Operating primarily in the $0.6$--$5.3\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ regime, the near-infrared suite is designed to trace rest-frame optical emission at high redshifts and to probe dust-obscured regions in the local universe.
*   **NIRCam** serves as the primary imager and wavefront sensor. Its deep multi-band imaging has proven crucial for discovering galaxies well into the Epoch of Reionization ($z > 10$), characterizing their morphologies with sub-kiloparsec resolution [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942L...9Y/abstract), [Martorano et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...694A..76M/abstract), and obtaining slitless grism spectroscopy ($R \approx 1500$) [Finkelstein et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983L...4F/abstract).
*   **[[nirspec|NIRSpec]]** is a versatile multi-object and integral field spectrograph operating from $0.6$--$5.3\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ [Maseda et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A..73M/abstract). A defining technological advancement of JWST is the NIRSpec MSA (Micro-Shutter Array). The array comprises $730 \times 342$ individually addressable doors, each measuring $0.2^{\prime\prime} \times 0.5^{\prime\prime}$, distributed over a $9\ \mathrm{arcmin}^2$ field of view. This enables simultaneous, high-multiplexing spectroscopy of hundreds of targets at resolving powers ranging from $R \approx 100-1000$ [Giardino et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASPC..507..305G/abstract), [Holwerda et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JAI.....550008H/abstract), up to $R \approx 2700$ [Maseda et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A..73M/abstract). Furthermore, the configuration of these microshutters allows NIRSpec to serendipitously detect faint, un-targeted emission-line galaxies within open microshutters during deep surveys [Maseda et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.3290M/abstract). It also features a $3^{\prime\prime} \times 3^{\prime\prime}$ Integral Field Unit for spatially resolved spectroscopy of complex targets.
*   **[[niriss|NIRISS]]** provides wide-field slitless spectroscopy and imaging. By avoiding the biases inherent to photometric pre-selection, its slitless modes are indispensable for discovering faint emission-line galaxies and resolving blended spectra using orthogonal dispersion orientations [Malkan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250900596M/abstract).

**Mid-Infrared Instrument**
Extending JWST's wavelength coverage from $5\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ to $28\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, [[miri-instrument|MIRI (instrument)]] offers both imaging and integral field spectroscopy. It represents a monumental leap over predecessor missions like the [[spitzer-space-telescope|Spitzer Space Telescope]], reaching completeness limits $\sim 100$ times deeper (e.g., $0.32\ \mu\mathrm{Jy}$ at $7.7\ \mu\mathrm{m}$) [Ling et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517..853L/abstract). MIRI delivers high-resolution mid-infrared imaging, achieving a point spread function full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of $\approx 0.2^{\prime\prime}$ at $5.6\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ [Östlin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...696A..57O/abstract). This unique capability is instrumental in characterizing heavily dust-obscured stellar populations, detecting warm molecular hydrogen outflows in active galaxies, and revealing the smooth, bulge-dominated rest-frame near-infrared stellar structure of galaxies at cosmic noon [Lai et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941L..36L/abstract), [Boogaard et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969...27B/abstract). The instrument's broadband filters (such as F560W and F770W) play a critical role in recovering accurate stellar masses and specific star formation rates for galaxies at $z \sim 10$ by extending observations into the rest-frame optical regime [Bisigello et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJS..231....3B/abstract). Utilizing these capabilities, the MEGA (MIRI EGS Galaxy and Active Galactic Nucleus) survey provides deep four-band MIRI imaging covering a large portion of the CEERS NIRCam field, aiming to study galaxy mass assembly and obscured AGNs in detail [Backhaus et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....170..300B/abstract).

Beyond extragalactic imaging, JWST's broad wavelength coverage grants it the unique ability to detect molecular signatures in exoplanetary atmospheres. For instance, recent Hubble Space Telescope observations of the hot Neptune HD 219666 b detected water but found a surprising lack of methane, highlighting the necessity for future JWST follow-up to solve this "missing methane" paradigm [Murphy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....169..286M/abstract). 

Together, these technical capabilities allow JWST to resolve the detailed morphology, mass distribution, and kinematics of early universe galaxies that were previously inaccessible or entirely invisible in prior [[hst-observations|HST observations]] [Barrufet et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522..449B/abstract).

## From Hubble to Webb: The Evolution of Deep Field Sensitivity

For decades, [[deep-field-campaigns|Deep field campaigns]] like the Hubble Ultra Deep Field (HUDF) provided the definitive census of early galaxy assembly. However, these [[hubble-space-telescope-deep-surveys|Hubble Space Telescope Deep Surveys]] faced a fundamental redshift boundary. Because the expansion of the universe shifts the rest-frame ultraviolet Lyman break into the infrared, the $\approx 1.7\ \mu\text{m}$ red cutoff of Hubble's Wide Field Camera 3 limited its horizon to $z \approx 11$ [Ellis et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763L...7E/abstract), [Yan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942L...9Y/abstract). The infrared optimization of JWST, specifically its exquisite sensitivity from $0.6$ to $28.5\ \mu\text{m}$, was explicitly designed to pierce this barrier and observe the Epoch of Reionization [Windhorst et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006NewAR..50..113W/abstract). 

Using deep [[nircam|NIRCam]] and [[nirspec|NIRSpec]] observations, programs such as [[jades|JADES]] (JWST Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey) have mapped the HUDF and other legacy fields to unprecedented depths [Eisenstein et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230602465E/abstract). This leap in sensitivity has pushed the spectroscopic frontier far beyond Hubble's limits, identifying and confirming luminous galaxies at $z > 14$, such as JADES-GS-z14-0 at $z = 14.32$ [Carniani et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Natur.633..318C/abstract), [Helton et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..729H/abstract). At these extreme distances, findings from the [[glimpse-survey|GLIMPSE survey]] have investigated the ultra-faint end of the ultraviolet luminosity function across $z \sim 9-15$, revealing minimal evolution in the faint-end slope and suggesting that ultra-faint galaxies contribute over 50% of the cosmic star formation rate density at $z \sim 12$ [Chemerynska et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026MNRAS.546f2267C/abstract). The observed high number density and large stellar masses (up to $\sim 10^9\ M_\odot$) of these early galaxies significantly exceed pre-launch theoretical expectations. Additionally, findings from the FRESCO survey have identified ultra-massive galaxies ($\log(M/M_\odot) > 11.0$) at redshifts $z=5-9$, suggesting an exceptional star formation efficiency capable of converting $\sim 50\%$ of available baryons into stars [Xiao et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Natur.635..311X/abstract). The literature currently reflects active debate over whether this tension requires a modification to standard $\Lambda\text{CDM}$ cosmology, the inclusion of massive primordial black holes, or astrophysical adjustments such as bursty star formation histories, enhanced star formation efficiencies, or a top-heavy initial mass function [Dolgov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231000671D/abstract), [Carniani et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Natur.633..318C/abstract), [Woodrum et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PNAS..12117375W/abstract).

At intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 2-8$), JWST's extended wavelength coverage has unveiled a substantial population of HST-dark galaxies. These are massive ($M_* \sim 10^{10}\ M_\odot$), star-forming systems that were completely invisible in rest-frame ultraviolet imaging due to severe dust attenuation ($A_V \sim 2-5$) [Barrufet et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522..449B/abstract), [Kokorev et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945L..25K/abstract). Observations utilizing [[miri-instrument|MIRI (instrument)]] and long-wavelength NIRCam filters demonstrate that these heavily obscured sources contribute critically to the cosmic star formation rate density, fundamentally revising our understanding of massive galaxy assembly at Cosmic Noon [Barrufet et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522..449B/abstract), [Smail et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958...36S/abstract). It is important to note that when defining these samples, deep extragalactic surveys like JADES and CEERS have also identified foreground T- and Y-type brown dwarf candidates at kiloparsec distances, which serve as potential contaminants for high-redshift galaxy selections [Hainline et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964...66H/abstract).

JWST's capability to trace rest-frame near-infrared emission also resolves long-standing morphological biases. While Hubble characterized many star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 2$ as highly clumpy, disk-dominated structures based on their unobscured UV light, JWST reveals that their underlying stellar mass distributions are often smoother and more bulge-dominated [Benton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974L..28B/abstract), [Boogaard et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969...27B/abstract). By mapping older stellar populations, JWST has shown that the high central surface densities associated with quenching were already established during the star-forming phase, providing new constraints on the structural evolution of galaxies [Benton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974L..28B/abstract), [Martorano et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...694A..76M/abstract).

## The High-Redshift Frontier and Cosmic Dawn

Prior to the launch of the James Webb Space Telescope, the observational frontier for [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|High-Redshift Galaxy Observations]] was fundamentally constrained by the near-infrared wavelength limits of [[hst-observations|HST observations]], which effectively capped the detectable redshift limit at $z \approx 11$. Utilizing the unprecedented sensitivity of [[nircam|NIRCam]] and [[nirspec|NIRSpec]], JWST has systematically shattered this barrier, yielding a surprising abundance of luminous galaxies well into the epoch of cosmic reionization and transforming our understanding of the first 500 million years of cosmic history. Theoretical preparations for this era established specific detection limits and color selection criteria—including the vital role of MIRI bands—for identifying chemically pristine Population III galaxies expected around $z \sim 10$ [Zackrisson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...740...13Z/abstract). 

Early [[deep-imaging-surveys|Deep imaging surveys]] using the Lyman break technique quickly identified numerous candidate objects at $z > 11$ [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942L...9Y/abstract). However, the literature immediately highlighted the necessity of rigorous spectroscopic confirmation, as catastrophic photometric redshift errors can cause dusty, intermediate-redshift galaxies (e.g., at $z \approx 4.9$) to masquerade as $z \approx 16$ candidates [Arrabal Haro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Natur.622..707A/abstract). Following these initial photometric selections, [[deep-spectroscopic-surveys|Deep spectroscopic surveys]]—most notably by the [[jades|JADES]] survey—have definitively confirmed luminous galaxies at extreme distances, including the robust detection of JADES-GS-z14-0 at $z = 14.32$ [Carniani et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Natur.633..318C/abstract). However, accurately recovering spectroscopic redshifts for some candidates, such as JADES-GS-z11-0 and JADES-GS-z13-0, can be significantly impacted by the presence of damped Lyman-$\alpha$ absorbers [Hainline et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240404325H/abstract). 

These observations have necessitated a re-evaluation of rapid mass assembly and metal enrichment in the early universe. JWST has revealed interacting systems such as a galaxy at $z = 9.31$ with an estimated stellar mass of $1.6 \times 10^9\ M_\odot$ and a gas-phase metallicity of $\sim 0.1 Z_\odot$. Such discoveries demonstrate that massive, chemically enriched galaxies were already in place merely $\sim 510$ Myr after the Big Bang [Boyett et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8..657B/abstract). Similarly, the spectroscopic detection of H$\alpha$ and [O III] emission from the luminous, young galaxy GHZ2/GLASS-z12 at $z=12.33$ indicates rapid metal enrichment reaching $\sim 0.1$ solar metallicity within just 30 Myr [Zavala et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240310491Z/abstract). Moreover, multi-wavelength observations have uncovered a galaxy "Quintet" situated in a protocluster at approximately 650 million years after the Big Bang, demonstrating that massive galaxy assembly in the early universe is actively driven by multiple gas-rich mergers [Fudamoto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251011770F/abstract). The complexity of early galaxy evolution is further highlighted by the discovery of CEERS2-588, the most massive galaxy securely confirmed at $z > 10$. This object exhibits a prominent Balmer break, indicating surprisingly weak star formation activity and rapid quenching within the last 10 Myr [Harikane et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260121833H/abstract).

The unexpected overabundance and high masses of these early galaxies have sparked an active debate regarding the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological model. While some authors argue these findings challenge theoretical predictions and may require new physical paradigms, such as structural seeding by primordial black holes [Dolgov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231000671D/abstract), [Dolgov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240106882D/abstract), the emerging consensus leans toward astrophysical solutions. Tensions with $\Lambda$CDM can be largely alleviated by invoking highly bursty star formation histories, elevated star formation efficiencies, or variations in the stellar initial mass function (IMF) that lower the true stellar mass-to-light ratio in the early universe [Woodrum et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PNAS..12117375W/abstract), [Sabti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvL.132f1002S/abstract). 

Furthermore, the exact masses of these systems remain sensitive to the interpretation of their spectral energy distributions. The ubiquitous presence of compact, obscured sources—often termed "Little Red Dots"—suggests that some of the rest-frame optical luminosity in the early universe may be powered by dust-reddened active galactic nuclei (AGNs) rather than pure stellar continua [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240710760L/abstract). Distinguishing between true high-redshift candidates and lower-redshift extremely red objects often requires longer-wavelength constraints from [[miri-instrument|MIRI (instrument)]] to break spectral degeneracies [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...987...60S/abstract).

Finally, the prolific ultraviolet output of these early, luminous galaxies has profound implications for cosmic reionization. If the high ionizing photon production efficiencies ($\xi_{\rm ion}$) inferred from these early JWST observations are combined with significant Lyman-continuum escape fractions, it implies an excess of ionizing photons that could theoretically finish reionization too early—a discrepancy actively debated in the literature as a potential "photon budget crisis" [Muñoz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240407250M/abstract). Additionally, FRESCO observations of H$\alpha$-emitting galaxies at $z=5-6$ demonstrate that Lyman-$\alpha$ escape fractions are strongly modulated by dust (observed UV slope $\beta_{\rm obs}$), indicating that UV-faint galaxies likely contribute the majority of ionizing photons during this epoch [Lin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..272...33L/abstract).

## Structural Evolution and Bulge Formation at Cosmic Noon

Prior to the deployment of JWST, our morphological understanding of massive star-forming galaxies at Cosmic Noon ($z \sim 2-3$) was largely shaped by rest-frame optical and ultraviolet imaging from the Hubble Space Telescope. These legacy observations consistently depicted such galaxies as clumpy and disk-dominated. However, rest-frame optical light is highly sensitive to unobscured young stellar populations and dust attenuation, providing a biased tracer of the underlying stellar mass distribution. By leveraging the sensitivity of [[nircam|NIRCam]] and [[miri-instrument|MIRI (instrument)]] at wavelengths up to $5.6 \ \mu\text{m}$, JWST has successfully mapped the rest-frame near-infrared emission of these high-redshift galaxies, providing a much more reliable proxy for their total assembled stellar mass.

Analyses of JWST imaging reveal a striking morphological dichotomy across wavelengths. Massive star-forming galaxies ($10^{10} < M_\star / M_\odot < 10^{12}$) at $1.5 < z < 3$ that appear disk-dominated and clumpy in the rest-frame optical are shown to be significantly smoother and more bulge-dominated in the rest-frame near-infrared [Benton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974L..28B/abstract), [Boogaard et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969...27B/abstract). Additionally, early [[smacs-j0723|SMACS J0723]] imaging has led to the discovery of red spiral galaxies at cosmic noon ($z \sim 1-3$), unveiling a population that is surprisingly much more common at high redshift than in the local universe [Fudamoto et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L..24F/abstract).

Quantitative structural measurements underscore this paradigm shift. Within the central $1 \text{ kpc}$ of these galaxies, radial surface brightness profiles steepen dramatically at longer wavelengths, shifting from a slope of $\sim 0.3 \text{ dex}^{-1}$ at $1.5 \ \mu\text{m}$ to $\sim 1.4 \text{ dex}^{-1}$ at $4.4 \ \mu\text{m}$ [Benton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974L..28B/abstract). Consequently, the total flux contained within the central kiloparsec is approximately seven times higher in the F444W filter than in the F150W filter [Benton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974L..28B/abstract). Effective radii ($R_e$) measured in the mid-infrared are also consistently smaller than those measured in the near-infrared. For example, dust-rich samples cross-matched with the [[atacama-large-millimeter-array-alma|Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA)]] yield an average size ratio of $R_e(\text{F560W})/R_e(\text{F160W}) \approx 0.7$ [Boogaard et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969...27B/abstract). The Sérsic index ($n$) heavily reflects this structural concentration; at $z > 1$, the rest-frame near-infrared Sérsic indices of star-forming galaxies are systematically higher than their optical counterparts, revealing the presence of dense, mature central bulges heavily obscured by dust [Martorano et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...694A..76M/abstract). 

These findings necessitate a revision of models concerning galaxy compaction and quenching. Previously, it was uncertain whether the dense cores characteristic of quiescent galaxies were assembled before, during, or after their star formation ceased. JWST observations demonstrate that the central surface density ratio between quiescent and star-forming galaxies drops precipitously from a factor of $\sim 10$ at optical wavelengths to $\sim 1$ in the rest-frame near-infrared [Benton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974L..28B/abstract). This indicates that the high central stellar densities traditionally associated with galaxy quenching are already fully established during the active star-forming phase. Ultimately, this supports a sequence of structural evolution in which gas inflows fuel extreme, centrally concentrated starbursts—rapidly building a massive bulge and driving galaxy compaction—well before the galaxy definitively quenches its star formation [Benton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974L..28B/abstract), [Ding et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993...91D/abstract).

## Obscured Active Galactic Nuclei and 'Little Red Dots'

One of the most surprising discoveries enabled by the James Webb Space Telescope has been the identification of a ubiquitous population of compact, highly obscured sources at high redshifts, colloquially termed "Little Red Dots" (LRDs) [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240710760L/abstract). Many of these objects were entirely undetected by previous deep optical surveys, including those conducted by the [[hubble-space-telescope-deep-surveys|Hubble Space Telescope Deep Surveys]], owing to heavy dust attenuation with visual extinctions reaching $A_V \sim 2-5$ [Barrufet et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522..449B/abstract), [Smail et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958...36S/abstract). Through [[nircam|NIRCam]] and [[miri-instrument|MIRI (instrument)]] imaging, LRDs are characterized by a distinct V-shaped spectral energy distribution (SED): a relatively flat, blue rest-frame ultraviolet continuum combined with a steeply rising, red rest-frame optical continuum [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240710760L/abstract). 

Following their photometric discovery, significant debate emerged in the literature regarding the primary power source of LRDs. While some studies initially proposed that they could be extremely compact, dusty star-forming galaxies, spectroscopic follow-up has increasingly favored the interpretation that LRDs are predominantly dust-reddened broad-line active galactic nuclei (AGNs) [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240710760L/abstract), [Tripodi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204983T/abstract). Theoretical modeling suggests that their unique SEDs can be reproduced by an AGN embedded within an extended distribution of dust and gas. In this scenario, a lack of small dust grains flattens the UV extinction curve, while cooler dust emission dominates the infrared, shifting the energy peak into the [[mid-infrared-astronomy|Mid-Infrared Astronomy]] regime [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240710760L/abstract). 

Spectroscopic observations with [[nirspec|NIRSpec]] have confirmed the AGN nature of several LRDs by revealing broad Balmer emission lines (such as H$\beta$) alongside high-ionization transitions. A notable example is CANUCS-LRD-z8.6 at a redshift of $z=8.63$, which exhibits both broad emission lines and very high electron temperatures characteristic of an AGN in a highly metal-poor environment ($Z < 0.1 Z_\odot$) [Tripodi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204983T/abstract). Moreover, JWST variability studies in fields such as COSMOS are revealing unexpected Type 2 AGN variability in the F356W band, which challenges traditional unified models of active galactic nuclei [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250814350S/abstract).

The identification of LRDs as obscured AGNs suggests a significantly higher number density of supermassive black holes in the early universe than predicted by pre-JWST theoretical models. Furthermore, the inferred black hole masses challenge standard accretion paradigms. For instance, the central black hole in CANUCS-LRD-z8.6 has an estimated mass of $M_{\rm BH} \approx 10^8 M_\odot$, which is substantially over-massive compared to its host galaxy when evaluated against local scaling relations [Tripodi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204983T/abstract). For instance, observations using GA-NIFS have revealed a 7-kpc extended [O III] halo around the sub-Eddington quasar J1342+0928 at $z=7.54$, suggesting significant past outflow events that further challenge simple super-Eddington accretion paradigms for early SMBHs [Trefoloni et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250907064T/abstract). These findings indicate that early supermassive black holes either formed from massive seeds or underwent periods of extreme, super-Eddington accretion, evolving more rapidly than their host galaxies [Tripodi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204983T/abstract).

The abundance of these obscured AGNs underscores the critical role of AGN feedback in early galaxy evolution. JWST's high-resolution capabilities have provided unprecedented views of how AGN radiation and winds interact with the interstellar medium. In both high-redshift sources and local analogues, JWST has resolved energetic, multi-phase outflows driven by AGNs. For instance, broad [O III] emission has been used to detect ionized gas outflows in a young starburst galaxy at $z=7.212$ [Ren et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.544.4722R/abstract). Observations of nearby ultra-luminous infrared galaxies also reveal biconical outflows of warm molecular gas reaching maximum projected velocities of up to $3000\ \text{km s}^{-1}$ [Yui Dan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241205859Y/abstract). Additionally, [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] has shown that intense AGN radiation fields actively modify surrounding dust populations, destroying small dust grains and suppressing polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) emission (e.g., the $3.3\ \mu\text{m}$ feature) within the central kiloparsec of the host galaxy [Lai et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941L..36L/abstract), [Lai et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957L..26L/abstract). These radiation-driven dusty outflows effectively displace material from the galactic nucleus, potentially quenching central star formation and subsequently altering the evolutionary trajectory and visibility of the galaxy over cosmic time [Nakazato et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241207598N/abstract).

The power of JWST extends to detailed structural mapping of known active galaxies. For instance, high-resolution integral field spectroscopy of Cygnus A reveals a density-stratified narrow-line region shaped by jet-ISM interactions and biconical outflows bearing rotational signatures [Ogle et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983...98O/abstract). In parallel, combined HST and JWST imaging has facilitated the identification of RBH-1, a runaway supermassive black hole candidate likely ejected from its host star-forming galaxy at $z \sim 0.96$ due to a gravitational-wave-driven merger [Islam et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260118986I/abstract).

## The Interstellar Medium and Molecular Gas Physics

The James Webb Space Telescope has provided unprecedented insights into the Interstellar Medium (ISM) and the physics of molecular gas, primarily utilizing the high-resolution [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] of [[nirspec|NIRSpec]] and [[miri-instrument|MIRI (instrument)]]. These instruments allow for the detailed mapping of molecular hydrogen (${\rm H}_2$) and Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs) across diverse galactic environments, bridging the gap between local ISM studies and the conditions of the early universe.

A major breakthrough in understanding star formation in primitive environments has been the detection of rotational ${\rm H}_2$ emission in the extremely metal-poor star-forming galaxy Leo P, which possesses a metallicity of only $\sim 3\%$ solar [Telford et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241021368T/abstract). Prior to JWST, cold molecular gas had not been definitively observed below $7\%$ solar metallicity, leading to theoretical tensions regarding whether early stars formed in predominantly atomic or molecular gas. The MIRI Medium Resolution Spectrometer (MRS) detected compact ($r \le 2.6\ \text{pc}$) ${\rm H}_2$ clouds surrounding an O-type star in Leo P. In contrast, deep observations with the [[atacama-large-millimeter-array-alma|Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA)]] yielded stringent upper limits on carbon monoxide (CO) emission [Telford et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241021368T/abstract), [Telford et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025Natur.642..900T/abstract). This establishes JWST's unique sensitivity to molecular gas in highly pristine environments where traditional CO tracers fail.

JWST has also resolved the complex interplay between PAH emission, star formation, and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback. In the local luminous infrared galaxy NGC 7469, high-resolution observations reveal that AGN radiation significantly alters the ISM on $\sim 100\ \text{pc}$ scales. While the circumnuclear starburst ring exhibits robust PAH emission, regions in the immediate vicinity of the AGN show an overall suppression of PAH features, particularly the $3.3\,\mu{\rm m}$ neutral PAH band [Lai et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941L..36L/abstract), [Lai et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957L..26L/abstract). Changes in the $3.4/3.3\,\mu{\rm m}$ aliphatic-to-aromatic ratio indicate that smaller dust grains are preferentially destroyed by the AGN's hard radiation field [Lai et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957L..26L/abstract). Conversely, in highly obscured merging systems like VV 114, JWST has mapped extended, filamentary PAH emission stochastically excited by UV and optical photons, accounting for up to half of the galaxy's $7.7\,\mu{\rm m}$ luminosity [Evans et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...940L...8E/abstract).

Warm ${\rm H}_2$ transitions act as powerful tracers of shocks and multiphase outflows. In the shocked intergalactic medium of Stephan's Quintet, MIRI imaging demonstrates that the F770W and F1000W filters are dominated by a combination of pure rotational lines of ${\rm H}_2$ and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) emission, mapping the dissipation of kinetic energy across a 45 kpc filament [Appleton et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951..104A/abstract). Furthermore, spectroscopic observations of the ultra-luminous infrared galaxy F08572+3915 NW have unveiled a massive, biconical outflow of warm molecular gas reaching a median projected velocity of $1100\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}$ and maximums up to $3000\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}$ extending out to $1.4\ \text{kpc}$. This demonstrates that quasar-driven winds can rapidly accelerate dense ISM material beyond the galactic escape velocity [Yui Dan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241205859Y/abstract).

Despite these advances, certain ISM dynamics remain poorly understood and actively debated. For example, joint [[hst-observations|HST observations]] and JWST imaging of the nuclear star cluster in the grand-design spiral NGC 628 reveal a $200 \times 400\ \text{pc}$ central cavity completely devoid of gas and dust. The exact physical mechanism responsible for dynamically clearing or evacuating this region remains unclear in the current literature, with multiple proposed scenarios failing to fully explain the observed mid-infrared photometry and structure [Hoyer et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944L..25H/abstract).

## Gravitational Lensing as a Natural Telescope

The James Webb Space Telescope leverages massive galaxy clusters as natural telescopes, utilizing gravitational lensing to magnify and resolve the earliest star-forming systems. The observatory's Early Release Observations prominently featured the galaxy cluster [[smacs-j0723|SMACS J0723]] (SMACS J0723.3-7327) at a redshift of $z=0.39$ [Alden et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RNAAS...6..247A/abstract), [Pontoppidan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...936L..14P/abstract). This massive cluster, previously cataloged by the Hubble Space Telescope through the RELICS (Reionization Lensing Cluster Survey) program, produces intense gravitational fields that distort background light into elongated lensed arcs [Golubchik et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...14G/abstract).

To accurately infer the intrinsic properties of these magnified background galaxies, researchers must construct detailed magnification maps and mass density models. Prior to JWST, parametric models based entirely on [[hst-observations|HST observations]] successfully identified multiple-image families and provided baseline mass models intended to facilitate future refinement once JWST data became available [Golubchik et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...14G/abstract). JWST's unprecedented spatial resolution and near-infrared sensitivity subsequently allowed for the actual refinement of these lens models by revealing finer, previously obscured substructures within the lensed arcs. This lensing boost, often quantified by a magnification factor $\mu$, has allowed instruments like [[nircam|NIRCam]] to push the redshift frontier, identifying dropout candidates at $z \approx 11-20$ behind SMACS J0723 that would otherwise remain far below the detection threshold of unlensed deep fields [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942L...9Y/abstract). These observations have revealed that $z > 9$ candidate galaxies in the SMACS 0723 field are exceptionally small, with intrinsic radii typically less than 0.5 kpc [Adams et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.4755A/abstract). 

Gravitational lensing also aids in characterizing dust-obscured "HST-dark" galaxies during cosmic noon ($z \sim 2-3$). For instance, a magnification of $\mu \approx 1.9$ in the Abell 2744 cluster permitted JWST to spatially resolve an edge-on dusty spiral with an intrinsic stellar mass of $\log(M_*/M_\odot) \sim 11.3$, demonstrating the utility of lensing for [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|High-Redshift Galaxy Observations]] [Kokorev et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945L..25K/abstract).

Beyond static galaxy properties, JWST exploits gravitational lensing for high-redshift time-domain astronomy. When background sources cross a cluster's lensing caustic—the region where theoretical magnification formally approaches infinity—individual stars can temporarily become luminous enough for direct detection. Repeated JWST observations of the MACS J0416.1-2403 cluster field revealed 12 such transients in highly magnified arcs. These sources represent individual stars at $z \approx 0.94-2.09$ whose extreme red colors and faint intrinsic magnitudes placed them well beyond the reach of prior optical surveys [Yan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..269...43Y/abstract). 

Furthermore, strongly lensed supernovae observed by JWST offer a vital cosmological probe. Because light from multiply-imaged supernovae travels along paths of different lengths and gravitational potentials, measuring their arrival time delays can independently constrain the Hubble constant, $H_0$. Predictive models have demonstrated that JWST's near-infrared sensitivity is capable of detecting lensed Type Ia and core-collapse supernovae with time delays of less than five years behind massive cluster lenses, providing a novel pathway to resolve current cosmological tensions [Petrushevska et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ARep...62..917P/abstract). In this context, JWST has facilitated the discovery of SN 2023aeax at $z = 2.15$, one of the most distant spectroscopically confirmed Type Ia supernovae, offering a critical tool to test for intrinsic Type Ia supernova luminosity evolution at high redshifts [Pierel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241111953P/abstract). Furthermore, JWST's capacity to investigate distant transients is exemplified by the spectroscopic identification of SN 2024aihh at $z=1.67$. As the most distant spectroscopically identified gamma-ray burst supernova (GRB-SN), these observations demonstrate the observatory's unique ability to study the GRB-SN connection nearly 10 billion years ago [van Hoof et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251211572V/abstract).

## Open Questions: Cosmological Tensions and Dark Matter Candidates

The discovery by the James Webb Space Telescope of an unexpectedly high number density of luminous, massive galaxies at high redshifts ($z > 10$) has prompted intense debate regarding the validity of the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology. Observations indicating the presence of galaxies with stellar masses approaching $10^9\ M_\odot$ within the first 300 to 500 Myr after the Big Bang challenge pre-launch theoretical models and extrapolate far beyond earlier [[hst-observations|HST observations]] [Carniani et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Natur.633..318C/abstract), [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942L...9Y/abstract). To assemble such massive systems so rapidly within a $\Lambda$CDM framework requires exceptionally high baryon-to-star conversion efficiencies, leading some researchers to propose modifications to the dark matter sector or early-universe physics.

Among the proposed cosmological solutions, supermassive primordial black holes have been suggested as early seeds for galaxy and quasar formation, potentially explaining the observed abundance of early massive systems and yielding a log-normal mass spectrum [Dolgov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231000671D/abstract), [Dolgov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240106882D/abstract). Alternatively, early galaxy formation could be enhanced by axion dark matter. Models invoking axion-like particles (ALPs) with a kinetic misalignment mechanism and masses in the range of $10^{-22} \text{ eV} < m_a < 10^{-19} \text{ eV}$ can generically increase early structure formation while remaining consistent with existing small-scale constraints [Bird et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230710302B/abstract). Some alternative interpretations of JWST's high-redshift results and observations of old stellar populations propose a significantly older universe—potentially up to 26 Gyr—to properly accommodate the rapid emergence of early massive galaxies and mature globular clusters [Llorente de Andrés et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AmJAA..11....1L/abstract). Conversely, the same abundance of high-redshift galaxies has been utilized to place stricter bounds on warm dark matter (WDM) models, with JWST rest-UV luminosity functions setting a lower limit on the thermal relic particle mass of $m_x \geq 3.2\ \text{keV}$ [Liu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968...79L/abstract). Further complicating the cosmological picture is the controversial finding of a potential non-random distribution in galaxy spin directions observed in JWST deep fields. Quantitative analysis suggests an asymmetry in rotation direction that aligns with some large-scale digital sky survey results, challenging assumptions of isotropy [Shamir et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PASA...41...38S/abstract).

Currently, the literature remains strongly divided, and there is no consensus that these observations strictly require new physics. Many astrophysicists argue that the apparent tension can be resolved by reevaluating galaxy formation astrophysics rather than abandoning established cosmology. A primary uncertainty is the mass-to-light ratio; JWST data demonstrate that the $M/L_{\rm UV}$ ratio can vary by two orders of magnitude for a given luminosity at $z > 7$, potentially biasing earlier cosmic stellar-mass density estimates by up to a factor of six [Santini et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942L..27S/abstract). Furthermore, models incorporating bursty star formation histories or a top-heavy stellar initial mass function (IMF) can produce highly luminous rest-UV emission from a much lower underlying total stellar mass [Woodrum et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PNAS..12117375W/abstract), [Prada et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230411911P/abstract).

Observational systematics also play a major role in this debate. Cosmic variance heavily impacts high-redshift luminosity functions, as early massive galaxies are expected to be strongly clustered, making small-area deep fields unrepresentative of the cosmic average [Steinhardt et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923....8S/abstract). Additionally, attempts to modify $\Lambda$CDM via power-spectrum enhancements to match JWST mass functions often conflict with established rest-UV luminosity functions at $z \approx 7-10$ from [[hubble-space-telescope-deep-surveys|Hubble Space Telescope Deep Surveys]] [Sabti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvL.132f1002S/abstract). Finally, spectroscopic follow-up has revealed that some extreme high-redshift candidates are actually lower-redshift dusty interlopers or powered by obscured active galactic nuclei (such as "Little Red Dots") [Arrabal Haro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Natur.622..707A/abstract), [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240710760L/abstract). 

Beyond the high-redshift universe, JWST is playing a pivotal role in investigating the Hubble tension—the discrepancy between the local measurement of the Hubble constant ($H_0$) and its value inferred from the cosmic microwave background. By observing local distance indicators at high infrared resolution, JWST mitigates the effects of dust extinction and stellar crowding. Programs such as the Chicago-Carnegie Hubble Program have utilized JWST to calibrate the Tip of the Red Giant Branch (TRGB) and J-region Asymptotic Giant Branch (JAGB) stars in nearby Type Ia supernovae host galaxies [Freedman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985..203F/abstract). Detailed measurements, such as constraints on the color dependence of the TRGB in near-infrared filters, empirically demonstrate its utility as a highly precise standardizable candle [Hoyt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..111H/abstract). These JWST observations yield local $H_0$ values (e.g., $68.81 \pm 1.79\ \text{km s}^{-1}\ \text{Mpc}^{-1}$ from TRGB) that are increasingly consistent with the standard $\Lambda$CDM model without requiring new physics [Freedman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985..203F/abstract).

## Multi-wavelength Synergies and Future Surveys

The exquisite sensitivity and spatial resolution of the James Webb Space Telescope are most powerful when combined with multi-wavelength observations and large-scale sky maps. Because JWST is uniquely optimized for deep, high-resolution [[near-infrared-astronomy|Near-Infrared Astronomy]] and mid-infrared spectroscopy, it relies on complementary facilities to provide a complete picture of galaxy assembly, from the cold interstellar medium to the large-scale cosmological web. The essential complementarity between HST and JWST has been uniquely demonstrated in regions like the Hubble Ultra Deep Field: while Hubble excels at probing unobscured young stars in the rest-frame ultraviolet, JWST unveils the older stellar populations and dusty starbursts, together painting a comprehensive picture of galaxy evolution [Windhorst et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241001187W/abstract). This multi-wavelength approach is exemplified by programs like the [[phangs-survey|PHANGS survey]]-HST survey, where HST UV-optical imaging provides a comprehensive census of star clusters that seeds detailed investigations into embedded stellar populations with JWST [Lee et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..258...10L/abstract).

**Submillimeter Synergies with ALMA**
The [[atacama-large-millimeter-array-alma|Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA)]] is a natural counterpart to JWST. While JWST traces stellar mass distributions, rest-frame optical emission lines, and warm dust, ALMA probes the cold molecular gas and far-infrared dust continuum. This combination has been critical for characterizing massive, heavily dust-obscured galaxies at $z = 2-8$ that were previously invisible to Hubble (often termed "HST-dark" galaxies) [Kokorev et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945L..25K/abstract), [Barrufet et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522..449B/abstract). Locally, joint JWST and ALMA observations have resolved multiphase gas interactions on sub-kiloparsec scales; in Stephan's Quintet, for instance, ALMA maps cold CO(2-1) gas while JWST traces highly turbulent, shocked $\mathrm{H}_2$ emission [Appleton et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951..104A/abstract). At the highest redshifts, ALMA detections of far-infrared fine-structure lines (such as [O III] $88\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) directly complement JWST's rest-frame optical spectroscopy of [O III] $\lambda 5007$, allowing for rigorous constraints on the physical conditions and metallicities of early H II regions [Moriwaki et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481L..84M/abstract). To fill the critical wavelength gap between JWST and ALMA (e.g., $24-240\ \mu\text{m}$), mission concepts like PRIMA are being proposed to study high-redshift black hole growth and dust production [Faisst et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250901674F/abstract). Broadly, the Kavli-IAU Workshop on Global Coordination has strongly recommended a future space-based far-infrared observatory to complement JWST by fully characterizing feedback and galaxy evolution from $30\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ to millimeter wavelengths [Organizing Committee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240907570O/abstract).

**Radio and 21-cm Interferometry**
To fully reconstruct the timeline and topology of the Epoch of Reionization, JWST data must be coupled with 21-cm interferometry from arrays like the Hydrogen Epoch of Reionization Array (HERA) and the Square Kilometre Array (SKA). JWST provides a direct census of the ionizing photon budget by measuring the ultraviolet luminosity functions and star formation efficiencies of early galaxies, while 21-cm observations map the large-scale distribution of neutral hydrogen bubbles [Beardsley et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800..128B/abstract), [Park et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.3891P/abstract). Joint analyses of JWST luminosity functions and 21-cm power spectra significantly reduce astrophysical degeneracies regarding the star formation efficiencies of faint galaxies [Dhandha et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.542.2292D/abstract). In the radio continuum, future facilities like the [[ngvla|ngVLA]] and SKA will target JWST-discovered high-redshift active galactic nuclei (AGNs). For example, radio signatures from the $\approx 8 \times 10^7\ M_{\odot}$ supermassive black hole candidate GHZ9 at $z \sim 10$ will help isolate AGN-driven emission from host galaxy star formation [Latif et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.tmp..238L/abstract).

**Context from Upcoming Wide-Field Surveys**
Because JWST operates with a relatively small field of view, its deep extragalactic surveys are fundamentally limited by cosmic variance—particularly when measuring the abundance of massive, highly clustered galaxies at early cosmic times [Steinhardt et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923....8S/abstract). This limitation will be overcome by upcoming wide-field surveys from the Roman Space Telescope, the [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]], and the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]]. Synthetic catalog modeling predicts that a $1\,\mathrm{deg}^2$ ultra-deep field with Roman could detect over $10^4$ galaxies at $z > 7$ down to $m_{\mathrm{AB}} \approx 30$ [Drakos et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926..194D/abstract). While Euclid and Roman provide the vast cosmological volume necessary to discover rare, extremely luminous early galaxies and strongly lensed systems, the Rubin Observatory will revolutionize our view of the time-domain and low-surface-brightness universe [Brough et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200111067B/abstract). Further enhancing this wide-field strategy, the planned CASTOR space telescope—a 1m-class observatory—will bridge the crucial UV-optical gap in the post-Hubble era alongside Roman and Euclid [Cheng et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167..178C/abstract). Additionally, the upcoming [[china-space-station-telescope-csst|China Space Station Telescope (CSST)]] will operate as a powerful Stage IV survey facility, performing multi-band imaging and slitless spectroscopy over vast areas to constrain dark energy and dark matter while identifying targets for JWST follow-up [Gong et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025SCPMA..6880402G/abstract). Together, these wide-area observatories will serve as foundational target registries, identifying the most extreme and transformative cosmic sources for JWST to subsequently characterize with targeted, high-resolution spectroscopy.
