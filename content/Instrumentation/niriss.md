The Near-Infrared Imager and Slitless Spectrograph (NIRISS) is a primary science instrument aboard the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]], designed to provide versatile imaging and spectroscopic capabilities across the [[near-infrared-astronomy|Near-Infrared Astronomy]] wavelength range. Optimized to balance high-multiplexing survey speed with specialized [[high-resolution-spectroscopy|High-resolution spectroscopy]] modes, NIRISS builds upon the legacy of earlier space-based [[slitless-spectroscopy|Slitless spectroscopy]] programs to act as a highly efficient "redshift machine." By dispersing light without a traditional entrance slit, NIRISS enables unbiased, large-scale spectroscopic surveys of emission-line galaxies across cosmic time. This approach overcomes the cosmic variance and photometric pre-selection biases inherent to traditional targeted spectroscopy, allowing astronomers to identify faint, high-redshift populations that would otherwise be missed [Weiner et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1209.1405W/abstract), [Malkan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250900596M/abstract). To achieve its scientific goals, the instrument operates in four primary modes: Wide-Field Slitless Spectroscopy (WFSS), Single Object Slitless Spectroscopy (SOSS), Aperture Masking Interferometry (AMI), and standard wide-field imaging. 

The diverse operational modes of NIRISS drive transformative findings across a wide range of astrophysical domains, from the Local Group to the epoch of reionization. In deep-field programs, NIRISS is frequently operated in parallel with [[nircam|NIRCam]] to expand the total footprint for multi-band spectral energy distribution fitting. This synergy refines strong gravitational lensing mass models in massive galaxy clusters and enables highly precise, crowded-field photometry of resolved stellar populations, routinely reaching the theoretical hydrogen-burning limit in nearby dwarf galaxies and globular clusters [Bezanson et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221204026B/abstract), [Weisz et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..268...15W/abstract). For [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|High-Redshift Galaxy Observations]], the WFSS mode utilizes orthogonal [[grism-spectroscopy|Grism spectroscopy]] to disentangle overlapping spectra [Malkan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250900596M/abstract) and map the intrinsic physical conditions—such as ionization parameters and metallicities—of distant galaxies [Shen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241023349S/abstract), [Shen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980L..45S/abstract). Furthermore, the instrument's high-contrast capabilities are realized through AMI, which uses a seven-hole non-redundant mask to achieve true imaging at the diffraction limit ($\sim 65\ \text{mas}$), providing an ideal tool for probing the complex accretion flows of active galactic nuclei. Conversely, the SOSS mode is highly optimized to deliver the ultra-high-precision time-series data necessary for exoplanet transit spectroscopy.

## Instrument Overview and Design Philosophy

The Near-Infrared Imager and Slitless Spectrograph (NIRISS) aboard the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] is designed to provide versatile imaging and spectroscopic capabilities across the [[near-infrared-astronomy|Near-Infrared Astronomy]]. Its design philosophy emphasizes a balance between high-multiplexing survey speed and specialized, [[high-resolution-spectroscopy|High-resolution spectroscopy]] observing modes. NIRISS achieves this through four primary configurations, most notably Wide-Field [[slitless-spectroscopy|Slitless spectroscopy]], Aperture Masking Interferometry, and Single Object Slitless Spectroscopy.

The Wide-Field Slitless Spectroscopy (WFSS) mode utilizes [[low-resolution-observations|Low-Resolution Observations]] orthogonal grisms to transform NIRISS into a highly efficient "redshift machine." This concept builds directly on early theoretical frameworks, such as the proposed "Baryon Oscillation Probe," which advocated using dispersive slitless imaging to conduct the massive, high-multiplex galaxy redshift surveys necessary to constrain dark energy [Glazebrook et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005NewAR..49..374G/abstract). Unlike targeted multi-object spectrographs that require prior photometric selection, [[grism-spectroscopy|Grism spectroscopy]] from space allows for unbiased, large-scale surveys of emission-line galaxies. This approach is critical for identifying faint, low-mass populations at redshifts $1 < z < 3.5$ that are frequently missed by traditional photometric dropout techniques, thereby overcoming cosmic variance in deep fields [Weiner et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1209.1405W/abstract), [Malkan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250900596M/abstract). The use of orthogonal dispersion directions is a key design choice to disentangle overlapping spectra in crowded regions. However, the slitless design introduces specific data reduction challenges: because the spectral line spread function is convolved with the spatial profile of the source, neglecting "morphological broadening" can lead to a systematic flux underestimation of $\approx 30\%$ [He et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960L..13H/abstract). Furthermore, the instrument's dispersed zodiacal light backgrounds exhibit $\sim 5\%$ spatial fluctuations and systematic artifacts (such as the "light saber" effect), which necessitate precise empirical background subtraction to ensure accurate flux calibration [Hviding et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..220H/abstract).

For high-contrast applications, the Aperture Masking Interferometry (AMI) mode employs a seven-hole non-redundant mask. This transforms the full telescope aperture into an interferometric array, enabling true imaging—independent of prior geometric assumptions—at a spatial resolution of $\sim 65\ \mathrm{mas}$ [Ford et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783...73F/abstract). Operating primarily with specific filters (e.g., the wide-band F277W, and medium-band F380M, F430M, and F480M), AMI achieves a binary point source contrast of $\sim 10^{-4}$ at small angular separations. This mode is uniquely optimized for probing complex, extended accretion flows around active galactic nuclei (AGN) and resolving tight binary systems where standard [[direct-imaging-astronomy|Direct imaging (astronomy)]] is otherwise diffraction-limited [Ford et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783...73F/abstract).

The Single Object Slitless Spectroscopy (SOSS) mode is tailored for ultra-high-precision time-series observations, primarily targeting exoplanet transit spectroscopy. The SOSS design provides the ultra-high-precision data necessary to measure subtle exoplanetary atmospheric signatures, such as the transmission spectra of sub-Neptunes. The rigorous data treatments enabled by SOSS have been central to resolving debates regarding exoplanet atmospheric compositions; for example, reanalyses of SOSS spectra of the sub-Neptune K2-18 b confirmed the presence of $\mathrm{CH}_4$ but found no statistically significant evidence for $\mathrm{CO}_2$ or dimethyl sulfide (DMS), revising the interpretation of the planet from a "hycean" world to an oxygen-poor mini-Neptune [Schmidt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250118477S/abstract), [Schmidt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....170..298S/abstract). 

Finally, standard imaging with NIRISS is frequently executed in parallel with [[nircam|NIRCam]] in deep treasury programs (e.g., UNCOVER, CANUCS). This parallel imaging philosophy expands the total survey area for multi-band spectral energy distribution fitting, which has been shown to improve both the scatter and catastrophic outlier rate of photometric redshifts by a factor of 2 to 3 in strong lensing cluster fields [Suess et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240413132S/abstract), [Bezanson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974...92B/abstract).

## Historical Development of Space-Based Slitless Spectroscopy

Space-based [[slitless-spectroscopy|Slitless spectroscopy]] leverages the exceptionally low [[near-infrared-astronomy|Near-Infrared Astronomy]] background to perform unbiased redshift surveys without the need for photometric pre-selection [Weiner et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1209.1405W/abstract). The instrument design of NIRISS builds directly upon a legacy of [[grism-spectroscopy|Grism spectroscopy]] programs, which evolved from serendipitous observations to high-precision treasury surveys. Early space-based slitless capabilities were demonstrated by the Space Telescope Imaging Spectrograph (STIS) and [[nicmos|NICMOS]] on the Hubble Space Telescope (HST). STIS imaging and spectroscopy provided some of the deepest-ever UV-optical datasets in regions like the Hubble Deep Field South (HDF-South), setting the fundamental sensitivity benchmarks that subsequent JWST deep-field parallel programs strive to exceed [Gardner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AJ....119..486G/abstract). Concurrent parallel HST surveys successfully measured the comoving number densities of H$\alpha$ and [O II] emitting galaxies out to $z \sim 1.7$ [Teplitz et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJS..146..209T/abstract). Simultaneously, infrared observatories such as [[akari|AKARI]] proved the viability of mapping wide-field near-infrared spectral features, successfully isolating polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and Br$\alpha$ emission in extended local galaxies, albeit at a lower spatial resolution of $\sim 5''$ [Kondo et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...751L..18K/abstract).

The methodology matured significantly with the installation of the [[wide-field-camera-3|Wide Field Camera 3]] (WFC3) on HST. The WFC3 Infrared Spectroscopic Parallel (WISP) survey utilized the G102 and WFC3/G141 grism elements to gather slitless spectra in pure-parallel mode. Reaching $5\sigma$ emission-line detection limits of $\sim 5 \times 10^{-17}$ erg s$^{-1}$ cm$^{-2}$, the WISP survey proved highly effective at identifying sub-$L^*$ star-forming galaxies at $0.3 < z < 3$, recording median star formation rates of $4 M_{\odot}$ yr$^{-1}$ [Atek et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...723..104A/abstract). 

Moving beyond parallel mode, the community transitioned to dedicated, contiguous mapping with the [[3d-hst-survey|3D-HST survey]]. Covering 625 arcmin$^2$ primarily with the WFC3/G141 grism, 3D-HST extracted rest-frame optical spectra for approximately 7,000 galaxies at $1 < z < 3.5$. This survey established space-based grism spectroscopy as a powerful "redshift machine," achieving redshift precisions of $\sigma(z) = 0.0034(1+z)$ (equivalent to $\sigma(v) \approx 1000$ km s$^{-1}$) while retaining the high spatial resolution necessary to measure the physical extent of emission lines [Brammer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJS..200...13B/abstract), [Weiner et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1209.1405W/abstract).

The analytical frameworks developed for these legacy surveys laid the operational groundwork for NIRISS on the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]]. Unlike traditional slit spectroscopy, slitless wide-field spectroscopy is highly sensitive to the morphological footprint of the source. If the spatial extent of a target is not accurately modeled, morphological broadening within the dispersed spectrum can lead to a systematic underestimation of line fluxes by $\sim 30\%$ [He et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960L..13H/abstract). Furthermore, the unprecedented sensitivity of NIRISS has required new, rigorous background subtraction techniques to mitigate spatially dependent structures and $\sim 5\%$ background artifacts—such as the "light saber" effect—that limit data quality in wide-field slitless modes [Hviding et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..220H/abstract). Building on the foundation of HST and AKARI, NIRISS incorporates these advanced data reduction techniques to execute surveys with unparalleled depth and high-precision spatial resolution.

## Exoplanet Atmosphere Characterization via SOSS

The Single Object Slitless Spectroscopy (SOSS) mode of the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] is uniquely optimized for obtaining the high-precision time-series data required to characterize exoplanetary atmospheres. By utilizing [[grism-spectroscopy|Grism spectroscopy]] to disperse the light of a single target star across the detector without slit losses, SOSS allows astronomers to measure minute wavelength-dependent variations in stellar flux as a planet transits its host. This yields a highly precise transmission spectrum, which is critically sensitive to molecular absorption signatures such as methane ($\text{CH}_4$) and carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$) in the terminator region of the exoplanet's atmosphere. 

A prominent test case for the capabilities—and the interpretive challenges—of the SOSS mode is the $\sim 250$ K sub-Neptune K2-18 b. Early studies analyzing the planet's transmission spectrum reported the presence of $\text{CO}_2$ and dimethyl sulfide (DMS) alongside $\text{CH}_4$. These initial claims generated immense interest, as they led to the hypothesis that K2-18 b was a prototype for "Hycean planets"—a proposed class of temperate, volatile-rich worlds featuring a hydrogen-dominated envelope overlying a global liquid water ocean. Under this interpretation, the tentative detection of DMS was even highlighted as a potential biological marker.

However, the literature regarding the atmospheric composition of K2-18 b is currently in active conflict. A subsequent comprehensive reanalysis of the planet's transmission spectrum has strongly challenged the Hycean interpretation [Schmidt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250118477S/abstract), [Schmidt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....170..298S/abstract). This updated study leveraged both first- and second-order NIRISS SOSS observations combined with [[nirspec|NIRSpec]] G395H data. To rigorously assess the robustness of the prior claims, the investigators processed the data through 60 distinct reduction treatments and performed over 250 independent atmospheric retrievals. 

The exhaustive reanalysis confirmed the detection of $\text{CH}_4$ at a significance of $\approx 4\sigma$, constraining its volume mixing ratio to a range of $-2.14 \leq \log_{10}(\text{CH}_4) \leq -0.53$ [Schmidt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....170..298S/abstract). Crucially, however, the reanalysis found no statistically significant or reliable evidence for either $\text{CO}_2$ or DMS across the multitude of retrieval frameworks [Schmidt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250118477S/abstract), [Schmidt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....170..298S/abstract). 

The absence of robust $\text{CO}_2$ and DMS absorption fundamentally alters the physical interpretation of the planet. Photochemical-climate and interior models applied to the revised transmission spectrum demonstrate that the composition of K2-18 b is entirely consistent with an oxygen-poor mini-Neptune [Schmidt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250118477S/abstract), [Schmidt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....170..298S/abstract). This re-evaluation explicitly removes the requirement for a liquid water surface or biological activity to explain the observed data. The debate over K2-18 b underscores the exquisite sensitivity of the NIRISS SOSS mode while highlighting the critical necessity of employing diverse, independent data reduction and retrieval models when interpreting subtle exoplanetary signatures.

## High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution and Reionization

The NIRISS instrument is uniquely capable of conducting unbiased [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|High-Redshift Galaxy Observations]] by acting as a powerful "redshift machine." Its wide-field [[slitless-spectroscopy|Slitless spectroscopy]] (WFSS) mode allows for the discovery of faint emission-line galaxies without the need for photometric pre-selection [Malkan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250900596M/abstract). Large programs such as the PASSAGE survey have compiled over 10,000 near-infrared spectrograms, offering a highly multiplexed [[deep-spectroscopic-surveys|Deep spectroscopic surveys]] view of galaxy evolution [Malkan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250900596M/abstract). These unbiased wide-field emission-line catalogs exhibit strong technical and scientific synergy with the targeted multi-object capabilities of the JWST Near-Infrared Spectrograph ([[nirspec|NIRSpec]]). By utilizing NIRSpec's ability to conduct targeted spectroscopy over a 9 arcmin$^2$ field in the $0.6-5.0\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ wavelength range, researchers can systematically follow up on NIRISS discoveries to jointly uncover the detailed early stages of galaxy assembly [Giardino et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASPC..507..305G/abstract). However, the literature notes that single-line emitters can suffer from an $\sim 18\%$ misidentification rate when their spectroscopic redshifts are compared against purely photometric catalogs [Huberty et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260102220H/abstract). Deep H$\alpha$ luminosity functions derived from these surveys reach intrinsic luminosities of $L_{\mathrm{H\alpha}} \sim 10^{40.5} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$ at $z \sim 1.3$, corresponding to star formation rates as low as $\sim 0.4 \ M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$ [Pang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251223157P/abstract). To measure these emission-line fluxes accurately, data reduction pipelines must account for morphological broadening, which can systematically underestimate fluxes by $\sim 30\%$ if the spatial extent of the galaxy is ignored [He et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960L..13H/abstract). Complementing these wide-field surveys, dedicated observations of individual lenses have provided spatially resolved insights. For example, CANUCS survey observations of a lensed galaxy pair at $z=0.87$ utilized NIRISS H$\alpha$ maps to spatially resolve star-forming clumps, uncovering localized variations in "burstiness" and identifying stages of environmental quenching across the interacting system [Estrada-Carpenter et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532..577E/abstract).

[[grism-spectroscopy|Grism spectroscopy]] from NIRISS also probes the physical conditions of the interstellar medium at cosmic noon ($1.7 < z < 3.4$). Spectral observations frequently reveal elevated $[\text{O III}]/[\text{O II}]$ ($O_{32}$) flux ratios, indicating that early galaxies possess higher ionization parameters and lower gas-phase metallicities than local analogs at a given [[stellar-mass-function|Stellar Mass Function]] and star formation rate [Shen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241023349S/abstract), [Shen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980L..45S/abstract). Furthermore, the mass-metallicity relation has been extended down to the dwarf galaxy regime ($\log(M_*/M_\odot) \sim 6.9$) at these redshifts [He et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960L..13H/abstract). When aided by gravitational lensing, NIRISS WFSS enables spatially resolved mapping of these properties, occasionally challenging standard evolutionary models. While morphological studies of Milky Way progenitors at $z = 2-5$ generally support standard "inside-out" mass assembly—where the central $\sim 2 \text{ kpc}$ stellar mass remains constant while the outer regions grow [Tan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241207829T/abstract), [Tan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...994...94T/abstract)—spatially resolved metallicity maps reveal anomalies. For example, a $z = 3.06$ dwarf galaxy exhibits a strongly inverted (positive) radial metallicity gradient of $\Delta \log(\text{O/H}) / \Delta r = 0.165 \pm 0.023 \text{ dex kpc}^{-1}$, an outlier behavior hypothesized to result from metal-poor gas inflows driven by tidal torques [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L..16W/abstract). The frequency and origin of such inverted metallicity gradients remains an active area of debate.

In the epoch of reionization ($z > 6$), NIRISS is pivotal for constraining the first generations of stellar populations. Observations of the lensed $z = 9.1$ galaxy MACS1149-JD1 demonstrate a clear Lyman break in its continuum spectrum without detectable Ly$\alpha$ emission. Crucially, high-resolution NIRISS data failed to confirm the strong Balmer break previously inferred from [[spitzer-space-telescope|Spitzer Space Telescope]] imaging. Instead, the data reveal a complex assembly history, with an underlying older stellar component surrounded by highly compact, younger star-forming regions [Bradač et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961L..21B/abstract). Similarly, observations of the massive dusty merger MACS0416-Y1 at $z=8.3$ with NIRISS and NIRSpec revealed a dramatic star formation burst distributed across multiple clumps, possessing an interstellar medium with extreme dust conditions that is already as chemically evolved as star-forming galaxies at cosmic noon [Harshan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977L..36H/abstract). Further into the reionization era, NIRISS has uncovered environmental quenching and bursty star formation in interacting "Little Red Dot" pairs at $z \sim 7$ [Mérida et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250117925M/abstract), and has identified highly magnified, extreme metal-poor star complexes at $z \simeq 6.6$ with massive ionizing photon production efficiencies ($\log(\xi_{\text{ion}}) > 26 \text{ erg Hz}^{-1}$) [Vanzella et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...678A.173V/abstract). Together, these findings illustrate a chaotic early stage of galaxy evolution characterized by ongoing mergers, rapid chemical enrichment, and inside-out structural growth.

## Gravitational Lensing and Cluster Science

NIRISS leverages gravitational magnification from massive foreground galaxy clusters to study extremely faint background populations that would otherwise fall below the detection limit of the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]]. In [[deep-imaging-surveys|Deep imaging surveys]] treasury programs such as UNCOVER, CANUCS, and MegaScience, NIRISS is frequently operated in parallel with [[nircam|NIRCam]] to efficiently map extensive cluster fields and their surroundings [Bezanson et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221204026B/abstract), [Bezanson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974...92B/abstract). The sensitivity of NIRISS emission-line maps provides a powerful contrast to earlier [[spitzer-space-telescope|Spitzer Space Telescope]] and [[w-m-keck-observatory|W. M. Keck Observatory]] surveys, which highlighted that obscured starburst populations in high-redshift clusters often appear unremarkable in standard optical imaging, thus requiring deep infrared capabilities to uncover their true activity [Kocevski et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...736...38K/abstract).

**Refining Strong Lensing Models**
Accurate reconstructions of cluster mass distributions require robust strong lensing models, which depend on securing spectroscopic redshifts for large families of multiply-imaged galaxies. NIRISS [[grism-spectroscopy|Grism spectroscopy]] is highly efficient at this task because it captures rest-frame optical emission lines—such as [O II] $\lambda3727$, [O III] $\lambda\lambda4959, 5007$, and H$\alpha$—at $1 < z < 3$ without the need for photometric pre-selection [Rihtaršič et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240610332R/abstract), [Rihtaršič et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...696A..15R/abstract). In the cluster MACS J0416, the addition of NIRISS and [[nirspec|NIRSpec]] data yielded a $\sim 20\%$ increase in the number of secure spectroscopic systems, reducing the root-mean-square positional error of the strong lensing model to $\sim 0.52''$ [Rihtaršič et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...696A..15R/abstract). Similarly, in Webb's first deep field, SMACS J0723.3-7327, NIRISS grism observations were central to producing the first large spectroscopic redshift catalog, identifying over 150 secure redshifts and uncovering new galaxy overdensities at $z=1.1$, $1.4$, and $2.0$ [Noirot et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.1867N/abstract). These extensive observations directly aid in model refinement; precision improvements have been successfully achieved by mapping the critical curves of clusters like Abell 370 [Gledhill et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...973...77G/abstract). Furthermore, NIRISS wide-field observations provide [[deep-spectroscopic-surveys|Deep spectroscopic surveys]] that verify multiple image systems, as demonstrated in the Abell 2744 field, delivering crucial constraints for future mass models [Watson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250400823W/abstract). 

**Extremely Magnified Stars and Transients**
Caustic crossings and high-magnification regimes allow NIRISS to probe individual stars and transient events at cosmological distances. In the Abell 2744 cluster field, NIRISS pre-imaging enabled the discovery of a highly magnified blue supergiant star at $z=2.65$ situated near a fold caustic [Chen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...940L..54C/abstract). The instrument has also detected highly magnified transient events, including a supernova candidate at $z=3.47$ [Chen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022TNSAN.166....1C/abstract) and a highly magnified transient at $z=3.78$ [Chen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023TNSAN.199....1C/abstract). Furthermore, a spectacular near-Einstein ring formed by a $z=3.6$ star-forming galaxy lensed by a foreground elliptical was characterized within the NIRISS footprint, yielding magnification factors ranging from $2.6 < \mu < 8.4$ [Adams et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250403571A/abstract).

**Resolved Studies of High-Redshift Dwarfs**
By stretching the images of background galaxies, gravitational lensing combined with the spatial resolution of NIRISS allows for spatially resolved mapping of low-mass dwarf galaxies. For example, observations of the $z=3.06$ lensed dwarf galaxy GLASS-Zgrad1 behind Abell 2744 resolved the $\sim 10^{8.6} M_\odot$ system into $\gtrsim 50$ spatial resolution elements. This revealed a robust, inverted (positive) radial metallicity gradient:
$$ \frac{\Delta \log(\text{O/H})}{\Delta r} = 0.165 \pm 0.023 \text{ dex kpc}^{-1} $$
This observation deviates from standard "inside-out" chemical enrichment models and remains a subject of active debate, though it may be driven by tidal gas inflows induced by a nearby massive galaxy [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L..16W/abstract). 

NIRISS has also aided in the study of even more extreme lensed systems, such as LAP1 behind MACS J0416. LAP1 is a pristine Population III candidate star complex at $z=6.639$ amplified by a magnification $\mu \gtrsim 100$. NIRISS and NIRSpec observations show that metal lines such as [O III] are remarkably weak or absent, establishing a stringent oxygen abundance limit of $12 + \log(\text{O/H}) \lesssim 6.3$ [Vanzella et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...678A.173V/abstract). Together, these observations demonstrate that the combination of cluster magnification and wide-field slitless spectroscopy enables the characterization of galaxies with intrinsic stellar masses down to $\log(M_*/M_\odot) \approx 7$ [Revalski et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...40R/abstract), [He et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960L..13H/abstract).

## Aperture Masking Interferometry (AMI)

The Aperture Masking Interferometry (AMI) mode of the Near Infrared Imager and Slitless Spectrograph (NIRISS) on board the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] is designed to enable high-contrast imaging at the diffraction limit of the observatory. To achieve this, the instrument employs a seven-hole non-redundant mask that effectively transforms the telescope's full pupil into an interferometric array. While this mask reduces the total instrument throughput to $15\%$, it provides a critical advantage: each baseline between a pair of holes is geometrically unique, ensuring that the spatial frequencies sampled by the array are non-redundant [Ford et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783...73F/abstract). AMI observations are conducted using a selected suite of filters—specifically the wide-band F277W ($2.77\ \mu\text{m}$), and medium-band F380M ($3.8\ \mu\text{m}$), F430M ($4.3\ \mu\text{m}$), and F480M ($4.8\ \mu\text{m}$)—and are recorded on a detector with square pixels measuring $65\ \text{mas}$ per side across a $\sim 2' \times 2'$ field of view [Ford et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783...73F/abstract).

By utilizing interferometric closure phases, the AMI mode isolates intrinsic phase information, rendering it highly resilient to optical aberrations and pointing jitter that would otherwise degrade the telescope's point spread function. This capability allows NIRISS to achieve a true imaging resolution of $\sim 65\ \text{mas}$ without requiring prior assumptions regarding the underlying spatial geometry of the target [Ford et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783...73F/abstract). In practice, the mode delivers a binary point-source contrast of $\sim 10^{-4}$ at small angular separations. This deep contrast is ideally suited for the [[direct-imaging-astronomy|Direct imaging (astronomy)]] of close stellar companions, exoplanetary systems, and the dense, coalescing binary nuclei found in merging galaxies. These capabilities offer a substantial leap over contemporary ground-based optical and [[near-infrared-astronomy|Near-Infrared Astronomy]] interferometers [Ford et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783...73F/abstract).

A major scientific driver for the utilization of the AMI mode is the investigation of Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) and the mechanisms driving AGN feedback. [[high-resolution-spectroscopy|High-resolution spectroscopy]] mapping of nuclear kinematics has been a long-standing goal in extragalactic astronomy to understand the dynamical structure of galaxies and the role of central black holes [de Zeeuw et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASPC..291..205D/abstract). The extreme luminosities of AGN, combined with the exceptionally small angular sizes of their central accretion flows, present a notoriously difficult observational challenge. Through aperture masking, NIRISS can directly map extended structures within these dense environments. Technical models indicate that the AMI mode can reconstruct complex extended structure at a pixel-to-pixel contrast of $\sim 10^{-2}$ around an $L=7.5$ central point source in integrations lasting only a few minutes [Ford et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783...73F/abstract). Resolving these compact circumnuclear environments is critical for testing theoretical models of black hole fueling and the kinetic or radiative feedback that ultimately regulates host galaxy evolution. Furthermore, the high-resolution near-infrared structural maps produced by the NIRISS AMI mode act as a powerful complement to the cold gas and dust maps generated by submillimeter facilities such as ALMA, providing a comprehensive multi-wavelength view of supermassive black hole accretion and its impact on the surrounding interstellar medium [Ford et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783...73F/abstract).

## Resolved Stellar Populations in the Local Group

The unparalleled sensitivity and spatial resolution of the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] have opened a new observational regime for analyzing resolved stellar populations in the Local Group. Utilizing the Near-Infrared Imager and Slitless Spectrograph (NIRISS) in parallel with [[nircam|NIRCam]], observers can construct exceptionally deep color-magnitude diagrams of nearby star clusters and dwarf galaxies. These contemporary JWST efforts build directly upon the legacy of pioneering programs like the Panchromatic Hubble Andromeda Treasury ([[phat-survey|PHAT survey]]), which resolved millions of stars in Andromeda and set the methodological foundation for current parallel NIRISS and NIRCam observations in nearby dwarf galaxies and clusters [Dalcanton et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJS..200...18D/abstract).

As demonstrated by the JWST Resolved Stellar Populations Early Release Science program, NIRISS imaging effectively probes environments that span factors of $\sim 10^5$ in luminosity and surface brightness, and $\sim 10^4$ in distance [Weisz et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..268...15W/abstract). Observations of the Milky Way globular cluster M92, the ultra-faint dwarf galaxy Draco II, and the star-forming dwarf Wolf-Lundmark-Melotte (WLM) highlight the instrument's unprecedented depth. 

In M92, the resulting color-magnitude diagrams successfully reach the theoretical hydrogen-burning limit ($< 0.08 M_\odot$; $M_{F090W} \sim +13.6$). In Draco II, NIRISS resolves the lowest-mass stars yet observed outside the Milky Way ($0.09 M_\odot$; $M_{F090W} \sim +12.1$), while observations in WLM penetrate $\sim 1.5$ magnitudes below the oldest main-sequence turnoff ($M_{F090W} \sim +4.6$) [Weisz et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..268...15W/abstract). These deep photometric datasets provide crucial empirical anchors for refining stellar evolution models. Comparisons with theoretical PARSEC stellar isochrones yield a strong qualitative match, though current literature notes a mild tension where models appear $\sim 0.05$ magnitudes too blue compared to the M92 $F090W - F150W$ data, indicating an area where theoretical frameworks or empirical calibrations may require further tuning [Weisz et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..268...15W/abstract).

Accurate extraction of stellar fluxes in dense environments relies heavily on specialized software, such as the DOLPHOT point-source photometry package, which has been optimized with dedicated modules for NIRISS and NIRCam [Weisz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271...47W/abstract). DOLPHOT enables highly precise measurements, with temporal photometric variations typically constrained to $\lesssim 0.01$ mag. However, observers must carefully manage systematic uncertainties inherent in crowded-field photometry. The primary sources of photometric error arise from mismatches between the empirical and model point-spread functions (PSFs), as well as aperture corrections, each contributing $\lesssim 0.01$ mag to the error budget. Upgrades to PSF modeling (e.g., WebbPSF v1.2), which account for detector-level effects like charge diffusion and interpixel capacitance, have significantly mitigated these uncertainties [Weisz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271...47W/abstract).

Despite the high overall precision, minor chip-to-chip zero-point variations on the order of $\sim 0.05$ mag have been observed across the parallel [[nircam|NIRCam]] detectors, alongside rare instances of large astrometric misalignments that can introduce more substantial photometric errors of up to $0.08$ mag [Weisz et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..268...15W/abstract), [Weisz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271...47W/abstract). Finally, analyses of these Local Group populations have validated the JWST exposure time calculators in uncrowded regimes where photon noise behaves as theoretically predicted. However, similar to historical challenges encountered during [[hst-observations|HST observations]], the calculators can underestimate photometric uncertainties in highly crowded fields, underscoring the necessity of using empirical globular cluster data for robust photometric calibration [Weisz et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..268...15W/abstract), [Weisz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271...47W/abstract).

## Data Reduction and Calibration Challenges

Processing data from the Near Infrared Imager and Slitless Spectrograph (NIRISS) aboard the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] presents several unique calibration challenges. Unlike traditional slit or [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]], NIRISS Wide-Field [[slitless-spectroscopy|Slitless spectroscopy]] (WFSS) operates without a spatial aperture. This design means that the intrinsic morphology of a target galaxy is directly convolved with its spectrum, an effect known as morphological broadening. If this broadening is not properly accounted for through 2D forward modeling, emission line fluxes are systematically underestimated by approximately $30\%$ [He et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960L..13H/abstract). 

Additionally, the lack of a slit leads to significant overlap among spectra from adjacent targets. Precise contamination modeling is therefore essential to isolate individual source fluxes. To mitigate blending, observers typically acquire WFSS data using two orthogonal grism orientations, allowing the crossing spectra to be geometrically disentangled [Malkan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250900596M/abstract). Specialized community tools, such as `pygrife`, have been developed specifically to handle forced extraction and contamination modeling in these highly crowded grism fields [Watson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250400823W/abstract).

Background subtraction poses another major hurdle, particularly due to the presence of dispersed zodiacal light. This background emission imprints complex, spatially varying structures across both imaging and WFSS data frames. Standard pipeline reference files have been shown to introduce systematic artifacts—most notably a "light saber" effect—and background level fluctuations on the order of $5\%$ [Hviding et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..220H/abstract). To correct for these spatially dependent anomalies, data reduction pipelines increasingly rely on empirical backgrounds derived directly from on-sky data in the F115W, F150W, and F200W filters, which provide a much more accurate representation of the true background structure [Hviding et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..220H/abstract).

In imaging modes, utilizing NIRISS for crowded-field stellar photometry requires exceptionally accurate models of the point-spread function (PSF). Tools like DOLPHOT depend heavily on WebbPSF to perform exact PSF matching. Recent updates to the WebbPSF models (e.g., version 1.2) incorporate subtle detector-level physics, such as charge diffusion and interpixel capacitance. These improvements have successfully reduced PSF-mismatch and aperture correction uncertainties to $\lesssim 0.01$ mag [Weisz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271...47W/abstract). Nevertheless, some minor calibration tensions remain unresolved within deep parallel fields; for example, early data releases revealed chip-to-chip zero-point variations of $\sim 0.05$ mag in the accompanying [[nircam|NIRCam]] data, highlighting the broader need for ongoing absolute flux calibration efforts across all JWST imaging detectors [Weisz et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..268...15W/abstract), [Weisz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271...47W/abstract). 

Finally, accurate kinematic analysis requires a precise understanding of the observatory's spectral resolution. In-flight evaluations of other [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] spectrographs, such as [[nirspec|NIRSpec]], have demonstrated that true on-orbit spectral resolutions ($R \equiv \lambda / \Delta\lambda$) can exceed pre-launch documentation estimates by anywhere from $5\%$ to over $50\%$. This highlights the fact that empirical derivation of the in-flight line spread function is generally necessary across JWST spectroscopic datasets to avoid systematic errors when measuring velocity dispersions or narrow emission line profiles [Shajib et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250703746S/abstract), [Shajib et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...702L..12S/abstract).

## Future Directions and Synergies

The extensive catalogs of emission-line galaxies generated by NIRISS wide-field [[slitless-spectroscopy|Slitless spectroscopy]] serve as a critical benchmark for validating [[future-astronomical-facilities-and-surveys|Future Astronomical Facilities and Surveys]]. Specifically, NIRISS datasets provide an indispensable methodological precursor for the slitless spectroscopy modes of the Euclid mission, the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, and the [[china-space-station-telescope-csst|China Space Station Telescope (CSST)]] [He et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960L..13H/abstract), [Pang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251223157P/abstract), [Huberty et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260102220H/abstract). A key synergy lies in the treatment of morphological broadening in [[grism-spectroscopy|Grism spectroscopy]]. Detailed simulations of galaxy shape effects, such as the half-light radius, emphasize that advanced modeling is crucial for correctly interpreting convolved slitless spectra in [[space-based-observatories-and-surveys|Space-Based Observatories and Surveys]] like Euclid to prevent systematic flux underestimations [Gabarra et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230112803G/abstract), [He et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960L..13H/abstract). Furthermore, while NIRISS provides excellent redshift agreement with photometric catalogs for multi-line emitters, it has revealed a calibration and algorithmic challenge for single-line emitters—which suffer from elevated misidentification rates—that future wide-field redshift surveys must carefully overcome [Huberty et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260102220H/abstract).

Beyond statistical redshift surveys, these forthcoming wide-field observatories will expand into dynamic time-domain and high-contrast regimes. The wide-field capabilities demonstrated by JWST parallel surveys preview the potential for observatories like the Roman Space Telescope to coordinate with gravitational wave experiments (e.g., LISA and LIGO-Virgo), enabling rapid wide-field searches to detect electromagnetic counterparts to binary neutron star mergers [Gehrels et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JPhCS.610a2007G/abstract). Furthermore, future observatories like Roman will carry a coronagraph instrument (CGI) to perform direct imaging and characterize giant exoplanets and debris disks [Noecker et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JATIS...2a1001N/abstract).

Moving forward, NIRISS is uniquely positioned to address open questions regarding early galaxy assembly and the formation of the first stars. When combined with extreme gravitational lensing magnification ($\mu \gtrsim 100$), deep imaging and spectroscopy from NIRISS and [[nirspec|NIRSpec]] can probe spatial scales and luminosities previously inaccessible. Targets like the LAP1 star complex, which exhibits extremely stringent oxygen limits alongside intense ionizing radiation, serve as prime candidates for future campaigns searching for pristine, metal-free Population III environments [Vanzella et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...678A.173V/abstract). Additionally, NIRISS continues to map highly unusual inverted (positive) radial metallicity gradients in distant ($z \sim 3$) dwarf galaxies, posing ongoing theoretical questions about whether these represent tidal inflows of pristine gas or a previously uncharacterized mode of high-redshift structural growth [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L..16W/abstract).

In the realm of exoplanet atmospheres, the high-precision Single-Object Slitless Spectroscopy (SOSS) mode of NIRISS continues to play a vital role in characterizing the compositions of sub-Neptunes and assessing the viability of proposed bio-signatures. As seen in the contested atmospheric retrievals of targets like K2-18 b, the unprecedented sensitivity of these instruments demands rigorous, independent data treatments [Schmidt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250118477S/abstract), [Schmidt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....170..298S/abstract). By validating reductions across multiple pipelines, NIRISS sets the standard for robustly distinguishing between temperate, ocean-bearing "hycean" worlds and common oxygen-poor mini-Neptunes, acting as a foundational tool in the ongoing search for extraterrestrial habitability.
