## Article

**Galactic gas dynamics and flows** encompass the complex hydrodynamical and magnetohydrodynamical processes that govern the life cycle of baryons within and around galaxies. Situated at the intersection of [[astrophysical-fluid-dynamics-and-turbulence|Astrophysical Fluid Dynamics and Turbulence]] and galaxy evolution, this field investigates how the [[multiphase-gas|Multiphase gas]] of the interstellar medium (ISM) couples to the extended [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] (CGM). These fluid interactions dictate the galactic "baryon cycle": the continuous process by which galaxies [[galactic-gas-inflow-and-infall|accrete primordial and recycled gas]] from the cosmic web, transport it inward to fuel [[star-formation-processes|star formation]], and subsequently eject metal-enriched material back into the halo. Understanding these multiscale [[galaxy-outflows|flows]] is essential for explaining the observed properties of galaxies, from their star formation efficiencies to their chemical abundance gradients and morphological transformations during [[galaxy-merger-physics|mergers]] [Hernquist et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989Natur.340..687H/abstract).

The global evolution of a galaxy is fundamentally limited by its gas supply and internal transport mechanisms. Theoretical and computational models have established that galaxies acquire their fuel through distinct [[gas-accretion-modes|Gas accretion modes]], transitioning from "cold mode" filamentary inflows that penetrate deep into lower-mass halos, to "hot mode" accretion where gas is shock-heated to the virial temperature in massive systems. Once within the galactic disk, gas is subject to internal torques from non-axisymmetric structures and is governed by supersonic [[macroturbulence|Macroturbulence]]. Rather than simply collapsing in free-fall, the gas remains strongly coupled to the host galaxy's gravitational potential. This creates a dynamically regulated environment where processes like galactic shear and continuous angular momentum transport dictate the accumulation of gas in regions like the [[central-molecular-zone|Central Molecular Zone]] and control the overall efficiency of [[star-formation-processes|Star Formation Processes]].

In response to star formation and central black hole accretion, galaxies drive massive [[galaxy-outflows|outflows]] that counteract cooling and accretion. Powered by [[stellar-and-agn-feedback|Stellar and AGN Feedback]], these highly energetic [[superwinds|Superwinds]] eject mass, metals, and magnetic fields out of the galactic disk. Historically modeled as simple, uniform analytical expansions, modern high-resolution [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] reveal these outflows to be highly stratified, multiphase phenomena. While hot, diffuse plasma often escapes the galactic potential, heavier, cooler material typically follows ballistic trajectories before raining back down onto the disk as [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]] [Kahn et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981ASSL...91....1K/abstract). Furthermore, the inclusion of [[magnetohydrodynamics-mhd|Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD)]] has proven critical for stabilizing these multiphase flows and explaining the magnetization of the broader circumgalactic environment through turbulent dynamo action.

Despite significant theoretical and observational advancements, many aspects of galactic gas dynamics remain unresolved frontiers. A central tension in the field lies in the origin of high-velocity cool gas observed in galactic outflows: whether it is directly entrained from the ISM or forms *in situ* via the rapid radiative cooling of the hot wind phase. Additionally, the extreme dynamic range of these flows—spanning sub-parsec star-forming cores to megaparsec gaseous halos—poses severe challenges for [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]]. Overcoming artificial overcooling and refining [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]] remain critical hurdles. Future progress heavily depends on resolving these phase interactions, alongside accounting for complex microphysics such as non-equilibrium ionization, to accurately interpret the wealth of kinematic and abundance data provided by next-generation observatories.

## Historical Development and Theoretical Foundations

The theoretical study of galactic gas dynamics traces its origins to the classic foundations of interstellar matter dynamics, which systematically described the physical states and velocities of interstellar clouds [Spitzer et al. (1968)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1968nim..book....1S/abstract). Early foundational work extensively utilized galaxy surface photometry and kinematics to deduce the physical meaning of morphological sequences in galaxy dynamics [Kormendy et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982SAAS...12..115K/abstract). Furthermore, chemical abundance and kinematic data soon indicated that the Galaxy's oldest stars formed during a period of rapid proto-galactic collapse [Gilmore et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989epg..conf..172G/abstract). The kinematics of the stellar halo have since provided strong constraints on Galaxy formation models and the broader history of hierarchical assembly [Sommer-Larsen et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.265..123S/abstract). 

From these early concepts, the field historically relied on idealized analytical frameworks, chief among them the Chevalier and Clegg (1985) model (hereafter the CC85 model). Serving as one of the most influential [[toy-models-in-astrophysics|Toy models in astrophysics]], the CC85 framework provides an analytical solution for a starburst-driven galactic wind by assuming steady, uniform mass and energy injection within a spherical starburst nucleus. A defining feature of the CC85 model is that the wind velocity is zero at the geometric center and accelerates outward, reaching supersonic speeds beyond the starburst radius. Early theories also established the mechanics of gas inflows in perturbed disk galaxies during [[galaxy-merger-physics|mergers]], exploring the fundamental theory of tidal drag and orbit decay [Barnes et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998giis.conf..275B/abstract). Crucially, numerical simulations indicated that the merging history of dark halos serves as a primary determinant of a galaxy's morphological type through baryonic angular momentum loss [Navarro et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...380..320N/abstract).

Early efforts also focused on global galactic accretion and hot flows. For example, self-similar solutions for galactic cooling flows demonstrated how infall remains cold until it reaches a critical radius or time that allows for the formation of a shock wave [Chevalier et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ApJ...329...16C/abstract). Complementary time-dependent models of hot flows in elliptical galaxies with dark matter halos showed strong agreement with early X-ray observations, establishing the importance of extended gravitational potentials in retaining hot gas [Loewenstein et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJ...319..614L/abstract). Observations later confirmed that the hot interstellar medium in early-type galaxies is exceptionally sensitive to the mass distribution and rotation, reflecting the integrated mass and energy input from ongoing stellar evolution [Pellegrini et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASSL..378...21P/abstract). Further analysis of galaxy clusters using Chandra X-ray spectra provided rigorous constraints on the cospatial multiphase temperature components within central cooling flows [Arabadjis et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004rcfg.proc....3A/abstract). On galactic scales, X-ray absorption studies successfully characterized a global $10^6$ K hot interstellar medium in the Milky Way, determining it possesses a vertical scale height of approximately $1$ kpc [Wang et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AIPC.1156..257W/abstract), while early numerical calculations of supernova-heated gas suggested a filling factor of less than $20\%$ for this hot ISM at the solar circle [Ferrière et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...503..700F/abstract). Similarly, models accounted for the interaction between these hot gaseous haloes and galactic disks, as well as the diffuse medium permeating the Local Group [Fabian et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991IAUS..144..237F/abstract). In larger group environments, theories introduced the concept of time-dependent circulating hot gas flows, where iron produced by Type Ia supernovae is transported outward and recirculated within the inner $100$ kpc [Mathews et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...615..662M/abstract).

However, the uniform injection assumption does not reflect the complex, stratified nature of real starburst cores or nuclear clusters. Recent theoretical developments have generalized the CC85 paradigm to include non-uniform, spherically-stratified mass and energy injection profiles scaling as $r^{-\Delta}$ [Nguyen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518L..87N/abstract). In marked contrast to the classical model ($\Delta=0$), a singular isothermal sphere profile ($\Delta=2$) yields a flow that maintains a constant Mach number of $\mathcal{M} = \sqrt{3/5} \simeq 0.77$ throughout the wind-driving volume. In such stratified models, the interior velocity is non-zero and fast; the interior flow can be approximated as $v_{r < R} \simeq 0.41 \, v_\infty$, where $v_\infty$ is the asymptotic terminal velocity. This substantially alters the expected central density and kinematic profiles [Nguyen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518L..87N/abstract).

As computational capabilities have advanced, the field has transitioned from analytical approximations to three-dimensional, high-resolution [[astrophysical-fluid-dynamics-and-turbulence|Astrophysical Fluid Dynamics and Turbulence]] models. Modern frameworks recognize that a [[multiphase-wind|Multiphase wind]] contains gas spanning temperatures from cold molecular clouds ($T \sim 10^2$ K) to hot X-ray emitting plasmas ($T > 10^6$ K) [Schneider et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...834..144S/abstract). This shift has exposed significant tensions in our understanding of how mass and momentum couple across these phases. Historically, [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]] in [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] assumed that the cool gas observed in outflows was directly entrained from the interstellar medium via "cloud crushing" near the central starburst. 

Recent high-resolution studies fundamentally challenge this entrainment paradigm. Resolving spatial scales down to $\Delta x < 0.1$ pc reveals that cool, dense material is often rapidly destroyed and mixed into the hot phase, efficiently mass-loading the hot wind [Schneider et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...834..144S/abstract). Instead of surviving direct ejection, the literature currently debates whether the fast-moving ($v \sim 1000$ km s$^{-1}$), cool ($T \sim 10^4$ K) gas frequently observed in absorption at large distances ($\gtrsim 1$ kpc) from the galaxy forms *in situ* via the rapid radiative cooling of the hot wind phase [Schneider et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862...56S/abstract), [Schneider et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...834..144S/abstract). 

The thermodynamic evolution of these flows is highly sensitive to numerical resolution. Properly resolving the local gas cooling length in simulated outflows fundamentally alters their thermodynamics, dramatically increasing both the predicted mass and energy fluxes compared to under-resolved models [Rey et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.5412R/abstract). Consequently, the historical view of galactic winds as simple, hot, adiabatic expansions has been thoroughly replaced by a picture of highly dynamic, radiatively cooling, multiphase flows that continuously shape the surrounding [[gaseous-halos|Gaseous halos]].

## Gas Accretion Modes and Filamentary Infall

To sustain their observed star formation rates over cosmic time, galaxies require a continuous supply of gas from the intergalactic medium, as their current internal reservoirs are insufficient to maintain long-term activity [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract), [Combes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ASPC..480..211C/abstract). This replenishment process, broadly categorized under [[galactic-gas-inflow-and-infall|Galactic Gas Inflow and Infall]], is essential for resolving the classical "G-dwarf problem" and maintaining the equilibrium mass-metallicity relation through the dilution of enriched interstellar gas with low-metallicity inflows [Gibson et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..273..205G/abstract), [Finlator et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..221F/abstract), [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1354D/abstract). Current estimates suggest the present-day Milky Way accretes gas at a rate of a few $M_\odot\text{ yr}^{-1}$, which is sufficient to balance its ongoing star formation [Richter et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430...15R/abstract). On larger scales, the measurement of peculiar velocities has historically served as a critical probe of large-scale gravitational dynamics and cosmic gas flows [Dekel et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ARA&A..32..371D/abstract). Peculiar velocity surveys have been widely utilized to reconstruct such cosmic flows and identify the sources of deviations from pure Hubble expansion, such as the massive gravitational pull of the Great Attractor [Courtois et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AN....333..436C/abstract).

The physical state and thermodynamic history of this accreting material generally fall into two distinct [[gas-accretion-modes|Gas accretion modes]]: "hot mode" and "cold mode" accretion. In the classical hot mode, gas falling into a dark matter potential well undergoes a strong accretion shock near the virial radius, heating it to the halo's virial temperature ($T \sim 10^6$ K). This creates a hot, quasi-hydrostatic gaseous halo that subsequently cools and settles onto the galactic disk [Brooks et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...694..396B/abstract), [van de Voort et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.2458V/abstract).

However, high-resolution [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] have revealed a paradigm-shifting bimodal distribution in accretion thermodynamics. In lower-mass halos ($M_{\text{halo}} < 10^{11} M_\odot$) and at high redshifts, gas predominantly enters the galaxy via cold flows [Brooks et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...694..396B/abstract). In this cold mode, dense gas streams along cosmic web filaments and penetrates deep inside the virial radius without undergoing a virial shock, maintaining temperatures of $T \lesssim 10^5$ K [van de Voort et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.2458V/abstract). This mode of cold accretion has been recognized as a critical mechanism for solving the classical angular momentum problem in disk formation [Combes et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..390..369C/abstract). These filamentary cold flows are particularly efficient at delivering fuel directly to the central disk, driving the rapid assembly of star-forming galaxies at early cosmic epochs by bypassing the long cooling times associated with hot halos [Brooks et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...694..396B/abstract).

The dominance of either accretion mode is strongly mass-dependent. The fraction of gas accreted via the hot mode increases gradually with halo mass, transitioning from less than $10\%$ in $\sim 10^{11} M_\odot$ halos to greater than $90\%$ in massive $\sim 10^{13} M_\odot$ halos [van de Voort et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.2458V/abstract). Consequently, the mass growth of typical star-forming galaxies is heavily dominated by cold flows bypassing the hot phase of the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]]. There is also substantial evidence for continuous, low-metallicity gas inflow directly from high-velocity clouds to the solar neighborhood, a process that is often locally modulated by spiral density waves [Casuso et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...557..681C/abstract).

Despite the robust theoretical predictions for gas accretion onto large-scale dark matter halos, a significant tension remains in the literature regarding the actual rate of gas accretion onto the central galaxies themselves. While halo accretion is governed primarily by gravity and structure formation, the penetration of [[extra-planar-and-circumgalactic-gas|Extra-planar and Circumgalactic Gas]] into the inner interstellar medium is highly sensitive to the implementation of [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]] for [[stellar-and-agn-feedback|stellar and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback]] [van de Voort et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.2458V/abstract). Energetic [[galaxy-outflows|outflows]] can stall or redirect inflowing filaments, meaning that the effective fuel supply for star formation is ultimately regulated by a complex, non-linear balance between cosmic accretion and multiphase galactic winds.

## Internal Transport and Non-Axisymmetric Dynamics

The radial transport of gas through galactic disks is primarily governed by gravitational torques arising from a [[non-axisymmetric-potential|Non-axisymmetric potential]], rather than relying on local viscous forces. Large-scale structures such as stellar bars and spiral arms induce severe non-circular motions in the interstellar medium. During these processes, gas and stars can exhibit markedly differing dynamics, leading to a "dual morphology" in spiral galaxies where the two components exhibit distinct pitch angles and degrees of flocculence [Berman et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003A&A...412..387B/abstract). Furthermore, the radial migration of material through resonant scattering significantly impacts galactic abundance gradients and overall secular evolution [Combes et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..271..119C/abstract). As gas flows through perturbing potentials, it experiences orbit crossings and shocks, which efficiently dissipate kinetic energy and extract angular momentum [Hopkins et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.1027H/abstract). At kiloparsec scales, bar-driven torques excite density waves near the Inner Lindblad Resonance, forcing gas to stream inward and accumulate in central ring-like structures [Chaves-Velasquez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241105684C/abstract). Furthermore, nuclear outflows can interact directly with these bar-driven dust lanes and streams, entraining cold gas from the outer Galactic regions ($0.5-1.7$ kpc) rather than solely from the central parsecs [Su et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251214081S/abstract).

Within these dense central environments, the gas can become self-gravitating, leading to the formation of massive clumps. The subsequent migration of these clumps serves as a key mechanism for internal mass transport. Gravitational encounters between massive clumps induce further angular momentum exchange; clumps that are torqued down become [[low-angular-momentum-gas|Low-angular momentum gas]] and migrate deeper into the galactic nucleus [Namekata et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...731...57N/abstract). During broader [[galaxy-merger-physics|galaxy interactions]], the triggered gas turbulence and fragmentation in mergers can efficiently drive extended starbursts in massive star clusters [Bournaud et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....51..107B/abstract). When galaxies physically collide, 3D N-body and hydrodynamics experiments reveal complicated flows and massive shock structures propagating perpendicular to the disk plane as density waves [Lamb et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Ap&SS.216..337L/abstract). 

This continuous inflow fuels the formation of [[gaseous-nuclear-bars|Gaseous nuclear bars]] and circumnuclear rings, setting the boundary conditions for eventual accretion onto the central supermassive black hole. The structural evolution of galactic cores is also strongly influenced by binary supermassive black hole dynamics and loss-cone depletion [Merritt et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006RPPh...69.2513M/abstract). In active systems, such as the Seyfert galaxy Mrk 573, near-nuclear outflows are often localized to within $< 1$ kpc and involve the *in situ* acceleration of material directly off the host fueling disk [Fischer et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...834...30F/abstract). Furthermore, thermal instability in heavily irradiated [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|AGN environments]] frequently drives the formation of two-phase gas inflows within the inner $1$ to $1000$ parsecs [Bu et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496.3161B/abstract). Interestingly, multi-wavelength observations have shown that black hole activity correlates tightly with kinematic gas-star misalignment in brightest group galaxies, a phenomenon likely driven by the injection of mechanical [[stellar-and-agn-feedback|AGN feedback]] [Raouf et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Galax..12...16R/abstract). Moreover, general galaxy rotation plays a significant thermodynamic role: rotation lowers the ISM temperature and X-ray luminosity, making rotationally supported systems more prone to thermal instabilities than their dispersion-supported counterparts [Negri et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MmSAI..84..762N/abstract).

In Milky Way-like galaxies, this bar-driven inflow channels gas into a dense ring or stream at radii of $r \approx 200-300 \text{ pc}$, a region known as the [[central-molecular-zone|Central Molecular Zone]] (CMZ) [Armillotta et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.4401A/abstract), which represents a massive concentration of gas driven by dissipative inflow towards the deep Galactic gravitational well [Morris et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ARA&A..34..645M/abstract). Gas in the CMZ typically settles into the stable $x_2$ family of periodic orbits, which are aligned perpendicular to the main stellar bar [Chaves-Velasquez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241105684C/abstract). Despite exhibiting extremely high gas volume densities—often $n \approx 10^4 \text{ cm}^{-3}$, two orders of magnitude higher than the typical galactic disk—the CMZ exhibits an anomalously low [[star-formation-processes|star formation efficiency]]. Within the CMZ, the distribution of dense gas and young stars is highly asymmetric, with star formation generally constrained to a $100$ parsec radius [Longmore et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Galax...6...55L/abstract). Advanced 3D simulations of the Galactic central parsec show that flow structures in this region are significantly altered when considering discrete stellar wind sources rather than a simple uniform medium [Coker et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..186..214C/abstract).

This suppression of star formation in dense nuclear gas is widely attributed to a "galactic bottleneck" effect, where the CMZ's low star formation efficiency is fundamentally linked to multiscale episodic cycles of matter transport and feedback [Bryant et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NewAR..9301630B/abstract). In the deep gravitational potential of the galactic center, strong tidal forces and pronounced orbital shear dominate the internal kinematics of molecular clouds. These background galactic forces prevent the gas from dynamically decoupling from the host potential to undergo free-fall collapse [Meidt et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...854..100M/abstract), [Meidt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...892...73M/abstract). Furthermore, the rapid inward transfer of angular momentum that drives the gas into the CMZ maintains high levels of supersonic turbulence, which acts to further stabilize the gas against fragmentation [Begelman et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...702L...5B/abstract).

Consequently, the star formation rate in the CMZ is highly episodic. It cycles through burst and quench phases with periods of tens to hundreds of megayears as the gas mass fluctuates and periodically reaches critical thresholds for decoupling. In the quasi-stationary phase of the nuclear ring, localized star formation events are often triggered dynamically after the dense gas passes the apocenter of its orbit, creating a "conveyor-belt" mechanism of sequential star formation [Chaves-Velasquez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241105684C/abstract). Through this complex interplay of orbital dynamics and self-gravity, the large-scale non-axisymmetric features of the galaxy strictly regulate the consumption of gas in its nucleus.

## Turbulence and the Multiphase Interstellar Medium

The interstellar medium (ISM) is a highly dynamic, driven fluid characterized by nested, interdependent flows of matter covering many orders of magnitude in spatial scale. Hierarchical assembly, cooling, and the accretion of gas generate high levels of [[macroturbulence|Macroturbulence]] in galactic disks. However, numerical simulations demonstrate that supersonic turbulence within molecular clouds dissipates rapidly—typically in less than a single free-fall time. Consequently, this turbulence must be continuously maintained by a combination of internal stellar feedback and external drivers such as spiral density waves, thermal instabilities, and galactic shear. Multiscale observations reveal ubiquitous velocity fluctuations spanning from $10^{-1}$ to $10^{3}$ pc, which exhibit both scale-free turbulent spectra and oscillatory flows with wavelengths of $0.3-400$ pc [Henshaw et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4.1064H/abstract). These continuously sculpt the [[multiphase-gas|Multiphase gas]] of the ISM. Early studies recognized the masses and dynamics of giant molecular clouds in the Milky Way as the primary sites of such complex galactic [[star-formation-processes|star formation]] [Solomon et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987NASCP2466...37S/abstract).

Recent theoretical frameworks challenge the classical assumption that giant molecular clouds (GMCs, typically $\sim 20-50$ pc) are fully decoupled, self-gravitating entities. Instead, gas kinematics on these scales strongly track the orbital motion of the host galaxy's background potential [Meidt et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...854..100M/abstract). Shear and Coriolis forces generate coherent motions that provide a distinct form of support against self-gravity, effectively creating a "galactic bottleneck" to star formation. Under this paradigm, gas can only collapse at the true free-fall rate deep within clouds, once it has fully decoupled from the galactic potential [Meidt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...892...73M/abstract). This bottleneck naturally explains observational anomalies such as the [[central-molecular-zone|Central Molecular Zone]] (CMZ), where absolute gas densities are extremely high but the star formation rate is heavily suppressed due to strong coupling to the deep central potential.

The transfer of angular momentum is equally critical in regulating [[molecular-gas-and-cloud-evolution|Molecular Gas and Cloud Evolution]]. In self-gravitating flows, inefficient angular momentum loss leads to the decay of turbulence, which subsequently triggers global non-axisymmetric instabilities that renew turbulent driving. Counterintuitively, gas that cools to a small fraction of its virial temperature will not necessarily fragment if the flow maintains supersonic, virial turbulence [Begelman et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...702L...5B/abstract). Conversely, when ambient ISM pressure heavily dominates a cloud's internal pressure, it can drive confining shockwaves that significantly accelerate the fragmentation rate [Zubovas et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442.2837Z/abstract). 

Modern models in [[astrophysical-fluid-dynamics-and-turbulence|Astrophysical Fluid Dynamics and Turbulence]] capture this complex scale-coupling. High-resolution magnetohydrodynamic zoom-in simulations tracking gas from kiloparsec scales down to sub-parsec resolution ($0.28$ pc) reproduce the full structural hierarchy: from supernova-driven superbubbles and large-scale atomic filaments down to dense GMC condensations [Zhao et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..240Z/abstract). In quieter regions, galactic shear alone can drive the formation of filamentary GMCs within flattened $100$ pc rotating structures, generating helical magnetic fields that further stabilize the multiphase environment. 

Ultimately, the thermodynamics and kinematics of the multiphase ISM dictate the global efficiency of star formation. The formation of star-forming regions is closely tied to the vertical turbulent crossing time of the galactic disk ($\sim 3-30$ Myr), while local gas depletion times range from $200-2000$ Myr, decreasing at higher gas surface densities ($\Sigma_{\rm gas}$) [Kocjan et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260202657K/abstract). Because supersonic turbulence acts as a global stabilizing force, the star-formation efficiency per free-fall time ($\varepsilon_{\rm SF}$) varies only weakly with density across most of the molecular phase. It is only in the highest-density protostellar cores—where turbulence can no longer prevent gravitational collapse—that $\varepsilon_{\rm SF}$ finally approaches values of $0.1-0.3$. Even during major galaxy interactions, where mergers drive large-scale flows, the cold phase fractions and star formation rates in interacting systems (like M51) are primarily controlled by local feedback-driven self-regulation rather than just the global dynamics [Tress et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.2973T/abstract).

## Feedback-Driven Outflows and Superwinds

Large-scale [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]] processes are fundamental to regulating the baryon cycle, chemical enrichment, and star formation histories of galaxies. These gaseous ejections are primarily powered by the mechanical and radiative energy injected by clustered supernovae (SNe) and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN)]], which collectively drive highly energetic [[superwinds|Superwinds]]. At stellar scales, the interaction of hypersonic stellar winds with the interstellar medium plays a major role in creating large-scale loop structures and density fluctuations within HII regions [Dyson et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981ASSL...91..125D/abstract). While massive galaxies host colossal winds, dwarf galaxy winds may be ineffective at removing the bulk ISM, yet they efficiently expel metal-rich stellar ejecta into the intergalactic medium [D'Ercole et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.309..941D/abstract). Direct [Ne II] kinematic evidence from dwarf starbursts, such as He 2-10, highlights ionized gas flowing through molecular material alongside very high star formation efficiency in clusters [Beck et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...814...16B/abstract).

While classical analytic models assume uniform mass and energy injection within a central volume, generalized models featuring spherically-stratified starburst cores demonstrate that non-uniform injection radically alters the interior velocity and density profiles of the wind [Nguyen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518L..87N/abstract). High-resolution hydrodynamical simulations reveal that these [[galaxy-outflows|massive outflows]] operate as a highly complex [[multiphase-wind|Multiphase wind]]. The hot, diffuse phase ($T > 5 \times 10^5$ K) carries the bulk of the kinetic energy and escapes the galactic potential with an energy loading factor of $\sim 0.02$ and a relatively low [[mass-loading-factor|Mass loading factor]] ($\eta = \dot{M}_{\rm wind} / \dot{M}_{\rm SFR} \sim 0.1$) [Kim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...853..173K/abstract). By contrast, the warm phase ($T \sim 10^4$ K) carries significant mass but often lacks the necessary velocity to escape the halo, eventually recycling back to the disk as [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]] [Kim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...853..173K/abstract), [Armillotta et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.4401A/abstract).

The physical origin of high-velocity cool gas detected at large distances ($> 1 \text{ kpc}$) in these outflows remains an active subject of debate in the literature. Traditional phenomenological models attribute this cool component to the direct entrainment and "cloud crushing" of dense interstellar material by the hot wind. However, as noted, it may form *in situ* through rapid radiative cooling provided the central star formation rate surface density is sufficiently high [Schneider et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...834..144S/abstract), [Schneider et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862...56S/abstract). Resolving the local gas cooling length is critical to capturing these thermodynamics; simulations that adequately resolve this scale report significantly boosted momentum and energy transport without invoking new subgrid physical channels [Rey et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.5412R/abstract).

In massive galaxies, [[stellar-and-agn-feedback|Stellar and AGN Feedback]] often operate in tandem. AGN-driven outflows provide the extreme energy required to halt catastrophic cooling flows and establish the observed $M-\sigma$ relation. In the context of momentum-driven AGN wind models, it is important to distinguish between "ejective feedback", which physically expels gas from the galaxy, and "preventative feedback", which primarily suppresses new inflows from replenishing the disk [Brennan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...860...14B/abstract). To effectively quench star formation on global scales, these winds must be momentum-driven with large momentum fluxes, typically requiring $\tau_w = \dot{p} / (L/c) \gtrsim 3$ [Debuhr et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.420.2221D/abstract). Multi-wavelength observational evidence now traces these fast outflows across different gas phases, connecting radiation, winds, and radio jets to provide a comprehensive picture of AGN feedback [Morganti et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017FrASS...4...42M/abstract). In particular, ultrafast outflows (UFOs) represent a crucial mode of galaxy-scale AGN feedback; these highly energetic winds interact with the inhomogeneous, two-phase interstellar medium to ablate dense clouds and drive energy-driven wind-blown bubbles [Wagner et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763L..18W/abstract). On a broader scale, [O III] kinematics in luminous AGNs distinguish these non-gravitational outflow motions clearly from the virial kinematics found in normal star-forming galaxies [Woo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839..120W/abstract). Multi-dimensional, time-dependent simulations further show that radiation-driven disk winds and winds driven from large-scale inflows significantly impact the AGN environment and broad-line region spectra [Proga et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..427...41P/abstract). Furthermore, high-resolution 3D simulations identify backflows within AGN cocoons as a mechanism for conveying millions of solar masses back into the central accretion region [Cielo et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.439.2903C/abstract). When considering the interaction of these outflows with the surrounding material, 3D hydrodynamical simulations of quasar-irradiated gas show that rotation induces fragmentation into dense cold clouds, which are then entrained in the hot flow via shear and thermal instabilities [Kurosawa et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...693.1929K/abstract).

On larger scales, recent studies of massive isolated galaxies highlight a stark morphological distinction in feedback signatures: while star-formation feedback is typically angularly restricted, black hole feedback flows exhibit a halo-wide dominance [Morgan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250116045M/abstract). Even larger structures are observed; for instance, the giant 70 kpc nebula around the radio-loud quasar 3C 57 displays highly disturbed kinematics likely resulting from both rotation and massive biconical outflows [Liu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...984..140L/abstract). Such powerful central engines—alongside extreme starbursts—can sweep up the interstellar medium to drive [[molecular-outflows|Molecular outflows]]. Simulations of galaxy mergers with embedded AGN predict distinct off-nuclear CO emission signatures and broad kinematic profiles for molecular gas entrained in these AGN-driven winds [Narayanan et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...642L.107N/abstract). These outflows are observable via CO emission, with velocities reaching hundreds of $\text{km s}^{-1}$ in starburst-driven systems [Salak et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...901..151S/abstract) up to $\sim 1000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ in the most powerful AGN-driven systems [Narayanan et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJS..176..331N/abstract), [Debuhr et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.420.2221D/abstract). Even low-luminosity AGN can have a profound localized impact; for example, observations of NGC 1266 illustrate how such engines can drive extremely dense winds ($n_e \approx 0.33-4.2 \text{ cm}^{-3}$) accompanied by detectable charge exchange X-ray emission [Lopez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251001321L/abstract).

Despite their enormous available power, the hydrodynamic momentum coupling of AGN winds to the galactic disk is highly sensitive to local geometry. Simple hydrodynamic models of AGN jets often fail to quench galaxies because the jet carves a "low-density channel" through the interstellar medium, allowing thermal energy to vent directly into the halo without doing significant work on the dense gas [Vernaleo et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...645...83V/abstract). Consequently, the geometric structure of the host galaxy dictates outflow efficiency: thick, diffuse gas disks confine the initial feedback bubble and maximize disruption, whereas thin disks allow the feedback energy to easily escape along polar paths [Sivasankaran et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.537..817S/abstract).

## The Galactic Baryon Cycle and Fountains

The galactic baryon cycle represents the continuous circulation of gas between a galaxy's star-forming disk and its extended [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]]. Because typical interstellar gas reservoirs are insufficient to maintain observed star formation rates over a Hubble time, continuous [[galaxy-gas-replenishment|Galaxy gas replenishment]] is strictly required to sustain secular evolution [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract), [Richter et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430...15R/abstract). The present-day Milky Way, for instance, requires an accretion rate of a few $M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ to maintain its star formation [Richter et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430...15R/abstract). 

Accretion onto galaxies is broadly bimodal. [[gas-accretion-modes|"Cold mode" accretion]] ($T \lesssim 10^5$ K), which streams along dense cosmological filaments, dominates the growth of typical star-forming galaxies and lower-mass halos ($M_{\rm halo} < 10^{11} M_\odot$), allowing gas to efficiently bypass the virial shock [Brooks et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...694..396B/abstract), [van de Voort et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.2458V/abstract). Conversely, "hot mode" accretion dominates in massive halos ($M_{\rm halo} > 10^{13} M_\odot$), where infalling gas is shock-heated to the virial temperature before gradually cooling onto the disk.

Once gas enters the disk and fuels star formation, [[stellar-feedback-and-winds|Stellar Feedback and Winds]] drive complex, outward flows. Supernova explosions inject thermal energy and momentum, creating hot superbubbles that break out of the galactic plane. High-resolution simulations demonstrate that these outflows consist primarily of a hot ($T > 5 \times 10^5$ K) wind phase and a warm ($5050 < T < 2 \times 10^4$ K) fountain phase [Kim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...853..173K/abstract). The hot phase can reach terminal velocities of $v_{\rm wind} \sim 350-500$ km s$^{-1}$, escaping the galactic potential and carrying the bulk of the feedback energy. The warm phase, however, carries the majority of the mass flux but rarely exceeds outward velocities of $50$ km s$^{-1}$ [Kim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...853..173K/abstract), [Armillotta et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.4401A/abstract). Unable to escape the halo, this warm material follows ballistic trajectories, radiatively cools, and falls back to the disk as [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]] [Kahn et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981ASSL...91....1K/abstract), [de Avillez et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..166..103D/abstract). Models investigating this galactic fountain cycle have utilized various diagnostics for radiatively cooling halo gas and MHD simulations of cloud formation via thermal instability to explain this return process [Shapiro et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991IAUS..144..417S/abstract). However, external environmental processes can drastically alter this cycle; for instance, ram-pressure stripping of satellite galaxies like the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC) can rapidly convert internal galactic fountains into expelled gas streams, removing material from the host's baryon cycle entirely [Bustard et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...863...49B/abstract). Recent studies from the FOGGIE simulations demonstrate that highly ionized species, such as O VI, observed in the CGM primarily trace the warm envelopes of cooler clouds that are accreting as part of this metal-enriched galactic fountain cycle [Lochhaas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251025844L/abstract).

The physical origin of the cool and dense gas observed in a [[multiphase-wind|Multiphase wind]] remains a subject of active debate in the literature. As previously highlighted, whether this cold gas is directly entrained or condenses *in situ* remains an active topic of research [Schneider et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...834..144S/abstract), [Schneider et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862...56S/abstract). Resolving the local cooling length in these diffuse outflows is critical for accurately capturing the mass transfer between phases [Rey et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.5412R/abstract). Furthermore, turbulence within the hot wind can stretch the cold gas, enhancing its surface area and increasing mass transfer between the phases to facilitate rapid entrainment [Ghosh et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251003552G/abstract).

The continuous ejection and recycling of gas via the fountain cycle fundamentally regulates galactic chemical evolution. By balancing metal-enriched outflows with pristine cosmological inflows, galaxies establish an equilibrium mass-metallicity relation [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1354D/abstract). These outflows also carry magnetic fields into the halo; magnetohydrodynamic models indicate that galactic outflows magnetize the circumgalactic medium early ($z > 1$), establishing a seed field that is further amplified by an *in situ* turbulent dynamo to $B \sim 0.1\,\mu\mathrm{G}$ at the virial radius by $z=0$ [Pakmor et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.3125P/abstract).

## Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) in Galactic Flows

While purely hydrodynamic models successfully capture many features of [[bulk-gas-dynamics|Bulk gas dynamics]], incorporating [[magnetohydrodynamics-mhd|Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD)]] is essential for understanding the stability, morphology, and energy transport within [[multiphase-gas|Multiphase gas]] systems. Magnetic fields operate across a vast range of scales, from magnetizing the extended [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] to regulating the small-scale dynamics of star-forming clouds and driving nuclear outflows. Early discussions of galactic magnetic fields emphasized their origins and their fundamental control over gas motions acting as hydromagnetic waves [Piddington et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975Ap&SS..37..183P/abstract).

The magnetization of the circumgalactic medium is a two-stage process strongly coupled to galactic feedback. [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] show that at high redshifts ($z > 1$), early galactic outflows eject magnetic flux alongside metal-enriched gas from the galactic disk into the halo. At lower redshifts, an in-situ turbulent dynamo takes over, amplifying the field over gigayear timescales. By $z = 0$, this dynamo action saturates, producing a typical magnetic field strength of $B \approx 0.1\,\mu\text{G}$ near the virial radius [Pakmor et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.3125P/abstract). While the global circumgalactic field is predominantly unordered on small scales, strong coherent outflows can align and order the magnetic field in the specific regions they displace. Despite widespread theoretical consensus regarding the efficiency of this turbulent dynamo, direct observational evidence of small-scale turbulent fields in the interstellar and circumgalactic media remains elusive, highlighting an ongoing tension in [[astrophysical-fluid-dynamics-and-turbulence|Astrophysical Fluid Dynamics and Turbulence]] that next-generation radio polarimetry aims to resolve [Beck et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015aska.confE..94B/abstract).

Within galactic winds, magnetic fields provide critical thermal and dynamic stabilization. In active galactic nuclei (AGN), MHD winds launched from clumpy molecular disks manifest as [[warm-absorber-outflows|Warm absorber outflows]] observed in UV and X-ray spectra. The embedded magnetic field is strictly required to thermally stabilize this warm, absorbing gas against a wide range of incident continuum spectral energy distributions and local metallicities [Bottorff et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...537..134B/abstract). Furthermore, simplified hydrodynamic treatments of AGN jets often fail to halt catastrophic cooling flows because the jet creates a low-density channel, allowing energy to escape without heating the core interstellar medium; the inclusion of MHD effects and plasma transport is frequently necessary to properly couple feedback energy to the multiphase environment [Vernaleo et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...645...83V/abstract). Furthermore, 3D MHD simulations of radio galaxies reveal that jets do not simply end in a termination shock, but rather form a complex 'shock-web complex' at the jet terminus, profoundly impacting electron transport and synchrotron emission [Tregillis et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...557..475T/abstract).

On intermediate interstellar scales, magnetic fields influence the formation of molecular clouds and structures. Multiscale MHD simulations demonstrate that galactic shear and compressive superbubbles form flattened, rotating, disk-like structures on typical scales of $\sim 100\text{ pc}$. The assembly of these rotating gas structures is intimately associated with the generation of helical magnetic fields, which subsequently thread the dense filamentary clouds condensing within them [Zhao et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240518474Z/abstract). 

Finally, the launching of large-scale winds is deeply tied to the central engine via [[magnetic-disk-accretion|Magnetic disk accretion]]. General relativistic MHD (GRMHD) models of two-temperature magnetically arrested disks (MAD) show that turbulent magnetic cascades dictate electron heating and emission from the central engine [Mościbrodzka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...981..145M/abstract). To ensure the accuracy of such models, rigorous convergence analyses of 3D GRMHD global disk simulations remain critical for accurately modeling the turbulent transport of angular momentum [Shiokawa et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PhDT.......584S/abstract).

## Chemical Evolution and Metallicity Gradients

Galactic chemical evolution is deeply coupled to the large-scale transport of [[multiphase-gas|Multiphase gas]]. Current consensus posits that star-forming galaxies evolve in a quasi-equilibrium state, balancing pristine [[net-gas-inflows|Net gas inflows]] from the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] against internal gas processing, metal dilution, and the ejection of enriched material via outflows [Finlator et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..221F/abstract), [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1354D/abstract). This continuous baryon cycle establishes the fundamental mass-metallicity-SFR relationships observed across cosmic time. For instance, analytical models constraining the overall gas accretion rate to roughly equal the combined star formation and mass outflow rates successfully reproduce the mass-metallicity relation observed at high redshifts ($z \sim 2$) [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract). In lower-mass systems, gas loss via external environmental processes is equally vital. For example, the analytical Infall-Ram Pressure Stripping (IRPS) model incorporates episodic gas loss after a period of time via ram pressure stripping, which is necessary to better fit the observed $\alpha$-element distributions of dwarf galaxies [Kvasova et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982...92K/abstract).

This chemical equilibrium is drastically disrupted during [[galaxy-merger-physics|tidal interactions and major collisions]]. Gravitational torques generated during mergers funnel massive amounts of gas toward the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]]. Because this material is predominantly drawn from the galactic outskirts, it is characteristically metal-poor. Consequently, these radial inflows systematically dilute the preexisting nuclear gas abundance prior to the onset of a central starburst [Kewley et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131.2004K/abstract), [Sparre et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.2720S/abstract). Observations confirm that galaxy pairs with small projected separations ($< 20 \text{ kpc}$) exhibit central metallicities that are systematically lower by $\sim 0.2 \text{ dex}$ compared to isolated field galaxies of similar luminosity [Kewley et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131.2004K/abstract). Because this diluted gas accretes onto the center on timescales much shorter than those typical of quiescent [[star-formation-processes|star-forming galaxies]], coalescing systems frequently deviate from the standard fundamental metallicity relation [Sparre et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.2720S/abstract). Furthermore, mergers can heavily quench star formation by physically removing up to $50\%$ of a galaxy's molecular gas into extended tidal tails [D'Onofrio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990..166D/abstract). Moreover, tidal interactions in elliptical galaxies can create dense filaments that quickly cool and accrete, leading to massive spikes in X-ray luminosity over periods of several gigayears [D'Ercole et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...533..799D/abstract).

Beyond the nucleus, large-scale gas flows also dictate the evolution of radial abundance gradients across the galactic disk. While isolated, unperturbed disks generally maintain the steep, negative metallicity gradients established during their initial formation, these gradients are actively modified by radial flows and episodic merger events. The radial transport of previously accreted gas during interactions acts to flatten gas-phase metallicity gradients across the disk; indeed, interacting galaxies are observed to possess significantly shallower abundance gradients than their isolated counterparts [Kewley et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...721L..48K/abstract), [Finlator et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..221F/abstract).

Despite the clear impact of interaction-driven flows, the precise contribution of secular, continuous radial gas inflows to the chemical evolution of stable disks remains a subject of ongoing debate. Theoretical models parameterizing the radial velocity of the interstellar medium, $v_{r,g}$, predict that continuous inward flows should cause stellar metallicity to decline more steeply with age in the inner galaxy relative to the outer disk. However, this prediction is in tension with recent observational constraints in the Milky Way, which indicate remarkably minimal declines ($\lesssim 0.1 \text{ dex}$) between young ($\sim 0-2 \text{ Gyr}$) and old ($\sim 8-10 \text{ Gyr}$) stellar populations across the entire disk [Johnson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251005223J/abstract). This discrepancy indicates that simple radial inflows are insufficient to explain disk-wide chemical evolution in isolation. Instead, the final radial metallicity distribution must emerge from a complex interplay of localized star formation, metal-poor accretion, and the turbulent mixing of enriched gas recycled via [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]] [Johnson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251005223J/abstract). Such fountain circulation is observationally supported by the detection of metal-rich high-velocity clouds in the inner Galaxy, which serve as direct tracers of nuclear winds and recycled fountain flows [Zech et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...679..460Z/abstract).

## Computational Methods and Subgrid Physics

[[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] are essential for modeling the fully general, non-spherical evolution of diffuse cooling gas and its interaction with dark matter halos. Historically, early smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH) simulations successfully demonstrated that incorporating detailed gas dynamics is absolutely essential for realistic modeling of hierarchical structure formation [Katz et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992PASP..104..852K/abstract). However, these simulations are inherently limited by numerical resolution. Consequently, physical processes occurring below the resolution limit—such as star formation, black hole accretion, and feedback—must be implemented via [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]]. Early iterations of these subgrid models often relied on simplified analytic frameworks that assumed uniform mass and energy injection. Implementing generalized, spherically stratified injection profiles is essential for capturing the non-zero interior velocities and constant Mach numbers predicted in realistic starburst cores [Nguyen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518L..87N/abstract). Recent large-volume efforts, such as the FLAMINGO simulations, demonstrate the vast reach of these subgrid prescriptions, showing that AGN feedback can suppress the gas mass power spectrum by up to $8\%$ on large scales and drive outflows reaching ten times the virial radius [Ondaro-Mallea et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241209526O/abstract).

A primary challenge in implementing [[stellar-and-agn-feedback|Stellar and AGN Feedback]] is preventing artificial overcooling, where injected thermal energy is radiated away before it can perform mechanical work on the [[multiphase-gas|Multiphase gas]]. Standard subgrid workarounds include injecting energy exclusively into a hot, diffuse phase or temporarily disabling radiative cooling. However, explicitly resolving the cooling length in galactic outflows fundamentally alters their energetics without requiring *ad hoc* feedback modifications. Adaptive mesh refinement strategies that increase resolution in outflowing gas to $18 \text{ pc}$ allow the hot phase ($T > 8 \times 10^4 \text{ K}$) to remain hotter for longer. This enhanced resolution boosts the time-averaged mass and metal outflow rates by a factor of two, and increases the energy loading factor by a factor of five at a distance of $5 \text{ kpc}$ [Rey et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.5412R/abstract).

To directly capture the microphysics of a [[multiphase-wind|Multiphase wind]], specialized [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]] are utilized in idealized setups. Simulations using the Cholla code have achieved resolutions of $\Delta x < 0.1$ pc over domains spanning $>100 \text{ pc}$ to study the coupling of mass and momentum between a hot wind ($n \approx 10^{-2.5} \text{ cm}^{-3}$, $T \approx 10^{6.5} \text{ K}$) and colder, denser components ($n \approx 10^2 \text{ cm}^{-3}$, $T \approx 10^2 \text{ K}$) [Schneider et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...834..144S/abstract). These high-resolution models challenge the traditional subgrid paradigm that cold gas in outflows is purely entrained from the interstellar medium, instead demonstrating the viability of condensation from the hot phase [Schneider et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...834..144S/abstract), [Schneider et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862...56S/abstract).

Because directly simulating these sub-parsec phase interactions within a full cosmological volume remains computationally unfeasible, researchers are developing more sophisticated two-fluid frameworks. For example, the Cold Gas Subgrid Model (CGSM) tracks unresolved cold gas as a distinct secondary fluid with its own mass density and bulk momentum, utilizing prescriptions derived from high-resolution "cloud-crushing" simulations to govern mass transfer and thermal instability [Butsky et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.535.1672B/abstract). Similarly, explicit multiphase frameworks like SMUGGLE utilize super-Lagrangian refinement to couple AGN feedback to the interstellar medium at $10\text{--}100 \text{ pc}$ scales, capturing how the topological structure of the gas disk dictates whether feedback drives massive outflows or vents harmlessly along the polar axes [Sivasankaran et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.537..817S/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite significant theoretical and observational advances, several fundamental aspects of galactic gas dynamics remain the subject of active debate. A major unresolved question in the study of [[galaxy-outflows|Galaxy outflows]] is the physical origin of high-velocity cool ($T \sim 10^4$ K) gas. Determining whether this material represents destroyed and reformed clouds or is seeded by thermal instabilities within the hot phase remains a critical frontier [Schneider et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...834..144S/abstract), [Schneider et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862...56S/abstract). Furthermore, simulations that dynamically resolve the cooling length down to $18$ pc demonstrate that the hot outflowing phase ($T > 8 \times 10^4$ K) stays hotter for longer, which prevents artificial overcooling and completely restructures the wind's energetics [Rey et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.5412R/abstract). 

Accurately diagnosing the properties of these multiphase flows relies heavily on absorption-line spectroscopy in the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]]. Here, a critical frontier is the treatment of non-equilibrium ionization. The standard assumption of photoionization equilibrium is increasingly being challenged by models incorporating non-equilibrium chemistry and ion-by-ion cooling. In high-velocity outflows, the ionization state of the material departs strongly from equilibrium, which is necessary to reproduce the relatively high ${\rm O\,VI}$ column densities and the low ${\rm N\,V}$ to ${\rm O\,VI}$ ratios frequently observed [Gray et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...875..110G/abstract). Failing to account for these non-equilibrium effects can severely bias the interpretation of the [[multiphase-circumgalactic-medium|Multiphase circumgalactic medium]]. As multi-wavelength observations improve, synthetic spectra of emission lines such as [C II], CO, and H$\alpha$ can be increasingly used to recover mass outflow rates from simulations; however, interpreting molecular outflows remains highly sensitive to the assumed CO-to-H$_2$ conversion factors [Howatson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543.3428H/abstract). Beyond the diagnostic challenges of the multiphase CGM, there remains a persistent difficulty in robustly identifying direct gas accretion in individual observational measurements. Consequently, there has been a significant shift toward large-scale statistical comparisons between cosmological simulations and observational surveys to constrain inflow rates [Faucher-Giguère et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..271F/abstract).

The sheer dynamic range of galactic flows necessitates capturing [[multi-scale-feedback-processes|Multi-scale feedback processes]]. Current [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] struggle to simultaneously resolve the sub-parsec physics of [[star-formation-processes|Star Formation Processes]] alongside the kiloparsec scales of [[superwinds|Superwinds]]. Recent multi-scale [[magnetohydrodynamics-mhd|Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD)]] zoom-in simulations are beginning to bridge this gap, tracing the hierarchical formation of structure from kiloparsec atomic filaments and superbubbles down to $0.28$ pc resolution star clusters [Zhao et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..240Z/abstract). Furthermore, to bypass computational bottlenecks, novel two-fluid subgrid frameworks are being developed to explicitly track the mass density and bulk momentum of unresolved cold gas interacting with the resolved hot medium, providing a more rigorous treatment of the galactic baryon cycle [Butsky et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.535.1672B/abstract).

Future progress will rely heavily on next-generation observatories testing specific kinematic predictions. For example, differentiating between classical uniform-injection wind models and newer spherically-stratified profiles—which predict completely different interior velocity gradients and Mach numbers—will be crucial [Nguyen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518L..87N/abstract). Upcoming high-resolution X-ray spectroscopy from missions such as XRISM will play a major role in distinguishing between these spatially-resolved kinematic models, ultimately refining our understanding of feedback in the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]].
