**Galaxy and Universe evolution** encompasses the comprehensive study of the physical processes that drive the structural, kinematic, and chemical transformation of the cosmos over cosmic time. Grounded in the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]], this field traces the progression of the Universe from microscopic primordial density fluctuations to the immense, highly organized macroscopic networks of the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract), [Peebles et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981lssu.book.....P/abstract). Within this framework, the universe's mass-energy budget is dominated by dark energy and cold dark matter. Structure formation proceeds via a "bottom-up" process known as [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]], where non-baryonic dark matter aggregates into gravitationally bound halos. The resulting [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]] provides the foundational gravitational potential wells into which diffuse intergalactic gas cools, condenses, and eventually ignites to form stars and assemble galaxies through the [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] process.

The observable properties of galaxies are fundamentally shaped by a continuous [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]] cycle of [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]], [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]], and chemical enrichment. As galaxies grow, their evolutionary trajectories are strongly regulated by energetic feedback mechanisms—primarily from supernovae and active galactic nuclei. These feedback processes prevent the runaway overcooling of gas and drive the eventual quenching of star formation, transforming blue, active disks into red, [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract), [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Concurrently, galaxies undergo profound [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]] through violent [[galaxy-merger-dynamics-and-evolution|Galaxy Merger Dynamics and Evolution]] and internal [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] dynamical instabilities. This evolution diversifies primordial stellar seeds into the rich array of rotationally supported disks and dispersion-dominated spheroids categorized by the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]]. Despite the highly non-linear and chaotic nature of these assembly pathways, galaxy populations exhibit remarkably tight empirical correlations, known as [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]], which tightly link their mass, luminosity, kinematics, and size.

While the hierarchical assembly model has achieved resounding success in reproducing the large-scale distribution of matter, the field is currently confronting significant theoretical and observational frontiers. Recent high-resolution observations probing the [[early-universe|Early universe]] have revealed morphologically mature, massive rotating disks at unexpectedly high redshifts ([[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]]), severely challenging the delayed assembly timescales predicted by standard models of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]]. Furthermore, active debate continues over the exact physical mechanisms governing galaxy quenching, the universality of the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] across different cosmic environments, and the persistence of galactic simplicity in the face of complex merger histories—some of which has inspired investigations into alternative modified gravity models. This article explores the historical development of galaxy evolution theory, details the physical mechanisms governing baryonic cycles and structural diversity, and reviews the open questions and cosmological tensions that are driving the next generation of [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Galaxy Surveys and Catalogs]].

## Historical Development of Galaxy Evolution Theory

The origins of [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] and modern galaxy evolution theory trace back to the 1920s. Nearly a century ago, the true nature of galaxies as distinct "island universes" was definitively established [West et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Natur.427...31W/abstract). This realization was largely driven by Edwin Hubble's identification of Cepheid variable stars in the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]]. Hubble subsequently introduced the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]], a morphological classification system extending from smooth, ellipsoidal early-type systems to structurally complex, disk-dominated late-type spirals. Although originally and erroneously interpreted as a direct evolutionary track, this classification laid the empirical groundwork for understanding galactic structural diversity.

Hubble's 1929 discovery of a linear velocity-distance relation demonstrated cosmic expansion, providing the observational foundation for the Hot [[big-bang|Big Bang]] cosmology [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract). Throughout the mid-20th century, early theories of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] heavily favored [[pure-luminosity-evolution-models|Pure Luminosity Evolution models]], wherein galaxies were thought to form via a single, rapid burst of [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] in the early universe, followed by isolated, passive evolution [Vincoletto et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421.3116V/abstract). 

A major theoretical shift occurred in the late 1970s. Observations of flat [[universal-rotation-curves|Universal Rotation Curves]] in spiral galaxies indicated that visible matter is embedded within vast, invisible halos, forcing the integration of a massive [[dark-matter-fraction|Dark matter fraction]] into evolutionary models. This realization spawned a two-stage formation framework: dissipationless dark matter first clusters via gravitational instability into bound halos, into which baryonic gas subsequently cools, condenses, and forms stars [Benson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhR...495...33B/abstract).

By the late 1990s, observations of accelerated expansion, combined with precision measurements of the cosmic microwave background, cemented the $\Lambda$CDM Concordance model as the dominant framework of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]] [Rees et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003RSPTA.361.2427R/abstract), [Bennett et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006Natur.440.1126B/abstract). In this Standard paradigm, the universe's mass-energy budget is tightly constrained to approximately $5\%$ baryons ([[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]]), $25\%$ cold dark matter, and $70\%$ dark energy. Under this model, pure luminosity evolution theories were largely superseded by the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], which posits that massive galaxies assemble incrementally over cosmic time through continuous [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] and repeated mergers of smaller progenitor systems [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract), [Glazebrook et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Natur.430..181G/abstract).

Despite its overwhelming success in reproducing the large-scale structure of the universe, the $\Lambda$CDM framework faces notable tensions in the current literature. Recent high-resolution observations have uncovered massive, morphologically mature rotating disks and central bulges at $z > 5$. These findings indicate that early structural [[mass-accretion-and-assembly|Mass Accretion and Assembly]] may have proceeded much more rapidly than predicted by standard hierarchical models [Lelli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...371..713L/abstract), [Fujimoto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240218543F/abstract). Furthermore, researchers note a stark contrast between the chaotic, multi-parameter merger histories predicted by cold dark matter simulations and the observed simplicity of [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]]. The tight correlation between baryonic mass and orbital velocity has prompted ongoing debate, with some literature highlighting that [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]]—such as Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND)—can predict these empirical scaling laws with surprisingly high accuracy without invoking dark matter [Disney et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455.1082D/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102499L/abstract).

## Hierarchical Dark Matter Assembly

In the standard $\Lambda$CDM model of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]], the universe is gravitationally dominated by non-baryonic Cold Dark Matter (CDM), which accounts for approximately 25% of the total cosmic mass-energy budget [Rees et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003RSPTA.361.2427R/abstract), [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract). Structure formation proceeds via a "bottom-up" or [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], where small-scale primordial density fluctuations are the first to undergo gravitational collapse. An overdensity grows linearly until it reaches a critical threshold, at which point it detaches from the cosmic expansion ([[expansion-history|Expansion history]]) and virializes into a bound [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|Dark Matter Halo Properties and Statistics]]:

$$ \delta = \frac{\rho - \bar{\rho}}{\bar{\rho}} \gg 1 $$

These initial halos form the foundational [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]] that provides the gravitational scaffolding for subsequent gas cooling, baryonic condensation, and overall [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]].

The assembly history of these dark matter halos is fundamentally driven by continuous [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]] and coalescence. As the universe expands, smaller halos merge to form progressively larger systems, a process systematically tracked using [[halo-merger-trees|Halo merger trees]]. While the abundance, mass-gap statistics, and clustering of these halos can be approximated analytically using the extended Press-Schechter formalism, the complex nonlinear gravitational dynamics of collisionless dark matter require high-resolution N-body simulations to accurately map the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]]. Landmark computational efforts have utilized massive [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]]—sometimes tracking over $2160^3$ particles in gigaparsec-scale volumes—to demonstrate how dark matter assembly faithfully reproduces the observed cosmic web [Springel et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005Natur.435..629S/abstract), [Frenk et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002RSPTA.360.1277F/abstract).

Within these simulations, the internal architecture of dark matter halos emerges with remarkable consistency. Halos generally develop universal [[density-profiles|Density profiles]] characterized by a running power-law shape, typical of the Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) [[pseudo-isothermal-profile|Pseudo-isothermal profile]]. High-resolution "zoom-in" simulations of the most massive cluster-scale halos ($M_{500} > 5.5 \times 10^{14} M_\odot$) show that this internal dark matter structure is established early ($z > 1$) and remains exceptionally robust to ongoing merging activity, exhibiting a structural dispersion as low as $0.15$ dex down to the present day ($z=0$) [Le Brun et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473L..69L/abstract), [Le Brun et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022EPJWC.25700026L/abstract).

Locally, precision rotation curve measurements confirm the presence of dark matter even in the inner regions of the Milky Way—well inside the solar circle—independent of prior assumptions regarding the global halo distribution ([[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]]). The hierarchical assembly of this dark matter and its associated baryons leaves distinct kinematic and chemical signatures; for example, the [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]] of the Milky Way exhibit a clear dichotomy between an inner component (which is prograde and has higher metallicity) and an outer component (which is retrograde and has lower metallicity), directly reflecting its complex accretion and merger history [Carollo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Natur.450.1020C/abstract).

Despite the resounding success of the hierarchical CDM paradigm on large scales, the literature reflects ongoing tensions regarding dark matter assembly on sub-galactic scales. Small-scale discrepancies, such as the cusp-core and missing satellite problems, persistently challenge the simplest collisionless dark matter models. Resolving these issues often requires the inclusion of complex baryonic feedback mechanisms or [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]], which can sometimes predict galactic scaling relations more accurately than standard CDM models without auxiliary hypotheses [McGaugh et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021SHPSA..88..220M/abstract), [Primack et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ARNPS..74..173P/abstract). 

Furthermore, recent observational studies have uncovered unexpectedly strong large-scale clustering in isolated, diffuse blue [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]]. While this pattern aligns with the halo assembly bias expected for older, low-mass halos, it contradicts existing models of galaxy evolution within the $\Lambda$CDM framework, prompting renewed [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|Galaxy Bias and Clustering Statistics]] investigations into self-interacting dark matter scenarios [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250403305Z/abstract). Consequently, while the large-scale scaffolding of the universe is well established, the fine-grained nature of hierarchical dark matter assembly remains an active, and occasionally conflicting, area of research.

## Baryonic Cycles: Cooling, Accretion, and Star Formation

In the standard paradigm of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], baryonic gas initially shock-heated within virializing dark matter halos must dissipate its thermal energy to form stars. Radiative cooling allows this gas to lose pressure support, condense, and flow toward the halo center. Theoretical arguments predict that efficient cooling occurs primarily in systems where the cooling time is shorter than the dynamical collapse time, yielding a characteristic [[transition-halo-mass-scale|Transition Halo Mass Scale]] of $\sim 10^{12} M_\odot$. However, pure cooling models predict that a vast majority of baryons should rapidly condense into stars, conflicting with the low [[total-baryon-fraction|Total baryon fraction]] observed in local galaxies. This "overcooling" problem necessitates energy injection, or feedback, from supernovae and active galactic nuclei to regulate the baryonic cycle and heat the circumgalactic medium [Benson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhR...495...33B/abstract). Alternatively, in the [[early-universe|Early universe]], gas may bypass the hot halo phase entirely via rapid [[cosmic-accretion|Cosmic accretion]] along [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] filaments. These "cold flows" drive turbulence and are capable of sustaining the high star-formation rates observed in early [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]] [Khochfar et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009RvMA...21...87K/abstract).

The conversion of cold, dense gas into stars is a complex multiscale process. The very first stars—metal-free Population III stars—condensed from primordial gas in low-mass halos during the cosmic dark ages, initiating the chemical enrichment of the Universe. In later epochs, the demographics of star formation are fundamentally characterized by the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. The IMF determines the mass distribution of newly formed stars and acts as the crucial scaling factor linking observed ultraviolet and infrared luminosities to inferred stellar masses. However, whether the IMF is universal across all cosmic environments remains a subject of active debate and constitutes one of the greatest sources of systematic uncertainty in modeling galaxy evolution [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...39H/abstract).

The global trajectory of baryonic condensation and stellar birth is captured by the cosmic star-formation rate density. Extensive multi-wavelength surveys have established a robust [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]], revealing that the Universe's star-formation activity peaked approximately 3.5 Gyr after the Big Bang at $z \approx 1.9$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). The overarching cosmic star formation history can also be framed as a [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] process characterized by a critical "transition redshift" at $z \approx 1.7$. During the earlier epoch, a 'hot mode' dominated, assembling spheroids primarily through violent mergers, whereas later times are characterized by a 'cold mode' that facilitates the steady growth of massive disks via continuous gas infall [Driver et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.430.2622D/abstract). Following this "cosmic noon," the global star-formation rate has declined exponentially with an e-folding timescale of 3.9 Gyr. Quantitatively, about 25% of the present-day stellar mass formed before this peak, half was in place by $z = 1.3$, and the remaining 25% has formed since $z = 0.7$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Remarkably, less than $1\%$ of today's stars formed during the epoch of reionization. 

Despite this consistent overarching picture of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]], observational limits continue to challenge our understanding of the earliest epochs. Accurate measurements of the star-formation rate density at $z > 3$ remain complicated by ambiguities regarding the universality of the IMF and by the highly uncertain dust extinction corrections required to account for heavily obscured starburst populations.

## Feedback Mechanisms and Galaxy Quenching

Because gas cooling in dark matter halos is highly efficient, early [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]] predicted that the vast majority of cosmic baryons should condense to form stars. However, observations show that global star formation is remarkably inefficient; only a small fraction of the total cosmic baryon budget resides in stars. This "overcooling problem" necessitates the inclusion of energetic baryonic feedback mechanisms that heat or expel cold gas, thereby regulating the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] of galaxies [Benson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhR...495...33B/abstract).

In low- to intermediate-mass galaxies, supernova feedback acts as the primary regulatory mechanism. Dying massive stars inject substantial thermal and kinetic energy into the interstellar medium. This energy can reheat cold gas, suppressing further collapse (negative feedback), or drive galactic-scale winds that expel material into the intergalactic medium. Conversely, expanding blast waves can sometimes compress local gas to trigger starbursts ([[starburst-galaxy-evolution|Starburst Galaxy Evolution]]). It is argued that the self-regulation driven by the competition between gravitational instabilities and mechanical supernova feedback keeps galaxy properties remarkably simple, tightly constraining [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] such as the relation between [[h-i-mass-function|H I mass function]] and surface area [Chakraborti et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732..105C/abstract).

For the most massive galaxies, supernova energy is insufficient to unbind gas from deep potential wells. Instead, AGN feedback becomes the dominant quenching mechanism. As matter accretes onto a central supermassive black hole, [[galaxy-black-hole-assembly|Galaxy-black hole assembly]] release vast amounts of energy through radiative outflows and relativistic jets. This energy injection offsets the cooling of hot gas in massive halos, effectively shutting down star formation. The co-evolution of supermassive black holes and their host galaxies—evidenced by similarities in their accretion and star-formation histories—strongly supports the necessity of AGN feedback in regulating the high-mass end of the galaxy population [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Outflowing gas driven by these mechanisms can be directly characterized via P-Cygni line shapes and fine-structure emission lines in the infrared [González-Alfonso et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PASA...34...54G/abstract).

Together, these feedback mechanisms drive galaxy quenching, a process responsible for the [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]] and spectral transformation of galaxies from blue, star-forming systems into "red and dead" [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]]. However, the exact nature of this transition remains a subject of active debate in the literature. While state-of-the-art cosmological hydrodynamical simulations (such as EAGLE, IllustrisTNG, Simba, and [[magneticum-simulations|Magneticum (simulations)]]) successfully reproduce many macroscopic galaxy properties, they tend to predict the existence of fully quenched galaxies with strictly zero star formation over the last several gigayears. In contrast, observational surveys in the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]] indicate that even the most quiescent galaxies typically maintain a residual [[specific-halo-mass-accretion-rate|Specific halo mass accretion rate]] of $\text{sSFR} \sim 10^{-12} \text{ yr}^{-1}$ [Corcho-Caballero et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.5108C/abstract). Furthermore, some far-infrared studies suggest that the observed cosmic evolution of galaxies favors a much gentler quenching process rather than the rapid truncation commonly implemented in analytic models and hydrodynamical simulations [Eales et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.3507E/abstract). Resolving these discrepancies remains a critical frontier in understanding how galaxies definitively cease star formation.

## Morphological Transitions and Structural Diversity

The structural diversity of galaxies, historically codified by the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]], arises from the continuous competition between [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]], discrete [[galaxy-merger-dynamics-and-evolution|Galaxy Merger Dynamics and Evolution]] events, and internal dynamical instabilities. Within the standard hierarchical paradigm, the fundamental dichotomy between rotationally supported disks and dispersion-dominated spheroids begins with the conservation of angular momentum ([[spiral-galaxy-dynamics|Spiral Galaxy Dynamics]]). As baryonic gas cools and condenses within the potential wells of [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|Dark Matter Halo Properties and Statistics]], it loses pressure support and falls inward. Assuming the gas largely conserves its initial angular momentum, it spins up and settles into a rotationally supported, cold disk in centrifugal equilibrium.

The assembly of these systems is typically characterized by [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]]. In this scenario, a compact, centrally concentrated proto-bulge forms early, followed by the gradual accretion of higher-angular-momentum material that builds the extended stellar disk ([[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]]). Recent high-resolution observations utilizing ALMA and JWST have challenged classical theoretical timelines by revealing that this morphological maturation can occur exceptionally early in cosmic history. For instance, massive, regularly rotating disks hosting mature [[galaxy-nuclei-and-complex-morphologies|Galaxy Nuclei and Complex Morphologies]] have been identified at $z \simeq 5$, when the Universe was only 1.2 Gyr old [Lelli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...371..713L/abstract). Even more extreme is the $10^{8.6} M_\odot$ galaxy JADES-GS+53.18343-27.79097 at $z > 7$, which exhibits a highly compact core ($r < 100$ pc) embedded in an actively star-forming disk ($r \approx 400$ pc), providing direct evidence for inside-out growth during the epoch of reionization [Baker et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230602472B/abstract), [Baker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..141B/abstract). Observations of massive rotating protodisks at $z \sim 2$ (roughly 3 billion years after the Big Bang) demonstrate that galaxies can also channel gas toward central bulges to fuel rapid growth without necessarily undergoing major mergers [Genzel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006Natur.442..786G/abstract). The presence of such dynamically cold, morphologically mature systems at cosmic dawn creates significant tension with hierarchical $\Lambda$CDM models, which traditionally predict a chaotic and late assembly phase for massive disks and bulges [Fujimoto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240218543F/abstract).

Beyond smooth accretion, [[galaxy-merger-dynamics-and-evolution|Galaxy Merger Dynamics and Evolution]] plays a critical role in driving [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]]. Major mergers violently disrupt the ordered rotation of gas and stars, redistributing angular momentum and funneling gas into the galactic center to fuel nuclear starbursts ([[starburst-galaxy-evolution|Starburst Galaxy Evolution]]) and supermassive black hole accretion. This violent relaxation frequently transforms rotationally supported disk galaxies into dispersion-dominated early-type spheroids. Consequently, the fraction of merging galaxies—which increases at higher redshifts—correlates strongly with the changing morphological mix of massive galaxies over cosmic time [Kaviraj et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609136K/abstract). In dense cluster environments, this hierarchical assembly manifests as the [[butcher-oemler-effect|Butcher-Oemler effect]], where higher-redshift clusters contain a significantly larger fraction of blue, actively star-forming galaxies compared to their local counterparts. The rapid evolution of these blue galaxies can be naturally explained by hierarchical models in which [[galaxy-cluster-properties-and-scaling-relations|Galaxy Cluster Properties and Scaling Relations]] undergo more rapid assembly and frequent merging [Kauffmann et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.274..153K/abstract). Despite their transformative impact on galaxy shapes, recent estimates suggest that only about one-third of the present-day stellar mass budget is directly attributable to major merger events [Kaviraj et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609136K/abstract).

In the absence of major mergers, galaxies can undergo prolonged periods of [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]. Internal dynamical instabilities, such as the formation of galactic bars in thin disks with high surface densities, can profoundly alter a galaxy's structure over gigayear timescales [Zhang et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..317Z/abstract). Bars facilitate the radial inflow of gas by stripping it of angular momentum, feeding central starbursts and active galactic nuclei. Furthermore, bars can buckle vertically out of the disk plane, giving rise to "pseudobulges"—central ellipsoidal structures that are dynamically and chemically distinct from classical bulges formed via chaotic mergers. Ultimately, a galaxy's observed morphology reflects a complex superposition of its early inside-out assembly, its violent merger history, and continuous reshaping by internal secular processes. Additionally, the intense conditions within early morphological assembly phases leave lasting structural artifacts. For example, [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]] are now theorized to be the natural outcome of high-pressure star formation occurring within gas-rich, turbulent high-redshift disks. Following their formation, these dense stellar clusters gradually migrate into the extended galaxy halos as the structural evolution of the galaxy proceeds [Kruijssen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..312..147K/abstract). In the present day, the Milky Way serves as a benchmark for this structural evolution in the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]]. Detailed comparisons reveal that the Milky Way’s structural parameters—such as scale length, height, and overall flatness—are largely typical of local spirals, although its thick disk appears notably thinner than predicted by some cosmological simulations [Guerrette et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24c5002G/abstract).

## Chemical Evolution and Metal Enrichment

The chemical composition of a galaxy's gas and stellar populations serves as a fossil record of its [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and overall assembly. Following the [[big-bang|Big Bang]], the baryonic Universe consisted almost entirely of primordial hydrogen, helium, and trace amounts of lithium. The production of heavier elements—nucleosynthesis—was initiated by the formation of the first generation of stars, known as Population III stars, which condensed within small, high-redshift [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|Dark Matter Halo Properties and Statistics]]. 

Because these massive Population III stars evolved rapidly, they ended their lives in energetic supernova and hypernova explosions, dispersing the first heavy elements into the pristine interstellar and intergalactic media [Nomoto et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ARA&A..51..457N/abstract). High-resolution [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]], such as the AEOS simulations, trace this early star-by-star chemical enrichment down to $1 \text{ pc}$ scales. These models demonstrate that initial nucleosynthetic events established early metallicity floors (e.g., $[\text{O/H}] \approx -4.0$) before subsequent stellar generations could form [Brauer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016366B/abstract), [Brauer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...41B/abstract). Driven by these early massive stars, the mean metallicity of the Universe had already risen to approximately $0.001 Z_{\odot}$ by $z = 6$, merely one billion years after the Big Bang [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract).

The chemical signature of these early nucleosynthetic yields is preserved today in extremely metal-poor stars (Population II). Observations of the Milky Way's halo and its surrounding [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] have identified survivor stars with metallicities as low as $[\text{Fe/H}] \sim -5.5$ [Frebel et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASSL..396..377F/abstract). The detailed abundance patterns of these ancient stars offer critical constraints on the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], mixing processes, and explosion energies of the very first stars, which otherwise remain unobservable [Nomoto et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ARA&A..51..457N/abstract), [Cowan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006Natur.440.1151C/abstract).

As galaxies grow via the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]] and continuous gas accretion, successive stellar generations continuously reprocess baryons. Core-collapse and thermonuclear supernovae, along with stellar winds, eject metals that fundamentally alter the cooling efficiency of the interstellar medium, thereby regulating future star formation. The [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] density peaked at $z \approx 1.9$, an epoch that drove the bulk of heavy element production across the Universe [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Additionally, chemical abundance patterns reveal that massive galaxies in the early Universe underwent extremely short, intense periods of star formation, rapidly elevating their heavy element concentrations [Kobayashi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.540..205K/abstract). 

There remains some theoretical debate regarding which galaxy morphological types dominate the cosmic metal budget over time. Pure luminosity evolution models suggest that early-forming elliptical galaxies dominate the total metal production, whereas models incorporating [[number-density-evolution|Number density evolution]] suggest that spiral galaxies are the primary producers [Vincoletto et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421.3116V/abstract). Regardless of the dominant host population, the continuous integration of nucleosynthetic yields over cosmic time has steadily enriched the cosmos. When integrating the heavy elements locked in stars with those dispersed throughout the intergalactic gas, the present-day overall mean metal abundance of the Universe is estimated to be remarkably close to the solar value [Edmunds et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997MNRAS.292..733E/abstract).

## Large-Scale Structure and the Cosmic Web

On scales larger than a few megaparsecs, the Universe departs from local homogeneity, exhibiting an intricate, cellular pattern known as the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]]. Driven by the [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]] of primordial density perturbations within the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]], matter organizes into a complex network of nodes, sheets, and filaments, separated by vast underdense voids [Coil et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013pss6.book..387C/abstract). The empirical foundation for our modern understanding of these vast structures was significantly advanced by major observational campaigns, such as the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey, which provided direct evidence that large-scale structure grew via gravitational instability and delivered robust constraints on the cosmological mass densities of both baryons and dark matter in [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Galaxy Surveys and Catalogs]].

The most prominent connecting features of this network are the long filaments of dark matter and gas, which frequently span lengths of $>10\, h^{-1}$ Mpc [Coil et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013pss6.book..387C/abstract). Theoretical models predict that the vast majority of the Universe's baryons—upwards of $90\%$ at all epochs—reside outside of galaxies in the intergalactic medium [Tejos et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..308..364T/abstract). A significant fraction of this material is theorized to be trapped within these unvirialized filamentary structures as a diffuse, Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM) [Kaastra et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.2324K/abstract). Meanwhile, vast regions of relatively empty space, known as voids, occupy the interstitial volumes between filaments, representing the lowest-density environments in the cosmos.

At the dense intersections of filaments lie [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]], the largest gravitationally bound structures in the Universe [Kravtsov et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..353K/abstract). Occupying spatial scales of $\sim 1-3\, h^{-1}$ Mpc, rich clusters are characterized by total masses of $M \sim 10^{14}-10^{15} M_\odot$ and deep gravitational potential wells that induce galaxy velocity dispersions of roughly $1000\, \text{km s}^{-1}$ . The hot gas trapped within these deep potential wells can be effectively studied using the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect, which serves as a critical probe for measuring the temperature evolution of the cosmic microwave background and mapping the dynamics of the intracluster plasma via the [[planck-sz-cluster-catalog|Planck SZ cluster catalog]]. This extreme environment plays a pivotal role in shaping galaxy properties; environmental effects such as ram-pressure stripping and strangulation drive profound morphological transformations. Consequently, the fraction of early-type galaxies reaches $\sim 80\%$ in regular clusters, compared to only $\sim 30\%$ in the general field .

On even larger scales, clusters group into massive associations known as superclusters. Observations of the local [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] indicate that the richest superclusters form a quasiregular pattern with characteristic separations of $120-140\, h^{-1}$ Mpc, often aligning within immense planar structures such as the Local Supercluster plane [Einasto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522082E/abstract). 

Modern cosmography maps these immense structures by analyzing the [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]] to identify "basins of attraction" (BoA)—three-dimensional domains where matter kinematically converges toward a common gravitational potential minimum. While earlier surveys identified the Milky Way as residing within the [[laniakea-supercluster|Laniakea Supercluster]], recent probabilistic reconstructions of the cosmic velocity field suggest that Laniakea may in fact be a sub-region drawn toward the much larger Shapley basin of attraction [Valade et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1610V/abstract). The largest individual basin of attraction recovered in the local Universe corresponds to the Sloan [[great-wall-large-scale-structure|Great Wall (large-scale structure)]], which encompasses an enormous comoving volume of approximately $15.5 \times 10^6\, (h^{-1} \text{Mpc})^3$ [Valade et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1610V/abstract). The physical origin of such immense, quasi-regular structural planes and whether their extreme scales can be entirely accommodated within standard $\Lambda$CDM models remains an area of active theoretical investigation [Einasto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522082E/abstract). From an information-theoretic perspective, the emergence of this complex cosmic web alters the macroscopic state of the universe; recent theoretical frameworks suggest that the formation of large-scale structures results in a $0.004\%$ drop in the total information entropy of the present universe [Sarkar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240703042S/abstract).

## Fundamental Scaling Relations

Throughout the cosmos, the [[global-observables-of-galaxies|Global observables of galaxies]] exhibit tight empirical correlations known as [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]]. These scaling laws link structural and kinematic parameters—such as mass, size, luminosity, and velocity—serving as critical constraints for models of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] [White et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997gsr..proc....3W/abstract).

One of the most robust kinematic correlations is the Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation (BTFR), which tightly correlates the total baryonic mass $M_b$ of a rotationally supported galaxy with its characteristic rotational velocity $V_c$. This relation typically follows a power law of the form $M_b \propto V_c^4$ [Marongwe et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251120188M/abstract). Recent deep [[cosmic-shear|Cosmic shear]] and kinematic surveys reveal that both late-type galaxies (LTGs) and [[early-type-fraction|Early-type fraction]] galaxies adherence to the same BTFR out to several hundred kiloparsecs [Lelli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102499L/abstract). Notably, the BTFR appears remarkably stable over cosmic time; while the [[luminosity-functions-and-scaling-relations|Luminosity Functions and Scaling Relations]] exhibit zero-point evolution, the total baryonic relation shows no significant zero-point shift from $z = 0.6$ to the present day, implying that the gas reservoirs converted into stars over the past 6 Gyr were already gravitationally bound to their host systems early on [Puech et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..277..138P/abstract). 

Closely related to the BTFR is the Radial acceleration relation (RAR), which links the total observed dynamical acceleration $g_{\text{obs}}$ to the Newtonian acceleration predicted from the baryonic mass distribution alone, $g_{\text{bar}}$. When combining galaxy data with Solar System data, this correlation holds across approximately 16 orders of magnitude in Newtonian baryonic acceleration [Lelli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102499L/abstract). The RAR highlights a characteristic, universal acceleration scale of $g_0 \sim 10^{-8} \text{ cm s}^{-2}$, below which the observed acceleration deviates from the Newtonian baryonic expectation [Walker et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ConPh..55..198W/abstract).

On the structural side, early-type galaxies are bound by the Fundamental Plane, which connects their effective radius, surface brightness, and central velocity dispersion. Along with the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]], these correlations trace the mass assembly of galaxies over time. Simulations suggest that the evolution of the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] is strongly tied to [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]] mechanisms, where early central growth naturally limits the degree of dramatic structural evolution expected at late times [Brook et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...639..126B/abstract).

The emergence of these remarkably tight scaling relations poses a significant theoretical challenge. In the standard $\Lambda$CDM framework, [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]] predicts that [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] should depend on a complex interplay of at least six independent parameters, including halo mass, angular momentum, baryon fraction, age, size, and chaotic merger histories [Disney et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455.1082D/abstract). The observation that diverse galaxies are effectively governed by a single parameter has led to conflicting interpretations in the literature. Some researchers argue that self-regulated baryonic processes, such as supernova feedback, effectively erase historical complexities and enforce simple scaling relations [Chakraborti et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732..105C/abstract). Conversely, others highlight that [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]], such as Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), successfully predicted the RAR and BTFR a priori without requiring a dark matter component, sustaining an active debate over the physical origin of galactic simplicity [Lelli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102499L/abstract), [McGaugh et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021SHPSA..88..220M/abstract).

## The Early Universe: Cosmic Dawn and Reionization

The [[early-universe|Early universe]] transitioned from the cosmic "dark ages" into the Cosmic Dawn with the gravitational collapse of the first non-linear density perturbations. This epoch is defined by the ignition of the first generation of metal-free, Population III stars. Theoretical models suggest that the earliest observable stars could have formed at $z \approx 65$, roughly $30$ million years after the [[big-bang|Big Bang]] [Naoz et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.373L..98N/abstract), [Madau et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003acfp.conf...39M/abstract). These initial stellar populations initiated the chemical enrichment of the cosmos, successfully raising the mean metallicity of the universe to approximately $0.001$ solar by $z = 6$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract).

The ultraviolet radiation emitted by these nascent stars and the first generations of galaxies fundamentally altered the state of the intergalactic medium (IGM) during the Epoch of Reionization. Current observational constraints strongly suggest that early star-forming galaxies provided the necessary Lyman-continuum photons to completely reionize the neutral hydrogen in the IGM by $z \approx 6$ [Bouwens et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..423..111B/abstract), [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). However, the literature remains ambiguous regarding the exact luminosity dependence and redshift evolution of the photon escape fraction. This leaves a remarkably tight ionizing photon budget that continues to be a subject of active research and theoretical uncertainty [Bouwens et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..423..111B/abstract), [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract).

Following the initial assembly at Cosmic Dawn, the universe progressed toward its peak epoch of galaxy assembly between $z=2$ and $z=4$. Observations during this era reveal distinct structural patterns where galaxies possessed enormous reservoirs of interstellar gas and dust, sustaining maximal star formation rates before eventually transitioning into the more quiescent structures seen today [Shapley et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ARA&A..49..525S/abstract).

A major frontier in [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] involves tracing the structural assembly of galaxies during the first billion years. Under the standard [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], galaxies are expected to grow chaotically through continuous mergers, only gradually settling into organized, rotating disks at later epochs. However, recent high-resolution observations from ALMA and JWST have revealed a surprising abundance of "morphologically mature" galaxies at extremely high redshifts. For example, dynamically cold, regularly rotating gas disks hosting massive central bulges have been detected as early as $z \simeq 5$, just $1.2$ billion years after the Big Bang [Lelli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...371..713L/abstract). Furthermore, clear evidence of [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]] has been observed in a system at $z \sim 7$, where a highly compact proto-bulge (with a half-light radius of $< 100$ pc) is surrounded by an actively star-forming disk just $700$ Myr after the Big Bang [Baker et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230602472B/abstract), [Baker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..141B/abstract). Other studies have resolved primordial rotating disks at $z \simeq 6$ into numerous dense star-forming clumps ($10-60$ pc radii) that appear to be driven by disk instabilities rather than violent mergers [Fujimoto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240218543F/abstract). Adding to these structural anomalies is the discovery of early quenched galaxies. Recent observations have identified quiescent, "napping" systems existing much earlier in the primordial Universe than previously anticipated, challenging established timelines regarding how rapidly massive galaxies can form their stars and entirely cease star formation [Antwi-Danso et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Natur.629...45A/abstract).

The discovery of such massive, ordered, and sometimes already-quenched galaxies so early in the cosmic timeline presents an emerging tension in modern cosmology [Primack et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ARNPS..74..173P/abstract). The literature is currently conflicting on whether these systems fundamentally challenge the standard $\Lambda$CDM model of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]]. Some authors argue that the rapid assembly of massive bulges and rotating disks occurs significantly faster than standard hierarchical models can comfortably predict, potentially requiring new mechanisms for accelerated gas accretion and feedback [Lelli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...371..713L/abstract), [Fujimoto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240218543F/abstract). Conversely, other studies propose that these unusually massive high-redshift objects do not break the cosmological paradigm, but instead represent highly biased, extreme overdensities that act as the progenitors of massive [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] assembling in rare protocluster environments [Rennehan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240606672R/abstract). Resolving this debate relies on expanding the statistical sample of high-redshift galaxies and accurately mapping their specific star formation rates and structural kinematics across the Cosmic Dawn.

## Open Questions and Future Frontiers

While the $\Lambda$CDM model remains the cornerstone of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]], high-precision astronomical surveys have increasingly exposed critical observational and theoretical tensions. At the fundamental physics level, the identity of the constituents dominating the Universe's mass-energy budget remains elusive. The physical nature of Dark Energy, which drives the accelerated cosmic expansion, is entirely unknown [Rees et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003RSPTA.361.2427R/abstract). Furthermore, local and early-Universe measurements are increasingly in conflict; the Hubble tension—a statistically significant discrepancy in measurements of the Hubble constant, $H_0$—and the $S_8$ tension regarding the amplitude of matter fluctuations represent severe challenges to the concordance model [Primack et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ARNPS..74..173P/abstract). To address these profound tensions, theorists are exploring novel frameworks such as the $\eta$CDM model, which proposes a stochastic evolution of large-scale patches to explain emergent dark energy and alleviate the persisting $H_0$ and $f\sigma_8$ tensions [Lapi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205823L/abstract).

On galactic scales, the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]] predicts a chaotic assembly history governed by multiple distinct parameters (such as mass, spin, and merger history). However, observations frequently reveal a "one-parameter" simplicity across diverse galaxy populations [Disney et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455.1082D/abstract), [Walker et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ConPh..55..198W/abstract). Tight [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]], such as the Baryonic Tully-Fisher Relation and the Radial Acceleration Relation, are reproduced with high accuracy by [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]] (MOND) without invoking dark matter [Lelli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102499L/abstract). Other alternative frameworks, such as General Relativity field self-interaction, have also been proposed to explain these dynamics without dark matter or Dark Energy [Deur et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170902481D/abstract). Within the dark matter paradigm, unexpected large-scale clustering observed in isolated, diffuse dwarf galaxies conflicts with existing models of galaxy evolution in $\Lambda$CDM, even though it aligns with standard halo assembly bias; resolving this may require exotic physics such as Self-interacting dark matter [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250403305Z/abstract).

The timeline of early galaxy assembly represents another major frontier. The recent discovery of massive, morphologically mature galaxies at very high redshifts ($z > 5$) strains standard evolutionary timelines. Observations of highly rotating disks and compact proto-bulges already in place within the first $700$ million to $1.2$ billion years after the Big Bang suggest that galaxies can assemble and undergo inside-out growth far more rapidly than typically predicted [Lelli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...371..713L/abstract), [Baker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..141B/abstract). 

At lower redshifts, the literature is currently conflicted on the exact nature of galactic quenching. While state-of-the-art cosmological simulations predict a large population of strictly "dead" galaxies, observational constraints suggest that many [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] may retain a residual specific star-formation rate of roughly $10^{-12} \text{ yr}^{-1}$ [Corcho-Caballero et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.5108C/abstract). Additionally, efforts to map the cosmic [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] are hampered by lingering uncertainties over whether the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] is truly universal across different cosmic environments and epochs [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...39H/abstract).

Addressing these open questions will rely heavily on next-generation observational facilities. While the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and the Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) probe the rest-frame optical and ultraviolet light of the earliest galaxies [Bradač et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..478B/abstract), [D'Odorico et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ExA....58...21D/abstract), radio arrays like the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) will map the evolution of neutral hydrogen gas through [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Galaxy Surveys and Catalogs]]. The planned eLISA mission will open a new window into the early Universe via gravitational wave astronomy, allowing researchers to trace the hierarchical mergers and co-evolution of supermassive black holes deep into the cosmic dark ages [eLISA Consortium et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1305.5720E/abstract), [Komossa et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUFM..29B.292K/abstract). Furthermore, the emerging field of nano-Hertz gravitational wave astronomy promises new breakthroughs; the angular cross-correlation between multi-frequency stochastic gravitational wave backgrounds and galaxy density surveys can uniquely trace the cosmic evolution and hierarchical merging of supermassive black holes [Sah et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240711669S/abstract).
