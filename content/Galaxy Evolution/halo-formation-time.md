## Revised Full Draft


In the standard $\Lambda$CDM model of cosmology, dark matter structures grow from the bottom up through [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]]. **Halo formation time** (or assembly time) is the characteristic epoch at which a dark matter halo has accreted a specified fraction—typically half—of its final virial mass. This fundamental parameter encapsulates a halo's complex [[mass-evolution|Mass evolution]] and [[time-dependent-dark-matter-assembly|Time-dependent dark matter assembly]] into a single, highly predictive metric. Theoretical expectations for these formation epochs trace back to analytical excursion set models based on the [[press-schechter-formalism|Press-Schechter formalism]], which demonstrate that less massive density perturbations cross the threshold for non-linear collapse earlier than their more massive counterparts. In modern astrophysical research, halo formation time is rigorously extracted from $N$-body simulations using [[halo-merger-trees|Halo merger trees]], which track the mass of a halo's main progenitor backward through time across discrete major mergers and smooth [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]].

A halo's [[formation-epoch|Formation epoch]] is intimately linked to both its internal dynamics and the evolution of the galaxies it hosts. Because collisionless cold dark matter preserves the memory of the background density of the Universe at the time of its initial collapse via violent relaxation [White et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996grdy.conf..121W/abstract), early-forming halos are systematically more centrally concentrated than late-forming ones [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..563N/abstract). This fundamental relationship governs universal [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]]. Furthermore, the assembly history of a [[parent-halo|Parent halo]] plays a decisive role in baryonic physics. A halo's age and its [[specific-halo-mass-accretion-rate|Specific halo mass accretion rate]] strongly dictate the star formation history and [[halo-quenching|Halo quenching]] of its central galaxy, as well as the depletion of its satellite subhalos. Cosmological simulations also robustly predict "assembly bias"—a secondary clustering dependence where the spatial distribution of halos relies not only on their mass but also on their formation time, with early-forming low-mass systems clustering far more strongly in dense cosmic environments.

Despite its foundational role, accurately defining and interpreting halo formation time presents significant theoretical and observational challenges. The traditional reliance on expanding spherical overdensity boundaries introduces "pseudo-evolution," an artifact where a halo's mass appears to grow at late times simply due to the decreasing background density of the Universe. This ambiguity has prompted a modern shift toward tracking physical, dynamical boundaries like the [[splashback-radius|Splashback radius]]. Furthermore, the pristine correlation between a halo's age and its inner density profile can be significantly altered by baryonic feedback, which can drive [[halo-expansion|Halo expansion]] and erase primordial dark matter cusps [Dutton et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..655D/abstract). Independently, dynamical mechanisms such as disk bars transferring angular momentum can also alter these inner profiles [Weinberg et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...580..627W/abstract). Several open questions also remain at the forefront of the field: observational detections of galaxy assembly bias remain highly contested, complicating the effort to link [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|Dark Matter Halo Properties and Statistics]] to observable galaxy populations. Simultaneously, researchers are probing the limits of standard assembly theory by analyzing extreme [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] detected by JWST, and by using halo collapse timescales to distinguish between Cold Dark Matter and alternative paradigms like Warm Dark Matter, where a lack of small-scale power significantly delays the formation of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]].

## Definitions and Mathematical Framework

In the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm of [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]], dark matter halos grow continuously via smooth accretion and discrete mergers. A halo's formation time provides a characteristic, single-parameter summary of its [[mass-evolution|Mass evolution]]. It is generally defined as the epoch when a halo has assembled a specified fraction $f$ of its final mass $M_0$ at the observation redshift $z_0$. The most common threshold is $f=0.5$, yielding the **half-mass assembly** epoch, often denoted by the redshift $z_{50}$. 

Alternatively, principal component analysis (PCA) can be used to define halo age dynamically; the first principal component accounts for approximately 60% of the variance in assembly histories and provides a rigorous definition of dynamical age that captures more complexity than a single mass threshold [Wong et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757..102W/abstract). Furthermore, the choice of definition strongly influences the perceived physical trends; defining formation time based on the initial assembly of the galaxy's baryonic core rather than the total dark matter mass can reveal phenomena like 'downsizing' in lower-mass halos, contrary to simplistic hierarchical expectations [Li et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.389.1419L/abstract).

**Main Progenitor Assembly**
The mathematical definition of assembly depends heavily on how mass is tracked backward through [[halo-merger-trees|Halo merger trees]]. The most widely used approach relies on the **main progenitor** history, which is synonymous with the halo's mass accretion history. The main progenitor is identified by tracing the most massive branch at each node of the merger tree back in time. Under this framework, the assembly time $t_a$ is defined as the time when the main progenitor mass $M_{\rm main}(t)$ first reaches half of the descendant's final mass:
$$ M_{\rm main}(t_a) = \frac{M_0}{2} $$
While $f=0.5$ is standard, other mass fractions are frequently used to characterize different phases of halo growth. For instance, the time at which the main progenitor reaches $f=0.04$ is sometimes utilized to define the [[formation-epoch|Formation epoch]] of the halo's tightly bound, centrally concentrated inner core [Giocoli et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422..185G/abstract). 

**Sum of All Progenitors**
An alternative definition incorporates the *entire* merger history rather than just the main trunk. Following the foundational approach of Navarro, Frenk, & White (1996) [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..563N/abstract), the formation time $t_f$ can be defined as the epoch when the sum of all progenitors exceeding a minimum mass threshold $M_{\rm min}$ equals half the final mass:
$$ \sum_{M_i > M_{\rm min}} M_i(t_f) = \frac{M_0}{2} $$
The threshold $M_{\rm min}$ can be set either as a fixed fraction of the descendant mass $M_0$ or as an absolute mass scale independent of the descendant. This formulation accounts for the fact that a halo's constituents may have already collapsed into smaller bound structures well before they merge into the primary host.

**Analytic Framework and Pseudo-Evolution**
Analytically, halo formation rates and the probability distributions of formation times are derived using excursion set theory, an extension of the [[press-schechter-formalism|Press-Schechter formalism]]. By tracking the random walk of density perturbations, this formalism allows for the calculation of conditional mass functions and merger rates, providing a robust mathematical framework that matches $N$-body simulations to varying degrees of accuracy [Percival et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..9330P/abstract)[Mitra et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011BASI...39..563M/abstract). Extracting these properties from simulations, however, poses distinct challenges; measuring individual halo properties, such as spin and inner density, requires high particle counts ($N > 100-400$) to avoid systematic numerical biases [Trenti et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711.1198T/abstract).

Furthermore, calculations of the mass accretion history are sensitive to the physical boundaries used to define halo mass. The literature notes a significant ambiguity caused by "pseudo-evolution," particularly for [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] ($M < 10^{12} M_\odot$). Because halo mass is traditionally defined as a spherical overdensity relative to a background reference density (which strictly decreases as the universe expands), the calculated halo mass increases over time even if the physical density profile of the dark matter remains completely static [Diemer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...766...25D/abstract). This coordinate effect complicates the purely physical interpretation of half-mass assembly times, as mass growth at late times may reflect the changing reference density rather than the true dynamical accretion of new material. In addition to these dark matter boundaries, observational characterization of the halo's baryonic content presents its own complexities; for instance, the Galactic halo is permeated by a Warm Ionized Metal Medium (WIMM) with a temperature of approximately $2900 \text{ K}$, which can be distinguished from the denser galactic disc medium via X-ray absorption analysis [Nicastro et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457..676N/abstract).

## Historical Development of Halo Assembly Theory

The theoretical framework for halo assembly originated with the realization that dark matter orchestrates galaxy formation, collapsing hierarchically before baryonic dissipation can occur. Early analytical models relied on the [[press-schechter-formalism|Press-Schechter formalism]], which provided a simple, albeit mathematically non-rigorous, basis for estimating the abundance and merger rates of dark matter halos. By applying this formalism, researchers calculated the first analytic halo formation rates, demonstrating good agreement with early Monte-Carlo and N-body simulations [Percival et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..9330P/abstract). 

This foundational work was subsequently refined by excursion set theory (often associated with the extended Press-Schechter or Bond-Cole-Efstathiou models), which allowed for the derivation of [[halo-merger-trees|progenitor distributions]]. Excursion set theory formally codified the paradigm of [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]], demonstrating analytically that low-mass halos generally cross the critical density threshold for non-linear collapse earlier than high-mass halos. Continuous improvements in excursion set models resolved early mathematical inconsistencies—such as the negative halo formation rates predicted by the older Sasaki formalism—yielding strictly positive formation rates at all mass scales [Mitra et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011BASI...39..563M/abstract).

As theoretical models matured, attention turned to the physical mechanisms governing the transition from a chaotic collapsing density perturbation to a virialized dark matter halo. Unlike baryonic gas, which dissipates energy through radiative cooling, collisionless cold dark matter reaches quasi-equilibrium via violent relaxation [White et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996grdy.conf..121W/abstract). During halo mergers, violent relaxation rapidly transforms the orbital energy of the progenitor halos into the internal binding energy of the remnant. This dissipationless relaxation process erases much of the initial condition memory, consistently producing nearly universal density profiles whose characteristic inner densities strongly reflect the background density of the Universe at the epoch of formation [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..563N/abstract). Additionally, researchers recognized that the persistence of phase-space substructure and debris streams in these CDM halos is fundamentally governed by the conservation of phase-space density [Helmi et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.339..834H/abstract).

The advent of high-resolution N-body simulations in the late 1990s revolutionized assembly theory by allowing researchers to track the exact mass accretion history of individual halos. This era introduced the widespread use of [[halo-merger-trees|Halo merger trees]], enabling rigorous operational definitions of a halo's age. A foundational and widely adopted definition, introduced by Navarro, Frenk, & White (1996), identifies the formation time, $t_f$, as the redshift at which the sum of all progenitors exceeding a minimum mass threshold first assembled half of the final descendant mass [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..563N/abstract).

While analytical models provide a powerful conceptual framework, they exhibit distinct discrepancies when compared directly to modern simulations. For instance, the extended Press-Schechter formalism systematically underpredicts the number of high-redshift halos and generally predicts later formation times than those observed in N-body simulations for masses ranging from $10^{-3} M_*$ to $8.7 M_*$, where $M_*$ is the characteristic non-linear mass scale [Lin et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.344.1327L/abstract). Today, N-body simulations serve as the primary tool for extracting detailed assembly metrics. Recent computational advancements have even applied machine learning algorithms to these simulated data sets, predicting halo formation times based on internal halo and baryonic properties with a median bias of $<4-9\%$ [Srivastava et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250414426S/abstract).

## Hierarchical Merging and Mass Growth Channels

In the standard cosmological framework, dark matter halos assemble their mass from the bottom up via the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]]. In this model, small initial density perturbations collapse early and subsequently coalesce to form increasingly massive systems. The trajectory of a halo's mass over time is formalized as its Mass accretion history (MAH), which is typically tracked using [[halo-merger-trees|Halo merger trees]] by following the most massive (main) progenitor at each branching point [Giocoli et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422..185G/abstract). Observationally, the detection of phase-space substructures, such as cold streams and shell-like patterns in galaxies like M87, provides direct evidence of this ongoing halo assembly [Romanowsky et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748...29R/abstract).

Halo growth is driven by distinct physical channels: smooth [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]], minor mergers, and major subhalo mergers. Cosmological $N$-body simulations demonstrate that the halo merger rate evolves strongly with redshift. For instance, at $z \lesssim 2$, the overarching merger rate of the halo population scales roughly as $(1+z)^3$ [Gottlöber et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...546..223G/abstract). When restricting the analysis to major mergers (typically defined by a mass ratio $m/M > 0.3$), the instantaneous destruction rate of subhalos falling into Milky Way-sized or larger systems is well-described by the fitting formula $dN/dt \simeq 0.03(1+f)\,\mathrm{Gyr}^{-1}(1+z)^{2.1}$, where $f$ is the subhalo-to-host mass ratio [Stewart et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...702.1005S/abstract). The efficiency of this growth is also highly environmentally dependent; halos located in dense cosmic environments experience mergers up to 2.5 times more frequently than their counterparts in cosmic voids [Fakhouri et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.394.1825F/abstract).

While major and minor mergers fundamentally transform [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]], flyby interactions represent a historically overlooked driver of mass assembly and dynamical heating. In a flyby event, a halo deeply penetrates the potential well of a primary system but possesses sufficient orbital energy to exit without merging. For halos with masses $M \gtrsim 10^{11} h^{-1} M_\odot$ at $z \lesssim 2$, the frequency of close flyby interactions equals or even surpasses the frequency of direct mergers [Sinha et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...751...17S/abstract). The demographic classification of these interactions depends sensitively on the chosen physical halo boundary. When halo boundaries are defined by the [[splashback-radius|Splashback radius]]—the physical boundary set by the first orbital apocenter of recently accreted matter—many transient flyby or "backsplash" systems are reclassified as bound satellite subhalos. Employing the splashback radius can effectively double the measured abundance of subhalos relative to traditional spherical overdensity boundaries [More et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...810...36M/abstract)[Diemer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909..112D/abstract).

Finally, when quantifying these mass growth channels, it is critical to distinguish physical mass accretion from coordinate-induced "pseudo-evolution." Because conventional halo masses (e.g., $M_{200c}$) are defined relative to a background reference density that decreases as the Universe expands, the virial radius artificially expands outward over time. This pseudo-evolution can account for almost the entirety of the late-time apparent mass growth for halos hosting [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] ($M \lesssim 10^{12} h^{-1} M_\odot$), even when the physical density profile of the dark matter remains entirely static [Diemer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...766...25D/abstract).

## Correlation with Internal Halo Structure

The assembly history of a dark matter halo leaves a profound imprint on its present-day internal kinematics and density distribution. Within the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm, the fundamental relationship between a halo's [[formation-epoch|Formation epoch]] and its internal structure is primarily governed by the background density of the Universe at the time of its collapse. 

N-body simulations demonstrate that, following violent relaxation, collisionless dark matter halos settle into a quasi-equilibrium [[core-halo-structure|Core-halo structure]] that is broadly independent of mass or initial conditions [White et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996grdy.conf..121W/abstract). This universal [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]] is classically described by the NFW profile:
$$ \rho(r) = \frac{\delta_{\rm char} \rho_c}{(r/r_s)(1+r/r_s)^2} $$
where $\rho_c$ is the critical density of the universe, $r_s$ is a characteristic scale radius, and $\delta_{\rm char}$ is a dimensionless characteristic overdensity [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..563N/abstract). The structural concentration of the halo is defined as the ratio of its outer boundary to its scale radius, typically $c \equiv R_{\rm vir}/r_s$. 

A cornerstone of modern galaxy formation theory is the concentration-mass-age relation. Because halo cores undergo inside-out, bottom-up assembly during the earliest phases of collapse, the inner density of a halo—parameterized by $\delta_{\rm char}$—reflects the mean cosmic density at the time of its formation. Halos that assemble early do so in a denser Universe, resulting in more tightly bound, highly concentrated structures. Conversely, massive halos, which typically assemble late in the hierarchical structure formation process, exhibit lower central concentrations [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..563N/abstract). By combining halo mass and formation time, the logarithmic concentration $\log_{10} c$ of a halo can be predicted with a precision of $\sim 0.09$ dex, compared to $\sim 0.13$ dex when using mass alone [Giocoli et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422..185G/abstract). At a fixed mass, the scatter in concentration is well-modeled by a log-normal distribution with a variance of $\sigma_{\log c} \simeq 0.12 \pm 0.02$.

While the NFW profile is often treated as universally applicable across 20 orders of magnitude in mass [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Natur.585...39W/abstract), there is notable tension in the literature regarding its exact shape. Some high-resolution simulations indicate that density profiles are not strictly universal, with the inner logarithmic slope showing a systematic mass dependence [Jing et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...529L..69J/abstract). Other studies suggest the empirical Einasto profile provides a superior fit, particularly showing that the concentration-mass relation flattens significantly across the redshift range $0 \le z \le 3$, where the average concentration of massive, relaxed halos approaches a constant value of $c \sim 3.5 - 4$ [Gao et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.387..536G/abstract). 

Beyond the radial density profile, a halo's formation time strongly dictates its three-dimensional shape. Halos that assemble early and have not experienced recent major mergers tend to be more spherical. In contrast, late-forming halos that have undergone recent major mergers often exhibit highly prolate geometries, with their major axes aligned along the direction of the last merger event or local cosmic web filaments. Observationally, the Milky Way's halo may be prolate, becoming increasingly elongated at larger radii as traced by the flaring of H I gas [Banerjee et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732L...8B/abstract). The internal stellar constituents also reflect these complex assembly modes; the duality of the Galactic stellar halo, where the inner and outer components display distinct rotation patterns and metallicities, underscores the diverse merging and accretion events that built the galaxy over time [Carollo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Natur.450.1020C/abstract). These internal stellar properties are further illuminated by the observed turnover in the Galactic halo's main-sequence luminosity function, which provides essential constraints on the fundamental mass distribution of these stellar halo constituents [Mould et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996PASP..108...35M/abstract). To robustly quantify these non-spherical shapes, especially in dwarf galaxies, recent methods employ the Jeans equations to define gravitational torque as a measure of line-of-sight projected ellipticity. This technique promises to distinguish non-spherical halos observationally utilizing data from Gaia and the Thirty Meter Telescope (TMT) [Evslin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...826L..23E/abstract). Theoretically, the [[halo-mass-gap-statistic|Halo mass-gap statistic]]—the logarithmic mass difference between a host halo and its most massive subhalo—correlates with both halo age and concentration in $N$-body simulations, with older, highly concentrated halos systematically exhibiting larger mass gaps. However, large scatter limits its use on an object-by-object basis. More importantly, when testing this observationally using the stellar mass-gap, no such trend with galaxy properties is found, preventing its reliable use as an observational proxy [Deason et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777..154D/abstract). 

Finally, while collisionless dark matter models predict persistent central cusps, the inclusion of baryonic physics introduces significant structural modifications. Gas cooling and dissipation can induce adiabatic contraction, further increasing inner dark matter densities [Gnedin et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1108.5736G/abstract). When comparing dark matter to these baryonic components, stellar halo density profiles typically drop off more steeply than dark matter profiles because stars form from the most tightly bound infalling material [Bullock et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..327...80B/abstract). Furthermore, dynamical friction acting on heavy baryonic clumps during [[early-galaxy-formation|early galaxy formation]] can flatten inner halo density cusps [Jardel et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691.1300J/abstract). Strong supernova feedback can also drive rapid fluctuations in the local gravitational potential, leading to [[halo-expansion|Halo expansion]] and the destruction of cusps to form flattened density cores in dwarf galaxies [Dutton et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..655D/abstract). Independently, dynamical mechanisms such as a disk bar transferring angular momentum to the dark matter can also erase these central cusps over time [Weinberg et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...580..627W/abstract).

## Halo Assembly Bias and Environment

In the standard excursion set formalism of [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]], the clustering of dark matter halos is assumed to depend exclusively on their mass. However, high-resolution $N$-body simulations have robustly demonstrated that halo clustering exhibits a secondary dependence on assembly history at a fixed halo mass—a phenomenon known as halo assembly bias [Wechsler et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...652...71W/abstract). Theoretical models also suggest that primordial non-Gaussianity introduces a scale-dependent modification to this halo bias as a function of mass and redshift [Matarrese et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...677L..77M/abstract).

For low-mass halos ($M < M_*$, where $M_*$ is the characteristic non-linear mass scale), objects that form earlier are more strongly clustered than their late-forming counterparts. Interestingly, this trend reverses for rare, [[high-mass-galaxies|high-mass halos]] ($M > M_*$), where late-forming systems exhibit stronger clustering [Wechsler et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...652...71W/abstract). This effect is intrinsically tied to the large-scale structure: halos of a given mass form systematically earlier in denser environments [Harker et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.367.1039H/abstract). This environmental dependence distinguishes between 'accreting' halos at cosmic web nodes and 'stalled' halos in filaments; stalled halos generally host older stellar populations and thicker galactic discs [Romano-Díaz et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.1809R/abstract). Furthermore, the frequency of major interactions is highly environment-dependent, with galaxy-mass halos experiencing mergers approximately $2.5$ times more frequently in the densest regions compared to cosmic voids [Gottlöber et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...546..223G/abstract)[Fakhouri et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.394.1825F/abstract).

Beyond simple formation epochs, halo bias is strongly correlated with the [[specific-halo-mass-accretion-rate|Specific halo mass accretion rate]] ($sMAH$). Halos with steeper early mass accretion histories host galaxies that undergo [[halo-quenching|Halo quenching]] earlier, and the amplitude of this assembly bias signal increases with redshift, reaching a factor of two at $z=1$ for halos in the $10^{11.5}$-$10^{12} h^{-1} M_\odot$ range [Montero-Dorta et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508..940M/abstract). Additional secondary bias dependencies have been confirmed for internal properties such as halo concentration and spin [Sato-Polito et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.1570S/abstract), and scale-dependent assembly bias has even been characterized along the density profiles of cosmic voids [Verza et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...940L..16V/abstract). Furthermore, the non-universality of halo bias on small scales for mini-halos at redshifts $z > 10$ complicates the extrapolation of these low-redshift scaling relations to the earliest epochs of galaxy formation [Reed et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.394..624R/abstract).

While assembly bias is a robust prediction of collisionless dark matter simulations, detecting its manifestation in luminous galaxies—termed "galaxy assembly bias"—remains highly debated. Because galaxies reside in dark matter halos, their observable properties might be expected to trace the [[time-dependent-dark-matter-assembly|Time-dependent dark matter assembly]] of their hosts. However, several observational studies have struggled to find this signature, reporting little to no correlation between the large-scale environment and the properties of central [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] at a fixed halo mass [Tinker et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1107.5046T/abstract)[Lin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...819..119L/abstract)[Sunayama et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220503277S/abstract). Some analyses suggest that apparent environmental correlations largely disappear when accounting for "splashback" halos (former subhalos) and defining age by peak historical mass rather than current mass [Tinker et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.2504T/abstract).

Conversely, recent observations offer evidence supporting galaxy assembly bias. For example, variations of $\sim 0.4$ dex in the stellar mass of red central galaxies at a fixed halo mass have been correlated with $1$-$3$ Gyr variations in halo formation time, tracking the large-scale density field [Oyarzún et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974...29O/abstract). Similarly, passive central galaxies are found to reside in halos with higher historical mass-increase rates than their star-forming counterparts [Lyu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...959....5L/abstract). This ongoing tension in the literature highlights the complex interplay between pristine dark matter dynamics and the baryonic physics governing galaxy evolution, remaining a critical challenge for linking [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|Dark Matter Halo Properties and Statistics]] to observable galaxy populations.

## Impact on Galaxy Evolution and Quenching

The assembly history of a dark matter halo intrinsically regulates the star formation history, structural evolution, and final stellar mass of its resident galaxies. Cosmological simulations demonstrate that at a fixed halo mass, halos with an earlier [[formation-epoch|Formation epoch]] and a steeper [[specific-halo-mass-accretion-rate|Specific halo mass accretion rate]] host central galaxies that reach their peak star formation at higher redshifts [Montero-Dorta et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508..940M/abstract). For [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] residing in halos with $M_{\rm halo} < 10^{12} M_\odot$, rapid recent halo growth correlates with lower current stellar masses and higher specific star formation rates, primarily because these systems possessed less cold gas in their past compared to slowly accreting halos [Feldmann et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...871L..21F/abstract). Conversely, early-forming halos provide more time for in situ star formation and hierarchical merging, typically resulting in higher central stellar masses [Zhu et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...639L...5Z/abstract)[Artale et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.3978A/abstract). Interestingly, at a constant stellar mass, central galaxies in higher-mass halos exhibit younger assembly times, potentially due to having larger gas reservoirs available for prolonged star formation [Jackson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497.4262J/abstract). This age dependence, where the scatter in the stellar mass-halo mass relation is driven by in situ growth at low masses and hierarchical assembly at high masses, is considered a primary driver of observed galaxy scaling relations [Gu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833....2G/abstract). The scatter in this stellar mass-halo mass relation is directly sensitive to the duty cycle of star formation and the correlation between star formation and the overarching halo accretion histories [Hahn et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191001644H/abstract). This scatter rises significantly in dwarf galaxies at a halo mass scale of $M_{\rm halo} \sim 10^9 M_\odot$ due to the severe [[halo-quenching|quenching]] of their progenitor halos by cosmic reionization [Kim et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240815214K/abstract).

The epoch of halo assembly is also closely tied to [[halo-quenching|Halo quenching]] and structural types. Major mergers are significantly more common in massive galaxies, potentially explaining why Hubble types correlate strongly with galaxy mass [Maller et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..251M/abstract). Passive central galaxies generally reside in halos characterized by higher mass-increase rates across cosmic time [Lyu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...959....5L/abstract). Quantitative models show that passive central galaxies assemble half of their final stellar mass approximately $2 \text{ Gyr}$ earlier than their star-forming counterparts at the same present-day mass [Lyu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...959....5L/abstract). Additionally, the discovery of massive relic galaxies, such as ZF-UDS-7329 at $z \sim 3.2$, provides high-resolution snapshots of early cluster core formation, highlighting how rapidly dense central regions can assemble and subsequently quench [Hartmann et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250106050H/abstract).

Within the Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD) framework, the satellite galaxy population is highly sensitive to the [[parent-halo|Parent halo]] formation time. Older halos consistently host fewer satellite galaxies than younger halos of the same mass [Zhu et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...639L...5Z/abstract)[Artale et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.3978A/abstract). This depletion occurs because subhalos in early-forming systems have had more time to undergo dynamical friction and merge with the central galaxy [Artale et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.3978A/abstract). This process drives the significant growth of intracluster mass between redshifts $0.5$ and $0.9$ through the continuous stripping and disruption of satellite galaxies in massive halos [Skibba et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...807..152S/abstract), which contributes heavily to the buildup of the intracluster light [Contini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167....7C/abstract). To map these relationships observationally, nonlinear clustering data can be inverted to determine the second moment of the halo occupation distribution in a model-independent manner [Cooray et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...576L.105C/abstract).

Despite robust theoretical predictions from $N$-body and hydrodynamical simulations, the observational detection of galaxy assembly bias remains a subject of active debate. Several studies find little to no correlation between the large-scale environment and the quenched fraction for low-mass central galaxies ($M_* \lesssim 10^{10} M_\odot h^{-2}$), suggesting that their quenching processes may be decoupled from the formation history of the halo [Tinker et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1107.5046T/abstract)[Lin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...819..119L/abstract)[Tinker et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.2504T/abstract). Similarly, weak lensing and cross-correlation measurements of central galaxies in massive clusters have shown no significant clustering amplitude differences linked to halo assembly proxies [Sunayama et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220503277S/abstract). However, other observations do detect definitive assembly bias signals, reporting that a $\sim 0.4 \text{ dex}$ variation in stellar mass at a fixed halo mass is associated with a $1-3 \text{ Gyr}$ difference in halo formation time for red central galaxies [Oyarzún et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974...29O/abstract). 

## Physical Boundaries and the Pseudo-evolution of Mass

The definition of halo formation time and mass assembly history intrinsically relies on the physical boundary assigned to a dark matter halo. Traditionally, halo mass and radius are defined using a spherical overdensity threshold relative to a reference density, such as the mean or critical density of the universe (e.g., the virial radius, $R_{200m}$). However, because this reference background density decreases as the universe expands, a halo's boundary mathematically expands outward over time. This leads to the phenomenon of "pseudo-evolution," where the enclosed mass of a halo increases due to the redshift-dependent reference density rather than the physical accretion of new material [Diemer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...766...25D/abstract). For halos with static density profiles in physical coordinates, this coordinate effect can account for almost the entire apparent mass growth between $z=1$ and $z=0$ for [[low-mass-galaxies|low-mass systems]] ($M \lesssim 10^{12} h^{-1} M_\odot$) [Diemer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...766...25D/abstract). Pseudo-evolution heavily influences perceived structural changes, driving the observed evolution of the low-mass end of the halo concentration-mass relation and complicating the interpretation of hierarchical merging and mass functions [Diemer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...766...25D/abstract).

To address the arbitrary nature of the virial radius, recent frameworks advocate for the [[splashback-radius|Splashback radius]] ($R_{\rm sp}$) as a physically motivated, dynamical boundary. The splashback radius marks the location where newly accreted matter reaches its first orbital apocenter after turnaround, manifesting as a steep drop in the dark matter density profile [More et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...810...36M/abstract). The scale of $R_{\rm sp}$ depends sensitively on the specific mass accretion rate of the halo: for rapidly accreting halos, $R_{\rm sp}$ is approximately $0.8-1.0 R_{200m}$, whereas for slowly accreting halos, it extends to roughly $1.5 R_{200m}$ [More et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...810...36M/abstract). Observational and simulation-based measurements across a wide range of masses ($10^{13} - 3 \times 10^{15} M_\odot$) show that the normalized splashback radius ($R_{\rm sp}/R_{200m}$) exhibits a U-shaped mass evolution, with an upturn driven heavily by massive halos at low redshift [Xu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971..157X/abstract).

Adopting the splashback radius fundamentally alters our understanding of halo mass assembly and the inventory of subhalos. During late-stage evolution, the inner regions of a halo (e.g., the mass enclosed within four scale radii, $M_{<4r_s}$) may remain perfectly static while the splashback mass ($M_{\rm sp}$) continues to grow significantly, indicating a "quiet" interior despite ongoing peripheral accretion [More et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...810...36M/abstract). Furthermore, because $R_{\rm sp}$ often extends well beyond the traditional virial radius for older, slowly accreting systems, redefining the halo boundary has a profound effect on the [[shmf-subhalo-mass-function|SHMF (Subhalo mass function)]]. Using the splashback radius can effectively double the abundance of subhalos compared to virial definitions, reclassifying the majority of transient "flyby" or backsplash galaxies as bound satellites [Diemer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909..112D/abstract).

## Assembly in Alternative Dark Matter Paradigms

While the standard $\Lambda$CDM framework successfully describes the assembly of massive halos, alternative dark matter paradigms are frequently investigated to address structural tensions at small scales. Historically, baryonic dark matter candidates—such as Massive Compact Halo Objects (MACHOs)—were considered viable alternatives, and extensive microlensing surveys were proposed and utilized to search for these dark baryonic objects across a wide range of masses [Paczynski et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...304....1P/abstract). However, long-term microlensing surveys have robustly demonstrated that MACHOs in the mass range of $10^{-7}$ to $0.02 M_\odot$ comprise less than 20% of the Galactic halo mass [Renault et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997A&A...324L..69R/abstract). This effectively rules them out as the primary dark matter component and necessitates a non-baryonic framework [Freese et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000fist.conf...18F/abstract). Clear observational evidence for these extended dark matter halos is provided by local dwarf spheroidals like Draco, where stellar velocity dispersions rule out models where mass strictly follows light and suggest the halo has not been tidally truncated [Kleyna et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563L.115K/abstract).

As a result, attention in the literature frequently shifts to Warm Dark Matter (WDM) models, which significantly alter the theoretical timeline of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]]. In the standard [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]] paradigm of Cold Dark Matter (CDM), the matter power spectrum increases monotonically toward smaller scales, causing the density fluctuations associated with [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] to collapse early in cosmic history. Conversely, WDM models are characterized by a non-negligible particle free-streaming length that introduces a sharp power spectrum cutoff at small scales. 

The primary physical consequence of this power spectrum cutoff is a mass-dependent delay in the halo formation time, as well as structural changes; for instance, WDM halos are predicted to be systematically more spherical than their CDM counterparts [Bullock et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002sgdh.conf..109B/abstract). Cosmological N-body simulations comparing CDM and WDM demonstrate that the delay in halo collapse time correlates inversely with the $z=0$ descendant halo mass [Lovell et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.3029L/abstract). For the most massive dark matter halos, initial collapse begins at effectively the same epoch in both models. However, for low-mass descendant halos, the lack of small-scale power in WDM significantly delays the onset of collapse. Consequently, the divergence in halo assembly histories between WDM and CDM is most pronounced in the context of dwarf galaxy formation. In such frameworks, dwarf galaxies may have experienced high star formation efficiency during an early fast-accretion phase before reionization suppressed later growth [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...790L..17M/abstract). To characterize this suppression phenomenologically across various mass scales, models often incorporate the 'Halo Occupation Fraction' (HOF), representing the fraction of halos hosting luminous galaxies as a function of mass and reionization redshift [Benitez-Llambay et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.4887B/abstract).

Beyond WDM, modifications to gravity are also actively explored; for example, halos in $f(R)$ gravity can be modeled using an effective density field that mimics standard $\Lambda$CDM dynamical properties [He et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvL.115g1306H/abstract). In these extreme early environments, the critical halo mass threshold for the very first Population III star formation depends sensitively on Lyman-Werner radiation and baryon-dark matter streaming velocities [Kulkarni et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...40K/abstract).

This mass-dependent assembly delay has important observational implications, particularly for studies of the JWST frontier epoch at redshifts $z > 10$. It had been hypothesized that a WDM power spectrum cutoff might drastically suppress the number density of early galaxies relative to CDM expectations. However, because the delayed collapse time in WDM applies almost exclusively to the progenitors of present-day dwarf galaxies, the massive halos capable of hosting the bright galaxies currently detectable by JWST assemble at nearly identical times in both paradigms [Lovell et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.3029L/abstract). Thus, current measurements of the galaxy mass function at $z \approx 10$ are unlikely to definitively distinguish between CDM and WDM cosmologies based on halo assembly times alone [Lovell et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.3029L/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite decades of refinement in the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm, the study of halo assembly continues to pose significant theoretical and observational challenges. Current research frontiers are increasingly focused on the extreme early universe, advanced computational techniques, and redefining the physical boundaries of dark matter halos.

A major open question is the behavior of halo assembly during the epoch of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]]. Recent observations of unexpectedly massive **JWST high-z galaxies** challenge standard cosmological predictions, as the conventional [[press-schechter-formalism|Press-Schechter formalism]] and Sheth-Tormen mass functions systematically underpredict the abundance of these early structures [Fakhry et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250723742F/abstract). Reconciling these observations may require modified matter power spectra that amplify small-scale structure formation or adjustments to the assumed star formation efficiencies [Fakhry et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250723742F/abstract). High-redshift observations also offer a potential testing ground for alternative dark matter models; for instance, Warm Dark Matter predicts a delay in halo collapse times that correlates inversely with descendant mass, an effect that could suppress the abundance of early dwarf galaxies [Lovell et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.3029L/abstract). Furthermore, measuring the internal relaxation and 'age' of massive cluster halos directly via strong and weak gravitational lensing offers a promising second-generation test for competing cosmological models [Taylor et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AdAst2011E...6T/abstract).

To bypass the computationally expensive nature of $N$-body [[halo-merger-trees|Halo merger trees]], researchers are increasingly turning to **Machine learning** and robust statistical tools. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and random forest models can now predict a halo's formation time directly from its $z=0$ baryonic and spatial properties with low predictive biases of $< 4-9\%$ [Srivastava et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250414426S/abstract). Similarly, advanced statistical methods, such as the use of Gaussian Processes, are being deployed to efficiently fit HOD parameters for star-forming emission-line galaxies [Rocher et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...05..033R/abstract). 

Machine learning models have also identified the **Intracluster light** (ICL) as a crucial indicator of halo age [Srivastava et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250414426S/abstract). Early-forming, highly concentrated halos assemble their diffuse ICL earlier (around $z \sim 0.6$), primarily through the tidal stripping of satellite galaxies [Contini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167....7C/abstract). The dynamical history of these largest structures also remains a key focus; recent studies indicate that massive clusters achieved advanced thermal equilibrium approximately $1.8 \text{ Gyr}$ ago ($z \sim 0.14$), driven primarily by their mass accretion history [Sereno et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.5214S/abstract). This history is reflected in the 'fundamental plane' of galaxy clusters—formed by their characteristic radius, mass, and X-ray temperature—which serves as a proxy for the halo's formation time [Fujita et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Galax...7....8F/abstract). To resolve substructures in these clusters, 'core-tracking' has emerged as a vital technique to follow small-scale substructure and 'orphan' galaxies that have lost substantial dark mass [Korytov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..24K/abstract). Furthermore, the detailed characterization of dark matter halo substructure—especially subhalos without baryonic counterparts—is critical for predicting gamma-ray annihilation signals and optimizing future telescope observing strategies [Sanchez Conde et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200701747S/abstract).

Improved metrics for halo age are also opening new avenues for **Cosmological parameter constraints**. While cluster abundance is routinely used to constrain cosmology, the dynamical "age" of clusters and instantaneous halo merger rates exhibit unique sensitivities. Specifically, recent episodes of major growth provide constraints in the $\Omega_m$-$\sigma_8$ plane that are nearly orthogonal to those derived from traditional halo abundance studies [Amoura et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.3459A/abstract). Additionally, the presence of long tidal tails in galaxy collisions can be used to independently constrain the mass and extent of interacting dark matter halos [Dubinski et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..136..260D/abstract).

Finally, the literature remains divided on the observational reality of galaxy assembly bias. While simulations robustly predict that low-mass halos forming earlier are more strongly clustered than late-forming ones of the same mass, observational efforts to link galaxy properties to this halo assembly bias remain highly conflicted [Tinker et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.2504T/abstract)[Sunayama et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220503277S/abstract)[Oyarzún et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974...29O/abstract). Deep observations of local halos continue to yield surprises that complicate this picture; for example, long-period variable stars in the halo of NGC 5128 reveal episodic star formation events linked to past mergers and AGN activity, showing how messy baryonic feedback can be [Aghdam et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...972...47A/abstract). Resolving this tension may ultimately require abandoning arbitrary spherical overdensity definitions in favor of physical boundaries like the [[splashback-radius|Splashback radius]], which naturally accounts for the mass accretion rate and substantially alters the subhalo census [More et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...810...36M/abstract)[Diemer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909..112D/abstract).
