**Inside-out formation** is the prevailing theoretical model for the structural and chemical evolution of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], positing that galaxies assemble their mass and initiate star formation in their dense central regions before progressively building their extended outer disks over cosmic time. Situated within the broader framework of $\Lambda$CDM [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], this spatial growth pattern is fundamentally driven by the cosmological [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] and the conservation of angular momentum. As dark matter halos grow hierarchically over cosmic time, baryons that accrete at later epochs possess higher specific angular momentum. Consequently, this late-arriving gas cools and circularizes at progressively larger galactocentric radii [Bouwens et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...489L..21B/abstract), [Kennicutt et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ARA&A..36..189K/abstract). This continuous outside-in fueling of gas naturally drives the inside-out buildup of the stellar disk, a mechanism central to understanding [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]] across the universe.

Empirically, the inside-out paradigm is robustly supported by the spatial distribution of stellar populations and chemical abundances. Spatially resolved spectroscopy reveals characteristic negative radial gradients in both stellar age and gas-phase metallicity, demonstrating that galaxies harbor older, metal-rich cores surrounded by younger, relatively metal-poor outskirts [Pérez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764L...1P/abstract). At [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|high redshifts]], this evolutionary pathway is understood through the lens of [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]]: an initial, rapid phase of intense, *in-situ* star formation builds a compact central [[galactic-bulges|proto-bulge]], followed by a prolonged, secular phase of cold and hot gas accretion that forms the extended disk. The age and metallicity distributions of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] serve as valuable fossil records of this localized [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]], tracing both the early high-pressure formation of the core and the subsequent radial buildup of the galaxy's extended structures.

Beyond mass assembly, the inside-out framework also governs the cessation of star formation in [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]]. Observations indicate that the structural transition to the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] largely occurs via inside-out [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]], where the central regions exhaust, heat, or evacuate their molecular gas reservoirs first, leaving active star formation to persist only in the galactic outskirts. The physical drivers of this central suppression are typically attributed to morphological quenching—where a massive central bulge dynamically stabilizes the gas disk against fragmentation—and feedback from active galactic nuclei (AGN). Consequently, as massive star-forming galaxies mature into [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]], they do so differentially from the core outward. 

Despite a strong observational consensus for massive systems, several critical open questions and theoretical tensions remain. The inside-out assembly mode exhibits a strong mass dependence; [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] often display flat or inverted age gradients, suggesting a fundamental transition to outside-in growth. Furthermore, significant discrepancies exist between spatially resolved observations, which overwhelmingly show inside-out quenching, and major cosmological hydrodynamic simulations, which frequently fail to replicate this spatial pattern in transitioning galaxies. Resolving these conflicts requires ongoing investigation into whether the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] varies with local environment, the exact mechanisms driving central gas depletion, and the role of the broader [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]] in stripping gas from the outskirts of cluster satellites.

## Conceptual Foundations and Historical Development

The conceptual framework of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] has undergone a profound evolution from isolated, global collapse scenarios to spatially resolved, cosmological accretion models. Historically, early theories posited a [[monolithic-collapse|Monolithic collapse]], wherein galaxies—particularly spheroids and [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]]—formed via a single, rapid gravitational collapse at high redshift, followed by the passive evolution of their stellar populations. In this classical picture, galaxies were largely assembled simultaneously, with structural properties determined early on and little subsequent size evolution.

The advent of the cold dark matter (CDM) cosmology drove a paradigm shift toward models governed by hierarchical clustering. The foundational Two-stage theory proposed by White & Rees (1978) posited that dark matter halos assemble first through hierarchical mergers, creating gravitational potential wells into which baryonic gas subsequently cools and condenses. This cosmological context inherently challenged earlier [[no-evolution-models|No-evolution models]] of galaxy sizes. As halos grow over time, the gas accreting at later epochs naturally possesses higher specific angular momentum, settling at progressively larger radii and driving the "inside-out" growth of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] [Bouwens et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...489L..21B/abstract).

To reconcile the hierarchical assembly of dark matter with the observed structural properties of massive galaxies, the community developed the framework of [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]]. In this model, galaxies experience an early, rapid phase of in-situ star formation at high redshift ($z > 2$), which builds a dense, compact proto-bulge or core fueled by cold gas streams [Johansson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1410.4359J/abstract). This is followed by a prolonged, slower second phase at $z < 3$, where the outer regions of the galaxy are assembled through both inside-out gas accretion fueling extended disk star formation and the deposition of stars via minor mergers ([[ex-situ-galaxy-growth|Ex-situ galaxy growth]]) [Cook et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.402.2113C/abstract), [Clauwens et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.3994C/abstract), [Ma et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250916138M/abstract). Analysis of JWST data up to $z \sim 7$ suggests that galaxy assembly and quenching are mass-driven and density-driven processes, with specific star formation rates showing no significant correlation with visual morphology at these early epochs [Conselice et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4857C/abstract).

This theoretical progression also reflects a transition from global to spatially resolved models of galaxy evolution. Early empirical and theoretical models were often restricted to describing the average properties of a galaxy—such as total gas mass and global star formation rate. However, modern observations have demonstrated that the transition to becoming [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]], as well as the assembly of stellar mass, occurs differentially across a galaxy's radius. Massive galaxies ($M_* > 10^{10.5} M_\odot$) are observed to grow their inner regions first, establishing older, metal-rich cores, while younger stellar populations continue to form in the extended, later-accreted outer disks [Pérez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764L...1P/abstract), [Whitney et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887..113W/abstract). Consequently, the inside-out paradigm has become the standard framework for understanding the spatially resolved [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and structural evolution of galaxies across cosmic time.

## Physical Mechanisms: Gas Accretion and Angular Momentum

The inside-out growth of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] is fundamentally driven by the cosmological [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] and the conservation of angular momentum. In the standard hierarchical framework, baryonic gas cools within the potential wells of dark matter halos and collapses to form rotationally supported disks. Reconstructed gas accretion histories from MaNGA indicate that massive galaxies accrete gas more rapidly and at earlier epochs, while persistent star formation in the current universe requires a continuous gas supply [Camps-Fariña et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...678A..65C/abstract). As dark matter halos grow over cosmic time, the gas accreting at later epochs originates from larger turnaround radii and consequently carries higher specific angular momentum. Upon cooling, this material circularizes at progressively larger galactocentric radii, naturally leading to the inside-out assembly of the stellar disk. The recent assembly of the Milky Way adheres strongly to this "leaky accretion disk" scenario, wherein coplanar gas inflow, ex-planar outflows, and *in-situ* star formation collectively govern the radial distribution of chemical abundances, reproducing the observed flattening of metallicity profiles at large radii [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...34W/abstract). Combining inside-out disk evolution models with the natal kicks of neutron star mergers successfully explains observed chemical abundance trends (such as [Eu/Fe] versus [Fe/H]) in the solar neighborhood, further linking local chemical signatures to global assembly patterns [Banerjee et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902L..34B/abstract).

The thermodynamic state of the accreting gas plays a critical role in pacing this radial growth. Hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate a mass- and redshift-dependent transition in how accretion proceeds. At high redshifts ($z \gtrsim 3$), galaxies are primarily fed by "cold-mode accretion" ($T \sim 10^4$ K), where unshocked gas streams penetrate the halo directly to the central regions. This rapid cold infall fuels intense, compact starbursts that build the galactic core or proto-bulge [Johansson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1410.4359J/abstract), [Woods et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442..732W/abstract). FIRE-2 simulations suggest that Milky Way-like galaxies undergo an early period of bursty star formation that forms the thick disc before transitioning to a steady phase approximately 6.5 Gyr ago that builds the thin disc [Yu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..889Y/abstract). As the halo grows more massive and the universe expands ($z < 3$), a stable virial shock develops, transitioning the dominant inflow mechanism to "hot-mode accretion". In this regime, gas is shock-heated to the virial temperature and forms an extended hot halo reservoir. The subsequent gradual cooling and settling of this gas predominantly fuels the extended, later-forming outer disk [Woods et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442..732W/abstract). This naturally sets up a [[multi-stage-formation|Multi-stage formation]] or [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] pattern: an early phase of rapid *in-situ* core formation followed by the steady, extended growth of the disk [Cook et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.402.2113C/abstract), [Clauwens et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.3994C/abstract), [Ma et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250916138M/abstract).

The regulation of star formation in these growing disks is frequently described using idealized [[bathtub-models|Bathtub models]] or equilibrium models. These models posit that star-forming galaxies exist in a slowly evolving equilibrium state governed by the mass conservation equation:
$$ \dot{M}_{\rm gas} = \dot{M}_{\rm in} - \dot{M}_{\rm out} - \dot{M}_{\star} $$
where $\dot{M}_{\rm in}$ is the gas accretion rate, $\dot{M}_{\rm out}$ is the outflow rate driven by stellar feedback, and $\dot{M}_{\star}$ is the star formation rate [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract), [Mitra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1184M/abstract). In these frameworks, the global [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] emerges as a natural consequence of self-regulation. At high redshifts ($z \sim 2-3$), reproducing observed trends requires sustained high star formation rates supported by gas accretion that is roughly equal to the sum of the star formation and outflow rates [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract). Furthermore, galaxies occupy a 'star forming plane' where continuous gas accretion and outflows regulate star formation activity, with variations in molecular-gas conversion efficiency being smaller than previously reported [Feldmann et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020CmPhy...3..226F/abstract). Alternative formulations such as the I$\kappa\varepsilon\alpha$ model provide a secularly stable framework where star formation rates are determined not solely by mass conservation, but by the energetic balance between stellar feedback and the deepening of the dark matter halo potential [Sharma et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.2418S/abstract).

Recent extensions of the bathtub model emphasize that galaxy radius acts as a crucial second parameter regulating this equilibrium. Observations indicate that at a fixed stellar mass, the star formation rate does not strongly correlate with the effective radius. To reconcile this with density-dependent star formation laws (e.g., the Kennicutt-Schmidt law), bathtub models require that smaller, more compact galaxies possess lower [[gas-fraction|gas fractions]] and weaker galactic winds to maintain the same net star formation rate as their larger counterparts [Lin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...93L/abstract). Furthermore, Milky Way Cepheid tracers confirm that the volumetric star formation law follows a slope of $\sim 2$, providing a more universal link between gas properties and star formation than surface density laws [Bacchini et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...632A.127B/abstract). Consequently, as a galaxy grows inside-out and its radius expands, the local efficiency of star formation, gas depletion timescales, and outflow loading factors must evolve radially. Over time, the inner regions deplete their gas and experience central suppression, while continuous gas accretion at the periphery sustains active star formation in the outer disk, driving the inside-out [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] process observed in massive galaxies [Colombo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A.367C/abstract).

## Observational Signatures: Age and Metallicity Gradients

The primary empirical signatures of inside-out galaxy formation are systematic, negative radial gradients in both stellar ages and chemical abundances. Because star formation propagates radially outward over cosmic time, the central regions of galaxies host older, more metal-rich stellar populations, while the outer disks are dominated by younger, relatively metal-poor stars. Spatially resolved spectroscopy in the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]] (e.g., from the CALIFA and MaNGA surveys) and high-resolution imaging at high redshifts have robustly confirmed these radial profiles.

In the local universe, integral field spectroscopy reveals that the spatially resolved [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] preserves the signal of [[morphological-downsizing|Morphological downsizing]]. More massive galaxies assembled their central spheroidal components and inner disks rapidly, typically 5-7 Gyr ago, followed by extended periods of star formation at larger radii [Pérez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764L...1P/abstract). This inside-out assembly is strongly mass-dependent. While galaxies with stellar masses $M_* > 10^{10.5} M_\odot$ exhibit characteristic negative gradients in near-ultraviolet to optical colors—where NUV - u and u - i color gradients are best explained by models where the inner regions of galaxies have significantly shorter e-folding growth timescales compared to the outer disks [Lian et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.4679L/abstract)—[[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] ($M_* < 10^{10} M_\odot$) frequently display flat or inverted (positive) age gradients, indicating a transition toward outside-in growth [Pan et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...804L..42P/abstract). Variations also exist within individual disks; for instance, chemical evolution models of M33 suggest inside-out formation in the inner disk ($r \leq 6$ kpc) but outside-in formation in the far outer disk [Robles-Valdez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.429.2351R/abstract). Interestingly, the apparent tension between ultraviolet and H$\alpha$ star formation indicators in these low-density outer disks can be resolved by considering the nature of the star formation itself; models incorporating clustered star formation naturally explain the observed radial H$\alpha$ cut-off in extended disk galaxies [Pflamm-Altenburg et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455..641P/abstract).

At higher redshifts, inside-out growth manifests as differential [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] evolution. Analyzing rest-frame optical and ultraviolet observations up to $z \sim 7$, the redshift-independent Petrosian radius $R_{\rm Petr}$ scales as:
$$ R_{\rm Petr} = \alpha(1+z)^\beta $$
where the outer radii grow significantly faster over cosmic time ($\beta < 0$) than the inner radii, a direct geometric consequence of mass being preferentially added to the galaxy outskirts [Whitney et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887..113W/abstract). Early snapshots of this process have now been detected as far back as the Epoch of Reionization [Baker et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230602472B/abstract).

These stellar age gradients are intrinsically linked to chemical evolution. The continuous accretion of pristine or low-metallicity gas onto the outer disk feeds ongoing star formation, diluting the local metallicity. Consequently, inside-out growth drives strong negative metallicity gradients. High-redshift observations of lensed galaxies confirm that primordial gradients were significantly steeper, pointing to rapid central enrichment before radial mixing and extended disk star formation flattened them over cosmic time [Jones et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...725L.176J/abstract).

The relationship between the local stellar surface density, gas surface density, and gas-phase metallicity is well-reproduced by analytical models extending the classical [[closed-box-model|Closed box model]]. Specifically, a local leaky-box model—assuming localized evolution except for outflowing materials—naturally generates the observed tight parameter relations regardless of the initial gas density [Zhu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.4494Z/abstract). In the Milky Way, temporal and spatial mapping of the magnesium abundance ([Mg/H]) provides direct confirmation of the leaky accretion disk scenario. A chemical evolution model with a mass-loading factor between 0 and 2 accurately matches the flattening of the Milky Way's metallicity profile at large radii, linking local disk assembly directly to the global inside-out paradigm [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...34W/abstract).

## Inside-Out Growth in the Early Universe

Recent high-redshift observations have confirmed that inside-out assembly establishes itself remarkably early in cosmic history, providing direct support for models of [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]]. The first direct detection of inside-out growth during the Epoch of Reionization was identified in the $z \sim 7$ galaxy JADES-GS+53.18343-27.79097 [Baker et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230602472B/abstract). This system, with a total stellar mass of $M_* = 10^{8.6} M_\odot$, exhibits a highly compact, mature core—a nascent [[galactic-bulges|proto-bulge]]—with a half-light radius of $144$ pc embedded within a vigorously star-forming disc extending to a radius of $468$ pc. The central stellar mass density of this early galaxy is already within a factor of two of the most massive present-day elliptical galaxies, despite the system being globally $1000$ times less massive. Crucially, its specific star formation rate profile rises strongly toward the outskirts, signifying active, ongoing disk assembly around a mature core [Baker et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230602472B/abstract). Analytical frameworks like the ABCD semi-analytic model further reveal that this high-redshift assembly is chemically complex, with pristine Population III star formation potentially persisting down to $z \sim 5$ within the peripheries of massive halos alongside more enriched Population II stars [Hegde et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250719581H/abstract).

By Cosmic Noon ($z \sim 2$), the structural and chemical signatures of inside-out growth become even more pronounced. Spatially resolved spectroscopy of strongly lensed star-forming galaxies at $z=2.00$, such as SDSS J120601.69+514227.8, reveals steep gas-phase metallicity gradients. Observations indicate a gradient of $-0.27 \pm 0.05$ dex kpc$^{-1}$, with near-solar central metallicities [Jones et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...725L.176J/abstract). While this physical gradient is steeper than those found in the local Universe, it is comparable when scaled by the effective radius at that epoch. This suggests that central enrichment occurs rapidly before subsequent radial mixing and extended disk star formation dilute the gradient over cosmic time [Jones et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...725L.176J/abstract).

Following this initial phase of rapid central assembly, massive galaxies at $z \sim 2$ begin to exhibit central suppression of star formation, marking an early onset of inside-out [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] [Spilker et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...883...81S/abstract). The spectroscopic confirmation of RUBIES-EGS-QG-1 at $z=4.896$ demonstrates that massive [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] ($10^{10.9} M_\odot$) could form and quench within just 1.2 billion years of the Big Bang [de Graaff et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240405683D/abstract). However, interpreting the spatially resolved properties of high-redshift galaxies remains complex, as observed red central cores may represent either older, quiescent proto-bulges or merely dust-obscured starbursts [Kamieneski et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...955...91K/abstract). Disentangling whether these cores are definitively older or merely obscured remains an active observational frontier in [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. Furthermore, empirical look-back models suggest that the decline of star formation at low redshifts is due to the cold gas fraction becoming significantly less capable of forming stars, rather than galaxies completely exhausting their gas reservoirs [Kudritzki et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910...87K/abstract).

## Structural Co-evolution: Bulges, Disks, and Nuclear Rings

The assembly of galactic structures is intimately tied to the [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] paradigm. In this framework, massive galaxies form their dense central cores—[[galactic-bulges|proto-bulges]]—rapidly at high redshift ($z > 2$) through intense, centrally concentrated star formation. This initial phase is followed by the extended, secular inside-out assembly of a gas-rich disk. Direct observational evidence for this sequence has been detected as early as the Epoch of Reionization [Baker et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230602472B/abstract), and is supported over cosmic time by the differential size evolution of galaxies [Whitney et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887..113W/abstract). Recent integral-field studies emphasize the structural diversity of early-type galaxies on the main sequence, distinguishing between 'MS-early_SF' galaxies—which exhibit centrally concentrated star formation—and 'MS-early_stellar' galaxies, which possess prominent bulges and display the suppressed central star formation characteristic of inside-out quenching [Koyama et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250716212K/abstract).

The balance between central and extended growth is strongly mass-dependent. In halos hosting galaxies with stellar masses below $M_* \sim 10^{9.5} M_\odot$, structural growth is often dynamically disorganized. Intermediate masses ($10^{9.5} < M_* < 10^{10.5} M_\odot$) are dominated by in-situ disk growth, while at the highest masses ($M_* > 10^{10.5} M_\odot$), in-situ star formation slows and growth is driven predominantly by the [[ex-situ-galaxy-growth|ex-situ]] accretion of stars onto the spheroidal halo [Clauwens et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.3994C/abstract). High-resolution VINTERGATAN simulations indicate that [[galaxy-merger-dynamics-and-evolution|major mergers]] only trigger efficient starbursts after the emergence of a settled galactic disc, as more turbulent high-z media cannot respond as collectively to tidal forcings [Segovia Otero et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.516.2272S/abstract). In extreme environments, [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] exhibit a specific inside-out growth driven by dry mergers, where the central mass density increases slowly while the outer profile slope grows shallower, often characterized by a high Sersic index of $n \sim 6$ [Bai et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...789..134B/abstract).

As galaxies mature, the cessation of star formation tracks the [[galactic-disk-evolution|structural buildup]] from the inside out. Central spheroids can dynamically stabilize the surrounding gas, suppressing fragmentation and leading to [[structural-transformation|morphological quenching]] [Gensior et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.495..199G/abstract). This inside-out [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] profoundly impacts the observed structural parameters of the galaxy, particularly complicating bulge-disk decompositions [Papaderos et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...658A..74P/abstract).

During the later secular phases of disk evolution, non-axisymmetric structures further modify the central mass distribution. Bar-driven evolution channels gas toward the galactic center, fueling the formation of nuclear discs and nuclear rings. Recent simulation results indicate that bar formation not only triggers inner gas inflows but also enhances stellar migration, creating a distinct peak in the stellar age distribution of the outer disk that corresponds to the epoch of the bar's assembly [Baba et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PASJ...77..916B/abstract). Integral-field spectroscopy reveals that these nuclear discs are structurally and chemically distinct from classical bulges; they are younger, more metal-rich, and exhibit lower $[\alpha/\rm{Fe}]$ enhancements [Bittner et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A..65B/abstract). Nuclear rings often act as the active, star-forming outer boundary of these continuous nuclear discs, expanding radially outward as the galactic bar evolves [Bittner et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A..65B/abstract). In systems like the Milky Way, this localized central enrichment sits within a broader "leaky accretion disk" framework, where coplanar gas inflows and inside-out chemical enrichment jointly dictate the structural and chemical gradients observed today [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...34W/abstract).

## Inside-Out Quenching and the Transition to Quiescence

The transition of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] from the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] to the population of [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] frequently proceeds via "inside-out quenching." Just as stellar mass assembly in these systems predominantly occurs from the inside out, the cessation of star formation radially progresses from the dense central regions toward the outskirts. 

Spatially resolved integral field spectroscopy, such as from the SDSS-IV MaNGA survey, demonstrates that inside-out [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] is the dominant mode across all local environments, and its prevalence strongly increases with halo mass [Lin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...50L/abstract). This inside-out progression is already evident at cosmic noon. At $z \sim 2$, massive star-forming galaxies ($M_* > 10^{11} M_\odot$) exhibit specific star formation rate (sSFR) profiles that are centrally suppressed by factors of $\sim 10$ relative to their extended disks [Tacchella et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...859...56T/abstract). Recent simulations from Magneticum Pathfinder suggest that quenching is not always a permanent final state, with many galaxies undergoing "on-off" cycles of quenching and rejuvenation on gigayear timescales [Fortun\'e et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320858F/abstract).

The central suppression of star formation is mirrored in the underlying molecular gas reservoirs. High-resolution interferometric imaging of compact $z \sim 2$ galaxies, such as COSMOS 27289, reveals that both the molecular [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]], defined as $f_{\rm H_2} \equiv M_{\rm H_2}/M_*$, and the molecular gas depletion time, $t_{\rm dep} \equiv M_{\rm H_2}/{\rm SFR}$, are minimized in the central 1-2 kpc and rise significantly at larger radii of 2-4 kpc [Spilker et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...883...81S/abstract). EDGE-CALIFA observations show that while molecular gas depletion initiates the move away from the main sequence, a sharp decline in star formation efficiency is required to drive galaxies into a fully retired state [Colombo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...644A..97C/abstract).

The primary physical drivers of inside-out quenching are closely tied to the build-up of central mass. "Morphological quenching" represents a leading theoretical framework for this phenomenon: the accumulation of a massive central [[galactic-bulges|spheroid]] increases the local gas velocity dispersion, which dynamically stabilizes the gaseous disk against gravitational fragmentation and prevents star formation even if cold gas remains present [Gensior et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.495..199G/abstract), [Lin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...50L/abstract). This morphological quenching hypothesis is strongly supported by observations from the Herschel Reference Survey, which confirm that galaxies with prominent bulges exhibit significantly lower global star formation efficiencies compared to their disk-dominated counterparts [Eales et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491...69E/abstract). Additionally, AGN feedback and rapid central gas consumption can physically evacuate or heat the nuclear gas, depriving the core of its star-forming fuel [Spilker et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...883...81S/abstract). Simulations suggest that quenching in high-mass galaxies is often triggered when central supermassive black holes reach a critical mass of $\sim 10^{7.5} M_\odot$, corresponding to a stellar bulge mass threshold of roughly $2-3 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$ [Taylor et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.4249T/abstract). Furthermore, while negative AGN feedback is frequently invoked to evacuate or heat gas, semi-analytic models of galaxy formation demonstrate that *positive* AGN feedback can also play a pivotal role. In these scenarios, positive feedback coexists with negative feedback to trigger discrete bursts of star formation from cooling gas fractions, complicating the simple inside-out cessation narrative [Contini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240510544C/abstract).

Failing to account for inside-out quenching can severely bias measurements of galactic structure. In traditional bulge-disk decompositions, extrapolating an exponential disk model into the quenched inner region overestimates the disk's central surface brightness. This oversubtraction of the disk leads to a systematic underestimation of the true bulge luminosity—an effect termed $\delta_{\rm io}$ [Papaderos et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...658A..74P/abstract).

## Globular Clusters as Tracers of Spatial Assembly

[[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] serve as crucial fossil records for reconstructing the spatial and temporal assembly of their host galaxies. Because they are among the oldest surviving stellar systems, their chemical and kinematic properties encode the physical conditions of the high-redshift universe, offering a unique quantitative window into the [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] paradigm and inside-out formation models. Recent analytical formulations of the two-phase model identify a critical 'supernova-free' regime at high redshift, where pristine gas sub-clouds can compactify efficiently into two distinct populations of [[massive-cluster-formation|massive globular clusters]] before being disrupted by stellar feedback [Chen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540.1235C/abstract).

The theoretical consensus indicates that massive cluster formation is a natural outcome of star formation within the highly pressurized interstellar medium of nascent, high-redshift galaxies [Kruijssen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..312..147K/abstract). The cosmic formation rate of globular clusters peaked at $z \sim 2.5$, preceding the overall peak of cosmic star formation at $z \sim 2$ by a factor of $1.1 - 1.6$ in time [Reina-Campos et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.5838R/abstract), [Joschko et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204105J/abstract). 

A defining characteristic of many globular cluster systems is their bimodal color and metallicity distribution, which directly traces different epochs and physical environments of galaxy assembly. Metal-poor (blue) clusters typically formed in small, low-mass protogalactic fragments at early times, with a median [[formation-epoch|lookback time]] of $\sim 12.1$ Gyr ($z \sim 3.7$). Conversely, metal-rich (red) clusters formed later, with a median lookback time of $\sim 10.2$ Gyr ($z \sim 1.8$), as the host galaxy grew its central mass through in-situ star formation and the hierarchical merging of more massive, pre-enriched subunits [Reina-Campos et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.5838R/abstract). 

Recent advances in cosmological hydrodynamical simulations, particularly the E-MOSAICS project, have demonstrated how the age-metallicity distribution of a globular cluster population can be inverted to reconstruct its host's spatial and temporal assembly history. E-MOSAICS couples subgrid models of cluster formation and disruption with the EAGLE galaxy formation simulations [Kruijssen et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.3134K/abstract). These models reveal that the age-metallicity distribution is a highly sensitive probe of a galaxy's mass growth, chemical enrichment, and minor merger history. By applying specific metrics to these distributions, it is possible to systematically reconstruct the host galaxy's [[galaxy-merger-dynamics-and-evolution|merger tree]], identifying all progenitor galaxies with stellar masses $M_* \gtrsim 10^8 M_\odot$ that were accreted between $1 \leq z \leq 2.5$ [Kruijssen et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.3134K/abstract).

Finally, the very survival of these clusters is intimately linked to their spatial kinematics. Clusters born in the dense, gas-rich inner disks of high-redshift galaxies are highly susceptible to disruption from impulsive tidal shocks. High-resolution simulations, such as EMP-Pathfinder, highlight that the graininess of the cold, dense interstellar medium causes intense tidal shocks that heavily sculpt the surviving mass function of these globular cluster populations [Reina-Campos et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.3144R/abstract). Their long-term survival is strongly dependent on cluster migration, a process wherein frequent high-redshift galaxy interactions and mergers dynamically scatter these massive clusters from the dense inner disk out into the more diffuse stellar halo [Kruijssen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014CQGra..31x4006K/abstract), [Kruijssen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..312..147K/abstract). Thus, the modern spatial distribution of [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]] reflects not only the intensely compact star formation characteristic of early inside-out core formation, but also the subsequent kinematic heating and [[ex-situ-galaxy-growth|Ex-situ galaxy growth]] that scatters them to larger, safer radii.

## Mass and Environmental Dependencies

The spatial distribution of galaxy growth and subsequent star formation cessation is strongly modulated by both stellar mass and the local [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]]. While inside-out formation is the dominant assembly mode for [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], observational evidence points to a distinct, mass-dependent transition in growth kinematics.

For low-mass systems ($M_* \lesssim 10^{9.5}\text{--}10^{10} M_\odot$), galaxy assembly often proceeds in an "outside-in" or highly disorganized manner [Clauwens et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.3994C/abstract), [Pan et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...804L..42P/abstract). Within this low-mass regime, rotational kinematics play a defining role: fast-rotating irregular galaxies tend to show centrally concentrated star formation, whereas slowly rotating dwarf irregulars exhibit much more distributed and quiescent star formation patterns [Grebel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004oee..symp..234G/abstract). As galaxies cross this mass threshold, their primary growth mode transitions to inside-out assembly, characterized by the buildup of central [[galactic-bulges|bulges]] followed by extended disk star formation [Pan et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...804L..42P/abstract). In the most massive regimes ($M_* \gtrsim 10^{11} M_\odot$), the cessation of star formation also proceeds inside-out. Spatially resolved observations show that these galaxies exhibit centrally suppressed specific star formation rates (sSFR) that can drop by a factor of $\sim 10$ relative to their outskirts, driving them toward the quenched sequence [Tacchella et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...859...56T/abstract). This early central assembly and subsequent suppression of star formation is a key feature of [[morphological-downsizing|Morphological downsizing]], which typically requires active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback or morphological stabilization in theoretical models to reproduce observed massive galaxy populations at high redshift [Bower et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.370..645B/abstract).

While internal mechanisms drive inside-out [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] in [[low-density-environments|field environments]], external forces in dense regions introduce competing outside-in processes. In rich clusters and massive groups with a halo mass $M_h > 10^{12.5} M_\odot$, environmental mechanisms affect satellite galaxies: strangulation cuts off the supply of new gas by removing the hot halo, while ram-pressure stripping preferentially removes the cold gas reservoirs from a galaxy's extended outer disk [Schaefer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.2851S/abstract). This external truncation suppresses star formation in the outskirts first, leading to outside-in quenching that develops over typical timescales of $1\text{--}3 \text{ Gyr}$ as galaxies are accreted into the cluster potential [Balogh et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...540..113B/abstract). These environmental stripping processes are primary drivers of both the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]] and the [[butcher-oemler-effect|Butcher-Oemler effect]], explaining the rapid decline of blue, star-forming galaxies in dense environments over cosmic time [Kauffmann et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.274..153K/abstract).

The exact balance between internal (inside-out) and environmental (outside-in) quenching remains a subject of active debate and reveals significant tensions between observations and theoretical models. Large-scale cosmological hydrodynamical simulations, such as Illustris and EAGLE, tend to predict outside-in quenching for transitioning "green valley" galaxies across most environments [Starkenburg et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874L..17S/abstract). However, this heavily conflicts with spatially resolved observations, which consistently show centrally concentrated star formation suppression in transition galaxies. Observational constraints indicate that at a fixed stellar mass, the fraction of galaxies exhibiting inside-out quenching is systematically higher than those undergoing outside-in quenching, suggesting that internal inside-out quenching is the dominant evolutionary pathway regardless of the local environment [Lin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...50L/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the inside-out assembly of [[high-mass-galaxies|High-mass galaxies]] is well supported by spatially resolved spectroscopy, several open questions and theoretical tensions remain regarding the universality of this growth mode. A primary conflict in the literature concerns the mass-dependent nature of galaxy assembly. Observations using the CALIFA and SDSS surveys reveal that while galaxies with stellar masses $M_* > 10^{10.5} M_\odot$ exhibit negative color gradients consistent with inside-out growth, [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] ($M_* < 10^{10} M_\odot$) frequently display positive or flat color gradients. This reversal suggests a fundamental transition to *outside-in stellar formation* at lower masses [Pérez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764L...1P/abstract), [Pan et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...804L..42P/abstract). Furthermore, detailed chemical evolution models of M33 suggest a hybrid assembly history: the inner disk ($r \leq 6$ kpc) grew inside-out, while the outer disk ($r > 6$ kpc) formed via outside-in accretion [Robles-Valdez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.429.2351R/abstract). Determining the precise physical origins of this mass-dependent transition—and whether it is driven by external environmental stripping or internal secular processes—remains a critical future direction.

Another significant tension exists between spatially resolved observations and cosmological hydrodynamic simulations regarding the radial direction of [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]]. Observations of galaxies on the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] consistently show inside-out quenching, characterized by a central suppression of specific star formation rates and molecular gas fractions [Tacchella et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...859...56T/abstract), [Lin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...50L/abstract). In stark contrast, large-scale cosmological simulations, such as Illustris and EAGLE, predict that transitioning Green Valley galaxies possess highly centrally concentrated star formation, implying an outside-in quenching process [Starkenburg et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874L..17S/abstract). This discrepancy suggests that current simulations may suffer from inadequate feedback implementation (e.g., overly efficient or improperly localized AGN and supernova feedback) and inaccurate subgrid models. Additionally, resolution issues in tracking the multiphase interstellar medium may prevent simulations from properly capturing the morphological stabilization of gas disks or the rapid central evacuation of molecular gas necessary to reproduce inside-out quenching profiles [Spilker et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...883...81S/abstract), [Gensior et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.495..199G/abstract).

Resolving these conflicts requires a deeper understanding of the local physics governing star formation. A foundational assumption in many analytic models and simulations is the existence of a universal star formation law (e.g., the Kennicutt-Schmidt relation) that operates uniformly across all environments. However, significant discrepancies arise depending on the choice of empirical or theoretical star formation laws; these choices profoundly impact predicted cold gas contents and the modeled emergence of a 'passive' galaxy sequence [Lagos et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1566L/abstract). For instance, tying star formation explicitly to dust shielding criteria rather than strictly to the molecular hydrogen abundance allows models to predict star formation at lower gas densities and higher temperatures, potentially altering theoretical expectations for the outer, metal-poor disks of growing galaxies [Byrne et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...871..213B/abstract). Furthermore, whether star formation efficiencies and the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] remain constant across different environments—such as in [[extreme-starbursts|Extreme starbursts]] versus quiescent disks—is an area of active debate [Krumholz et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhR...539...49K/abstract). Furthermore, identifying the exact physical mechanism that primarily drives central quenching—whether it is AGN feedback, morphological quenching by a growing central bulge, or energetic outflows—is still ambiguous. Future spatially resolved observations with JWST and next-generation ELTs will be crucial to determining how cold-mode versus hot-mode gas accretion sustains inside-out disk growth at late times, and how variations in the local star formation efficiency dictate the global assembly and quenching of galaxies.
