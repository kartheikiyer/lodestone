## Article

**Disc transformations** refer to the morphological, kinematic, and chemical evolution of late-type, star-forming [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] into more bulge-dominated, quiescent systems such as lenticular (S0) or [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. Historically, the Hubble tuning fork was viewed as a static classification scheme; however, modern extragalactic astrophysics recognizes it as a dynamic evolutionary track along which galaxies migrate over cosmic time [Zhang et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518..613Z/abstract), [Pfenniger et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ASSL..209..291P/abstract). This structural metamorphosis encompasses a variety of physical processes that redistribute a galaxy's internal angular momentum, dynamically heat its stellar orbits, and deplete its cold interstellar medium (ISM) reservoirs. As a consequence, dynamically cold, rotationally supported discs transition into thicker, pressure-supported systems, a process invariably accompanied by the quenching of active star formation and a shift from the blue cloud to the red sequence [van Dokkum et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12547V/abstract). 

The drivers of these transformations are broadly categorized into internal secular mechanisms, environmental effects, and merger-driven processes. Internally, the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] is mediated by non-axisymmetric structures such as a [[galactic-bar|Galactic bar]] or transient spiral [[density-wave-theory|density waves]]. These features exert gravitational torques that drive radial gas inflows and stellar migration, steadily building central [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] and reshaping the disc over gigayear timescales [Sellwood et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993RPPh...56..173S/abstract), [Sellwood et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). Externally, galaxies residing in dense clusters are subjected to extreme hydrodynamical and gravitational forces. Mechanisms such as ram-pressure stripping, starvation, and continuous [[tidal-interactions-and-morphological-disturbance|Tidal Interactions and Morphological Disturbance]] (often termed tidal harassment) actively sweep away gas and impulsively heat stellar discs. Furthermore, while violent major [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]] were classically thought to inevitably destroy fragile discs to form massive spheroids [Schweizer et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000RSPTA.358.2063S/abstract), modern frameworks emphasize that gas-rich ("wet") major mergers often preserve enough angular momentum to permit subsequent [[disc-regrowth|Disc regrowth]]. Minor mergers and continuous [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] also play a vital role, vertically heating the primary galaxy to form [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] without entirely destroying the underlying rotation.

This article explores the myriad pathways of [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]] across cosmic history, contrasting the slow secular processes of the local Universe with the extreme, gas-rich conditions of high-redshift discs subject to [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]]. It details the observational signatures and [[galaxy-kinematics|kinematic evolution]] associated with these transitions, alongside the critical role of gas dynamics in driving galaxy quenching. Finally, it addresses several pressing open questions in contemporary astrophysics. These include the disputed ability of radial migration to vertically thicken discs, the relative importance of active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback versus internal gravitational instabilities in halting star formation, and whether high-redshift compact spheroids ("red nuggets") strictly evolve into giant ellipticals or instead undergo "disc cloaking" to become the central bulges of modern spiral galaxies.

## Historical Development and the Hubble Sequence

The Hubble tuning fork was originally conceived as a static morphological classification scheme, organizing galaxies from "early-type" spheroids to "late-type" spirals and irregulars. However, observational and theoretical advances prompted a paradigm shift, recasting the sequence as a dynamic evolutionary track. Galaxies are now understood to undergo continuous morphological evolution, generally migrating from late-type, gas-rich [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] to early-type systems over cosmic time [Zhang et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518..613Z/abstract), [Pfenniger et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ASSL..209..291P/abstract).

A major catalyst for this shift was the Toomre merger hypothesis. Introduced as an alternative to monolithic collapse models, the hypothesis posited that violent gravitational interactions could destroy fragile stellar discs, producing ellipsoidal remnants akin to modern [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] [Schweizer et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998giis.conf..105S/abstract). In its most extreme formulation, all elliptical galaxies are the products of disc galaxy major mergers. During these events, typically involving mass ratios closer to unity (e.g., $\mu = M_2/M_1 \gtrsim 1/4$), strong tidal forces drive the violent relaxation of stellar orbits. Similar structural metamorphoses occur on smaller mass scales; for example, tidally stirred [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|dwarf discs]] transform into pressure-supported spheroidal systems that closely satisfy the equilibrium kinematic relation:
$$ V_{\rm max} = \sqrt{3}\sigma_* $$
where $V_{\rm max}$ is the maximum halo circular velocity and $\sigma_*$ is the one-dimensional central stellar velocity dispersion [Kazantzidis et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...726...98K/abstract). Detailed kinematic studies further revealed that many galaxies traditionally classified as giant ellipticals actually contain embedded stellar disks. This underlying disc presence strongly supports the hierarchical clustering and merging (HCM) concept of galaxy assembly over simple monolithic collapse scenarios [Burstein et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000bgfp.conf...85B/abstract). The dynamic nature of the Hubble sequence is further observationally supported by findings that the fraction of peculiar galaxies increases significantly with redshift, reaching approximately $30\%$ of the population by $z = 1$ [Abraham et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..275...89A/abstract).

The realization that galaxies transition between morphological classes introduced a critical systematic effect known as progenitor bias. Because spiral galaxies continually transform into early-type galaxies, the high-redshift population of early-types represents only an incomplete, mature subset of the modern population. If this ongoing morphological evolution is ignored, the homogeneity and red colors of the high-redshift sample artificially inflate estimates of their formation epoch. When models explicitly account for disc-to-spheroid transformations, the corrected mean formation redshift for stars in early-type cluster galaxies drops significantly, potentially to $z \sim 2$ [van Dokkum et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12547V/abstract).

While the Toomre hypothesis emphasized violent external triggers, internally driven [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] also propels systems along the Hubble sequence. Non-axisymmetric perturbations, such as a [[galactic-bar|Galactic bar]] or spiral density waves, drive angular momentum redistribution that can secularly transform late-type spirals (Sc/Sd) into early-type spirals (Sa/Sb) by building central [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] [Zhang et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518..613Z/abstract). 

Despite the historical dominance of the merger paradigm, the literature remains conflicted on the primary driver of morphological transformation at high redshift. While classical models rely on major mergers to assemble spheroids, observational studies suggest that major mergers are not the dominant driver of structural transformation or star formation at the epoch of peak cosmic star formation ($z \sim 2$) [Lofthouse et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2895L/abstract). Instead, minor mergers, cold gas accretion, and [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] in gas-rich primordial discs are increasingly cited as the dominant mechanisms for early bulge growth and structural transformation [Bournaud et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..355B/abstract). Furthermore, recent simulations indicate that major mergers do not always permanently destroy discs; wet major mergers can frequently lead to the [[disc-regrowth|Disc regrowth]] of a stellar disc if sufficient gas is preserved in the remnant [Peschken et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1375P/abstract).

## Internal Secular Evolution

Internal secular evolution encompasses the slow, internally driven rearrangement of mass and angular momentum within a galactic disc, proceeding largely independently of external environmental forcing or major mergers [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). Driven primarily by global non-axisymmetric instabilities—such as a [[galactic-bar|Galactic bar]] and spiral arms—these processes can significantly alter a galaxy's structure, theoretically driving [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]] along the Hubble sequence from late-type (Sc/Sd) to early-type (Sa/Sb) configurations over cosmological timescales [Zhang et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518..613Z/abstract), [Pfenniger et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ASSL..209..291P/abstract).

Central bars are highly efficient drivers of this angular momentum transport. By exerting gravitational torques, bars strip angular momentum from cold gas in the outer disc, channeling it toward the central kiloparsec [Sellwood et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..431K/abstract). This sustained gas inflow frequently triggers nuclear starbursts and gradually builds a rotationally supported, disc-like central stellar component known as a pseudobulge ([[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]]) [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..431K/abstract). Indeed, HST-based analyses of surface brightness profiles show that classical de Vaucouleurs $r^{1/4}$ profiles are rare in bulges, which often exhibit signatures of rapidly rotating, flattened disc-like systems [Balcells et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003EAS....10...23B/abstract). Continuous gas accretion can replenish the disc and temporarily weaken the primary bar, though the subsequent accumulation of disc mass may trigger secondary bar instabilities, leading to multi-episodic evolutionary cycles over a Hubble time [Bournaud et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...392...83B/abstract). Assessing a disc's susceptibility to such bar formation is complex; standard bar stability criteria used in semi-analytic models, such as the Efstathiou-Lake-Negroponte criterion, are often insufficient to distinguish truly stable discs, necessitating more detailed dynamical treatments [Athanassoula et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.390L..69A/abstract). Additionally, these non-axisymmetric features, such as rotating bars or ovals, can induce resonance phenomena that act on the gas motions within the disc to form galactic rings [Buta et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996FCPh...17...95B/abstract). Such non-axisymmetries are not limited to the inner disc; approximately $36\%$ of disc galaxies exhibit global [[lopsided-galaxies|kinematic lopsidedness]] in their outer rotation curves, which is likely a disc dynamical response to residing within a distorted or asymmetric dark matter halo [van Eymeren et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...530A..29V/abstract).

Spiral arms also mediate substantial internal restructuring. At the corotation resonance of a transient spiral mode, stars can undergo significant radial migration—shifting their orbital guiding radii inward or outward while preserving nearly circular orbits [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). This radial mixing flattens metallicity gradients and alters the expected age-metallicity relations across the disc. The exact nature of the spiral perturbations driving this migration remains a subject of active debate in the literature. While some theoretical frameworks treat spirals as long-lived, quasi-[[stationary-density-wave|Stationary density wave]]s capable of secularly heating the disc over billions of years [Zhang et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518..613Z/abstract), modern cosmological simulations predominantly indicate that spirals are transient, recurring features. In this view, spiral modes persist on gigayear timescales but fluctuate in amplitude and saturate due to non-linear orbit deflections near corotation on timescales of hundreds of Myr [Quinn et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722793Q/abstract), [Sellwood et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.2610S/abstract). Observations of red clump stars in the Milky Way trace the Outer spiral arm well into the third Galactic quadrant and provide evidence that older stellar populations exhibit warped asymmetries similar to the gas disc [Uppal et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221008206U/abstract). Moreover, high-resolution 21 cm surveys like the Canadian Galactic Plane Survey (CGPS) reveal complex dynamics in the Milky Way's outer disc, including radially varying pattern speeds and multiple shock features [Foster et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..438...16F/abstract).

This stellar radial redistribution fundamentally shapes a galaxy's overall surface brightness profile, generating the distinct Type I (uncruncated), Type II (downbending), and Type III (upbending) disc breaks. Specifically, Type II downbending breaks are significantly strengthened by populations of outward-moving stars that accumulate at the migration front [Ruiz-Lara et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...608A.126R/abstract). However, numerical models suggest that the distinct Type II and III surface brightness profile breaks in the outskirts of disc galaxies can also be accurately reproduced as remnants of major merger events [Athanassoula et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..321...61A/abstract). While radial migration effectively redistributes mass and metals within the midplane, its ability to drive vertical disc heating is contested. Although some theories suggest that outwardly migrating stars might mechanically puff up the outer disc, dynamical models demonstrate that adiabatic cooling during outward migration counteracts the expected heating. Consequently, radial migration alone cannot reproduce observed [[thick-disks|Thick disks]]; for example, the outward migration of bulge stars contributes less than $1\%$ to the thick-disc mass at $2.5$ scale lengths in Sa-type galaxies, and this contribution drops to zero in Milky Way-like systems [Minchev et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012EPJWC..1907002M/abstract). 

Although classical [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] operates on multi-gigayear timescales at $z \sim 0$, analogous processes were highly accelerated in the early Universe. In gas-rich, highly turbulent high-redshift discs ($z > 1.5$), [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] can cause massive gas clumps ($10^8 - 10^9 M_\odot$) to migrate inward and coalesce into central bulges on timescales of just a few $10^8$ years [Bournaud et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..355B/abstract). Additionally, "Early Accelerated Secular Evolution" (EASE) driven by transient stellar spirals may have rapidly redistributed angular momentum and triggered early [[compaction-events|Compaction events]]—a mechanism enabled by the significantly shorter orbital and dynamical timescales present at cosmic noon [van der Wel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...704A.147V/abstract).

## Environmental Mechanisms of Transformation

The evolution of disc galaxies is inextricably linked to their host environment, a dependence most clearly manifested in the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]]. Observations reveal a stark decrease in the spiral fraction and a corresponding enhancement of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] and lenticular (S0) systems in dense cluster and group environments since $z \sim 1$ [van Dokkum et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12547V/abstract). This transition is driven by a combination of hydrodynamical and gravitational processes that actively deplete cold gas reservoirs and dynamically heat stellar components, ultimately quenching star formation and triggering [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]]. Additionally, environmental processing can drive structural transitions within the early-type population itself. Theoretical arguments suggest that the transformation of disky ellipticals into lenticulars (S0s) involves equatorial diameter expansion and subsequent rotational evolution, providing a natural explanation for the observed differences in their aspect ratios [Schachar et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...505..613S/abstract).

The primary hydrodynamical mechanisms responsible for gas depletion are ram pressure stripping and strangulation (sometimes referred to as starvation). Ram pressure stripping occurs when a galaxy traverses the dense intracluster medium at high velocities, exerting a hydrodynamic pressure $P \approx \rho_{\rm ICM} v^2$ that can overcome the disc's restoring gravitational force, actively sweeping away its cold interstellar gas. In contrast, strangulation refers to the stripping or cessation of the extended hot gas halo, cutting off the supply of fresh cooling gas and forcing the disc to slowly fade as it exhausts its remaining cold fuel. The relative dominance of these mechanisms remains an active area of debate and likely varies by environment and galaxy mass. Recent integral-field spectroscopic studies of edge-on lenticulars in the Fornax cluster reveal constant or growing radial extents of mono-age stellar populations over the past $8 \text{ Gyr}$, arguing that strangulation—rather than the more truncation-prone ram pressure—was the dominant quenching mechanism for these specific discs [Martig et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251119632M/abstract). Conversely, specific correlations between radial metallicity gradients and angular momentum in Local Group dwarfs suggest that ram pressure is required to explain why environmentally processed [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|dwarf spheroidals]] have steeper metallicity profiles, since tidal stripping alone would preserve the flat profiles of their rotating progenitors [Leaman et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MmSAI..85..504L/abstract). 

Beyond hydrodynamical gas removal, [[tidal-interactions-and-morphological-disturbance|Tidal Interactions and Morphological Disturbance]] profoundly perturb disc structure. In massive clusters with high velocity dispersions ($\sigma > 700 \text{ km s}^{-1}$), the dominant gravitational driver is the interaction with the global cluster potential, whereas in lower-mass groups, discrete galaxy-galaxy interactions dictate the evolution [Cervantes-Sodi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414..587C/abstract). Repeated, high-speed encounters with cluster substructure—a process termed tidal harassment—can impulsively heat dynamically cold, fragile late-type discs, funneling gas inwards and thickening the stellar distribution to produce dwarf spheroidal or dwarf elliptical morphologies [Carraro et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..253C/abstract). This evolutionary link is reinforced by integral-field spectroscopy of Virgo cluster dwarf ellipticals, which supports their origin as defunct, late-type dwarf spirals or irregulars. These environmentally transformed dwarfs often retain levels of rotational support akin to giant fast-rotating systems [Ryś et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013hsa7.conf..373R/abstract). 

The efficiency of these tidal transformations is highly dependent on orbital parameters. N-body simulations indicate that global tidal fields trigger significant morphological disruptions when the mean group density enclosed by the galaxy's orbit exceeds $0.3 - 1.0$ times the central mean density of the disc galaxy [Villalobos et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.2401V/abstract). Furthermore, discs with internal rotation that is prograde relative to their orbital motion are far more susceptible to mass loss and structural degradation than their retrograde counterparts, which can retain their disc structures for much longer periods [Villalobos et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.2401V/abstract). In extreme prograde encounters, the interaction can result in 'galaxy shredding,' a process where the progenitor disc is violently disrupted into dusty, irregular fragments, while its resistant central bulge emerges relatively intact as a newly formed dwarf elliptical [Thomson et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992MNRAS.257..689T/abstract).

Importantly, environmental processing is not restricted to the dense cores of massive clusters. Structural transformations, including an elevated elliptical-to-spiral ratio and reduced star formation rates, are also observed as galaxies migrate from voids towards the spines of large-scale cosmic filaments [Kuutma et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...600L...6K/abstract). As noted earlier, failure to account for these ongoing, environmentally driven morphological shifts can lead to severe "progenitor bias," whereby the formation redshifts of cluster early-types are systematically overestimated if the recent transformation of accreted gas-rich discs is neglected [van Dokkum et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12547V/abstract).

## Merger-Driven Morphological Transitions

[[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]] are fundamental drivers of morphological transformation, radically redistributing mass, angular momentum, and gas within interacting systems. Historically, the standard paradigm of galaxy formation posited that major mergers—typically defined by mass ratios $\mu \gtrsim 1/4$—inevitably destroy fragile rotation-supported structures. During these violent events, rapidly fluctuating gravitational potentials induce violent relaxation. This collisionless process scatters stellar orbits, erasing the initial kinematic memory of the progenitors and transforming them into pressure-supported, ellipsoidal remnants [Schweizer et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000RSPTA.358.2063S/abstract). Consequently, major mergers have long been considered the primary formation channel for massive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]].

However, contemporary cosmological simulations have fundamentally revised this picture by highlighting the crucial role of the cold gas fraction. While dry (gas-poor) major mergers reliably produce quenched ellipticals, gas-rich ("wet") major mergers frequently permit disc survival or initiate subsequent [[disc-regrowth|Disc regrowth]] [Peschken et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1375P/abstract). In these scenarios, the pre-existing stellar populations are dynamically heated to form an ellipsoidal bulge, but the dissipative gas retains sufficient angular momentum to cool and settle into a newly formed, rotationally supported disc [Peschken et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1375P/abstract). Incorporating gas dissipation during major mergers into hierarchical models is essential for reproducing the observed size-mass and angular momentum-mass relations across different galaxy morphologies [Zoldan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.1376Z/abstract). Simulations demonstrate that such remnants commonly evolve back into star-forming spiral galaxies, provided that central active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback is not strong enough to completely quench the remnant [Sparre et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.3946S/abstract).

The relative importance of major mergers as the dominant mechanism for morphological transformation remains a subject of active debate, particularly at high redshifts. While major mergers are frequent in the early Universe, observational surveys at the epoch of peak cosmic star formation ($z \sim 2$) indicate that only a small fraction ($\sim 21\%$) of actively star-forming spheroids exhibit the morphological disturbances expected from recent major mergers [Lofthouse et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2895L/abstract). This suggests that at high redshift, other mechanisms—such as [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] or steady cold gas accretion—may rival or surpass major mergers in driving the structural evolution of galaxies [Lofthouse et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2895L/abstract).

In contrast to major mergers, minor mergers (typically involving mass ratios $\mu \lesssim 1/10$) generally leave the primary galactic disc intact but induce significant structural and kinematic alterations. The accretion of satellite galaxies acts as a primary agent for vertical dynamical heating, transferring orbital kinetic energy into the primary galaxy and converting thin discs into [[thick-disks|Thick disks]]. Beyond simple thickening, the tidal disruption of satellites leaves distinct localized signatures in the host's stellar populations. For instance, the accretion of old stars from satellites can transform pre-existing U-shaped radial stellar age profiles into an old plateau in the outer disc. Furthermore, active star formation within satellites as they temporarily orbit through these outer regions, combined with radial motions, can drive inverted age-metallicity relations [Ruiz-Lara et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...586A.112R/abstract). Counter-rotating stellar and gaseous discs, along with the ubiquity of warps in outer H I distributions, serve as definitive tracers of past [[galaxy-accretion|mass accretion]] with misaligned angular momentum [Combes et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000bgfp.conf...99C/abstract). Even extremely minor encounters leave dynamic imprints; for example, recent models indicate that the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy's latest pericentre passage approximately $35\text{ Myr}$ ago triggered the observed spiral corrugation wave and warp in the Milky Way's H I disc [Binney et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.535.1898B/abstract). Furthermore, torques generated during these minor interactions can drive gas toward the central parsecs, fueling the growth of classical bulges and central starbursts [Schweizer et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000RSPTA.358.2063S/abstract), [Segovia Otero et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.516.2272S/abstract).

Observationally, the transitional phase of these interactions is characterized by [[post-merger-galaxies|Post-merger galaxies]] exhibiting transient tidal tails, bridges, and highly distorted isophotes. Because violent relaxation operates on the order of a few dynamical times, the most extreme morphological disturbances fade relatively quickly, leaving behind structurally transformed galaxies that subsequently resume secular evolution along the Hubble sequence.

## Kinematic Evolution and Disc Thickening

The [[galaxy-kinematics|kinematic transformation]] of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] is fundamentally linked to the temporal evolution of their vertical scale heights and internal velocity dispersions. High-resolution cosmological simulations of high-redshift galaxies indicate that early discs were violently unstable and dynamically hot, characterized by elevated gas velocity dispersions ($\sigma \simeq 23-38 \text{ km s}^{-1}$) and rotational-to-dispersion ratios of $V/\sigma \simeq 7$ [Kohandel et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.1250K/abstract). In these turbulent, gas-rich environments, gravitational instability is governed by the Toomre Q parameter. At kiloparsec scales, these local disc instabilities are primarily driven by stellar self-gravity, which remains strongly coupled to the molecular gas during the fragmentation process [Romeo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469..286R/abstract). Furthermore, the structure of the interstellar medium itself alters disc stability. Interstellar turbulence makes the outer disc significantly more susceptible to star-dominated instabilities and critically influences the transition between different instability phases as the disc cools [Hoffmann et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.1511H/abstract). Recent infrared observations of high-redshift galaxies suggest a sequential, "downsizing" assembly scenario where pressure-supported [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] form first, followed by the delayed, $Q$-regulated settling of dynamically colder thin discs [Tsukui et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240915909T/abstract), [Tsukui et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540.3493T/abstract).

Once cold thin discs assemble, they undergo continuous kinematic evolution driven by internal [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]. The progressive increase in stellar velocity dispersion over time is quantified by the velocity ellipsoid, which describes the anisotropic distribution of random stellar motions. Transient spiral modes and structures governed by [[density-wave-theory|Density wave theory]] are highly efficient at scattering stars within the disc plane, thereby increasing the radial dispersion. Giant molecular clouds (GMCs) then act to redirect a fraction of this in-plane kinetic energy into the vertical direction, driving vertical heating. The resulting shape of the velocity ellipsoid correlates strongly with galaxy morphology, providing an observational diagnostic for the specific radial and vertical heating agents at work across the Hubble sequence [Gerssen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.2726G/abstract). New 2D velocity mapping algorithms allow for the reconstruction of non-axisymmetric velocity fields, highlighting widespread non-circular motions and asymmetries in the discs of nearby galaxies [Sylos Labini et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.1560S/abstract).

A major point of tension in the literature is the exact role of radial migration in disc thickening. While non-axisymmetric structures like bars and spirals readily drive radial mixing by redistributing angular momentum at corotation resonances, the ability of this mechanism to independently produce thick discs is highly contested. Because secular heating processes and radial migration alone struggle to reproduce the observed scale heights of thick discs, radial migration is generally deemed insufficient to drive wholesale vertical transformations without external environmental influences [Sellwood et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract), [Minchev et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012EPJWC..1907002M/abstract). In these thicker components, distinctive chemical signatures emerge; for instance, positive or inverted radial metallicity gradients observed at heights greater than $1\text{ kpc}$ above the midplane originate from younger, metal-rich stars formed in situ, superimposed on an older, metal-poor background of outwardly migrating stars [Miranda et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...587A..10M/abstract).

Given the limitations of internal heating, external kinematic perturbations—specifically [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]]—are recognized as crucial drivers of vertical heating. While major mergers typically destroy fragile disc structures to form spheroids, minor mergers and continuous [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] kinematically heat the host galaxy. These events deposit both in-situ and accreted stars into thicker, hotter orbital configurations without entirely disrupting the underlying rotation [Ruiz-Lara et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...586A.112R/abstract). As discs kinematically heat and evolve, they often exhibit flaring—an increase in scale height with radius. Cosmological simulations demonstrate that Milky Way and M31 analogues present a wide diversity of flaring profiles, typically possessing scale heights $1.5-2$ times larger in their outer regions compared to the inner disc [Sotillo-Ramos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3915S/abstract). Spatially resolved studies of mono-age stellar populations in edge-on systems support this dichotomy: while recent star formation remains constrained to a thin, unperturbed plane, older populations exhibit moderate thickening and mild flaring in the outer disc directly linked to ancient merger events [Martig et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251119632M/abstract).

## The Role of Gas Dynamics and Quenching

The evolution of [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]] along the Hubble sequence is fundamentally tied to the depletion or removal of their interstellar medium (ISM). The conversion of gas into stars generally follows scaling relations in which the local gas surface density evolves into a stellar surface density. Internally, the availability of this gas supply is regulated by a critical threshold surface density, below which star formation is strongly suppressed even if some gas remains [Kauffmann et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.281..475K/abstract). Furthermore, the continuous inside-out growth of star-forming discs relies heavily on gas accreting with rotational velocities lower than the local circular speed. Extra-planar gaseous haloes show a rotational lag compared to the midplane, providing insight into the exchange of material between the disc and the intergalactic environment [Fraternali et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASSP....3..271F/abstract). Additionally, the temporal evolution of disc winds plays a critical role in regulating accretion flows and driving galaxy-scale feedback, potentially influencing the long-term quenching of disc systems [Castro Segura et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260102028C/abstract). The resulting radial metallicity gradients observed in these discs are incompatible with models where gas simply flows inward from the disc edge, highlighting the complex kinematics of continuous gas accretion [Fraternali et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181108432F/abstract). When the cold gas supply is exhausted or physically removed, the cessation of star formation—termed quenching—drives the transition of blue-cloud spirals into quiescent early-type systems. Indeed, while most star formation since $z \sim 1$ occurs within discs, large numbers of these star-forming galaxies are simultaneously and systematically building up spheroids and transitioning to the red sequence [Bell et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..4407B/abstract).

Observational surveys have identified a prominent intermediate stage in this morphological transformation: the so-called "passive disks." These systems populate the red sequence but retain clear disc morphologies, typically classified as early-type Sa-Sb spirals. Data from the COSMOS survey indicate that up to $60\%$ of galaxies transitioning onto the red sequence evolve through this passive disk phase [Bundy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...719.1969B/abstract). Because these passive disks often feature larger bulges than their star-forming counterparts, their origin likely involves more complex dynamics than the simple fading of blue discs (so-called [[faded-spirals|Faded spirals]]), potentially requiring internal stabilization or concurrent secular bulge growth [Bundy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...719.1969B/abstract).

The rate and physical nature of ISM depletion are highly sensitive to galactic environment, underpinning the well-known [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]]. In massive clusters, interactions with the deep global potential and the dense intracluster medium dominate the removal of gas [Cervantes-Sodi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414..587C/abstract). These processes include ram-pressure stripping, which actively sweeps cold gas from the disc, and [[tidal-interactions-and-morphological-disturbance|tidal harassment]]. Conversely, in smaller groups, galaxy-galaxy interactions predominantly drive morphological changes [Cervantes-Sodi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414..587C/abstract). Even beyond bound clusters, galaxies approaching large-scale cosmic filaments exhibit an elevated elliptical-to-spiral ratio and reduced star formation. This suggests that the cut-off of cosmological gas accretion, known as starvation or strangulation, plays a pivotal role in lower-density environments [Kuutma et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...600L...6K/abstract).

The literature remains actively debated regarding the dominant quenching mechanism for transitioning discs. Traditional models emphasize ram-pressure stripping and tidal harassment as the primary drivers of S0 formation in clusters. However, recent spatially resolved studies of mono-age stellar populations in Fornax cluster lenticulars reveal that their disc thicknesses have remained remarkably constant over the past $\sim 6$ Gyr. The preservation of these radial and vertical structures is inconsistent with aggressive tidal shocking or ram-pressure stripping, suggesting instead that strangulation is the dominant quenching mechanism for these specific galaxies [Martig et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251119632M/abstract). 

In the lower-mass dwarf regime, however, ram pressure appears dynamically necessary. Studies of [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]] show that tidally induced transformations of rotating dwarf irregulars into dispersion-supported dwarf spheroidals should theoretically preserve an initially flat metallicity profile. Observations of isolated versus environmental dwarfs suggest that ram-pressure stripping is required alongside tidal stirring to explain the steepened radial metallicity gradients and altered angular momentum distributions seen in Local Group dwarf spheroidals [Leaman et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MmSAI..85..504L/abstract). Ultimately, gas dynamics and quenching represent a multi-pathway process, heavily dependent on both the progenitor's mass and its specific host environment.

## High-Redshift Transformations and Violent Instabilities

The evolutionary pathways of galaxies at $z \gtrsim 1.5$ are fundamentally different from those in the local Universe. Observations reveal that mature [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]], including well-defined discs and bulges, can be established as early as $1.2 \text{ Gyr}$ after the Big Bang ($z > 4$), indicating that assembly and transformation processes can operate much more rapidly than classical models predict [Wardlow et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...371..674W/abstract). Consequently, the diverse morphologies defining the traditional Hubble sequence—ranging from late-type discs to massive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]—are already established in cosmological simulations by $z \sim 3$ [Fiacconi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.1957F/abstract). Indeed, cosmological frameworks demonstrate that the fundamental Hubble sequence is structurally established around $z=1.5$. Following this epoch, the overall stellar mass budget of the Universe becomes increasingly dominated by the growth of pressure-supported spheroids as discs progressively quench and transform [Trayford et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483..744T/abstract). Furthermore, these high-redshift discs are typically gas-rich and highly turbulent. Simulations of synthetic [C II] emission in high-redshift galaxies indicate velocity dispersions of $\sigma \simeq 23-38 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ and highly unstable dynamical states. The successful formation and stabilization of these early discs is strongly promoted by centrally concentrated mass profiles, which stabilize the system against disruptive global 'breathing modes' that would otherwise destroy rotationally supported structures [Hopkins et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.2241H/abstract).

This turbulent, gas-rich environment frequently triggers [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] (VDI). High-redshift discs globally fragment, producing [[clumpy-disks|Clumpy disks]] populated by giant gas and stellar clumps of $10^{8-9} M_\odot$ [Bournaud et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..355B/abstract). Using Fourier-Laguerre techniques to measure disc asymmetry, studies of JWST-observed galaxies at $1 < z < 4$ reveal that lower-mass galaxies at cosmic noon exhibit significantly higher morphological asymmetry, largely driven by this intense, localized star formation [Ganapathy et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241111972G/abstract). Due to their massive nature, these clumps experience strong dynamical friction, causing them to migrate inward and coalesce in the galactic center over short timescales. This violent instability drives intense gas inflows toward the nuclear region, facilitating the rapid concurrent growth of classical bulges, supermassive black holes, and [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] [Bournaud et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..355B/abstract), [Forbes et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.1552F/abstract).

The relative importance of internal instabilities versus major mergers at high redshift remains a subject of active debate. While traditional hierarchical models emphasize major mergers as the primary mechanism for bulge formation, observational surveys reveal that most star-forming spheroids at $z \sim 2$ lack the morphological disturbances expected from recent major mergers [Lofthouse et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2895L/abstract). Consequently, rapid high-redshift transformations may instead be driven by internal mechanisms, such as Early Accelerated Secular Evolution (EASE). In the EASE framework, widespread structural compaction and heating are driven by the strong gravitational torques of short-lived, transient spiral arms operating over just $10-30$ orbital periods [van der Wel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...704A.147V/abstract).

A prominent signature of this violent early evolution is the formation of "red nuggets"—compact, massive, quiescent spheroids prevalent at $z \sim 2.5$. The evolutionary fate of these objects has been extensively debated. While they were historically assumed to evolve almost exclusively into modern giant ellipticals via subsequent dry mergers, recent evidence supports a "disc cloaking" scenario [Hon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3410H/abstract). In this model, red nuggets accrete cold gas and undergo minor mergers to grow a surrounding rotational disc. The original compact spheroid is thus "cloaked," effectively becoming the bulge of a modern spiral or lenticular galaxy. Detailed multi-component decompositions of local galaxies suggest that [[disc-regrowth|Disc regrowth]] and cloaking, rather than pure spheroidal merging, may be a dominant evolutionary pathway for early compact galaxies.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Next-generation observatories have fundamentally altered the timeline for the emergence of mature [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]. Historically, massive discs with prominent bulges were thought to assemble slowly over billions of years. However, recent ALMA observations have revealed a regime of "speedy" galaxy evolution, with mature rotating discs and bulges existing as early as $1.2\text{ Gyr}$ after the Big Bang ($z > 4$) [Wardlow et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...371..674W/abstract). Furthermore, observations of edge-on systems across cosmic history indicate a sequential "downsizing" in disc assembly. Massive galaxies transitioned from single [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] to double (thick and thin) discs roughly $8\text{ Gyr}$ ago, whereas lower-mass galaxies delayed this transition until $\sim 4\text{ Gyr}$ ago [Tsukui et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240915909T/abstract), [Tsukui et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540.3493T/abstract). 

To reconcile the rapid rise of early-type morphologies at cosmic noon ($1 < z < 3$) with standard formation timescales, frameworks such as Early Accelerated Secular Evolution (EASE) propose that the shorter orbital times at high redshifts allow transient spirals to drive rapid compaction and bulge formation [van der Wel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...704A.147V/abstract). This rapid structural evolution highlights a major open question regarding the fate of high-redshift "red nuggets" (compact massive spheroids). The literature remains conflicting on whether these nuggets strictly undergo dry mergers to become the cores of modern [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], or if they experience "disc cloaking"—accreting gas to grow an extended disc and ultimately serving as the central bulges of modern spiral galaxies [Hon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3410H/abstract).

The relative importance of internal gravitational instabilities versus external feedback mechanisms in quenching star formation and finalizing disc transformations is heavily debated. Cosmological simulations demonstrate that wet major mergers frequently fail to permanently destroy discs; instead, post-merger gas accretion allows for significant [[disc-regrowth|Disc regrowth]], resulting in star-forming spirals rather than quenched ellipticals [Sparre et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.3946S/abstract), [Peschken et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1375P/abstract). Consequently, theorists argue that AGN feedback is required to definitively quench merger remnants [Sparre et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.3946S/abstract). However, some researchers caution against over-relying on AGN feedback, highlighting that [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] and clump migration can efficiently grow bulges and suppress star formation without invoking massive central black holes [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..431K/abstract).

Finally, the precise dark matter halo connection to disc structural evolution remains an active frontier. The host halo's geometry directly influences non-axisymmetric disc perturbations; for example, globally [[lopsided-galaxies|Lopsided galaxies]] are found to preferentially reside in asymmetric dark matter haloes with high spin [Varela-Lavin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.5853V/abstract). Internally, the efficiency of mechanisms like radial migration in driving vertical disc heating is strongly disputed. While some models treat radial migration as a primary driver for the formation of [[thick-disks|Thick disks]], others argue that adiabatic cooling during outward migration nullifies vertical heating, making early gas turbulence or satellite accretion the true drivers of thick disc structures [Minchev et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012EPJWC..1907002M/abstract), [Noguchi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494L..37N/abstract).
