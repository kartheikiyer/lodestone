## Article

In the study of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], the evolution of galaxies is broadly driven by two contrasting regimes: rapid, violent interactions such as [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]], and slow, internal processes collectively known as secular evolution [Kormendy et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982SAAS...12..115K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). Secular evolution refers to the gradual redistribution of mass and [[angular-momentum-in-galaxy-formation|Angular Momentum in Galaxy Formation]] within a galaxy over timescales vastly exceeding the local dynamical time ($t \gg t_{\rm dyn}$). Unlike the stochastic, externally driven growth that dominates the hierarchical assembly of [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|massive early-type galaxies]], secular processes operate primarily through [[internal-stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|Internal Stellar and Galactic Dynamics]]. The principal engines of this slow transformation are [[non-axisymmetric-perturbation|non-axisymmetric perturbations]] within [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]—most notably the [[galactic-bar|Galactic bar]] and spiral [[stationary-density-wave|density waves]] [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). By exerting continuous [[galactic-torques|gravitational torques]] on the stellar and gaseous components, these structures fundamentally reshape [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] from within.

A primary consequence of this internal restructuring is the systematic [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]] of galaxies along the Hubble sequence, slowly shifting gas-rich, late-type spirals into more centrally concentrated, earlier-type systems [Zhang et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518..613Z/abstract). As angular momentum is transferred outward to the disk and dark matter halo, cold interstellar gas is funneled inward. This radial gas inflow fuels central [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|starbursts]] and drives the growth of [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]]—dense central mass concentrations that masquerade as classical bulges but retain the exponential surface brightness profiles and [[ordered-rotation|rotationally supported]] [[galaxy-kinematics|Galaxy kinematics]] of their progenitor disks [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). Furthermore, the collective scattering of stellar orbits by transient spiral arms and molecular clouds leads to vertical disk heating and radial [[stellar-migration|Stellar migration]], altering the structural and chemical makeup of the galaxy over gigayear timescales. 

To sustain these internal gas-driven instabilities over the age of the Universe, isolated disks must operate as open systems, continuously replenished by external [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] from the cosmic web. However, despite the success of the secular paradigm in explaining the localized growth and kinematics of disk galaxies, significant theoretical tensions remain, particularly regarding the abundance of bulgeless galaxies, the dynamics of dark matter halos, and the origins of disk gradients.

## Physical Foundations of Secular Evolution

In the standard cosmological paradigm, the formation of galaxies is initially driven by hierarchical clustering and rapid, violent [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]]. The final [[angular-momentum-in-galaxy-formation|stellar angular momentum]] of a galaxy is heavily influenced by the angular momentum of its progenitor Lagrangian patch in the early Universe, which dictates the timing and orbital parameters of subsequent mergers [Cadiou et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.3459C/abstract). Broadly, structural evolution follows three mass-dependent growth regimes: rapid gas accretion dominating at high redshifts, steady star formation fueling disk-dominated growth at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 1-2$), and dry mergers driving spheroid build-up at low redshifts, with active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback playing a key role in promoting inside-out growth in [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|massive systems]] [van de Voort et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..778V/abstract), [Clauwens et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.3994C/abstract). However, once a galactic disk settles into equilibrium, its subsequent structural development is largely governed by secular evolution [Kormendy et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982SAAS...12..115K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). While rapid mergers dominate the assembly of massive galaxies at low redshifts, secular evolution and gas accretion are the dominant evolutionary mechanisms for lower-mass systems and isolated [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] [Steinmetz et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Ap&SS.284..325S/abstract), [Cook et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.402.2113C/abstract). While mergers are certainly significant in cosmological frameworks, they are estimated to account for only approximately one-third of the total stellar and black hole mass assembly at the present day, emphasizing the overall dominance of other processes like secular evolution [Kaviraj et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609136K/abstract). 

In a self-gravitating disk, [[non-axisymmetric-structures|Non-axisymmetric structures]] generate [[galactic-torques|gravitational torques]] that facilitate the outward transfer of angular momentum and the corresponding inward flow of gas and stars [Combes et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..325C/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). If left solely to internal mechanisms, the redistribution of gas would be governed by the [[viscous-timescale|Viscous timescale]] $t_{\nu}$. Theoretical models of viscous disk evolution demonstrate that if the star formation timescale $t_\star$ is comparable to the viscous timescale ($t_\star \approx t_\nu$), the disk naturally evolves toward a state where the stellar surface density $\Sigma_\star$ follows an exponential profile:
$$ \Sigma_\star(R) \propto \exp \left( -\frac{R}{R_d} \right) $$
where $R_d$ is the disk scale length. The incorporation of star formation thresholds into these viscous evolution models helps explain the stark structural diversity observed among galaxies, distinguishing the slow evolution of low surface brightness (LSB) spirals from the concentrated star formation found in blue compact dwarfs [Noguchi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...555..289N/abstract). In addition to growing the disk and building central concentrations, secular processes driven by a growing bar can dynamically alter pre-existing classical bulges, causing them to absorb angular momentum through resonances and transform into fast-rotating, triaxial structures [Saha et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421..333S/abstract).

To sustain bar and spiral instabilities over a Hubble time and prevent complete gas exhaustion, disk galaxies must act as open systems, continuously replenishing their reservoirs via the [[galaxy-accretion|accretion of cold gas]] from cosmic filaments [Block et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...394L..35B/abstract), [Combes et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..8616C/abstract). Galaxies operate in a slowly evolving equilibrium between gas inflow, ejective feedback (outflows), and star formation, where stochastic perturbations are naturally driven back toward this equilibrium state [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). A crucial element in this equilibrium is [[wind-mechanical-energy|mechanical feedback]] from supernovae, which competes against gravitational instabilities to regulate star formation and effectively maintain consistent neutral hydrogen surface densities across different galactic environments [Chakraborti et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732..105C/abstract). Alongside filamentary inflows, evidence indicates that external gas acquisition in blue, star-forming galaxies can lead to counter-rotating gas components that rapidly shed angular momentum and funnel into the nucleus to drive central growth [Chen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016NatCo...713269C/abstract). Numerical models suggest that to match the observed frequency of bars and spirals, spiral disks may need to double their mass every $\sim 10$ Gyr via external accretion [Block et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...394L..35B/abstract). Through these combined internal and environmentally-modulated pathways, secular processes dictate the progressive tightening of [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] and the inside-out growth of stellar disks over cosmic time [Zhang et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004IAUS..220..417Z/abstract), [Bell et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..389B/abstract).

## Historical Development of the Secular Paradigm

For much of the twentieth century, the prevailing cosmological view treated galaxies as "island universes"—isolated systems whose physical characteristics were largely imprinted at birth. Under this monolithic collapse model, [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] was considered static once formed, and the Hubble Sequence was understood merely as a fixed taxonomic scheme rather than a developmental pathway. 

The dismantling of this static paradigm began with the recognition that galaxies could be fundamentally altered by their dynamical histories. Early numerical simulations, most notably by Toomre & Toomre in 1972, demonstrated that violent tidal interactions and [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] could entirely reshape galaxies, transforming disks into ellipticals. Concurrently, the concept of slow, internal restructuring—what would become the secular paradigm—began to take shape. In the late 1970s, researchers such as John Kormendy advanced the idea that a galaxy could dynamically "interact with itself" over extended timescales [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015HiA....16..316K/abstract). Theoretical and observational work demonstrated that non-axisymmetric perturbations within the disk act as principal engines for redistributing mass and angular momentum [Kormendy et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982SAAS...12..115K/abstract).

This realization profoundly shifted the interpretation of the Hubble Sequence. Rather than viewing morphological types as innate, researchers proposed that internal secular evolution could drive [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]], slowly shifting gas-rich, late-type galaxies into more concentrated, early-type systems [Pfenniger et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ASSL..209..291P/abstract). Consequently, the structural regularities observed along the Hubble Sequence began to be understood as temporal [[evolutionary-sequences|Evolutionary sequences]] driven by internal dynamical mechanisms [Zhang et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518..613Z/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..317Z/abstract). A cornerstone of this historical development was the reclassification of galactic bulges, separating merger-built classical bulges from secularly grown [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract).

## Bars as Drivers of Galactic Transformation

A [[galactic-bar|Galactic bar]] is the principal engine of internal secular evolution in many [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]. Emerging from a global bar instability when a rotationally supported disk reaches a critical surface density, the bar facilitates profound morphological transformations over gigayear timescales [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). However, observationally, the prevalence of these structures depends heavily on the condition of the host disk. Observational data from Galaxy Zoo 2 shows the likelihood of a galaxy hosting a bar is strongly anticorrelated with its [[normalized-star-formation-rate|specific star formation rate]] (SSFR), likely driven by the retarding dynamical effect of high gas fractions on bar formation [Cheung et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...779..162C/abstract). Furthermore, the fraction of barred galaxies has increased significantly over cosmic time; bars were significantly rarer at $z > 0.7$, but have proliferated over the last 6 Gyr, particularly in high-mass, red galaxies, as disks have become more dynamically stable [Chacón et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260100106C/abstract), [Merrifield et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..275..175M/abstract).

The non-axisymmetric potential of the bar exerts continuous [[galactic-torques|gravitational torques]] on the gas disk. Because the gas is collisional and dissipative, these torques cause it to lose angular momentum and stream inward. This radial inflow frequently stalls at inner Lindblad resonances to form nuclear rings, or it continues to the nucleus to fuel central starbursts and the growth of supermassive black holes [Combes et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..8616C/abstract), [Knapen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gama.conf..201K/abstract). Statistical analysis of color profiles suggests that bars also act as homogenization mechanisms, mixing stellar populations and resulting in flatter or even positive color gradients in barred galaxies [Gadotti et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..230..237G/abstract).

Over their lifetimes, bars themselves undergo structural evolution. A fully developed bar is susceptible to a vertical bending mode known as the buckling instability. This dynamical event vertically heats the inner region of the bar, scattering stars out of the disk plane and forming a boxy or peanut-shaped structure, while the [[outer-bar|Outer bar]] remains thin and extended [Debattista et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...645..209D/abstract), [Athanassoula et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph.10113A/abstract). Observational evidence also reveals a strong correlation between a bar's strength and its pattern speed, suggesting that bars weaken as they slow down over time due to angular momentum losses [Merrifield et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..275..175M/abstract).

While robust, a [[galactic-bar|Galactic bar]] can ultimately be dissolved if the central mass concentration grows sufficiently large. Simulations suggest that a bar can be destroyed if the central mass grows to $\sim 20\%$ of the total disk mass [Debattista et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...645..209D/abstract), or if a central supermassive black hole reaches $\sim 0.5\%$ of the disk mass [Hozumi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005PASJ...57..719H/abstract). When the central supermassive black hole accretes sufficient mass, AGN feedback becomes a dominant force; in large elliptical galaxies and [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|massive early-types]], energy from the central black hole fundamentally halts star formation by heating and aggressively ejecting gas from the system [Cattaneo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.460..213C/abstract). Nevertheless, bars remain remarkably long-lived structures in many galaxies. Recent spatially resolved spectroscopic surveys, such as the TIMER project, have successfully derived bar ages ranging from $1$ to $13\text{ Gyr}$, demonstrating that bar-driven secular evolution is an ongoing process that has been shaping galaxies since the early Universe [de S\'a-Freitas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320864D/abstract), [de Sá-Freitas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...698A...5D/abstract). 

## Gas Inflow and the Formation of Pseudobulges

As cold gas is funneled into the central kiloparsec by non-axisymmetric torques, it reaches extreme densities that trigger vigorous nuclear starbursts [Combes et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..8616C/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract). Notably, these central starbursts are not exclusively triggered by violent [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]]; semi-analytic models demonstrate that they can also result from large secular gas accretion events or structural changes, such as small disk radii, that substantially increase the central gas surface density [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.4454W/abstract). 

Alternatively, in gas-rich high-redshift disks, gravitational instabilities can lead to the formation of massive mass clumps that sink to the center and merge; however, this violent internal clumping process provides a channel for building *classical* bulges, not pseudobulges [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.392..253N/abstract). True [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] arise over gigayear timescales as the combination of steady inward gas transport and subsequent central star formation builds a dense mass concentration. Because this material is assembled gradually from the disk rather than through violent mergers, the resulting structure is fundamentally distinct from a classical bulge [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract). However, despite their distinct formation pathways, pseudobulges and classical bulges are not mutually exclusive and can frequently coexist within the same host galaxy [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract).

Pseudobulges retain a kinematic and structural memory of their disky origins. They are characterized by [[ordered-rotation|Ordered rotation]] and smaller [[isotropic-velocity-dispersion|velocity dispersions]] than their classical counterparts, falling below the standard Faber-Jackson relation [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). Photometrically, they exhibit flatter, more disk-like shapes and approximately exponential surface brightness profiles, typically well-described by a Sérsic index $n \lesssim 2$ [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). Furthermore, they frequently host active star formation, nuclear rings, and secondary nuclear bars [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract). The growth of a pseudobulge establishes a complex feedback loop with the host galaxy's [[internal-stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|Internal Stellar and Galactic Dynamics]], potentially disrupting the orbital families that support the large-scale bar and occasionally leading to bar dissolution.

## Spiral Arms, Disk Heating, and Radial Migration

[[non-axisymmetric-perturbation|Non-axisymmetric perturbations]], particularly spiral arms, act as fundamental engines of internal secular evolution throughout the broader disk. Through resonant scattering and collective instabilities, these patterns gradually reshape the phase-space distribution of the [[stellar-component|Stellar component]].

One of the primary dynamical consequences of transient [[stationary-density-wave|spiral density waves]] is disk heating. As stars interact with ephemeral spiral arms, their random velocity components within the disk plane increase. This planar heating is moderated by subsequent scattering events with giant molecular clouds, which redirect a fraction of this planar kinetic energy into vertical motions, progressively thickening the stellar disk over time [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract), [Binney et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..230...63B/abstract). Recent models of [[spiral-galaxy-dynamics|Spiral Galaxy Dynamics]] demonstrate that open, transient spirals distribute angular momentum exchanges and associated dynamical heating over a much wider spatial extent than canonical stationary density waves. This Early, Accelerated Secular Evolution (EASE) is particularly efficient at high redshifts ($1 < z < 3$), where orbital timescales are shorter, allowing massive galaxies to undergo substantial structural transformation over just $\approx 10-30$ orbital periods [van der Wel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250902847V/abstract). Furthermore, in galaxies hosting non-linear density-wave modes, collisionless shocks play a dominant role in driving secular evolution rates that differ qualitatively from classical predictions [Zhang et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1012.0277Z/abstract). In fact, nonlinear density wave theory, which treats these waves as solitons, demonstrates that a constant group velocity of the nonlinear density waves can naturally support the flat rotational velocity curves observed in spiral galaxies [Vukcevic et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....161..118V/abstract).

In addition to kinematic heating, spiral arms drive [[stellar-migration|Stellar migration]] by radially displacing the guiding centers of stellar orbits. A critical mechanism for this transport is radial migration occurring near the corotation resonance of transient spiral modes. Scattering at corotation can significantly alter a star's angular momentum and guiding radius while maintaining a nearly circular orbit, relocating stars radially without significantly contributing to the overall thermodynamic heating of the disk [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). Because stars can traverse large radial distances over their lifetimes, migration effectively mixes the disk's stellar populations, flattening radial abundance gradients and complicating the interpretation of local galactic archaeology. Complementary to this dynamical mixing, multiphase models of chemical evolution indicate that radial abundance gradients in isolated spiral disks generally flatten over time as the inner regions reach enrichment saturation faster than the outskirts [Mollá et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...475..519M/abstract).

## Morphological Transformation along the Hubble Sequence

Modern dynamical frameworks suggest that the Hubble sequence can, in part, be interpreted as a temporal evolutionary track. Through slow, internally and environmentally driven [[disc-transformations|Disc transformations]], late-type, gas-rich systems (such as Sc, Sd, and Sm galaxies) can gradually evolve into earlier-type (Sa, Sb, and S0) systems [Pfenniger et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ASSL..209..291P/abstract), [Zhang et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518..613Z/abstract). However, while the overarching trend shifts toward earlier types, this evolution is not strictly unidirectional; due to episodic gas accretion, a galaxy's morphology can oscillate and transiently turn back toward later types between bar phases [Combes et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004IAUS..222..383C/abstract). To systematically map this sequence and distinguish between varying star-formation quenching mechanisms across populations, researchers frequently employ continuity equations and phenomenological models [Lilly et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..141L/abstract). 

This [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]] is primarily governed by the continuous [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass assembly]] at the core, building dense [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]]. Observations of late-type spirals show a restricted, scale-free ratio of bulge-to-disk scale lengths, strongly supporting the theory that these inner components grow secularly out of the disk material itself rather than through external accretion [Courteau et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...457L..73C/abstract). In the "spiral rebuilding" scenario, continuous gas accretion and minor mergers since $z \sim 1$ can systematically drive the growth of early-type spirals with significant disk masses [Hammer et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..9907H/abstract). Accompanying this internal growth is a progressive expansion of the disk. Recent studies using the radial location of stellar mass density truncations (edges) as a size indicator reveal a more dramatic disk growth rate ($1.5 \text{ kpc/Gyr}$) than measurements based solely on half-light radii [Buitrago et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...682A.110B/abstract). Interestingly, cosmological models indicate that pronounced inside-out growth in simulated disks can actually eliminate the expected evolution in the [[size-mass-relation|size-mass relation]], maintaining its structural consistency despite significant mass growth over cosmic time [Brook et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...639..126B/abstract).

As internal processes increase the central concentration and the bulge-to-total luminosity ratio, the galaxy's overall morphology shifts toward an earlier Hubble type. Some contemporary models even propose a "Galaxy End Sequence," treating galaxies as a unitary population that follows a curved [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]], smoothly transitioning without a sharp structural break between traditional late and early types [Eales et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.3125E/abstract). During this transition, a galaxy may exhibit stabilized resonance features driven by previous bar phases, occasionally manifesting as an [[s0-ring-galaxy|S0-ring galaxy]] where the remaining gas has been swept into specific orbital resonances before star formation completely ceases. Despite structural evolution, the mass fundamental plane—linking size, mass density, and dynamics—has remained remarkably stable for quiescent galaxies since at least $z \sim 2$ [Bezanson et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...779L..21B/abstract).

While some models successfully parameterize the progression along the Hubble sequence purely as a secular temporal sequence [Zhang et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518..613Z/abstract), ongoing continuous external [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] and discrete minor mergers remain essential to sustain the instabilities driving these transformations over cosmic time [Block et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...394L..35B/abstract), [Zhou et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.3723Z/abstract). Furthermore, high-resolution simulations suggest galaxy evolution often involves multiple cycles of quenching and rejuvenation on gigayear timescales rather than a simple linear path to quiescence [Fortun\'e et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320858F/abstract).

## Environmental Secular Evolution

While internal instabilities drive mass inward, secular evolution is also profoundly shaped by a galaxy's external environment. Environmental secular evolution refers to the slow transformation of gas-rich, star-forming spiral and irregular (S+Im) galaxies into gas-poor, quiescent lenticular (S0) and spheroidal (Sph) galaxies [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). 

The primary mechanisms include ram-pressure stripping, starvation (or strangulation), and harassment. As galaxies fall into groups and clusters, the hydrodynamic pressure of the intracluster medium strips away their cold interstellar gas [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). Simultaneously, starvation occurs when the extended hot gas halo is removed, cutting off the cosmological supply of pristine gas required to replenish the disk [Zhou et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.3723Z/abstract). Stripped of their fuel, these galaxies undergo gas quenching, moving off the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] and migrating onto the red sequence. Recently quenched galaxies (RQGs) represent a critical transitional phase between star-forming and passive populations, often accompanied by the rapid build-up of a spheroidal component [Ichikawa et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...843L...7I/abstract). Furthermore, cumulative high-speed tidal encounters with other cluster members dynamically heat the stellar disk, steadily increasing its thickness and velocity dispersion [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). The COSMOS Wall study highlights the pronounced effect of dense environments, revealing that galaxies within massive groups inherently possess higher metallicities and reach metal enrichment far earlier than their isolated field galaxy counterparts [Zhou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250319785Z/abstract).

The efficacy of these mechanisms depends heavily on a galaxy's hierarchical position and the broader [[galaxy-cluster-dynamical-state|Galaxy Cluster Dynamical State]]. Central galaxies, residing at the bottom of deep gravitational potential wells, retain a larger fraction of their gas and sustain extended star formation. In contrast, low-mass [[galactic-satellite-galaxies|Galactic satellite galaxies]] experience rapid cessation of star formation and chemical enrichment typical of a closed-box model [Zhou et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.3723Z/abstract). Understanding this transition is crucial for cosmological surveys; if the morphological transformation of infalling spirals into early-type galaxies is not explicitly modeled, studies suffer from "progenitor bias," artificially overestimating the mean formation redshift of the cluster's early-type galaxy population [van Dokkum et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12547V/abstract). Morphologically, environmental secular processes leave distinct structural imprints. [[galaxy-spheroid-mass|Spheroidal galaxies]] are structurally continuous with the disks of S0 galaxies and low-luminosity late-type systems; they can be viewed as "defunct" late-type galaxies that have been transformed by environmental harassment and stripping [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). Additionally, large-scale cosmological simulations suggest that gravitational shock heating of gas during the collapse of large-scale structures can preemptively cut off cold gas inflows, causing the specific star formation rate to decline heavily as a function of environment even before a galaxy is fully assimilated into a dense cluster core [Cen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741...99C/abstract).

## Observational Diagnostics and Integral Field Spectroscopy

Modern observational techniques have revolutionized the study of secular evolution, allowing astrophysicists to cleanly separate the structural and kinematic signatures of internally evolved [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] from those of merger-built classical bulges.

Photometrically, the surface brightness profiles of central galactic components offer a primary diagnostic. Classical bulges typically exhibit steep profiles well-described by a Sérsic index $n \gtrsim 2$, while pseudobulges display nearly exponential profiles with $n \lesssim 2$ [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). The true nature of these components is most conclusively revealed through their [[stellar-dynamics-and-kinematics|Stellar Dynamics and Kinematics]]. Because pseudobulges assemble gradually via angular momentum redistribution, they are predominantly rotationally supported. In the [[v-sigma-ellipticity-plane|V/sigma-ellipticity plane]], pseudobulges display $V/\sigma$ ratios significantly higher than expected for isotropic, pressure-supported spheroids of the same ellipticity [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract).

Integral field spectroscopy (IFS) has become indispensable for resolving these signatures. Large IFS surveys map the radial variation of stellar ages, metallicities, and line-of-sight velocity distributions across galaxy disks. Crucially, spatially resolved spectroscopy enables researchers to decompose the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] of discrete sub-components. For example, the TIMER survey utilizes MUSE data to disentangle the star formation history of the nuclear disc from the underlying background population, providing methodologies to directly derive bar ages [de S\'a-Freitas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320864D/abstract), [de Sá-Freitas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...698A...5D/abstract). In addition, the radial Tremaine-Weinberg (TWR) method is a vital tool for observationally determining how pattern speeds vary arbitrarily with radius in barred and spiral disks [Meidt et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...676..899M/abstract). Beyond continuous stellar populations, [[stellar-streams-and-moving-groups|moving groups]] and globular cluster systems act as unique dynamical "amalgamations" that preserve discrete kinematic memories; mapping these systems allows researchers to reconstruct a galaxy's complex deconstruction, hierarchical assembly, and major merger history against its secular background [West et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..245..420W/abstract).

Tracking the secular transport of cold gas relies heavily on high-resolution sub-millimeter interferometry, such as ALMA. ALMA allows for the direct mapping of molecular gas kinematics within the central zone of influence of supermassive black holes. However, observational mapping (such as the NUGA survey) highlights a significant ambiguity regarding large-scale gas flows and active galactic nucleus fueling. Gravity torque maps show evidence of ongoing molecular inflow and central fueling in only about one-third of observed active galaxies. In the majority of cases, infalling gas is transiently stalled in resonant rings rather than flowing smoothly into the nucleus [García-Burillo et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JPhCS.372a2050G/abstract), [Combes et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..353..155C/abstract). This intermittent fueling implies that secular central growth and AGN activity proceed in discrete, stochastic episodes even within a long-lived barred potential.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Although secular evolution successfully explains the structural transformation of disk galaxies, several fundamental tensions with the standard cosmological paradigm remain unresolved. Broadly, the $\Lambda$CDM framework continues to face ongoing challenges related to the intricate processes of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], the persistence of the Hubble tension, and the notorious missing satellite galaxies problem, all of which contextualize the limitations of our current evolutionary models [Primack et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ARNPS..74..173P/abstract).

**The Bulgeless Galaxy Problem:** In the hierarchical clustering paradigm of galaxy formation, frequent [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] are expected to subject almost every massive galaxy to violent relaxation, inevitably forming a classical, pressure-supported bulge. However, observations reveal that a significant fraction of giant field galaxies in the local Universe—including those with massive circular velocities $V_{\rm circ} \sim 200 \text{ km s}^{-1}$—are essentially pure disks, hosting only dynamically cold nuclear star clusters with velocity dispersions of $\sigma \sim 25-40 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). Explaining the survival and ubiquity of these fragile, pure-disk systems remains one of the most prominent open questions in extragalactic astrophysics.

**The Bar Pattern Speed Paradox:** Numerical simulations consistently predict that rotating bars should lose angular momentum to the surrounding massive dark matter halo via dynamical friction, causing the bar's pattern speed to rapidly decrease (the Weinberg prediction). Conversely, observational measurements of [[galaxy-kinematics|Galaxy kinematics]] indicate that real bars rotate rapidly, with the ratio of the corotation radius to the bar semi-major axis typically residing between $1.2$ and $1.5$ [Sellwood et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996LNP...474...43S/abstract). This sharp discrepancy between theory and observation suggests incomplete physical treatments of halo density profiles or angular momentum transfer efficiency.

**Secular Evolution vs. Innate Gradients:** The distinction between true secular evolution and properties established at birth is strongly debated. While processes like [[stellar-migration|Stellar migration]] are frequently invoked to explain radial and vertical metallicity gradients in galactic disks [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract), high-resolution hydrodynamic simulations argue these gradients may actually be "innate." In this view, gradients arise naturally from the inside-out and upside-down formation of the disk: the gaseous disk is born thick and gradually thins and enriches as it settles into centrifugal equilibrium, meaning present-day kinematics faithfully reflect gas conditions at the epoch of formation rather than subsequent secular orbit scattering [Navarro et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.3648N/abstract). 

Disentangling these primordial structural properties from subsequent internal evolution will require future large-scale statistical studies combining multi-wavelength integral-field spectroscopy with advanced cosmological simulations.
