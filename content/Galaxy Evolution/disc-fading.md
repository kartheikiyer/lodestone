**Disc fading** is the gradual reduction in surface brightness and progressive reddening of a galactic disc following the suppression (or "quenching") of its star formation. Driven by the mechanics of stellar population evolution, this secular process occurs as the supply of cold gas is either exhausted or removed, halting the creation of massive, short-lived OB stars. As these highly luminous blue stars evolve off the main sequence and leave behind compact remnants, the integrated light of the galaxy becomes dominated by an aging, lower-mass stellar population. Consequently, the disc’s luminosity decays significantly over time [Hogg et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...488L..95H/abstract), causing dwarf and intermediate-mass galaxy populations to dim by one or more magnitudes [Driver et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...466L...5D/abstract). This drives a sharp, continuous increase in the stellar mass-to-light ratio ($M/L$), permanently altering the galaxy's photometric footprint and marking a critical late-stage phase of [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]].

In the broader context of extragalactic astrophysics, disc fading is widely recognized as a primary mechanism linking the star-forming blue cloud to the quiescent red sequence. As star formation ceases, many [[red-sequence-transition-galaxies|Red-sequence transition galaxies]] pass through a passive, optically dimming phase [Bundy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...719.1969B/abstract). Because the extended blue disc fades more rapidly than the older, dispersion-dominated central bulge, the galaxy's apparent bulge-to-total luminosity ratio naturally increases. While simple photometric color-morphology relations have historically suggested a dramatic morphological shift, modern studies utilizing mass-weighted kinematics reveal that for galaxies below $M_\star \simeq 10^{11} M_\odot$, the underlying dynamical support frequently remains unchanged. Thus, the apparent transition from a late-type spiral to an early-type lenticular (S0) galaxy is often a purely photometric effect driven by a fading disc, a period during which secular features such as [[outer-rings|Outer rings]] can become highly prominent. 

The physical triggers and evolutionary timescales of disc fading are deeply intertwined with a galaxy's environment and assembly history. While secular fading occurs gradually in isolated field galaxies, dense environments like galaxy groups and clusters drastically accelerate the process through external gas-removal mechanisms such as ram-pressure stripping and strangulation. While ram-pressure stripping rapidly removes cold gas, slow mechanisms like strangulation can gradually truncate star formation while largely preserving the established hierarchical structure of the primordial thick and thin disc components [Martig et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251119632M/abstract).

Despite its foundational role in modern galaxy evolution paradigms, the sufficiency of pure disc fading to explain the full parameter space of quiescent galaxies remains a major point of tension in the literature. Current research vigorously debates whether passive fading alone can account for the substantial angular momentum deficits and enhanced bulge luminosities observed in local S0s, or if genuine [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]] via major mergers and tidal interactions is strictly required. Complicating this tension is "progenitor bias"—the reality that the high-redshift spirals that quenched to form today's passive galaxies possessed intrinsically different kinematics than local star-forming discs. Future observational efforts are focused on resolving these structural debates, quantifying the quenching efficiencies of AGN feedback, and leveraging JWST to explore newly discovered high-redshift faded discs and transitional "Little Red Dots" in the early Universe.

## Physical Mechanisms of Disc Fading

Disc fading is fundamentally driven by the passive evolution of a galaxy's stellar populations following the quenching of star formation. Theoretical models of [[galactic-disk-evolution|disc evolution]] along the Hubble sequence suggest that star formation histories are primarily regulated by the history of cold gas accretion onto the disc [Kampakoglou et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.380..646K/abstract). In these models, disc star formation is often maintained at a critical threshold surface density $\Sigma_c \sim V_c/R$ by continuous accretion until quenching occurs [Kauffmann et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.281..475K/abstract). In the framework of stellar population synthesis, an actively star-forming disc is highly luminous and typically blue due to the presence of massive, short-lived OB stars. Once cold gas accretion is halted and star formation ceases, the main sequence evolution of these massive stars dictates that they quickly exhaust their nuclear fuel and evolve into compact remnants. Consequently, the integrated light of the disc becomes increasingly dominated by older, lower-mass, and redder stellar populations. To accurately measure this transition, astronomers utilize the 4000 Å break alongside dust attenuation (IRX) indices to effectively disentangle reddening caused by dust from the true passive aging of stellar populations [Johnson et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJS..173..392J/abstract). Furthermore, wide-area infrared observations at redshifts above 1 are essential for measuring stellar populations and star formation rates in fading discs, as they effectively mitigate the impact of substantial internal dust extinction [Malkan et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001fcgp.work..195M/abstract). Additionally, estimates of quenching efficiency and the exact cessation of star formation may be biased by ionizing photon leakage, which can decrease the observed $H\alpha$-to-FUV star formation rate ratio by up to 25% [Relaño et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.2933R/abstract).

This transition yields a dramatic increase in the stellar mass-to-light ratio, denoted as $\Upsilon_\star$, as the disc dims and reddens. Photometric models of fading starbursts and dwarf galaxies indicate that their luminosity decay as a power law, scaling roughly as $L \propto t^{-1.3}$, which is driven by the rapid fading of the youngest stellar bursts [Hogg et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...488L..95H/abstract). Reproducing the observed radius-luminosity relations in such disc evolution models requires carefully accounting for initial mass function (IMF)-dependent stellar mass-to-light ratios and halo concentration parameters [Dutton et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..1256D/abstract). It is worth noting that while the integrated disc light of a galaxy fades significantly, compact radio sources in starburst progenitors (such as M82) may exhibit slower fading rates over time than initially hypothesized [Ulvestad et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...424L..29U/abstract). For intermediate-sized disc galaxies, an assumed exponential decline in star formation with an e-folding time of $\sim 3$ Gyr can produce a surface brightness dimming of $\Delta M_B \approx 1.6$ magnitudes since $z=1$ [Melbourne et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...660...81M/abstract). In lower-mass systems, fading can cause dwarf populations to dim by $1.0 - 1.4$ magnitudes since $z=0.5$ [Driver et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...466L...5D/abstract). However, observations of systems like the Sculptor dwarf irregular galaxy (SDIG) show that even in a relatively quiescent state without current star formation, a galaxy may only fade by less than a magnitude in the B-band if it retains a high HI-to-blue-luminosity ratio [Heisler et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997MNRAS.285..374H/abstract). Overall, the typical time-scale for a disc to fade and traverse the "green valley" into the red sequence is estimated at $1 - 2$ Gyr [Bremer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476...12B/abstract), though in some cluster environments, this gradual decline can stretch over $1 - 5$ Gyr [Cantale et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...589A..82C/abstract).

Because disc fading is a critical phase of galactic evolution, its role in driving genuine [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]] remains a subject of active debate in the literature. Observations using mass-weighted kinematic tracers reveal that galaxies below $M_\star \approx 10^{11} M_\odot$ undergo almost no physical structural change during quenching; the apparent increase in the galaxy's concentration is largely an optical illusion caused by the rising mass-to-light ratio of the fading disc [Rigamonti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..98R/abstract). The observed increase in concentration during fading should be viewed within the context of non-linear scaling relations, which suggest that the stellar concentration of early-type galaxies varies continuously with stellar mass and luminosity [Graham et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....48..231G/abstract). In this secular view, the pre-existing bulge simply becomes more visually prominent as the disc dims, potentially explaining the formation of lenticular (S0) galaxies without requiring major dynamic events [Bremer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476...12B/abstract), [Maltby et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.1506M/abstract). In the highest-mass systems ($L > 3L^*$), red galaxies appear to have been largely in place by $z\sim 0.9$, following simple passive fading of their stellar populations with minimal mass growth since that epoch [Cool et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...682..919C/abstract).

Conversely, several studies argue that passive fading alone is insufficient to explain the structural differences between spiral and S0 galaxies. Comparisons of local spiral and S0 populations demonstrate that the observed differences in photometric concentration and the deficit of stellar spin in S0s cannot be reproduced by passive aging alone; even after 5 Gyr of simulated disc fading, the resulting mock galaxies do not fully match observed S0 kinematics [Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.2247C/abstract). Furthermore, bulge luminosities in early-type galaxies are physically enhanced at small clustercentric distances, indicating that dynamic processes—such as tidal interactions or major mergers—are required alongside fading to complete the morphological transformation [Christlein et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...616..192C/abstract), [Querejeta et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..321..114Q/abstract). Thus, while disc fading is universally recognized as the primary mechanism altering the photometric appearance of quenched discs, its sufficiency in explaining the full dynamical transition to early-type morphologies remains contested.

## Historical Development and the Spiral-to-S0 Transition

The relationship between spiral and lenticular (S0) galaxies on the Hubble sequence has long been anchored by the hypothesis that S0s are the faded descendants of spirals. In this classical framework of S0 formation, the truncation of star formation leads to a progressive dimming of the stellar disc, naturally increasing the apparent bulge-to-total luminosity ratio without requiring discrete dynamical heating.

Observational evidence strongly supports the existence of this transitional phase; an estimated $60\%$ of [[red-sequence-transition-galaxies|Red-sequence transition galaxies]] pass through a passive spiral phase before fully joining the quiescent population ([Bundy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...719.1969B/abstract)). Studies of cluster environments show that both the bulge and disc components of S0s exhibit color-magnitude relations consistent with passive aging post-quenching, pointing toward environment-mediated disc fading operating over $1$ to $5\text{ Gyr}$ timescales ([Head et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440.1690H/abstract), [Cantale et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...589A..82C/abstract)). This is corroborated by globular cluster specific frequencies in S0s, which match the expected zero-points for faded spiral discs ([Barr et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007A&A...470..173B/abstract)). Crucially, when evaluating these systems using mass-weighted rather than luminosity-weighted kinematics, the quenching process frequently appears to drive almost no true [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]]. Instead, the apparent structural differences are largely an artifact of the disc's mass-to-light ratio increasing as the young stellar populations fade, leaving the underlying mass distribution relatively undisturbed ([Rigamonti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..98R/abstract), [Bremer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476...12B/abstract)).

However, the literature is explicitly conflicting on whether secular disc fading alone is sufficient to explain all S0 properties. Early photometric studies argued that simple disc-fading models cannot account for the observed morphological differences, noting that S0 bulges are systematically brighter than those of late-type galaxies. This discrepancy implies that physical bulge enhancement via mergers or tidal interactions is a necessary component of the transition ([Christlein et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...616..192C/abstract)). 

Kinematic data further complicate the simple fading scenario. Locally, S0s possess higher photometric concentrations and lower stellar spin than their star-forming counterparts. Dynamical models simulating instantaneous quenching followed by $5\text{ Gyr}$ of passive fading demonstrate that while fading does reduce measured spin (by rendering the dispersion-dominated bulge relatively brighter), it quantitatively fails to reproduce the full kinematic separation between spirals and S0s ([Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.2247C/abstract)). This tension is exacerbated by progenitor bias: the high-redshift progenitors of modern passive discs possessed intrinsically higher spin than local spirals, demanding active dynamical evolution rather than purely passive fading to reach observed S0 states ([Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.2247C/abstract)).

To reconcile these findings, contemporary models recognize multiple morphological transformation pathways. For instance, N-body simulations reveal that major mergers can yield disc-dominated S0s with the requisite angular momentum deficits, providing an alternative to simple ram-pressure stripping or starvation ([Querejeta et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..321..114Q/abstract)). Ultimately, the spiral-to-S0 transition is now understood to be decoupled in time—with star formation quenching typically occurring much faster than morphological transformation—and heavily mass-dependent, where pure disc fading dominates at stellar masses below $10^{11} M_\odot$ and merger-driven processes dictate the evolution above this threshold ([Oxland et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.3651O/abstract), [Rigamonti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..98R/abstract)).

## Photometric Signatures and Surface Brightness Profiles

As star-forming systems quench and become [[red-sequence-transition-galaxies|Red-sequence transition galaxies]], the cessation of star formation manifests photometrically as a systematic dimming of the stellar disc. Observations of the radius-luminosity relation reveal that large ($R_{1/2} > 5$ kpc) and intermediate-sized discs have experienced a rest-frame $B$-band dimming of $\Delta M_B \approx 1.53$ to $1.65$ mag since $z=1$, which is quantitatively consistent with an exponential decline in star formation with an e-folding time of $\sim 3$ Gyr [Melbourne et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...660...81M/abstract). In local cluster environments, low-mass late-type galaxies exhibit a clear correlation between redder optical colours and fainter surface brightnesses, directly tracing the fading tracks of aging stellar populations as they exhaust or lose their gas [Janz et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...647A..80J/abstract).

The structural signature of disc fading is most effectively isolated through multi-wavelength bulge-disc decomposition and Sérsic fits. Furthermore, the development of automated software tools to extract residual morphological substructure from HST and JWST images has proven crucial for distinguishing secular disc structures from the fading of distinct tidal features [Mantha et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.2643M/abstract). Because the blue disc fades relative to the older, more stable bulge, the global morphology of a galaxy can appear to undergo a [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]] even if the underlying stellar mass distribution remains largely unchanged. Single-component Sérsic fits of green valley galaxies demonstrate this effect: while luminosity-weighted $g$-band profiles indicate disc-dominated morphologies, mass-sensitive $K$-band profiles closely resemble those of passive red-sequence galaxies [Bremer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476...12B/abstract). Two-component modelling confirms that the morphological evolution from blue to red is predominantly driven by colour changes and increasing mass-to-light ratios within the disc, with a massive bulge already in place prior to quenching [Bremer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476...12B/abstract). Consequently, the global red sequence of lenticular (S0) galaxies is a composite of these component trends. In the Coma cluster, for example, S0 bulges are systematically redder than their surrounding discs by $0.09 \pm 0.01$ mag in $g-i$ and $0.16 \pm 0.01$ mag in $u-g$, consistent with a scenario where the disc has faded while the bulge remains a distinct, older, or more metal-rich population [Head et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440.1690H/abstract).

Fading also leaves distinct imprints on the radial surface brightness profiles of galaxies during later stages of [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]]. A comparison of local field and cluster S0 galaxies shows that while pure exponential (Type I) and up-bending antitruncated (Type III) profiles are common, down-bending truncated (Type II) breaks are exceptionally rare ($<5\%$) [Maltby et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.1506M/abstract). Furthermore, the break strength in S0s is generally weaker than in star-forming spirals. In many Type III S0s, the excess light at large radii can be attributed to the bulge component dominating over a heavily faded outer disc, a photometric signature that strongly supports the evolving bulge-to-disc ratio inherent to the fading scenario [Maltby et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.1506M/abstract). As a counterpoint to the purely fading scenario, simulations suggest that Type II and Type III surface brightness breaks may also result from the radial redistribution of stars and the accretion of satellite material rather than strictly passive evolution [Ruiz-Lara et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...608A.126R/abstract). Furthermore, extremely deep near-infrared imaging from the EDGES survey indicates that some apparent surface brightness profile breaks in local galaxies may actually be attributable to the transition into an extended stellar halo component [Staudaher et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.1995S/abstract). However, there is a key methodological caveat to such deep imaging analysis: ultra-deep images can artificially overestimate thick disc brightness by a factor of four if the point spread function (PSF) wings of the dominant thin disc are not properly deconvolved [Martínez-Lombilla et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...629A..12M/abstract).

Despite these compelling photometric signatures, the literature remains divided on whether passive disc fading is sufficient to fully explain the parameter space of early-type galaxies. Indeed, for lower-mass systems, local S0s appear structurally and dynamically identical to faded spirals when examined with mass-weighted kinematics [Rigamonti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..98R/abstract). Conversely, other researchers argue that simple disc fading models cannot reproduce the high bulge luminosities observed in many cluster early-types. In these models, purely secular fading is insufficient, and discrete physical processes—such as merger-driven bulge enhancement or tidal interactions—are strictly required to match the observed central surface brightnesses [Christlein et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...616..192C/abstract).

## Evolution Across the Green Valley

The traditional optical color-magnitude relation presents a strongly bimodal distribution of galaxies: a star-forming blue cloud and a quiescent red sequence, separated by the sparsely populated green valley. The migration of galaxies across this valley represents a critical phase of galaxy evolution, historically assumed to be coupled with a major [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]] from late-type spirals to early-type ellipticals. However, recent photometric and kinematic decompositions demonstrate that for galaxies below a critical stellar mass of $M_\star \simeq 10^{11} M_\odot$, the transition is primarily driven by the secular fading of the stellar disc rather than dynamical upheaval [Bundy et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...719.1969B/abstract), [Rigamonti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..98R/abstract).

Large-scale surveys indicate that the total stellar mass in the red galaxy population increased by a factor of $\sim 3.6$ from $z\sim 1.1$ to $z\sim 0.1$, consistent with mass-dependent downsizing where luminous blue galaxies cease star formation earlier than their fainter counterparts [Beare et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...815...94B/abstract). The stellar mass contained specifically within this migrating red population has grown by a factor of 2.3 between $z\sim 1.1$ and $z\sim 0.3$, indicating a slowly decreasing but significant rate of migration from the blue cloud [Beare et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...873...78B/abstract). Observations from the GAMA survey indicate that typical green valley galaxies in the mass range of $10^{10.25} < M_\star/M_\odot < 10^{10.75}$ already possess a significant central bulge prior to the decline in their star formation rates [Bremer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476...12B/abstract). The photometric evolution from blue to red is overwhelmingly dominated by color changes in the extended disc component as star formation ceases and the youngest stellar populations fade. 

During this phase, galaxies often exhibit transitional morphological features, such as looser spiral arms and an increased prevalence of [[outer-rings|Outer rings]] [Smith et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.4575S/abstract). JWST observations of morphological asymmetry suggest that lower-mass galaxies generally exhibit higher asymmetry due to active star-forming regions, while higher-mass galaxies transitioning to quiescence show smoother infrared profiles [Ganapathy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.539..661G/abstract). The relative abundance of green valley galaxies implies a characteristic crossing time of $1$ to $2\text{ Gyr}$, a timescale that appears surprisingly independent of the local environment [Bremer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476...12B/abstract). 

The exact magnitude of the morphological changes during this migration remains an active area of debate, heavily dependent on the observational tracers used. When analyzed using standard luminosity-weighted images, transitioning galaxies appear to undergo significant structural changes, as the fading of the blue disc naturally increases the apparent concentration and bulge-to-total luminosity ratio. However, mass-weighted kinematic analyses suggest that, for the lower-mass systems, galaxies undergo almost no true kinematic transformation during quenching; their mass-to-light ratios simply increase as the disc fades [Rigamonti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..98R/abstract). Consequently, as much as $60\%$ of galaxies transitioning onto the red sequence may pass through a "passive disc" phase, retaining their underlying rotational support [Bundy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...719.1969B/abstract).

Despite the success of the disc fading model in explaining the photometric properties of many green valley galaxies, tensions persist regarding the detailed kinematics of the resulting red sequence lenticulars (S0s). Dynamical models of purely fading discs struggle to entirely account for the lower stellar spin and higher physical concentration observed in local S0s compared to their putative spiral progenitors, suggesting that intrinsic dynamical evolution or progenitor bias must still play a role [Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.2247C/abstract). Furthermore, it is widely agreed that pure disc fading cannot explain the structural differences in galaxies above the $M_\star \simeq 10^{11} M_\odot$ threshold, where major mergers and violent relaxation dominate the quenching process [Rigamonti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..98R/abstract). Similarly, some environmental studies argue that physical bulge enhancement is strictly necessary to reproduce the structural properties of early-type cluster galaxies, rendering simple fading models insufficient without additional tidal interactions [Christlein et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...616..192C/abstract).

## Environmental Drivers: Stripping and Strangulation

The transition of star-forming spirals into quiescent lenticular (S0) and early-type galaxies is significantly accelerated in dense environments. While secular [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]] occurs in the field, the dense intra-cluster medium (ICM) and immense gravitational potentials of galaxy groups and clusters introduce external gas-removal mechanisms. These environmental drivers—primarily ram-pressure stripping and gas strangulation—truncate star formation and trigger rapid disc fading.

Observations of intermediate-redshift clusters ($0.5 \lesssim z \lesssim 0.8$) reveal that environmental quenching produces a prominent population of passive spirals. In these environments, roughly 50% of spiral galaxies exhibit disc $V-I$ colours more than $1\sigma$ redder than their field counterparts, with the reddest cluster discs having ceased star formation at least $\sim 5$ Gyr ago [Cantale et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...589A..82C/abstract). The impact of the cluster environment is strongly radially dependent. For instance, deep structural decompositions of S0 galaxies in the Coma cluster demonstrate that global galaxy colours become systematically bluer at larger distances from the core; this trend is driven directly by a correlation between disc colour and cluster-centric radius, favouring environment-mediated disc fading [Head et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440.1690H/abstract). 

A persistent debate in the literature concerns the relative dominance of the two primary environmental quenching mechanisms: ram-pressure stripping (the rapid, hydrodynamic removal of cold gas by the ICM) versus strangulation (the cessation of cosmological gas accretion, leading to gradual gas exhaustion). Phase-space tracking of galaxies falling into groups ($13 \leq \log(M_{\rm halo}/M_\odot) < 14$) and clusters ($\log(M_{\rm halo}/M_\odot) \geq 14$) shows that quenching occurs much faster than [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]] in both environments [Oxland et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.3651O/abstract). This temporal decoupling guarantees a phase where galaxies exist as passive, fading discs before any morphological shift into classical early-types can occur. 

Recent integral field spectroscopy provides nuanced constraints on these gas removal processes. An analysis of mono-age stellar populations in Fornax cluster edge-on lenticulars reveals that the radial extent of the discs has remained constant or grown over the past 8 Gyr, with minimal vertical heating [Martig et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251119632M/abstract). Because violent ram-pressure stripping would typically truncate the gas disc from the outside-in, the sustained radial extent in these galaxies strongly points to strangulation as the dominant quenching mechanism [Martig et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251119632M/abstract). Supporting this gradual starvation framework, studies of H I-rich galaxies from the BLUEDISK survey demonstrate that recent gas accretion often occurs at large radii and does not immediately lead to the growth of CO-emitting molecular discs, suggesting long timescales (at least 5 Gyr) for this gas to migrate and contribute to star formation [Cormier et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.1724C/abstract). At lower masses, late-type dwarf galaxies in the Virgo and Fornax clusters exhibit a clear correlation between redness and fading surface brightness that aligns with theoretical fading tracks following gas removal; however, early-type dwarf galaxies surprisingly lack this correlation, suggesting that additional evolutionary processes are at play [Janz et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...647A..80J/abstract).

Importantly, a galaxy's star formation is often curtailed before it ever reaches the cluster core. Substantial pre-processing occurs in infalling group environments, suppressing star formation rates prior to cluster entry [Cantale et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...589A..82C/abstract), [Oxland et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.3651O/abstract). However, the literature remains divided on whether environmental disc fading alone can fully explain the S0 population. While disc fading naturally increases the apparent bulge-to-total ratio by dimming the extended disc, some studies argue that simple fading models cannot account for the enhanced bulge luminosities observed at small cluster-centric radii, suggesting that tidal interactions and bulge enhancement must operate in tandem with disc fading [Christlein et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...616..192C/abstract).

## Kinematic Transitions and Progenitor Bias

A central challenge in evaluating disc fading as the primary driver of spiral-to-lenticular (S0) transitions is the interpretation of galaxy kinematics. Observations consistently show that local S0s possess lower stellar spin and greater angular momentum deficits than their star-forming spiral counterparts of comparable mass. However, this apparent kinematic transition is heavily influenced by the choice of observational tracer. 

When star formation ceases, the stellar disc passively fades, causing its mass-to-light ratio ($M/L$) to increase significantly relative to the older, dispersion-dominated bulge. Consequently, luminosity-weighted kinematic measurements exhibit a substantial decrease in rotational support as galaxies fall below the star-forming main sequence [Rigamonti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..98R/abstract). In contrast, when mass-weighted tracers are employed, this kinematic shift largely vanishes for galaxies with stellar masses below $M_\star \simeq 10^{11} \mathrm{M}_\odot$ [Rigamonti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..98R/abstract). This suggests that for lower-mass systems, quenching entails almost no [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]]; the observed decline in spin is largely a photometric illusion driven by the fading disc. For massive galaxies above $M_\star \simeq 10^{11} \mathrm{M}_\odot$, however, genuine increases in dispersion support are observed across all tracers, indicating that different, likely merger-driven, quenching mechanisms dominate at the high-mass end [Rigamonti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..98R/abstract).

Despite the strong effect of $M/L$ variations, it remains actively debated whether passive fading alone can entirely account for the kinematic differences between spirals and S0s. Dynamical models simulating sudden quenching and subsequent passive aging demonstrate that while 5 Gyr of disc fading does increase central concentration and reduce measured stellar spin, these changes are quantitatively insufficient to bridge the entire gap between local spirals and S0s [Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.2247C/abstract). Furthermore, integral field observations from surveys like CALIFA reveal angular momentum deficits in S0s that are difficult to reconcile with simple secular fading or ram-pressure stripping [Querejeta et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..321..114Q/abstract). To fully reproduce the structural properties of many S0s, additional physical processes—such as major mergers or tidal interactions—may be required to actively build the bulge and dissipate angular momentum [Christlein et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...616..192C/abstract), [Querejeta et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..321..114Q/abstract).

The interpretation of these kinematic differences is further complicated by progenitor bias: the high-redshift spiral galaxies that quenched to form today's passive S0s may have differed dynamically from the star-forming spirals observed in the local Universe. Comparisons with cosmological simulations, such as the EAGLE suite, indicate that the progenitors of current passive galaxies likely had slightly *higher* stellar spins than present-day star-forming discs of equivalent mass [Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.2247C/abstract). Because progenitor bias posits a higher initial spin for the ancestral population, it moves the expected kinematic distribution further away from the pure disc fading scenario. Consequently, while disc fading is a critical component of the blue-to-red transition, the combined constraints from progenitor bias and angular momentum deficits imply that intrinsic dynamical evolution must also play a key role in the transition from star-forming to passive discs [Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.2247C/abstract).

## Structural Diversity and Disc Components

For the vast majority of lower-mass systems, quenching and the subsequent migration to the red sequence often proceed without major [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]]. When evaluated using mass-weighted kinematics, these systems show minimal changes to their underlying dynamical support. The apparent morphological evolution traditionally observed in luminosity-weighted tracers is instead primarily driven by secular disc fading, which steadily increases the mass-to-light ratio ($M/L$) as star formation ceases and young stellar populations die out [Rigamonti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..98R/abstract).

Understanding how fading impacts specific disc components requires disentangling the complex history of [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]]. Observational evidence from edge-on systems across cosmic time indicates that thick discs generally form first, followed by the emergence of a younger, embedded thin disc [Tsukui et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540.3493T/abstract). This sequential formation exhibits a distinct "downsizing" pattern: the transition from single to double discs occurred $\sim 8$ Gyr ago in massive galaxies ($10^{9.75} - 10^{11} M_\odot$), but was delayed until $\sim 4$ Gyr ago in lower-mass systems ($10^{9.0} - 10^{9.75} M_\odot$) [Tsukui et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540.3493T/abstract). Theoretical frameworks suggest that thick discs are built through direct cold gas accretion from the cosmic web, while thin discs assemble more gradually from shock-heated gas [Noguchi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494L..37N/abstract).

During the fading phase, the established vertical and radial structures of the disc are largely preserved. Spatially resolved studies of mono-age stellar populations in edge-on lenticulars show a remarkably constant half-mass height ($z_{50}$) for stellar populations younger than $\sim 6$ Gyr. This suggests that slow quenching mechanisms like strangulation operate gently, avoiding the secular vertical heating or severe flaring typically associated with violent environmental processing or major mergers [Martig et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251119632M/abstract). Radially, the extent of these mono-age populations remains constant or continues to grow over time, a structural record consistent with models of inside-out growth governed by the accretion of angular momentum prior to the complete truncation of star formation [Fraternali et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181108432F/abstract), [Martig et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251119632M/abstract).

While the underlying mass distribution may remain stable, the visual morphology of the disc transforms significantly as star formation fades, highlighting the growing prominence of secular features such as bars and rings. Galaxies traversing the "green valley"—a transitional phase strongly associated with fading discs—display a notably higher incidence of prominent [[outer-rings|ring structures]] and looser spiral arms compared to their active progenitors in the blue cloud [Smith et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.4575S/abstract). 

Furthermore, disc instabilities in high-surface-density progenitors can accelerate and complicate this visual transformation. Rest-frame optical observations from the CANDELS survey confirm that strong barred structures were already prevalent in massive discs as early as $z\sim 1.5$, suggesting dynamical maturity early in the fading process [Simmons et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.445.3466S/abstract). The formation of a bar in a gravitationally unstable disc induces non-circular motions and extensive radial mixing. Over time, this internal dynamical evolution flattens the density profile, decreasing the central surface brightness by $\sim 1.5$ magnitudes and nearly doubling the disc scale length. Consequently, disc instabilities coupled with passive fading can effectively transform a typical high-surface-brightness spiral into a giant low-surface-brightness galaxy [Noguchi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328..353N/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While disc fading is widely accepted as a primary driver for galaxies transitioning from the blue cloud to the red sequence, its exact role in forming lenticular (S0) galaxies remains a subject of active debate. Some studies suggest that the observed differences in concentration and bulge-to-total ($B/T$) mass ratios between spirals and S0s can be entirely explained by secular disc fading, as the dimming of the star-forming disc naturally enhances the relative prominence of the older bulge [Bremer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476...12B/abstract), [Maltby et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.1506M/abstract). However, this "pure fading" scenario is contested by evidence indicating that the observed angular momentum deficits and high bulge luminosities of S0s require physical bulge enhancement through major mergers or tidal interactions [Christlein et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...616..192C/abstract), [Querejeta et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..321..114Q/abstract), [Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.2247C/abstract). The extent to which simple fading drives [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]] versus dynamic kinematic heating remains unresolved.

There is also ambiguity regarding quenching timescales and the specific physical mechanisms responsible for gas depletion. Estimated timescales for traversing the green valley and fading to the red sequence range broadly from $1$ to $5\text{ Gyr}$ [Bremer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476...12B/abstract), [Cantale et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...589A..82C/abstract). In dense environments, it is clear that star formation quenching occurs more rapidly than morphological transformation [Oxland et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.3651O/abstract), but the relative dominance of ram pressure stripping (rapid gas removal) versus gradual strangulation is debated. Recent integral field data for edge-on lenticulars suggests that strangulation may dominate in certain cluster environments, preserving or even growing the radial extent of the disc over the past $8\text{ Gyr}$ [Martig et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251119632M/abstract). Furthermore, quenching mechanisms may be heavily mass-dependent: below $M_\star \simeq 10^{11} M_\odot$, disc fading without significant structural change appears to dominate, while merger-driven quenching may be necessary for higher-mass systems [Rigamonti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..98R/abstract).

The role of active galactic nuclei (AGN) feedback in accelerating or maintaining disc fading is another critical frontier. Demographic studies indicate that AGN spend significantly more time in a fading phase than a rising phase [Esparza-Arredondo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...905...29E/abstract). Observations of X-ray cavities suggest that fading nuclei can leave behind bubbles of hot gas ($E_{\text{bub}} \sim 10^{54}-10^{55}\text{ erg}$), injecting mechanical energy into the host galaxy [Sartori et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457.3629S/abstract). However, the precise timescale of AGN fading and its direct efficiency in quenching star formation across the wider disc remains to be rigorously quantified. Some models argue that quenching is fundamentally a mass-driven process governed by AGN feedback across all masses and redshifts, independent of morphology [Conselice et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4857C/abstract).

Finally, JWST observations are revolutionizing the study of high-redshift faded discs and early [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]]. JWST has traced the emergence of distinct thin and thick discs back over $10\text{ Gyr}$, revealing a "downsizing" pattern where high-mass galaxies transition to double discs earlier ($\sim 8\text{ Gyr}$ ago) than low-mass systems ($\sim 4\text{ Gyr}$ ago) [Tsukui et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240915909T/abstract), [Tsukui et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540.3493T/abstract). Furthermore, JWST has uncovered a population of "Little Red Dots" (LRDs)—compact sources with blue rest-frame ultraviolet slopes but red optical slopes. Recent evidence from a system at $z > 5$ has identified a transitional object bridging a normal AGN and an LRD phase, suggesting that environmental interactions may trigger the emergence or fading of an LRD [Mérida et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251006408M/abstract). Characterizing how these early quenched discs and transitional AGN relate to the massive, passive local populations remains a primary objective for future surveys.
