In extragalactic astrophysics, a **major merger** is defined as the gravitational coalescence of two galaxies of comparable mass, typically characterized by a baryonic or dark matter mass ratio greater than 1:4 ($\mu \ge 0.25$). As a cornerstone of the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], these highly energetic interactions are fundamental drivers of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. The [[galaxy-merger-dynamics-and-evolution|Galaxy Merger Dynamics and Evolution]] of a major encounter is governed by dynamical friction, which drains the orbital kinetic energy of the progenitor galaxies and causes their orbits to decay. During final coalescence, rapidly fluctuating gravitational potentials induce violent relaxation, a collisionless mechanism that redistributes stellar orbits, though the kinematic memory of the progenitor systems can partly survive. Historically encapsulated by the Toomre merger hypothesis, major collisions were long thought to inevitably destroy rotationally supported disks, transforming them entirely into dispersion-dominated [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]].

Modern theoretical and observational frameworks, however, emphasize that the structural and kinematic outcomes of major mergers depend sensitively on the initial gas fractions of the progenitor galaxies. In gas-rich ("wet") and highly dissipative encounters, strong gravitational torques strip angular momentum from the interstellar medium, funneling immense volumes of cold gas into the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]]. This localized inflow triggers [[violent-starbursts|Violent starbursts]] and drives the rapid accretion of supermassive black holes, frequently manifesting as heavily obscured [[dual-active-galactic-nuclei|Dual active galactic nuclei]]. Crucially, if the gas fraction is sufficiently high ($f_{\rm gas} > 0.5$), the residual gas can avoid complete consumption and settle post-coalescence to rebuild extended [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]] [Springel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...622L...9S/abstract), [Hopkins et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691.1168H/abstract). Conversely, dissipationless ("dry") major mergers between gas-poor progenitors assemble the extended stellar envelopes of the most [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] without triggering new star formation, predominantly yielding massive, slow-rotating spheroids with kinematically distinct cores [Bois et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1654B/abstract).

The prevalence and impact of major mergers evolve strongly across cosmic time. Observational demographics indicate that the [[volumetric-merger-rate|Volumetric merger rate]] and major [[pair-fraction|Pair fraction]] peaked at redshifts $z \sim 1.2-1.5$. However, the exact contribution of merger-induced starbursts to the global cosmic star formation budget—especially compared to the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]—remains a subject of active inquiry. This article explores the fundamental physics of major mergers, tracing their evolutionary stages from initial morphological disturbances to the final properties of [[post-merger-galaxies|Post-merger galaxies]]. It further examines the methodologies used to identify these events across cosmic time, such as non-parametric structural indicators [Conselice et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....126.1183C/abstract) and integral field kinemetry. Finally, the article reviews current debates in the literature, including the tension between the modest cosmic star formation driven by mergers at high redshift, the true frequency of Compton-thick binary AGNs, and the ongoing challenge of differentiating between surviving and rebuilt galactic disks using next-generation data from the [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] epoch.

## Definitions and Dynamical Mechanics

A major merger is traditionally defined as the collision and subsequent merging of two galaxies of comparable mass. In the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], these events represent a dominant channel for [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] and [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]]. In the context of typical galaxy histories, hierarchical scenarios suggest that systems like the Milky Way gathered the majority of their mass approximately 8-10 Gyr ago, balancing continuous [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] with periodic merger events [Combes et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.265..417C/abstract). Quantitatively, the boundary separating major from minor interactions is typically set at a **1:4 mass ratio** (or a mass fraction $\mu \ge 0.25$), where $\mu = M_2 / M_1$ and $M_1 \ge M_2$. However, defining this ratio is highly dependent on the physical metric used. The mass ratio can be evaluated using the total dark matter halo mass, the stellar mass, or the total **baryonic mass** (stars plus cold gas). Because the scaling between halo mass and baryonic mass is non-linear and evolves over cosmic time, an encounter classified as a major merger based on dark matter halos may correspond to a minor merger in stellar mass, and vice versa [Stewart et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ASPC..419..243S/abstract). Careful specification of the mass definition is therefore critical when comparing observational merger rates with cosmological simulations.

The dynamical evolution of a major merger is governed by the continuous dissipation of orbital energy and angular momentum. For an encounter to result in a merger rather than a fly-by, the initial specific orbital energy must be sufficiently low relative to the internal velocity dispersion of the progenitors. As the extended dark matter halos and galactic envelopes overlap, they are subjected to **dynamical friction**. This gravitational drag arises from the overdense wake created as the dense cores move through the diffuse background field of dark matter particles and stars. The friction drains the orbital kinetic energy of the galaxies, causing their orbits to decay and shrink over a characteristic timescale, $\tau_{\rm df}$.

During the initial interaction and [[first-core-passage|First core passage]], strong [[tidal-interactions-and-morphological-disturbance|Tidal Interactions and Morphological Disturbance]] induce non-axisymmetric morphological perturbations in the progenitor disks, frequently taking the form of tidal tails, bridges, and pronounced central bars. These asymmetric structures exert powerful **gravitational torques** on the material within the galaxies. While stars are collisionless, the interstellar medium is highly dissipative; the torques compel the cold gas to rapidly shed its angular momentum, driving massive radial inflows toward the galactic centers , [Hopkins et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691.1168H/abstract). This central concentration of gas sets the stage for ensuing starbursts and the fueling of dual active galactic nuclei.

The interaction culminates in **coalescence**, the final phase where the secondary and primary galactic nuclei merge. During this rapidly changing gravitational potential, violent relaxation redistributes the energies of the stellar orbits. While violent relaxation redistributes stellar orbits, the process is not fully efficient; kinematic memory of the progenitor disks can partly survive the merger event [Barnes et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ASPC...92..415B/abstract). This violent mixing typically results in a spheroidal geometry, driving the [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]] from rotationally supported disk galaxies to dispersion-dominated [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. However, if the progenitors retain extremely high gas fractions ($f_{\rm gas} > 0.5$), dissipation can mitigate the chaotic effects of violent relaxation, allowing the remnant to rebuild or preserve a massive rotating disk post-coalescence [Springel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...622L...9S/abstract), [Hopkins et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691.1168H/abstract). Furthermore, numerical simulations indicate that non-adiabatic shock heating during the final coalescence phase of a major merger generates a hot gas wind and forms an extended gaseous halo within the dark matter potential [Cox et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...607L..87C/abstract).

## Historical Context: The Merger Hypothesis

Historically, the formation of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] was primarily understood through the lens of the [[monolithic-collapse|Monolithic collapse]] model. Originally formulated in the 1960s and 1970s, this model posited that elliptical galaxies form in a single, intense burst of star formation at high redshift, coincident with the rapid gravitational collapse of a gas cloud to equilibrium. Following this initial starburst, the resulting galaxy would undergo purely passive stellar evolution. While the monolithic collapse scenario naturally explained the remarkably homogeneous and uniformly old stellar populations observed in local ellipticals, it struggled to account for the strong environmental dependence of galaxy morphology and lacked a clear physical motivation within emerging cosmological frameworks. 

A paradigm shift occurred when Toomre (1977) introduced the "merger hypothesis." Conceived as a direct alternative to monolithic collapse, this hypothesis boldly proposed that all elliptical galaxies formed via the major mergers of pre-existing disk galaxies. Toomre quantitatively justified this evolutionary path using observational demographics from the New General Catalog. He noted that roughly 11 out of 4,000 nearby galaxies exhibited pronounced tidal tails—unambiguous signatures of recent, major gravitational interactions. By assuming a characteristic observability timescale for these features of $\tau_{\rm mrg} \approx 5 \times 10^8$ yr, Toomre demonstrated that integrating this instantaneous merger rate over a Hubble time could successfully account for the approximately 800 elliptical galaxies in the same catalog. This conceptual framework laid the foundation for the [[toomre-merger-sequence|Toomre merger sequence]], which orders interacting galaxies from initial approach through late-stage coalescence into a fully relaxed remnant.

Despite its demographic successes, the merger hypothesis faced immediate theoretical challenges. Most notably, Ostriker (1980) raised a phase-space density objection: massive giant ellipticals often exhibit central velocity dispersions up to $\sigma \sim 300 \ {\rm km \ s^{-1}}$ and extremely dense cores. Because collisionless stellar dynamics conserve phase-space density, it was deemed physically impossible to form the dense core of a massive elliptical solely through the dissipationless (dry) merging of diffuse, present-day spiral galaxies. 

This apparent contradiction was resolved by recognizing that high-redshift progenitors were significantly more compact and that many possessed dense central bulges. Simulations demonstrated that the inclusion of rotating bulges in the progenitor galaxies helps to reduce the core radii of the remnants, creating dense centers comparable to observed ellipticals [Hernquist et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...409..548H/abstract). Additionally, the critical role of the interstellar medium was acknowledged; gas-rich mergers induce strong non-axisymmetric gravitational torques that strip angular momentum from the gas, driving massive inflows toward the remnant's center and triggering intense nuclear starbursts . These centralized bursts rapidly build up a dense, slowly rotating spheroidal core that bypasses the phase-space constraints of purely collisionless dynamics. Furthermore, the rapidity of star formation relative to the dynamical timescale is a key determinant of a remnant's chemical properties, with faster star formation leading to more metal-enriched, elliptical remnants [Bekki et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...497..108B/abstract).

Ultimately, the merger hypothesis found its natural theoretical home within the $\Lambda$CDM [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], where dark matter halos and their hosted galaxies assemble "bottom-up" via the continuous accretion of smaller structures. By the early 1990s, cosmological models pairing dark matter merger trees with baryonic cooling confirmed that merger-driven assembly naturally predicts the observed dominance of massive ellipticals in dense cluster environments. Augmented by observations of transitional remnants like NGC 7252—which exhibits both ongoing star formation and an $r^{1/4}$ light profile—the field reached a consensus that the Hubble morphological sequence is primarily a sequence of decreasing merger damage [Schweizer et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990dig..book...60S/abstract). Today, major mergers are unequivocally established as a dominant mechanism in [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]].

## Morphological Transformation and the Spheroid Formation

Major mergers are traditionally viewed as the primary mechanism for the [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]] of galaxies, converting rotationally supported [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]] into dispersion-dominated [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. During a major collision, rapidly fluctuating gravitational potentials drive violent relaxation, which scatters stellar orbits, redistributes angular momentum, and erases much of the progenitors' initial kinematic memory. However, the precise morphology and kinematic structure of the resulting [[post-merger-galaxies|Post-merger galaxies]] depend sensitively on the mass ratio, orbital parameters, and initial gas fractions of the merging systems. 

High-resolution $N$-body and hydrodynamic simulations demonstrate that major mergers do not produce a monolithic class of ellipticals, but rather a kinematic dichotomy of "fast" and "slow" rotators. Binary mergers with mass ratios between 3:1 and 6:1 predominantly yield fast rotators, which retain a significant degree of angular momentum and preserve some progenitor disk structure [Bois et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1654B/abstract). More symmetric mergers (1:1 and 2:1) can produce either fast or slow rotators. The slow rotators frequently exhibit kinematically distinct cores built directly from the stellar components of the progenitors, a feature that often requires the primary progenitor to have a retrograde spin relative to the orbital angular momentum [Bois et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1654B/abstract), [Bois et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASPC..477...97B/abstract). Detailed simulations show that the nuclear stellar properties of these massive ellipticals, such as flat cores and tangentially biased orbits, can result from SMBH scouring during major mergers (with mass ratios $q > 1/3$), which simultaneously produce these [[stellar-and-galactic-core-structure|Stellar and Galactic Core Structure]] centers [Rantala et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872L..17R/abstract). Structurally, the stellar remnants of major mergers tend to settle into oblate configurations, whereas the surrounding dark matter halos generally become prolate or triaxial, with the stellar minor axis oriented nearly perpendicular to the halo major axis [Novak et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...646L...9N/abstract).

The classic "fragile disk" hypothesis posits that major mergers inevitably destroy [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]. However, recent literature highlights a fundamental dependence on the progenitor gas fraction ($f_{\rm gas}$). In highly dissipative, gas-rich ("wet") encounters where $f_{\rm gas} > 0.5$, rapid gas cooling allows the surviving gas to settle and reform an extended star-forming disk post-coalescence [Springel et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...622L...9S/abstract), [Hopkins et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691.1168H/abstract). This mechanism explains how a large fraction of local thin disks could survive or rebuild despite the high merger rates predicted by hierarchical clustering [Puech et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753..128P/abstract). Furthermore, unequal-mass gas-rich mergers can exhaust the interstellar medium through centralized starbursts while preserving some disk kinematics, providing a natural pathway for the creation of S0 (lenticular) galaxies [Bekki et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...502L.133B/abstract). Similarly, dry minor mergers can preserve the strong photometric coupling between bulges and disks by triggering internal secular evolution, providing an alternative pathway for S0 galaxy evolution without dissipational effects [Eliche-Moral et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...552A..67E/abstract). Because of this residual star formation, spheroidal [[post-merger-galaxies|Post-merger galaxies]] are often observed to be bluer than the general early-type population [Carpineti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.420.2139C/abstract).

Conversely, for the most [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] ($M_* > 10^{11} M_\odot$), the fraction of accreted stars reaches up to $80\%$, indicating that mergers play the dominant role in shaping their morphology, whereas halo spin is more critical for dwarfs [Rodriguez-Gomez et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467.3083R/abstract). In fact, the observed [[mass-evolution|Mass evolution]] of massive galaxies since $z \sim 3$ can be largely explained by their total merger history, with these systems undergoing an estimated 4.5 mergers per galaxy since that epoch [Bluck et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...747...34B/abstract). Late-time structural assembly for these massive systems is dominated by dissipationless or "dry" major mergers between already [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] progenitors. Because they lack the cold gas necessary to rebuild disks or trigger centralized nuclear starbursts, dry mergers effectively increase the effective radius and stellar mass of the remnant without altering its fundamental stellar population [McIntosh et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.388.1537M/abstract). This process is critical for the buildup of extended outer envelopes in massive ellipticals and [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]]. Additionally, major mergers are essential in shaping the globular cluster populations of these remnants; the intrinsic scatter in the [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]] system-halo mass scaling relation for massive galaxies is primarily driven by a few major mergers that result in the accretion of old, red globular cluster populations . A proposed persistent kinematic signature of these major merger events in early-type systems is the "$\sigma$-bump"—a plateau or bump in the stellar velocity dispersion profile ($\sigma \propto r^\beta$) at intermediate radii ($1-3 R_{\rm eff}$), which is reliably produced in major merger simulations but absent in galaxies formed via monolithic collapse or minor accretion [Schauer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783L..32S/abstract).

## Dissipational vs. Dissipationless Mergers: 'Wet' and 'Dry' Events

The structural and kinematic outcomes of [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]] are fundamentally governed by the gas mass fraction, $f_{\rm gas}$, of the progenitor systems. This distinction defines whether the interaction is dominated by dissipative gas physics or dissipationless stellar dynamics, dictating the evolutionary pathway of the resulting [[post-merger-galaxies|Post-merger galaxies]].

**Wet (Dissipational) Mergers**
"Wet" mergers involve gas-rich progenitor galaxies. During these events, strong gravitational torques strip angular momentum from the interstellar medium, driving cold gas into the central regions. This dissipative inflow generates shocks and produces a highly concentrated reservoir of molecular gas, which frequently triggers intense nuclear starbursts and fuels supermassive black hole accretion . Despite the high local efficiency of these bursts, observational consensus from multi-wavelength surveys indicates that major mergers contribute only a modest fraction ($\lesssim 10\%$) to the total [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] density at $z \sim 1-2$ [Robaina et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704..324R/abstract), [Lofthouse et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2895L/abstract). 

Historically, it was assumed that major mergers invariably undergo violent relaxation that destroys galactic disks, leaving behind pressure-supported [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. However, recent hydrodynamic simulations demonstrate that in highly gas-dominated interactions ($f_{\rm gas} > 0.5$), the residual gas can avoid complete consumption during the starburst phase. This gas eventually settles to rebuild an extended, star-forming stellar disk [Springel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...622L...9S/abstract), [Hopkins et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691.1168H/abstract). Consequently, dissipational major mergers often yield disk-dominated remnants that are kinematically classified as "Fast Rotators" [Bois et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1654B/abstract). 

**Dry (Dissipationless) Mergers**
Conversely, "dry" mergers involve gas-poor progenitors, typically [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] that are already quiescent. Because they lack a cold gas reservoir, dry mergers do not trigger new star formation, allowing the remnant to remain on the red sequence. In fact, dry merging is directly responsible for the observed tilt and tightening of the color-magnitude relation at the high-luminosity end [Skelton et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ASPC..419..267S/abstract). 

Dry mergers are now recognized as the primary driver of late-time ($z < 1$) size and mass growth for massive galaxies. Purely stellar-dynamical interactions efficiently expand the effective radius and thicken the stellar envelopes of the remnants. This mechanism is especially critical in the formation of [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]], which are estimated to acquire $60\% - 80\%$ of their total stellar light through dissipationless major mergers with luminous early-type progenitors [Kluge et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..267...41K/abstract). Without gas to provide centralized dissipation and preserve angular momentum, major dry mergers between early-type galaxies typically produce "Slow Rotators"—massive spheroids characterized by triaxial dark matter halos and kinematically distinct stellar cores constructed from progenitor stars [Novak et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...646L...9N/abstract), [Bois et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1654B/abstract).

**Damp Mergers and Observational Tensions**
The boundary between wet and dry events is not strictly binary. So-called "damp" mergers represent an intermediate regime wherein the progenitors contain some gas—enough to induce mild central star formation, but falling below the threshold necessary to trigger the formation of massive, metal-rich globular cluster subpopulations [Forbes et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...659..188F/abstract). 

Furthermore, the literature reflects ongoing tension regarding the strict classification of a "dry" merger. Deep 21-cm radio observations frequently detect substantial neutral hydrogen (H I) reservoirs ($M_{\rm HI} > 10^8 M_\odot$) in the immediate environs of early-type pairs that meet the optical color and morphological criteria for dry mergers [Donovan et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AJ....134.1118D/abstract). This suggests that purely dissipationless events may be exceptionally rare in practice, and that low-level gas dissipation plays a subtle but persistent role in [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] even on the red sequence.

## Triggered Star Formation and Starbursts

Major mergers between gas-rich [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] are classic triggers of [[violent-starbursts|Violent starbursts]]. As two galaxies interact, strong gravitational torques induce non-axisymmetric perturbations, such as bars, in the stellar and gaseous disks. Since the gas and stars respond differently to these perturbations, the gas efficiently loses angular momentum to the stellar component, driving massive gas inflows toward the potential wells of the remnant [Hopkins et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691.1168H/abstract). These gas inflows can transport large quantities of cold molecular gas into the inner kiloparsec, dramatically increasing the central gas density and triggering a nuclear starburst [Bournaud et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....51..107B/abstract). In addition to torque-driven inflows, shock-induced star formation within colliding gas clouds and turbulent fragmentation can drive more extended star-forming regions during the interaction [Chien et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhDT........19C/abstract), [Bournaud et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....51..107B/abstract).

Observationally, the enhancement in the [[unusually-high-specific-star-formation-rates|Unusually high specific star formation rates]] (sSFR) becomes most pronounced at small projected separations, typically $r_p < 20$ kpc, peaking as the systems approach final coalescence [Robaina et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704..324R/abstract), [Duan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241104944D/abstract). In the local Universe, these events frequently manifest as ultraluminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs) and heavily dust-obscured starburst galaxies. In these systems, the enhanced central star formation burst is primarily driven by a substantial increase in the available molecular gas fraction, rather than an increase in star formation efficiency [Garay-Solis et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952..122G/abstract).

Despite the dramatic nature of individual merger-induced starbursts, their overall contribution to the cosmic star formation history is relatively modest. Deep surveys indicate that major mergers account for $<10\%$ of the total star formation rate density at $z \sim 0.6-2.0$, with the majority of cosmic star formation driven by [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] in isolated [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]] [Robaina et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704..324R/abstract), [Lofthouse et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2895L/abstract). Furthermore, recent studies in the local Universe suggest that even gas-rich major mergers may exert only a minimal impact on the global star formation rate and efficiency of the overall population, indicating that extreme starbursts are relatively rare outcomes [Li et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953...91L/abstract). Furthermore, the starburst efficiency of major mergers exhibits a strong redshift dependence. High-redshift gas-rich mergers are notably less efficient at producing starbursts than their local counterparts. This diminished efficiency is attributed to the fact that high-redshift disks are already highly turbulent and gas-rich, such that the additional compressive turbulence and gas inflows induced by a merger provide only a marginal enhancement over the pre-existing, elevated baseline star formation rate [Cen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1111.0707C/abstract), [Fensch et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.1934F/abstract).

Following the peak starburst phase, major mergers are also strongly implicated in the subsequent rapid shutdown of star formation. Observations of [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] reveal that the frequency of rapid quenching following a starburst is $30-60$ times higher in [[post-merger-galaxies|Post-merger galaxies]] than in a control sample of non-merging systems [Ellison et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517L..92E/abstract). This quenching is likely driven by a combination of rapid gas exhaustion during the starburst and energetic feedback from concurrent [[galaxy-black-hole-assembly|Galaxy-black hole assembly]] and active galactic nucleus activity triggered during the late stages of coalescence.

## Supermassive Black Hole Growth and Dual AGN

Major mergers are a primary mechanism for driving vast quantities of cold gas into the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]]. As gravitational torques strip angular momentum from the interstellar medium, gas funnels inward to fuel central starbursts and trigger intense AGN accretion . Observations across cosmic time indicate that the bulk of supermassive black hole (SMBH) mass assembly occurs during these merger-driven events [Treister et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Sci...328..600T/abstract). At higher redshifts, ALMA observations of $z \sim 4.8$ quasars demonstrate that while some host galaxies show companion sub-millimeter galaxies indicative of major mergers, others exhibit ordered gas kinematics, suggesting diverse fueling mechanisms for early SMBH growth [Trakhtenbrot et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180101508T/abstract).

The triggering of active galactic nuclei (AGN) is strongly dependent on the interaction stage. While enhanced star formation is often visible during early pericentric passages, the fraction of galaxies hosting an AGN peaks during late-stage coalescence. Significant increases in AGN incidence are observed when the projected separation between the merging nuclei falls below $10 \ {\rm kpc}$, with the strongest enhancements occurring at separations of $< 4 \ {\rm kpc}$ and in [[post-merger-galaxies|Post-merger galaxies]] [Stemo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923...36S/abstract), [Ellison et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.3627E/abstract), [Comerford et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963...53C/abstract). Multiwavelength SED analysis of nearby AGN hosts confirms that late-stage mergers exhibit significant elevations in star formation rates, AGN luminosities, and nuclear obscuration compared to earlier interaction stages [Troncoso et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250917720T/abstract). The temporal sequence of these events reveals a characteristic delay: the onset of peak AGN activity typically lags the peak of global star formation by $\sim 10^8 \ {\rm yr}$ [Schawinski et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.108S/abstract), [Barrows et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...850...27B/abstract). 

As the nuclei of the progenitor galaxies merge, both supermassive black holes can accrete simultaneously, appearing as [[dual-active-galactic-nuclei|Dual active galactic nuclei]] (dual AGN) before eventually forming a bound binary SMBH. During this process, the mass ratios of supermassive black hole pairs do not necessarily trace the initial mass ratio of their host galaxies, as differential accretion during the merger can significantly shift the pair toward equal mass [Callegari et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...729...85C/abstract). A classic local example of this phenomenon is the major merger NGC 6240, which hosts double active nuclei [Max et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Sci...316.1877M/abstract), similar to other close pairs like NGC 3393, which demonstrates that such sub-kiloparsec SMBH binaries can also be formed via minor mergers [Fabbiano et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Natur.477..431F/abstract). 

However, there is significant tension in the literature regarding the true frequency of dual AGNs in major mergers. Optical surveys frequently conclude that dual AGNs are exceedingly rare, occurring in only $0\%$ to $8\%$ of interacting systems [Teng et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753..165T/abstract). Conversely, the extreme gas column densities funneled to the center during a major merger dictate that the resulting AGN is often a heavily obscured AGN, rendering it invisible at optical wavelengths. When pre-selected using mid-infrared colors or hard X-ray observations, the detection rate of dual AGNs in late-stage mergers is substantially higher. These deeply buried engines are often hidden behind Compton-thick columns of gas and dust:
$$ N_{\rm H} \gtrsim 10^{24} \ {\rm cm}^{-2} $$
Recent NuSTAR and XMM-Newton observations of mid-IR-selected dual AGN candidates reveal that non-detections in these systems are consistent with high obscuring columns, though confirming the true binary nature of the buried AGNs remains observationally difficult [Pfeifle et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954..116P/abstract). Observations in the infrared and X-ray bands demonstrate that luminous obscured black holes with bolometric luminosities of
$$ L_{\rm bol} > 2 \times 10^{44} \ {\rm erg \ s}^{-1} $$
show a pronounced excess of late-stage nuclear mergers ($17.6\%$) compared to inactive control samples ($1.1\%$), confirming that optical studies systematically miss the peak phase of merger-driven SMBH growth [Koss et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.563..214K/abstract), [Satyapal et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848..126S/abstract), [Pfeifle et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...875..117P/abstract). The increased rate of major mergers among [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] suggests a higher black hole merger rate, potentially placing gravitational-wave signals from these binaries within the detection sensitivity of pulsar timing arrays [McWilliams et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1211.4590M/abstract).

Following this heavily obscured accretion phase, the energy released by the central engine becomes a dominant factor in the subsequent evolution of the host. The injection of kinetic and thermal energy via black hole feedback is hypothesized to heat or expel the remaining molecular gas, effectively quenching the merger-induced starburst and leaving behind a quiescent, spheroidal remnant [Treister et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Sci...328..600T/abstract), [Carpineti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.420.2139C/abstract).

## Cosmic Evolution and Redshift Dependence

The frequency and impact of major mergers evolve significantly across cosmic time, playing a critical role in the structural assembly of galaxies. Observational evidence indicates that the major [[pair-fraction|Pair fraction]] for [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] ($M_* > 10^{10} M_\odot$) peaks between $z \sim 1.2$ and $z \sim 1.5$ [Ryan et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...678..751R/abstract), [Mantha et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.1549M/abstract). Over their lifetimes, these massive systems are estimated to undergo an average of $\sim 0.8$ major mergers since $z \sim 3$ [Conselice et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...940..168C/abstract).

The evolution of the [[volumetric-merger-rate|Volumetric merger rate]] depends heavily on the methodology used to track galaxy populations. If the major merger rate is parameterized as a power law,
$$ \mathcal{R}_{\rm merge} \propto (1+z)^m $$
the index $m$ varies significantly based on sample selection. For galaxy samples selected by a constant comoving number density, the rate evolves strongly with $m = 3.0 \pm 1.1$ [Lotz et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...742..103L/abstract). Conversely, samples selected by a constant stellar mass threshold exhibit a much flatter evolution, with $m = 0.1 \pm 0.4$ [Lotz et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...742..103L/abstract). Furthermore, the merger rate depends heavily on the star formation properties of the progenitors: the rate for [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] increases steeply with redshift ($m = 4.5 \pm 1.3$), whereas the rate for [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] remains consistent with no evolution ($m = 1.1 \pm 1.2$) [Lackner et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....148..137L/abstract).

A primary source of tension in interpreting these evolutionary trends is the observational merger visibility time, $T_{\rm obs}$. Converting an observed pair fraction into a true merger rate requires robust constraints on $T_{\rm obs}$, which is highly uncertain. To reconcile observational pair fractions with theoretical merger rates derived from cosmological simulations, empirical models suggest that $T_{\rm obs}$ must decrease at higher redshifts. Proposed scalings include a power-law dependence, $T_{\rm obs} \propto (1+z)^{-2}$ [Mantha et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.1549M/abstract), or a linear decrease, $T_{\rm obs} = -0.36(1+z) + 2.39 \text{ Gyr}$ [O'Leary et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.3215O/abstract).

While the epoch of peak merging roughly aligns with the peak of the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] at $z \sim 2$, major mergers are surprisingly sub-dominant in driving the cosmic star formation budget. At $z \sim 1-2$, major mergers account for less than $10\%$ of the total cosmic star formation rate density [Robaina et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704..324R/abstract), [Lofthouse et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2895L/abstract). This modest contribution arises because high-redshift, gas-rich mergers are significantly less efficient at triggering starbursts than their local counterparts. Progenitor galaxies at these epochs already possess high gas fractions, strong compressive turbulence, and dense bulges, all of which suppress the relative enhancement of star formation during the interaction [Cen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1111.0707C/abstract), [Fensch et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.1934F/abstract). 

Recent deep JWST observations have extended these measurements into the [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] epoch. At $4.5 < z < 11.5$, the major merger pair fraction continues to increase up to $z \simeq 8$, reaching approximately $21\%$, before flattening out to $z = 11.5$ [Duan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240709472D/abstract). Beyond binary interactions, cosmological simulations indicate that mergers are strongly clustered in time, frequently involving three or more galaxies in 'multiple merger' events [Mack et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251003525M/abstract). This is observationally supported by the discovery of 'JWST's Quintet' at $z=6.7$, which confirms that multiple simultaneous mergers were active drivers of galaxy growth and circumgalactic medium enrichment in the early universe [Hu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250304032H/abstract). Similarly, observations of the hyperluminous obscured quasar W2246-0526 at $z=4.6$ reveal it is involved in a multiple merger with three companions, connected by tidal dusty bridges that feed the central engine [Díaz-Santos et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Sci...362.1034D/abstract). In this early universe regime, the binary merger rate stabilizes at $\sim 6 \text{ Gyr}^{-1}$, and major mergers contribute roughly $71\%$ as much to galaxy stellar mass growth as in-situ star formation from gas accretion, highlighting their fundamental role in the earliest stages of galaxy assembly [Duan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240709472D/abstract).

## Observational Signatures and Kinematics

Identifying major mergers and assessing their evolutionary stage relies on a combination of morphological and kinematic tracers. During a collision, rapid changes in the gravitational potential drive violent relaxation, while tidal forces strip material into the circumgalactic environment, leaving structural imprints that can persist for billions of years. 

**Morphological Disturbance and Fine Structure**
The most visually striking signatures of a recent interaction are tidal tails and bridges, which arise when dynamically cold, extended disks are subject to strong [[tidal-interactions-and-morphological-disturbance|Tidal Interactions and Morphological Disturbance]] [Schweizer et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000RSPTA.358.2063S/abstract). In gas-rich or dissipative encounters, these features often host localized knots of star formation. As the remnant relaxes, it may exhibit long-lived fine structures such as shells and ripples. These features are formed by the phase-wrapping of tidally stripped material from the progenitor galaxies as it oscillates within the potential well of the remnant. To contextualize these features globally, the Stellar Stream Legacy Survey provides a systematic sample of tidal features around thousands of nearby galaxies, offering a statistical baseline for comparing observed accretion signatures with cosmological predictions [Martínez-Delgado et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...671A.141M/abstract). However, interpreting these features requires caution. While often attributed to major dry mergers, deep simulations show that red tidal features in [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] can also be produced by minor mergers that involve no new star formation [Kawata et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...648..969K/abstract).

To identify mergers in large surveys objectively, astronomers rely on non-parametric structural indicators such as the CAS parameters (Concentration, Asymmetry, and Clumpiness) [Conselice et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....126.1183C/abstract) and the Gini-$M_{20}$ system. Elevated asymmetry and clumpiness typically trace the [[highly-distorted-galaxies|Highly distorted galaxies]] characteristic of pre-coalescence and coalescing mergers. However, converting a fraction of morphologically disturbed galaxies into a [[volumetric-merger-rate|Volumetric merger rate]] requires an accurate estimate of the observational timescale, $T_{\rm obs}$, over which a merger exhibits these signatures. Calibrations using hydrodynamical simulations indicate that $T_{\rm obs}$ is highly dependent on the chosen morphological metric and the mass ratio of the progenitors, representing a dominant source of uncertainty in empirical merger rates [Lotz et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...742..103L/abstract). Observing the H I morphology can sometimes provide clear merger visibility for up to $\sim 0.7$ Gyr [Holwerda et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2426H/abstract). Recently, convolutional neural networks and deep learning techniques have been trained on simulated mock images to predict the time since first pericentric passage with a median error of $\sim 38$ Myr, offering a continuous mapping of merger stages from single images without the need for traditional structural parameters [Koppula et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210205182K/abstract).

**Kinematics and Kinemetry**
In addition to photometric morphology, integral field unit (IFU) spectroscopy provides crucial 2D kinematic maps of the stellar and gaseous components. The technique of kinemetry—which models velocity and velocity dispersion maps using harmonic expansions—is widely used to quantify kinematic asymmetries and distinguish rotationally supported disks from merging systems [Shapiro et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...682..231S/abstract). 

Despite the utility of kinemetry, classifying galaxies at high redshift remains challenging due to "merger-disk mimicry." Seeing-limited observations can blur the orbital motion of a merging pair, causing it to masquerade as a single rotating disk. Simulation-based forward modeling suggests that this effect may inflate the estimated fraction of high-redshift disk galaxies by 5-15% [Simons et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874...59S/abstract). Furthermore, some [[post-merger-galaxies|Post-merger galaxies]] rapidly rebuild rotating gas disks that display smooth velocity gradients, rendering them kinematically indistinguishable from isolated disks post-coalescence [Hung et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...803...62H/abstract).

Within the relaxed remnants, deep kinematic mapping can reveal permanent signatures of a past major merger. Massive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] frequently host kinematically distinct cores (KDCs), which can form from the stellar components of the progenitors during major mergers that produce slow rotators [Bois et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1654B/abstract). Another proposed long-lived kinematic relic is the "$\sigma$-bump"—a plateau or localized peak in the radial stellar velocity dispersion profile between $1$ and $3 R_{\rm eff}$. Unlike tidal shells that eventually dissipate, simulations suggest the $\sigma$-bump is a permanent feature in the collisionless stellar component of major merger remnants [Schauer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783L..32S/abstract). Beyond purely kinematic relics, long-lived photometric signatures such as distinct globular cluster populations trace the remnant's history. In the 3-Gyr-old merger remnant NGC 1316, second-generation [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]] have been observed to survive and evolve toward the properties of 'old' populations found in normal ellipticals, providing a crucial evolutionary link between merger-induced starbursts and mature early-type galaxies [Goudfrooij et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002IAUS..207..245G/abstract).

## Disk Survival and Rebuilding

Historically, major mergers were assumed to inevitably destroy the fragile stellar structures of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], transforming them via violent relaxation into dispersion-dominated [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. However, contemporary models of [[galaxy-merger-dynamics-and-evolution|Galaxy Merger Dynamics and Evolution]] demonstrate that the initial gas fraction ($f_{\rm gas}$) of the progenitor galaxies dictates the structural fate of the remnant. In gas-dominated mergers ($f_{\rm gas} > 0.5$), a rotationally supported disk can frequently survive the interaction or rapidly reform shortly thereafter [Springel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...622L...9S/abstract), [Hopkins et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691.1168H/abstract).

During the encounter, gravitational torques strip angular momentum from the gas, driving much of it into the nucleus to fuel a starburst. However, gas at larger radii that escapes these internal torques retains its specific angular momentum. Following coalescence, this highly dissipative residual gas cools and settles into an extended, star-forming planar configuration rather than a pure spheroid [Springel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...622L...9S/abstract), [Bullock et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IAUS..254...85B/abstract). 

There remains an active debate in the literature over whether these systems technically represent **disk survival** or **disk rebuilding**. The distinction relies on whether the final structure is composed of stellar populations that kinetically survived the initial collision, or if it is predominantly rebuilt from a residual gas reservoir post-coalescence. Observational morpho-kinematic studies suggest the latter is remarkably common; roughly half of the thin disks in the local Universe may have been rebuilt following gas-rich major mergers over the past 9 Gyr [Puech et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753..128P/abstract). Furthermore, hierarchical models suggest that gas-rich mergers at high redshift are essential for forming the thick disk components of spiral galaxies, providing the requisite heating and subsequent gas settling [Brook et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASSP....3..551B/abstract). 

The mass ratio and orbital geometry of the encounter are equally critical parameters in determining the remnant's morphology. The specific orbital configuration of a merger dictates the structural fate of the remnant; spiral-in orbits tend to preserve or reform disk components, while direct collision orbits preferentially build spheroidal bulges and inner stellar halos [Wu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A.374W/abstract). While strictly equal-mass (1:1) and 2:1 mergers can yield a mix of slow and fast rotators depending on orbital parameters, 3:1 and 6:1 encounters preserve a significant degree of the progenitor disk structure and almost exclusively yield fast rotators [Bois et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1654B/abstract), [Bois et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASPC..477...97B/abstract). Furthermore, the alignment of the orbital angular momentum with the intrinsic spins of the progenitors strongly influences whether the specific angular momentum is conserved to rebuild a disk or randomized into a central spheroid [Pedrosa et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...567A..47P/abstract).

Beyond purely hydrodynamic and gravitational effects, magnetic fields have recently been identified as a key factor in disk rebuilding. Magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulations show that magnetic fields can stabilize the gas against severe fragmentation during the merger, altering how angular momentum is transported. Consequently, cosmological MHD models consistently produce remnants with more extended disks and more well-defined spiral arms compared to purely hydrodynamic equivalents [Whittingham et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506..229W/abstract).

## Mergers in High-Density Environments

In the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], the probability and efficiency of a major merger depend critically on the local galaxy density and the velocity dispersion of the surrounding environment. In the cores of rich, virialized clusters, typical velocity dispersions ($\sigma \sim 1000$ km s$^{-1}$) vastly exceed the internal velocity dispersions of individual galaxies. These high encounter speeds render direct galaxy-galaxy major mergers highly inefficient. Indeed, cosmological hydrodynamic simulations of galaxy clusters reveal that environmental effects may be a more dominant driver of galaxy spin-down and morphological transformation than mergers themselves, though minor mergers still have a significant cumulative impact on massive galaxies [Choi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837...68C/abstract). However, in galaxy groups and the infalling regions of [[unrelaxed-galaxy-clusters|Unrelaxed galaxy clusters]], where the environmental velocity dispersion is comparable to the internal velocities of the galaxies, major mergers are both frequent and transformative.

An important exception to the suppression of mergers in cluster cores is the phenomenon of galactic cannibalism. Through dynamical friction, massive [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] lose orbital kinetic energy and sink toward the bottom of the cluster's potential well, where they eventually coalesce with the central galaxy. This process is fundamental to the assembly of [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] (BCGs). Photometric and kinematic observations indicate that minor mergers alone are insufficient to explain the extended surface brightness profiles of BCGs. Instead, these systems likely undergo approximately three major mergers with luminous elliptical progenitors to assemble $60\% - 80\%$ of their total accreted stellar light [Kluge et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..267...41K/abstract). At $z \sim 1$, the major merger rate for BCGs is measured to be $0.38 \pm 0.14$ Gyr$^{-1}$, which is sufficient to account for the factor of two stellar mass growth observed in BCGs between $z=1$ and the present day .

Clusters themselves grow through the continual accretion of smaller sub-structures in a [[group-cluster-merger|Group-cluster merger]]. The pre-processing of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] often occurs in "supergroups"—bound structures of multiple infalling groups that have yet to virialize into a single cluster. For example, the Ursa Major supergroup is an early-stage assembly of at least six distinct groups destined to coalesce into a $\sim 8 \times 10^{13} M_\odot$ cluster [Wolfinger et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PASA...33...38W/abstract). Sub-cluster merging generates significant tidal forces that can drive the [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]] of gas-rich spirals into early-type lenticulars and trigger moderate starbursts before the galaxies are fully incorporated into the primary cluster core [Moss et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..230..487M/abstract). On smaller but equally dense scales, multiple mergers within compact groups can rapidly build massive spheroids. For instance, a rare quintuple dry merger of massive quiescent galaxies observed at $z=0.18$ represents a unique laboratory for witnessing the real-time assembly of an isolated massive elliptical galaxy [Filho et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443..288F/abstract).

At the largest scales, cluster-scale major mergers dissipate immense amounts of orbital kinetic energy through shocks and turbulence in the intracluster medium (ICM). Simulations of interacting clusters in IllustrisTNG reveal that prominent bow shocks and substantial gas loss are common during core passage, even for subclusters that are an order of magnitude smaller in mass than their host cluster [Łokas et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...673A.131L/abstract). These collision-induced shocks propagate through the ICM, generating double radio relics that serve as probes of the merger's geometry and timescale (e.g., CIZA J2242.8+5301, [van Weeren et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011JApA...32..505V/abstract)). The merger process also facilitates the metal enrichment of the ICM by enhancing ram-pressure stripping and galactic winds . In the most extreme "dissociative" mergers, such as RM J150822.0+575515.2, the collisional hot gas is physically separated from the collisionless dark matter and stellar components, providing a direct laboratory for mapping dark matter distributions [Stancioli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966...49S/abstract). High-resolution X-ray observations of such events frequently reveal complex ICM substructures, including cold fronts and slingshot tails, which mark the orbital trajectories of the merging subclusters [Machacek et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958...93M/abstract). Moreover, evidence of major merging can be found even in clusters previously thought to be dynamically mature. Recent weak lensing analysis of the Perseus cluster has identified a missing subcluster halo and a mass bridge, providing direct evidence that this supposedly relaxed system recently underwent a $\sim 3:1$ major merger [HyeongHan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240500115H/abstract).

The exact influence of cluster mergers on the star formation rates of member galaxies remains a subject of active debate. While some studies report interaction-triggered starbursts during sub-cluster accretion, others find no statistically significant enhancement of the star-forming fraction in merging clusters relative to relaxed clusters. This tension suggests that apparent enhancements in low-redshift clusters may simply arise from the mixing of unquenched, newly accreted field populations rather than merger-triggered starbursts [Çakır et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250604799C/abstract). However, at higher redshifts, the role of the environment becomes unambiguous: in protoclusters at $z > 5$, the major merger fraction is enhanced by $>3\sigma$ compared to the coeval field, leading to markedly elevated specific star formation rates [Marcelin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250518262M/abstract).

## Mergers in the Dwarf Regime

The [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]] predicts that galaxy assembly is largely scale-free, meaning that major mergers should be ubiquitous even among the smallest dark matter halos. Indeed, cosmological models indicate that dwarf-dwarf mergers dominated the total galaxy interaction rate in the early Universe [Mićić et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944..160M/abstract). Despite the observational challenge posed by their low surface brightness, the remnants of these low-mass interactions are increasingly detected, proving that major mergers critically influence the [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]] of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]]. 

Direct evidence for major mergers in the dwarf regime can be found in the Local Group. The Andromeda II dwarf spheroidal galaxy, which has a stellar mass of merely $\sim 10^7 M_\odot$, contains a highly coherent stellar stream rotating around its projected major axis. The kinematics of this stream strongly suggest that Andromeda II is the post-coalescence remnant of a major merger between two distinct dwarf galaxies, demonstrating that dynamically disruptive mergers operate down to the lowest galactic mass scales [Amorisco et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.507..335A/abstract). At intermediate redshifts, analogous systems have been observed, such as a Magellanic-type galaxy at $z=0.12$ ($M_* \sim 3 \times 10^9 M_\odot$) that exhibits tidal streams and binary components indicative of an ongoing major merger [Koch et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...815..105K/abstract).

Remarkably, dwarf-dwarf mergers are capable of driving gas inflows that fuel central star formation and trigger [[dual-active-galactic-nuclei|Dual active galactic nuclei]], much like their high-mass counterparts. Recent X-ray and infrared observations of merging dwarfs with projected separations $< 5$ kpc have revealed dual, luminous X-ray sources associated with actively accreting massive black holes. These systems feature distinct tidal bridges characteristic of late-stage coalescence [Mićić et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944..160M/abstract), implying that dwarf-dwarf mergers are critical for the early growth of massive black holes before these systems are incorporated into more [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]].

The remnants of these isolated dwarf interactions frequently become the building blocks for larger halos. The outer stellar envelopes of massive galaxies are heavily populated by debris from [[accreted-dwarf-galaxies|Accreted dwarf galaxies]]. As these systems fall into a massive host, tidal forces unbind them, leaving behind extended stellar streams and depositing both [[accreted-stellar-populations|Accreted stellar populations]] and globular clusters into the host's halo [Forbes et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127.3394F/abstract). A quintessential example of this process is the ancient merger between the Milky Way and Gaia Enceladus, a massive dwarf that contributed roughly 10% of the Galaxy's mass $\sim 10$ Gyr ago. High-precision chemical and kinematic analyses reveal that Gaia Enceladus was not destroyed immediately; rather, it survived at least two orbital passages. Stars stripped during early passages from the dwarf's metal-poor outskirts now exhibit high kinetic energy, while those originating from its inner regions were deposited during later passages and possess lower kinetic energy [Skúladóttir et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250600409S/abstract). In addition to Gaia Enceladus, recent archaeological analysis of the Milky Way identifies another major merger event, the [[kraken-merger|Kraken merger]] (sometimes termed 'Low-energy-Kraken-Heracles'), which occurred a mere 1.5 billion years after the Big Bang and deposited the bulk of its mass in the inner Galaxy [Massari et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260118896M/abstract). Such detailed archaeological records underscore how the hierarchical accretion of dwarf systems continuously shapes the host galaxy's extended structure.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While major mergers are well-established as key drivers of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], significant debates remain regarding their specific role across cosmic time. One of the primary active debates centers on whether major mergers significantly enhance star formation at high redshift ($z > 2$). Some theoretical and observational studies suggest that high-redshift, gas-rich mergers are significantly less efficient at triggering starbursts than their local counterparts due to high initial gas fractions and pre-existing turbulence [Cen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1111.0707C/abstract), [Fensch et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.1934F/abstract). Conversely, recent JWST observations probing the Epoch of Reionization ($z = 4.5-11.5$) indicate that mergers contribute up to $\sim 71\%$ as much to stellar mass growth as secular star formation, suggesting they remain a fundamental driver of early galaxy assembly [Duan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240709472D/abstract). Reconciling the tension between simulation predictions and these early universe measurements requires further constraints on gas dynamics and feedback, an area where large-volume hydrodynamical models like IllustrisTNG are actively being tested against new data [Schechter et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250701092S/abstract), [Koncz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250909355K/abstract).

Another major unresolved question is the role of major mergers among various quenching pathways. The literature is highly conflicting on whether major mergers are a dominant mechanism for shutting down star formation and driving galaxies onto the red sequence. Some models and observations argue that major mergers trigger rapid quenching post-coalescence, leading to the formation of [[post-starburst-galaxies|Post-starburst galaxies]] [Treister et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Sci...328..600T/abstract), [Ellison et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517L..92E/abstract). Others assert that environmental starvation and halo mass are the primary drivers of quenching, rendering major mergers a statistically insignificant pathway for the overall galaxy population [Weigel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...845..145W/abstract), [Gabor et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..350G/abstract). Similarly, the true frequency of merger-triggered [[dual-active-galactic-nuclei|Dual active galactic nuclei]] remains debated; while optical surveys suggest they are rare, infrared and hard X-ray selections consistently reveal much higher fractions of obscured binary accreting black holes in late-stage mergers [Satyapal et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848..126S/abstract), [Koss et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.563..214K/abstract). Adding to this debate, recent data from the unWISE survey suggest that the connection between mergers, star formation, and AGN activity may be weaker than theoretically predicted, with significant enhancement only visible at very small separations [Chishala et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251219236C/abstract).

There is also a persistent tension when comparing simulation vs observation regarding morphological transformations. For instance, observations have revealed a puzzling class of advanced merger remnants that exhibit exponential, spiral-like light profiles but possess pressure-supported, elliptical-like kinematics, representing a missing link in current N-body models [Jog et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...393L..89J/abstract). Furthermore, while classic models predicted the inevitable destruction of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] during major interactions, modern hydrodynamical simulations indicate that highly gas-rich major mergers ($f_{\rm gas} > 0.5$) can result in the survival or rapid rebuilding of extended [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]] [Springel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...622L...9S/abstract), [Hopkins et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691.1168H/abstract). It remains unclear how to cleanly distinguish observationally between a rebuilt disk and one that survived the interaction intact. In addition to structural changes, the impact of these interactions on chemical properties requires further study; simulations suggest that radial metallicity gradients in disk galaxies are remarkably resilient, with major mergers typically leaving only moderate and short-lived imprints on chemical distributions [Renaud et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240910598R/abstract).

Future progress in the field relies heavily on refining the observational timescales ($T_{\rm obs}$) used for merger identification. Assuming a constant observability timescale can lead to severe discrepancies between observed merger fractions and theoretical rates, particularly at high redshift where $T_{\rm obs}$ is expected to evolve [Mantha et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.1549M/abstract), [O'Leary et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.3215O/abstract). Furthermore, seeing-limited kinematic data at high redshift often struggles to distinguish the orbital motion of merging systems from the intrinsic rotation of unperturbed disks, leading to potential misclassifications [Simons et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874...59S/abstract). To overcome these limitations, researchers are increasingly turning to machine learning techniques. Deep learning models, such as convolutional neural networks trained on cosmological simulations, have recently demonstrated the ability to predict merger stages directly from single images with a median error of $\sim 38$ Myr, offering a promising avenue to bypass the biases inherent in traditional morphological parameters [Koppula et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210205182K/abstract).
