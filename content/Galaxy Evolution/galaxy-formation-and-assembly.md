**Galaxy formation and assembly** refers to the physical processes through which primordial gas and dark matter coalesce to build the diverse population of galaxies observed across cosmic time. Within the standard $\Lambda$ Cold Dark Matter ($\Lambda$CDM) cosmological framework, this evolution is fundamentally driven by the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], wherein small dark matter halos form first via gravitational instability and subsequently merge to build progressively larger structures [Efstathiou et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983FCPh....9....1E/abstract), [Katz et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...391..502K/abstract). As these non-baryonic halos assemble, they provide the deep gravitational potential wells necessary to capture intergalactic gas through [[cosmic-accretion|Cosmic accretion]]. This baryonic material undergoes radiative cooling, loses pressure support, and condenses toward the center of the halo to ignite star formation and fuel central [[galaxy-black-hole-assembly|Galaxy-black hole assembly]], driving the overall [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]] within the galaxy. 

Modern theoretical consensus characterizes the mass assembly of massive galaxies through a [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] model. The initial phase is dominated by rapid, dissipational collapse at high redshift, triggering intense *in-situ* star formation that rapidly builds the dense galactic core or bulge. This is followed by a protracted, quiescent late-time phase where mass growth is primarily driven by [[ex-situ-galaxy-growth|Ex-situ galaxy growth]]—the steady accretion of preexisting stars from infalling [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] [Johansson et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..354J/abstract). Throughout this assembly history, a galaxy's evolution is tightly regulated by a complex "baryon cycle" of gas inflows and energetic feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei (AGN). These feedback mechanisms are essential for preventing the overproduction of stars, reproducing [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|Galaxy chemical enrichment mechanisms]] [Tinsley et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980FCPh....5..287T/abstract), and driving [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]]—the process by which galaxies transition off the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] and undergo [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]] from actively star-forming [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] to passive spheroids.

Despite the broad explanatory power of the hierarchical paradigm, several critical tensions remain at the forefront of extragalactic astrophysics. Observational surveys continually reveal a high abundance of massive, fully assembled quiescent galaxies at high redshifts ($z > 2$), explicitly contradicting the protracted late-time assembly predicted by standard $\Lambda$CDM models. Furthermore, reconciling the specific alpha-element enhancement of massive ellipticals with current AGN feedback prescriptions remains challenging, prompting ongoing debate over whether the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] is universally constant or varies systematically with a galaxy's star formation rate. This article explores the interconnected processes governing galaxy assembly, detailing the mechanics of gas dynamics, stellar feedback, and the profound influence of the larger [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]] and the cosmic web. It also examines how relic systems like [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] serve as fossil tracers of merger histories [Brodie et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ARA&A..44..193B/abstract), and outlines how next-generation observatories will address the unresolved theoretical frontiers of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]].

## Introduction to Galaxy Assembly in the ΛCDM Framework

In the standard $\Lambda$ Cold Dark Matter ($\Lambda$CDM) cosmology, the formation of galaxies is fundamentally driven by the gravitational instability of primordial density fluctuations. As the universe expands, overdense regions decouple from the Hubble flow, turn around, and undergo collapse and virialization to form bound dark matter halos [Benson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhR...495...33B/abstract). Within this [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], structure formation proceeds from the bottom up: small dark matter halos form first and subsequently assemble into progressively more massive systems through a continuous sequence of mergers and smooth accretion [Katz et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...391..502K/abstract), [Dayal et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhR...780....1D/abstract). 

The luminous components of galaxies emerge within these purely gravitational structures through a process of baryonic dissipation. Following the foundational two-stage framework of White & Rees (1978), non-baryonic dark matter provides the gravitational potential wells into which baryonic gas falls. As this shock-heated gas emits radiation, its cooling time often becomes shorter than the dynamical age of the halo. Consequently, the gas loses pressure support and condenses toward the center of the potential well. Once the local density of the cold gas exceeds that of the dark matter, the baryonic material becomes self-gravitating, eventually fragmenting to ignite star formation and fuel central [[galaxy-black-hole-assembly|Galaxy-black hole assembly]] [Efstathiou et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983FCPh....9....1E/abstract), [Benson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhR...495...33B/abstract). This complex interplay of gravitational kinematics, hydrodynamical cooling, and subsequent feedback mechanisms dictates the [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]] within the host halo.

Moving beyond the historical [[monolithic-collapse|Monolithic collapse]] scenario—which posited that galaxies form in a single, rapid collapse of a massive gas cloud—modern theoretical and numerical consensus strongly supports a [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] model for massive galaxies. Under this paradigm, galaxy assembly is characterized by two distinct stages. The first is an early, rapid phase of dissipational collapse at high redshift, dominated by intense *in-situ* star formation that builds the dense central core or bulge [Cook et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.397..534C/abstract), [Johansson et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..354J/abstract). The second is a protracted, quiescent late-time phase where mass growth is primarily driven by the *ex-situ* accretion of older stars from merging satellite galaxies. This slow, hierarchical merging is responsible for building the spatially extended [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]] observed around massive ellipticals today [Johansson et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..354J/abstract).

Despite the broad successes of the $\Lambda$CDM framework in explaining hierarchical structure formation, significant tensions remain in the literature regarding the precise timing of [[mass-accretion-and-assembly|Mass Accretion and Assembly]]. Hierarchical models naturally predict a protracted assembly history, implying that the most massive galaxies should be the last to fully assemble their stellar mass. However, observational evidence reveals a high abundance of massive, quiescent galaxies already in place $3-6 \text{ Gyr}$ after the Big Bang ($z > 2$) [Glazebrook et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Natur.430..181G/abstract), [Collins et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.458..603C/abstract). This discrepancy highlights an active debate: the observed rapid assembly and early formation epochs of massive galaxies challenge the later assembly times predicted by standard $\Lambda$CDM merger trees, prompting ongoing research into whether heavily modified baryonic feedback prescriptions or alternative dark matter physics (such as "evanescent matter") are required to reconcile theoretical models with high-redshift observations [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract), [Peebles et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AnP...524..591P/abstract).

## Historical Paradigms: From Monolithic Collapse to Hierarchical Merging

Historically, the formation of massive galaxies was described by the [[monolithic-collapse|Monolithic collapse]] scenario. Inspired by the uniform, old stellar populations of elliptical galaxies, this early paradigm posited that galaxies form via a single, rapid burst of intense star formation at high redshift during the catastrophic collapse of a massive gas cloud. Following this initial burst, the resulting galaxy was thought to undergo passive evolution to the present day.

The paradigm shifted substantially with the recognition of dark matter's dominant dynamical role in the universe. This led to the seminal White & Rees theory (1978), which introduced a fundamental two-stage formation framework within a cold dark matter ($\Lambda$CDM) cosmology. In this model, dark matter halos form first through gravitational instability and hierarchical clustering; subsequently, baryonic gas cools, condenses, and forms stars within the potential wells of these dark halos. To prevent the overproduction of faint galaxies, this theory also established the necessity of baryonic feedback to regulate the overall efficiency of star formation.

Building upon this foundation, modern computational models and observations have solidified the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]] [Katz et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...391..502K/abstract). Within this framework, massive galaxies undergo a process known as [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]]. The first phase is an early, rapid dissipational collapse at high redshift that drives intense *in-situ* star formation, creating a dense central core or bulge. The second phase is a protracted, relatively quiescent period dominated by the accretion of older, metal-poor stars from merged satellite galaxies, which gradually builds the extended stellar halo [Johansson et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..354J/abstract). Conversely, other two-phase models propose that this late, quiescent accretion phase specifically favors the development of disc components around preformed spheroids [Cook et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.397..534C/abstract).

Despite the broad successes of hierarchical assembly, significant tensions remain between theoretical predictions and observational data. Early hierarchical models naturally predicted that the most massive halos assemble late, implying that their resident galaxies should feature younger stellar populations. However, empirical evidence reveals an "anti-hierarchical" trend where the most massive galaxies actually host the oldest stellar populations—a phenomenon widely referred to as downsizing. Modern theory resolves this baseline age-mass inversion by distinguishing between a galaxy's [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and its total mass assembly history: while massive dark matter halos assemble late, the constituent stars in their progenitor subunits formed very early. Accurate reconstruction of this history is essential, as the use of parametric star formation history models can bias inferred cosmic star formation rate densities and lead to an underestimation of mass-weighted ages compared to direct observations [Carnall et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...873...44C/abstract).

Even with this distinction, the precise timing of massive galaxy assembly remains a point of conflict in the literature. Observations have uncovered a surprisingly high abundance of fully assembled, massive quiescent galaxies merely 3 to 6 Gyr after the Big Bang [Glazebrook et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Natur.430..181G/abstract). For instance, the stellar masses of some [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] observed 9 billion years ago are found to be more than $90\%$ assembled, explicitly contradicting models that predict a protracted assembly history over a Hubble time [Collins et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.458..603C/abstract). This discrepancy highlights ongoing theoretical ambiguities; while some high-resolution models have been successfully tuned to match these high-redshift observations, the intrinsic $\Lambda$CDM assembly often occurs later than the data suggest. This tension has even prompted alternative proposals, such as the introduction of "evanescent matter" to accelerate the early assembly of protogalaxies [Peebles et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AnP...524..591P/abstract).

## Modes of Mass Assembly: In-situ Growth and Ex-situ Accretion

In the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm, the [[mass-accretion-and-assembly|Mass Accretion and Assembly]] of galaxies proceeds via two fundamental channels: the *in-situ* conversion of primordial and accreted cold gas into stars within the primary gravitational potential, and the *ex-situ* accretion of preexisting stars through galaxy mergers. This dichotomy forms the basis of the [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] model, which characterizes the mass assembly history of massive galaxies across cosmic time [Cook et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.397..534C/abstract), [Johansson et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..354J/abstract).

The first phase of assembly operates predominantly at high redshift ($z > 2$) and is characterized by rapid, dissipational collapse. During this epoch, deep gravitational potential wells drive intense, compact in-situ star formation, rapidly building the central spheroids or bulges of massive galaxies [Katz et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...391..502K/abstract), [Oñorbe et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732L..32O/abstract). Observational evidence of this intense early phase is preserved in the metal-rich sub-populations of [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]], which typically form during these massive, dissipational starbursts [Brodie et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ARA&A..44..193B/abstract), [Tonini et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...762...39T/abstract).

Following the exhaustion or feedback-driven ejection of cold gas, the mass assembly history transitions into a slower, relatively quiescent second phase. Late-time growth is overwhelmingly dominated by the ex-situ accretion of older stellar populations from infalling satellite galaxies. This occurs via continuous minor mergers and occasional [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]], which together deposit stars into the outskirts of the main galaxy, gradually building extended [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]] [Johansson et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..354J/abstract). Spatially resolved observations from surveys like MaNGA further reveal that this mass assembly leaves distinct spatial signatures on a galaxy's quenching history: massive galaxies typically undergo an inside-out quenching mode, whereas low-mass galaxies exhibit an outside-in mode that is heavily regulated by their local environment [Ge et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24c5006G/abstract).

The relative importance of [[ex-situ-galaxy-growth|Ex-situ galaxy growth]] is strongly mass-dependent. Semi-analytic models indicate that the ex-situ mass fraction increases slowly with host halo mass, but rises sharply for massive galaxies with stellar masses $\log(M_*/M_\odot) \gtrsim 11$ [Lee et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...836..161L/abstract). For central galaxies, ex-situ accretion overtakes in-situ star formation as the primary mass growth channel once the host dark matter halo mass exceeds $\log(M_{200}/M_\odot) \sim 13$ [Lee et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...836..161L/abstract). Conversely, satellite galaxies rarely enter a merger-dominated growth phase, as environmental effects truncate their accretion histories prior to substantial ex-situ buildup [Lee et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...836..161L/abstract). However, even in the lowest mass regimes, dwarf-dwarf mergers demonstrate triggered star formation that persists well after the coalescence of the two systems, often manifesting as bluer colors in galaxies with tidal debris [Kado-Fong et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....159..103K/abstract).

Despite the theoretical success of the two-phase paradigm in explaining local scaling relations and kinematic morphologies, there remains significant tension regarding the exact timing of mass assembly. Hierarchical models generically predict a protracted mass assembly history, where massive galaxies acquire a large fraction of their stellar mass at late epochs. However, observational surveys consistently identify a surprisingly high abundance of nearly fully assembled, massive quiescent galaxies as early as $3$ to $6$ Gyr after the Big Bang ($z \gtrsim 2$) [Glazebrook et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Natur.430..181G/abstract). For instance, some brightest cluster galaxies appear to have already acquired $>90\%$ of their final stellar mass by $z \sim 1.5$, directly conflicting with the extended hierarchical growth predicted by large dark-matter simulations [Collins et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.458..603C/abstract). This discrepancy between the protracted assembly predicted by standard models and the early, rapid growth inferred from observations remains a central, unresolved challenge in [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract). This is further illustrated by the evolutionary pathways of Luminous Red Galaxies (LRGs), which exhibit two distinct trajectories: 'fast-growing' systems that assemble $80\%$ of their mass as early as $z \sim 5$, and 'slow-growing' systems that reach the same state much later at $z \sim 1.5$ [Montero-Dorta et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848L...2M/abstract).

## Gas Dynamics and the Cosmic Baryon Cycle

The baryonic evolution of galaxies is governed by the cosmic baryon cycle—a continuous interplay of [[cosmic-accretion|Cosmic accretion]] from the cosmic web, star formation, feedback-driven outflows, and the eventual re-incorporation of ejected gas. In standard hierarchical models, gas within dark matter halos cools radiatively; once its cooling time falls below the halo age, it loses pressure support and condenses toward the center to fuel star formation. Tracking the detailed phase of this cold gas is equally vital; semi-analytic models like GAEA now incorporate dynamic molecular hydrogen (H2) partitioning, revealing how [[h2-regulated-star-formation|H2-regulated star formation]] laws heavily influence the cosmic H2/HI ratio, particularly by regulating early star formation at high redshifts [Xie et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469..968X/abstract).

Beyond the traditional shock-heated hot halo model, high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate the critical role of "cold flows" at high redshift. These unshocked streams of cold gas are supplied directly via the filaments of the cosmic web, penetrating deep into the halo to fuel vigorous starbursts. Supernova-driven outflows are often insufficient to halt this cold accretion at $z \sim 9$, where the ensuing metal enrichment can actually enhance star formation [Powell et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..277..234P/abstract). In the local Universe, this fueling mechanism persists strongly in low-density regions; void galaxies maintain higher specific star formation rates and lower metallicities due to sustained [[smooth-gas-infall|Smooth gas infall]] [Molina-Calzada et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250706781M/abstract).

The balance of gas accretion and outflows leaves distinct chemical signatures, establishing a tightly regulated mass-metallicity relation. Fluctuations in the cold gas supply govern the scatter in this relation: a suppressed gas supply drives up metallicity as star formation consumes the remaining fuel, whereas fresh accretion episodes dilute the interstellar medium [De Lucia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.3215D/abstract). Observations of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]]'s disk assembly support a "leaky accretion disk" model, requiring a mass-loading factor (the ratio of outflow rate to star formation rate) of $\eta \approx 0-2$ for stars formed within the last 4 Gyr [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...34W/abstract).

Gas ejected by stellar feedback is not necessarily lost to the intergalactic medium permanently. Semi-analytic models require mass-dependent timescales for the re-accretion of ejected gas to correctly reproduce the evolution of the galaxy stellar mass function up to $z \sim 7$ [Fontanot et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...842L..14F/abstract). In [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] and intermediate-mass systems, deviations below the mass-metallicity relation are often driven by late-time gas cooling that is dominated by the recycling of previously ejected material rather than pristine accretion [De Lucia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.3215D/abstract).

Conversely, a galaxy's gas supply can be abruptly truncated by its [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]]. When a system falls into a massive group or cluster, it becomes a [[satellite-galaxies|satellite galaxy]] and is subjected to "strangulation" (or starvation). In this process, the satellite's extended hot gas reservoir and cosmological cold flows are stripped, cutting off the supply of new gas. In classic semi-analytic formulations, this process was often modeled as instantaneous, completely removing the extended gas reservoir upon accretion. However, reproducing the observed abundance of passive satellite galaxies introduces tension with this assumption. Recent models argue that to match local passive fractions, gas cooling must actually continue onto satellites—at rates dictated by their pre-infall halo properties—even after their parent dark matter substructure is stripped below simulation resolution limits [De Lucia et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.5041D/abstract). Without continuous long-term replenishment from the cosmic web, the satellite eventually exhausts its cold gas pool, ceasing star formation and passively evolving.

## The Star-Forming Main Sequence and Starburst Events

Observations of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] reveal a tight correlation between their instantaneous star formation rate (SFR) and total stellar mass ($M_*$), a scaling relation widely known as the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] (SFMS). For galaxies residing on the SFMS, star formation proceeds in a relatively steady, secular manner, typically fueled by rotationally supported gas disks. In the local Universe, normal disk galaxies like the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] exhibit a specific star formation rate ($\text{sSFR} \equiv \dot{M}_* / M_*$) on the order of $0.1 \text{ Gyr}^{-1}$. This translates to a gas consumption timescale roughly comparable to the age of the Universe, characterizing a quiescent mode of mass assembly.

The position of a galaxy relative to the SFMS is deeply tied to its morphological and structural evolution. The detailed structural properties of disk galaxies also reflect their efficiency; for instance, spiral galaxies with a higher number of arms are generally found to be less efficient at forming stars, tending toward higher overall stellar masses [Porter-Temple et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515.3875P/abstract). This has been robustly confirmed by the cross-validation of Galaxy Zoo classifications between DESI and KiDS datasets, reaffirming the strong dependence of stellar mass and star formation rate on spiral arm number [Holwerda et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PASA...41..115H/abstract). As galaxies transition off the SFMS toward the passive population, they enter the 'green valley'. These transitional 'green valley' galaxies represent a critical evolutionary phase where [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]], specifically the buildup of a central bulge, occurs concurrently as star formation is actively shut down [Gu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884..172G/abstract). They exhibit a nearly monotonic trend toward higher Sérsic indices, smaller effective radii, and higher stellar mass densities [Brennan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..619B/abstract). This structural transformation is inextricably linked to [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]], where processes such as [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]], violent disk instabilities, and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback drive central bulge growth and deplete or heat the available cold gas, effectively shutting down star formation and driving the galaxy below the SFMS [Brennan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..619B/abstract). Interestingly, early-type galaxies residing on the main sequence follow distinct pathways: they can exhibit centrally concentrated star formation driven by their environment, or display extended, disk-like star formation regulated by internal secular processes [Koyama et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250716212K/abstract).

In stark contrast to the secular evolution characterizing the SFMS, the *starburst mode* is defined by transient, exceptionally intense episodes of star formation. Starburst galaxies exhibit sSFRs that are a factor of $10$ to $100$ higher than their SFMS counterparts, yielding gas consumption timescales as short as $10^8 \text{ yr}$. This vigorous activity requires the rapid accumulation of vast cold gas reservoirs into highly compact volumes, typically $\sim 1 \text{ kpc}$ in size and often concentrated in the galactic nucleus. Such extreme gas inflows are primarily triggered by strong dynamical instabilities or gas-rich [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]]. Furthermore, recent quantitative findings from the HSC-SSP suggest that [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]], rather than mergers, may be the primary driver of both star formation and active galactic nuclei (AGN) activity in the local Universe [Omori et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...989...73O/abstract). Consequently, the most extreme starbursts are often found in strongly interacting systems with highly perturbed morphologies.

The coexistence of the steady SFMS and the chaotic starburst mode raises critical questions regarding the universality of the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. While standard models of [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]] heavily rely on the assumption of a universal IMF across all environments, recent empirical evidence challenges this paradigm. Measurements of the high-mass IMF slope indicate a strong dependency on the star formation rate; highly star-forming galaxies produce proportionally more massive stars, resulting in flatter power-law IMF slopes than those seen in quiescently star-forming systems [Gunawardhana et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.1647G/abstract). A variable, SFR-dependent IMF fundamentally impacts the interpretation of galactic light: the enhanced luminosity from massive stars in starbursts can lead to significant underestimates of the underlying stellar mass if a universal IMF is incorrectly assumed [Fontanot et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..341..124F/abstract). Resolving this tension remains a major objective for accurate models of [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] across cosmic time.

## Structural Evolution and Morphological Transformation

The structural evolution of a galaxy is fundamentally tied to its [[mass-accretion-and-assembly|Mass Accretion and Assembly]] history, dictating its kinematic profile and the relative admixture of rotationally supported ("cold") [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] and dispersion-supported ("hot") [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] or [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]]. Recent observations confirm that this kinematic morphology is universally bimodal across different star-formation states and environments, cleanly separating galaxies into distinct cold disk and hot spheroid populations independently of their stellar mass [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..165W/abstract).

Within the standard [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], galactic structure develops through a combination of dissipative collapse, violent merging, and internal secular processes. Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations indicate that massive galaxies undergo a [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]]: an early, rapid phase dominated by intense, dissipative *in-situ* star formation that builds a dense central core or bulge, followed by a prolonged, quiescent phase where the extended stellar halo is assembled via the accretion of satellite subunits [Johansson et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..354J/abstract). During periods of stable, high-angular-momentum gas accretion, cooling gas settles into rotationally supported discs. This naturally leads to an [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]] scenario, fundamentally driven by the systematic redistribution of gas and its conservation of angular momentum within the evolving dark matter halo [Kepner et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...520...59K/abstract). Massive star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 2$ are frequently observed as large, thick rotating discs characterized by high internal turbulence and gas fractions [Förster Schreiber et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Msngr.145...39F/abstract). Similar properties are also observed in galaxies at $1 < z < 1.6$, which remain dynamically distinct from the thinner discs of the local Universe [Vergani et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...546A.118V/abstract).

[[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]] primarily occurs when this stable disc growth is disrupted. [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] of comparable-mass galaxies typically destroy fragile discs, triggering violent relaxation that redistributes angular momentum and leaves behind an elliptical, dispersion-dominated remnant. While gas-rich major mergers build bulges and drive starbursts, multiple *dry* (gas-poor) mergers are a primary mechanism for shaping structural scaling relations—such as the Fundamental Plane—and are responsible for excluding certain regions in the galaxy mass-radius plane at late times [D'Onofrio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...694A.229D/abstract). However, structural transformation is not exclusively driven by mergers; internal [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] plays a critical role. Gas-rich discs can develop non-axisymmetric bar instabilities that efficiently funnel gas inward. These bars may subsequently buckle out of the disc plane, forming central pseudo-bulges without the need for a major merger event. In addition, bulges can form via the collapse of clumpy, gas-rich disks at high redshift, potentially explaining the presence of old, disk-like, alpha-rich stellar populations in systems like the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] [Gerhard et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006EAS....20...89G/abstract). More extreme morphological features also arise from specific dynamical interactions. For example, cosmological simulations demonstrate that $\approx 83\%$ of [[ring-galaxies|Ring galaxies]] form via "drop-through" collisional interactions with companion galaxies, while the remaining $17\%$ are generated by long-lived secular bar instabilities [Elagali et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.2951E/abstract).

A galaxy's morphology is tightly correlated with its star-formation activity. As galaxies move off the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] and undergo [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]], they exhibit a nearly monotonic structural shift characterized by a higher Sérsic index, a smaller effective radius, and a higher central central stellar density [Brennan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..619B/abstract). This structural evolution is quantitatively tracked via the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]]. Detailed structural decompositions from the GAMA survey provide distinct stellar mass-half-light radius relations for bulges, discs, and spheroids, serving as a local benchmark for tuning hydrodynamical simulations like EAGLE [Lange et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.1470L/abstract). While semi-analytic models successfully reproduce the size-mass scaling for disc and quiescent galaxies up to $z \sim 2$, significant tensions remain in the literature regarding bulge-dominated systems. Simulated massive, bulge-dominated galaxies often exhibit sizes systematically smaller than observed estimates. This discrepancy is generally attributed to overly efficient prescriptions for high-redshift disc instabilities or excessive gas dissipation due to insufficient feedback mechanisms in models [Zoldan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.5649Z/abstract).

## Feedback Processes and Quenching Mechanisms

In the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological framework, gas cooling and gravitational collapse alone overpredict the cosmic star formation efficiency, leading to a theoretical overabundance of both highly luminous and very faint galaxies. To reconcile models with the observed galaxy luminosity function and stellar mass function, energy and momentum injection from baryonic processes—collectively known as feedback—are required to regulate or suppress star formation. These processes generally bifurcate by mass scale: supernova feedback regulates [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], while Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) feedback dominates in massive halos.

**Supernova Feedback and the Low-Mass Regime**
In galaxies residing in halos with masses below $M \sim 10^{12} M_\odot$, the primary regulatory mechanism is stellar feedback, primarily driven by supernovae (SNe) and stellar winds. Massive stars inject thermal and kinetic energy into the interstellar medium, reheating cold gas and driving galactic-scale outflows that eject material into the circumgalactic medium. Strong stellar-driven outflows are strictly required in semi-analytic and hydrodynamical models to reproduce the galaxy stellar mass function up to $z \sim 7$ and to track the cosmic star formation rate density back to $z \sim 10$ [Fontanot et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...842L..14F/abstract). Quantitatively, studies of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]]'s disk assembly using "leaky accretion disk" chemical evolution models constrain the mass-loading factor—the ratio of the gas outflow rate to the star formation rate—to be between 0 and 2 [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...34W/abstract).

**AGN Feedback and Quenching**
For halos exceeding $M \sim 10^{13} h^{-1} M_\odot$, the deep gravitational potential makes SNe feedback inefficient at driving outflows. Instead, [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] is driven by AGN feedback, powered by the accretion of matter onto central supermassive black holes. This energy release is crucial for moving massive galaxies off the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] and transforming them into [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] [Brennan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..619B/abstract). This quenching process is strongly correlated with structural transformations; as galaxies drop below the main sequence, they exhibit a nearly monotonic trend toward higher Sérsic indices, smaller effective radii, and higher central stellar densities [Brennan et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..619B/abstract), [Brennan et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451.2933B/abstract). While powerful AGN are frequently invoked to quench massive galaxies, observations from surveys such as GAMA note that quasar host galaxies are predominantly star-forming and show increased SFRs over recent epochs, suggesting that nuclear activity is often triggered by secular processes rather than major mergers [Stone et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251122453S/abstract). Furthermore, studies of the stellar-to-halo mass relation (SHMR) indicate that while halo concentration and formation time drive the scatter in this relation for low-mass halos, the energetic effects of AGN feedback significantly increase this scatter and dominate the relation in massive halos [Pei et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.2262P/abstract).

Radio-mode feedback—where mechanically powerful jets periodically heat the circumgalactic medium—is widely invoked as a "maintenance mode" to prevent the late-time cooling of hot halo gas in [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|High-mass early-type galaxies]]. However, the literature reveals significant tensions in standard AGN feedback prescriptions. Current models struggle to simultaneously reproduce the observed mass-metallicity relation and the specific $\alpha$-element enhancements ($[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$) of massive ellipticals. For instance, while artificially truncating star formation in galaxies above $M_* \approx 10^{10.5} M_\odot$ can yield the short star formation timescales required to match $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ ratios, it concurrently results in total stellar metallicities that are significantly lower than observed [De Lucia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466L..88D/abstract). Resolving this tension may require fundamental adjustments, such as introducing metal-rich winds or adopting a non-universal [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] that varies with star formation rate [De Lucia et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466L..88D/abstract), [Fontanot et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..341..124F/abstract).

**Positive Feedback**
While feedback is predominantly modeled as a negative (preventative or ejective) process, there is an emerging consensus that "positive" AGN feedback also plays a significant role. In this regime, AGN outflows and jets compress surrounding cold gas, triggering localized, accelerated bursts of star formation. Recent semi-analytic models demonstrate that positive AGN feedback can coexist with its negative counterpart, and its inclusion significantly improves theoretical fits to empirical scaling relations, such as the black hole-bulge mass relation and the stellar-to-halo mass relation [Contini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240510544C/abstract), [Contini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..274...41C/abstract).

## The Role of Environment and the Cosmic Web

Galaxies do not evolve in isolation, but rather within the large-scale scaffolding of the cosmic web, which comprises dense nodes ([[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]]), interconnected filaments, planar sheets, and underdense voids. However, these voids are not entirely empty; deep surveys have led to the discovery of 'tendrils'—coherent, thin chains of galaxies rooted in filaments that populate these regions, demonstrating that the apparent sizes and emptiness of cosmic voids are heavily dependent on survey sensitivity [Alpaslan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440L.106A/abstract). The [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]] encapsulates the long-standing observation that denser environments preferentially host early-type, passive, and bulge-dominated systems, whereas field and void environments are typically dominated by blue, star-forming [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]. Recent analyses confirm that galaxies located closer to three-dimensional cosmic web features, such as filaments and nodes, are systematically more massive and passive than their isolated counterparts [Kraljic et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474..547K/abstract). Additionally, measurements from the LEGA-C survey reveal significant environmental gradients in the $D_n(4000)$ index and $H\delta$ equivalent widths for massive quiescent galaxies at $z \sim 0.6-1.0$, providing strong evidence that massive galaxies systematically form earlier in overdense environments [Sobral et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926..117S/abstract). Conversely, galaxies residing in voids maintain higher star formation rates and lower gas-phase metallicities, a consequence of sustained cold gas accretion from their pristine surroundings [Molina-Calzada et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250706781M/abstract). Furthermore, group environments directly influence chemical cycles, evidenced by an observed decrement in the gas-phase metallicity for galaxies residing in groups compared to similarly massive galaxies in the field [Lara-López et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AN....334..466L/abstract).

The physical mechanisms driving these environmental trends depend fundamentally on a galaxy's hierarchical status within its parent dark matter halo. [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]] reside at or near the bottom of the primary gravitational potential well and can continue to grow through smooth gas cooling and minor mergers. In contrast, [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] are subjected to a host of "nurture" processes upon infall into a larger halo. These include ram-pressure stripping of their cold gas, tidal stripping of their dark matter and stellar outskirts, and "strangulation"—the cessation of cosmological gas accretion. Together, these mechanisms drive environmental [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]]. Observations of galaxy groups up to $z \approx 0.3$ show an increasing ratio of red to blue galaxies, indicating that satellite quenching is a significant, ongoing process [Vázquez-Mata et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499..631V/abstract). Furthermore, phase-space analyses reveal that star-forming group members predominantly occupy regions characteristic of recent accretion, whereas passive satellites dominate the virialized cluster cores [Barsanti et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...857...71B/abstract). 

Deconstructing the galaxy stellar mass function by halo mass using GAMA data reveals that star-forming galaxies dominate the low-mass regime, while more massive halos host a significantly higher fraction of passive central systems [Sbaffoni et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251219310S/abstract). The evolutionary pathway of a satellite often begins well before it reaches a massive cluster. "Pre-processing" within lower-mass galaxy groups is a crucial factor in satellite quenching, with the delay between infall and quenching scaling inversely with the host halo's mass [Gu et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201004166G/abstract). This environmental influence is further highlighted by the concept of "assembly conformity," where fossil groups assemble both their halo and stellar mass earlier than normal groups, resulting in more stellar-deficient centrals and satellites [Li et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...898...39L/abstract). Environmental effects also extend to the centers of massive halos: low-mass galaxies in cluster centers exhibit "coordinated assembly," possessing the high specific alpha-enhancement ($[\alpha/\rm Fe]$) and old stellar ages typically characteristic of much more massive systems [Gu et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201004166G/abstract). 

However, the exact mapping between dark matter halo assembly and galaxy properties—often termed "assembly bias"—remains an area of active debate. While cosmological models predict that clustering depends on halo formation time at a fixed mass, robustly detecting this signature via observational proxies (such as specific star formation rate or resolved star formation histories) has proven challenging, frequently yielding statistically insignificant results in the low-mass regime ($M \approx 10^{12} h^{-1} M_\odot$) [Lin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...819..119L/abstract). However, measurements in the GAMA survey yield evidence for halo assembly bias, demonstrating that low-mass halos located in knots—the high-density intersections of the cosmic web—are systematically older than those found in underdense voids [Tojeiro et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.3720T/abstract). Nonetheless, it is clear that the anisotropic tides of filaments [Kraljic et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474..547K/abstract) and the local intracluster medium work in tandem to reshape galaxies as they migrate and assemble along the cosmic web.

## Globular Clusters as Fossil Tracers of Assembly

[[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] serve as highly durable fossil records of a galaxy's assembly. By bridging the astrophysics of individual star formation in turbulent molecular clouds with the large-scale dark matter properties of their host galaxies, globular clusters link phenomena across vastly different physical scales [Beasley et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020rfma.book..245B/abstract). Acting as robust tracer populations, the total number of globular clusters ($N_{GC}$) in a galaxy is found to scale linearly with the virial mass ($M_{vir}$) of the host dark matter halo across roughly five orders of magnitude [Burkert et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....159...56B/abstract). This near-universal relationship is formalized as:

$$M_{vir} = 5 \times 10^9 M_{\odot} \times N_{GC}$$

This scaling holds regardless of the host galaxy's morphological type and is maintained by the continuous, hierarchical merging of subhalos [Burkert et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....159...56B/abstract), [Valenzuela et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230501007V/abstract). However, tensions remain at the low-mass extreme: the abundance of clusters in some dwarf [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] suggests that their current dark matter halo masses may be systematically underestimated if they adhere to this same formation paradigm [Burkert et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....159...56B/abstract).

The chemical composition of globular cluster systems strongly reflects the [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] model. Most massive galaxies exhibit a bimodal color and metallicity distribution among their clusters [Brodie et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ARA&A..44..193B/abstract). The metal-poor subpopulations (typically "blue" clusters) are thought to have formed early ($z \sim 3-4$) in primordial, low-mass dark matter fragments that were subsequently accreted onto the main galaxy [Brodie et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ARA&A..44..193B/abstract), [Tonini et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...762...39T/abstract). In contrast, the metal-rich ("red") subpopulation generally formed later ($z \sim 2$) within the main progenitor galaxy during gas-rich, dissipational [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] or intense starburst phases [Tonini et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...762...39T/abstract).

By mapping the age-metallicity distribution of a galaxy's cluster population, astrophysicists can explicitly reconstruct its merger tree. Cosmological simulations demonstrate that the diverse age-metallicity phase space of a globular cluster system is highly sensitive to the timing, mass ratio, and metal enrichment of past accretion events [Kruijssen et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.3134K/abstract). Utilizing these distributions, it is possible to identify the specific properties of accreted progenitor galaxies with stellar masses $M_* > 10^8 M_{\odot}$ from the epoch of peak cluster formation at $z \sim 1-2.5$ [Kruijssen et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.3134K/abstract). 

Finally, the spatial distribution of these clusters acts as a kinematic tracer of [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]]. Detailed phase-space analysis of globular cluster systems, such as in the giant elliptical M87, reveals cold streams and shell-like patterns that indicate massive, recent accretion events in central cluster galaxies [Romanowsky et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748...29R/abstract). Observational evidence points to mass stratification within globular cluster systems, wherein fainter clusters are preferentially deposited at larger galactocentric radii [Dou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250921975D/abstract). This radial sorting cannot be easily explained by secular internal processes like tidal dissolution; rather, it provides direct kinematic evidence for the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], in which later-accreted, lower-mass satellites are stripped of their clusters in the extended outskirts of the growing galactic halo [Dou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250921975D/abstract).

## Chemical Enrichment and the Mass-Metallicity Relation

The chemical composition of a galaxy serves as a fossil record of its [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and its continuous gas exchange with the intergalactic medium. Tracing this enrichment history back to the earliest epochs, high-resolution star-by-star cosmological simulations (such as the AEOS project) are now modeling early chemical enrichment, demonstrating how the very first generations of Population III stars established definitive metallicity floors within the first 300 Myr of cosmic time [Brauer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...41B/abstract). In a theoretical [[closed-box-model|Closed box model]], the gas-phase metallicity $Z$ evolves with the baryonic gas fraction $\mu = M_{\mathrm{gas}}/M_{\mathrm{tot}}$ strictly according to the nucleosynthetic yield. However, galaxies in the hierarchical paradigm act as "leaky boxes" regulated by cosmic inflows and feedback-driven outflows. This deviation is often quantified by the effective yield, $y_{\mathrm{eff}}$. Variations in $y_{\mathrm{eff}}$ provide a powerful diagnostic of the accumulated impact of supernovae and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback on the metal enrichment of galaxies [Zerbo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023BAAA...64..244Z/abstract). In the [[milky-way|Milky Way]], temporally and spatially resolved [Mg/H] abundances indicate that the galactic disk assembly adheres to a "leaky accretion disk" model, requiring a mass-loading factor (the ratio of outflow rate to star formation rate) of $0$ to $2$ to match theoretical yields [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...34W/abstract). Beyond the local universe, instruments have increasingly leveraged spatially resolved chemical properties—such as those measured by the MASSIV survey—to directly probe the ongoing mass assembly and chemical evolution of actively star-forming galaxies at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 1-2$) [Contini et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..277..134C/abstract).

The well-established correlation between a galaxy's stellar mass and its metallicity—the mass-metallicity relation (MMR)—reflects the mass-dependent efficiency of metal retention. The scatter around the MMR is not random; it is fundamentally regulated by fluctuations in gas accretion and the resulting cold gas content [De Lucia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.3215D/abstract). An influx of pristine gas drives a galaxy below the mean MMR by diluting the interstellar medium and triggering new star formation, whereas a restriction in the gas supply elevates the metallicity. Detailed studies using large hydrodynamical simulations (such as Illustris, IllustrisTNG, and EAGLE) further explain that the mass-metallicity relation exhibits a tertiary dependence on the specific star formation rate, a feature that is highly sensitive to the 'burstiness' of the implemented stellar feedback [Garcia et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.3342G/abstract). This scatter is also intimately linked to [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] and stellar kinematics. In low-mass systems ($M_* < 10^{10} M_\odot$), rotationally supported galaxies typically exhibit lower metallicities than dispersion-dominated ones. This trend inverts at higher masses, where AGN feedback and [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]] begin to dominate the baryonic cycle and drive chemical evolution [Zenocratti et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496L..33Z/abstract).

The abundance ratio of $\alpha$-elements to iron, denoted $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$, provides a critical clock for measuring assembly timescales. Because $\alpha$-elements are rapidly released by core-collapse supernovae while iron enrichment is delayed via Type Ia supernovae, an elevated $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ ratio indicates a rapid, intense burst of star formation followed by sudden [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]]. Massive [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|High-mass early-type galaxies]] universally exhibit such $\alpha$-enhancements, but reproducing this pattern remains a significant theoretical challenge. Standard AGN feedback models struggle to simultaneously match the observed MMR and the specific $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ enhancements in massive galaxies [De Lucia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466L..88D/abstract). Enforcing the short star formation timescales required to match the $\alpha$-enhancement often results in total stellar metallicities that are significantly lower than observed, indicating that AGN feedback alone cannot explain the positive correlation between $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ and galaxy mass.

Resolving these tensions may require revisiting foundational assumptions, particularly the universality of the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. Emerging observational and theoretical evidence suggests that the IMF varies systematically with a galaxy's star formation rate, with highly star-forming galaxies producing proportionally more massive stars and exhibiting flatter IMF slopes [Gunawardhana et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.1647G/abstract). Because a top-heavy or variable IMF would profoundly alter assumed nucleosynthetic yields and stellar mass-to-light ratios, it offers a potential mechanism to reconcile the observed alpha-enhancements and mass assembly histories without relying exclusively on extreme feedback models or exotic metal-rich winds [De Lucia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466L..88D/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Frontiers

While the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]] successfully explains many aspects of local galaxy populations, significant observational tensions persist, particularly concerning early structure formation and the fundamental physics of dark matter. 

A primary challenge lies in the timing of high-redshift assembly. Standard hierarchical models predict a protracted, late-time mass assembly for [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]]. However, spectroscopic surveys reveal that the most massive systems were already in place and often fully quenched $3-6$ Gyr after the Big Bang [Glazebrook et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Natur.430..181G/abstract). Furthermore, observations indicate that [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]] were almost fully assembled $4-5$ Gyr after the Big Bang, having grown to more than $90\%$ of their final stellar mass. This strongly contrasts with some dark matter halo models, which predict they should have assembled only $\sim 22\%$ of their mass by that epoch [Collins et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.458..603C/abstract). The discovery of large, massive rotating disk galaxies at $z \sim 2$ further challenges the chaotic, merger-driven formation timescales expected in the young Universe [Genzel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006Natur.442..786G/abstract). The literature remains divided on how to resolve this: some researchers suggest that improved baryonic feedback prescriptions—such as those implemented in cosmological hydrodynamical simulations like EAGLE and semi-analytic models like GAEA—can reproduce these high-redshift populations without altering the underlying cosmology [Furlong et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.4486F/abstract), [Fontanot et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...842L..14F/abstract). Others propose theoretical modifications, such as the introduction of "evanescent matter" to accelerate the early collapse of protogalaxies [Peebles et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AnP...524..591P/abstract).

A related long-standing tension lies in simultaneously reproducing the correct number densities of highly obscured dusty star-forming galaxies and massive quiescent galaxies at high redshift. To resolve these competing requirements, recent studies employ advanced Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) calibration frameworks for semi-analytic models (like L-Galaxies) to systematically map the vast parameter space of galaxy formation physics [Araya-Araya et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250415283A/abstract).

Another tension arises at the intersection of galaxy dynamics and dark matter physics. Recent observations of the galaxy cluster Abell 3827 reveal significant dark matter offsets between the stellar and dark matter components of a central galaxy, measured at $1.62^{+0.47}_{-0.49}$ kpc [Schaller et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453L..58S/abstract). Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations relying on standard collisionless cold dark matter heavily struggle to reproduce this phenomenon; they predict offsets randomly drawn from a Maxwellian distribution with $\sigma \leq 196$ pc, well below the gravitational softening lengths typically employed in such models [Schaller et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453L..58S/abstract). If astrophysical baryonic effects cannot account for such large offsets, these observations may compel revisions to the collisionless dark matter paradigm.

The precise timing of early cosmic milestones is additionally complicated by cosmic scatter. Large-scale fluctuations in the number density of galaxies at high redshift imply that events like the formation of the first stars and the reionization of the intergalactic medium did not occur uniformly, but at significantly different times across different regions of the Universe [Barkana et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...609..474B/abstract). Because many cosmological simulations are limited by computational volume and periodic boundary conditions, they artificially truncate this scatter, which may cause them to underestimate the abundance of high-redshift galaxies by up to an order of magnitude [Barkana et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...609..474B/abstract).

Resolving these conflicts requires the unprecedented capabilities of next-generation observatories. JWST is poised to revolutionize the study of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] by directly measuring the spatially resolved kinematics and metallicity gradients of massive galaxies at $z > 3$ [Perna et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IAUS..373..268P/abstract) and detecting the primordial formation of proto-[[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] at $z \sim 6$ [Forbes et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018RSPSA.47470616F/abstract), [Joschko et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204105J/abstract). Concurrently, the upcoming Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) will map the southern sky in deep, multi-epoch optical and near-infrared photometry. This will provide the immense statistical samples necessary to robustly constrain the roles of local environment and large-scale cosmic scatter in driving galaxy evolution over cosmic time [Robertson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NatRP...1..450R/abstract).
