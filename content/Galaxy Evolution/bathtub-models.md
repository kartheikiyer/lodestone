## Article

In astrophysics, **bathtub models** (also known as gas regulator models) are foundational analytical frameworks used to describe [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] by treating a galaxy's interstellar medium as an open, highly regulated gas reservoir. Emerging as a necessary paradigm shift away from the traditional [[closed-box-model|Closed box model]]—which fundamentally failed to explain the sustained star formation and observed metallicities of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract)—bathtub models emphasize continuous "**baryon cycling**." In this framework, the galactic system is governed by a fundamental mass-balance equation: the gas reservoir is continuously fueled by cosmological [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] from the cosmic web (the "faucet") and depleted by the sink terms of star formation and feedback-driven galactic outflows (the "drain"). By employing a minimal set of physically grounded macroscopic parameters, such as the [[average-star-formation-efficiency|Average star formation efficiency]] and the mass-loading factor of outflows, these models provide a highly efficient, mathematically transparent alternative to complex [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] and [[semi-analytic-modeling|Semi-analytic modeling]].

The core predictive power of the bathtub model stems from the assumption that galaxies naturally evolve toward a quasi-steady state (QSS) [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). In this equilibrium, the time derivative of the gas mass approaches zero, meaning the cosmological inflow perfectly balances the combined rates of gas consumed by the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] and gas expelled by winds. The dynamic behavior of this self-regulating system is dictated by the equilibrium timescale ($\tau_{eq}$), which represents the time required for a galaxy to restore balance following a perturbation in [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] [Peng et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.3643P/abstract). Because energetic feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei inherently shortens $\tau_{eq}$, galaxies can rapidly smooth out fluctuations. Notably, the bathtub framework elegantly explains the tight correlation between [[mass-evolution|Mass evolution]] and star formation rate known as the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] [Dekel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.2071D/abstract), as well as the observed [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]], linking a galaxy's chemical enrichment directly to its outflow efficiency and gas accretion rate [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract).

Despite the remarkable success of the minimal bathtub framework in capturing the mean evolution of star-forming galaxies, significant theoretical tensions remain. A prominent open question is the model's breakdown at "cosmic noon" ($z \sim 2$); standard analytical formulations struggle to simultaneously reproduce the era's [[normalized-star-formation-rate|Normalized star formation rate]] and the observed [[galaxy-mass-fractions|Galaxy mass fractions]] [Dekel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.2071D/abstract). Furthermore, the fundamental assumption of equilibrium is challenged by the stochasticity of cosmic accretion, particularly in [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] where the equilibrium timescale may exceed the age of the Universe [Peng et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.3643P/abstract). To address these limitations, recent advances have extended the model to incorporate spatially resolved scales, tracking [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]] and localized structural parameters to better constrain the physics of self-regulation. Ultimately, resolving these discrepancies requires deeper observational constraints on the circumgalactic medium to break degeneracies between the various feedback mechanisms that drive the sinks of the bathtub.

## Historical Context and the Shift from Closed-Box Models

Early models of galactic chemical evolution relied heavily on the [[closed-box-model|Closed box model]]. In this framework, a galaxy begins with a fixed mass of pristine gas and forms stars over time without accreting new material from or ejecting enriched material into the [[galaxy-and-igm-evolution|Galaxy and IGM evolution]]. While the closed-box approach provides a useful mathematical baseline and naturally yields a negative [[steep-age-and-metallicity-gradients|Steep age and metallicity gradients]] when combined with a [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]] of index $n>1$, it fundamentally struggles to match observational realities. Textbook theory notes that closed-box models predict [[abundance-gradients|Abundance gradients]] with amplitudes that are too small to match observations unless [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]] via gas inflows is invoked. Interestingly, some closed-box models are actually better at explaining specific carbon-to-oxygen (C/O) and metallicity-to-oxygen (Z/O) abundance ratios in typical irregular galaxies than outflow models [Carigi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514..787C/abstract), but they fail globally when confronted with the continuous [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and other [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] of the overall galaxy population. If the true nucleosynthetic yield is roughly constant across environments, the observed [[luminosity-functions-and-scaling-relations|Luminosity Functions and Scaling Relations]] strictly dictate that galaxies cannot evolve as closed boxes.

The definitive turning point in this historical development came with the advancement of high-redshift observations tracking the cosmic star formation history. Rather than relying solely on [[global-observables-of-galaxies|Global observables of galaxies]] to infer history, observers developed a "third way" of tracking galaxy assembly by directly measuring processes like mergers and [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] over cosmic time [Conselice et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..277..267C/abstract). Indeed, for the [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] observed between $z=1$ and $z=3$, mergers and gas accretion are found to be roughly equal drivers of structural assembly [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract). As surveys probed the universe at "cosmic noon" ($z \sim 2-3$), they produced vital demographic summaries of high-redshift [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]], such as Lyman-break and sub-millimeter galaxies, which quickly became essential observational targets for testing early open-box models [Gawiser et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ASPC..352..177G/abstract). These observations uncovered galaxies maintaining extraordinarily high [[instantaneous-star-formation-rate|Instantaneous star formation rate]] (SFRs) over extended periods. A closed-box system would exhaust its gas reservoir far too quickly to support such vigorous, continuous activity. This physical contradiction necessitated a theoretical shift towards "open" models. A foundational step in this transition was the Erb model [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract), which demonstrated analytically that the sustained star formation observed at high redshifts strictly requires the continuous accretion of gas. By enforcing a mass balance where the gas accretion rate is approximately equal to the combined SFR and mass outflow rate, the Erb model successfully reproduced the observed [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]] at $z \sim 2$ [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract).

This realization catalyzed a broader paradigm shift in our understanding of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. Previously, much of a galaxy's structural growth was attributed to discrete, violent merger events within early hierarchical models, although these frameworks often struggled to reproduce the exponential cut-off at the bright end of the luminosity function while simultaneously maintaining observed galaxy colors [Heyl et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.274..755H/abstract). However, the strict requirement of continuous gas replenishment led to the formulation of modern equilibrium or "bathtub" models [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). Consequently, the field transitioned away from viewing galaxies as isolated, initial-condition-dependent islands, establishing the modern consensus that they are self-regulating engines deeply coupled to their surrounding environments.

## Theoretical Foundations and the Quasi-Steady State

The theoretical foundation of the modern bathtub model rests on treating a galaxy's interstellar medium as an open, highly regulated gas reservoir governed by mass continuity equations [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). By treating galaxies as open systems fed by the cosmic web, the rate of change of the gas mass is dictated by the balance between gas inflows, gas consumption via the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]], gas removal via feedback-driven outflows, and the re-accretion of previously ejected material. This continuous exchange of gas between a galaxy and its surrounding environment is termed **baryon cycling**, a key component of [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]]. Early steady-state cooling flow models with gas loss provided an important early conceptual basis for the behavior of the sink terms used in these modern gas regulator frameworks [Sarazin et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ApJ...345...22S/abstract), which can be further simplified through a [[simplified-cooling-model|Simplified cooling model]]. To simplify this framework over cosmic time, galaxy growth can be modeled through a "baryonic growth function" governing gas availability and a "physics function" governing the efficiency of converting that gas into stars, cleanly reproducing observed galaxy [[mass-evolution|Mass evolution]].

The sink terms are parameterized using macroscopic physical properties. The star formation rate is linked to the available gas mass via a characteristic gas depletion timescale $\tau_{\mathrm{SF}}$, highlighting the processes of [[gas-depletion-and-consumption|Gas Depletion and Consumption]] such that $\dot{M}_* = M_{\mathrm{gas}} / \tau_{\mathrm{SF}}$. To improve the reproduction of observed gas properties, modern formulations explicitly link the star formation rate to molecular hydrogen (H2) abundance rather than the general cold gas reservoir [Power et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015aska.confE.133P/abstract). Meanwhile, gas removal via stellar and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback is characterized by the mass-loading factor $\eta$, defined as the ratio of the outflow rate to the star formation rate ($\eta = \dot{M}_{\mathrm{out}} / \dot{M}_*$). 

The response time of this system, known as the equilibrium timescale ($\tau_{\rm eq}$), acts as the central parameter driving the dynamic behavior of the gas regulator model [Peng et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.3643P/abstract). Physically, $\tau_{\mathrm{eq}}$ represents the $e$-folding time required for a galactic system to respond to perturbations in the accretion rate and self-regulate. Because $\tau_{\mathrm{eq}}$ depends inversely on the mass-loading factor and star formation efficiency, energetic feedback inherently shortens the time required to achieve equilibrium. In addition to ejecting gas, feedback mechanisms regulate galaxies by throttling the inflow rate. This **preventive feedback** limits the cooling and condensation of halo gas, effectively suspending the circumgalactic medium in a marginally unstable state through mechanisms like precipitation-regulated feedback [Voit et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...808L..30V/abstract).

On timescales $t \gg \tau_{\mathrm{eq}}$, the homogeneous solution of the differential equation decays, and the galaxy loses memory of its initial conditions, approaching an asymptotic behavior known as the quasi-steady state (QSS) [Dekel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.2071D/abstract). In the QSS, the gas reservoir ceases to grow rapidly relative to the cosmic flow rates ($\dot{M}_{\rm gas} \approx 0$), and star formation strictly tracks the cosmological inflow. To match observed [[galaxy-mass-fractions|Galaxy mass fractions]] at high redshift, bathtub models naturally require an average mass-loading factor of $\eta \simeq 1-3$ [Dekel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.2071D/abstract). More recently, extensions to the bathtub framework, such as the FEGA model, have sought to refine these feedback prescriptions by incorporating positive AGN feedback as a burst of star formation to further improve fits to the stellar-to-halo mass relation [Contini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..274...41C/abstract).

## Explaining the Fundamental Scaling Relations

Bathtub models naturally reproduce many of the [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] observed across cosmic time. By treating galaxies as open reservoirs in a quasi-steady state, these analytical solutions elegantly link global galaxy properties directly to cosmological accretion and feedback efficiencies.

**The Mass-Metallicity Relation (MZR):** In a quasi-steady state, the equilibrium gas-phase metallicity is dictated by the effective nucleosynthetic yield of the stellar population and the fraction of gas expelled by galactic winds. Analytical models demonstrate that matching the observed [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]] at cosmic noon ($z \sim 2$) requires an outflow rate that is roughly proportional to the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract). Consequently, more massive galaxies—which typically possess deeper potential wells and lower effective mass-loading factors—retain a larger fraction of their enriched yields, driving the positive correlation between stellar mass and metallicity [Mitra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1184M/abstract).

**The Fundamental Metallicity Relation:** If cosmological gas accretion is treated as a stochastic process, galaxies will experience temporal oscillations around their equilibrium state. An influx of pristine gas will simultaneously trigger an upward excursion on the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] and dilute the existing interstellar medium, lowering its metallicity. This mechanism naturally produces the observed anticorrelation between star formation rate and gas-phase metallicity at a fixed stellar mass [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443..168F/abstract). Look-back models utilizing these equilibrium principles correctly capture the SFR-dependence of the MZR and mirror the redshift-dependent relationships between interstellar gas mass and stellar mass seen in complex [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] [Kudritzki et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910...87K/abstract).

**The Star-Forming Main Sequence:** The self-regulating behavior of the bathtub framework gracefully produces the tight correlation between stellar mass and star formation rate [Peng et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.3643P/abstract). For galaxies residing on the main sequence, self-regulation implies that the [[normalized-star-formation-rate|Normalized star formation rate]] (sSFR) closely mirrors the specific [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] rate, tracking the cosmological inflow and scaling roughly as $\text{sSFR} \simeq [(1+z)/3]^{5/2} \text{ Gyr}^{-1}$ at high redshifts ($z = 3-8$) [Dekel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.2071D/abstract). 

**Constraints from Scatter:** The intrinsic scatter in these scaling relations provides strict quantitative constraints on the physics of self-regulation. By analyzing the observed scatter, researchers can bound the galaxy-to-galaxy variation in outflow efficiencies. Observational constraints dictate that the scatter in the mass-loading factor at a fixed mass must be remarkably small, $\lesssim 0.1$ dex [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443..168F/abstract). When calibrated via Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo techniques against observed scaling relations from $0 < z < 2$, equilibrium models successfully describe the mean evolution of both stars and metals across cosmic time [Mitra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1184M/abstract). While upward excursions from the main sequence are often observationally attributed to major mergers, [[semi-analytic-modeling|Semi-analytic modeling]] indicates that starbursts are not always merger-driven and can alternatively result from high gas surface densities in compact disks or rapid cooling events [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.4454W/abstract).

## Comparison with Semi-Analytic Models and Hydrodynamic Simulations

While bathtub models represent macroscopic, quasi-equilibrium systems through a minimal set of differential equations, they offer essential physical baselines against which to evaluate the predictive power of more complex frameworks, such as [[semi-analytic-modeling|Semi-analytic modeling]] (SAMs) and [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] [Furlanetto et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.3394F/abstract). For a comprehensive introduction to these differing approaches, pedagogical reviews serve as foundational references detailing the principles of semi-analytical modeling versus hydrodynamical simulations [Baugh et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006RPPh...69.3101B/abstract). Because bathtub models are analytically transparent and rely on a small number of physically motivated parameters, they are highly computationally efficient and immune to the "memory effects" of manual parameter tuning. Despite differing computational approaches, there is a remarkable convergence of model predictions across different methodologies regarding the core physical processes shaping global galaxy properties [Somerville et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ARA&A..53...51S/abstract). Analytical treatments further suggest that galaxy formation efficiencies can be modeled as functions of host halo mass and redshift alone [Neistein et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.405.2717N/abstract).

A persistent challenge across all these model classes is matching the evolutionary history of galaxies at high redshift. Comparative studies of SAMs reveal that many models overproduce [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] at $z \sim 2.5 - 3.0$, sometimes by nearly an order of magnitude [Asquith et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.1197A/abstract). Similarly, models often fail to reproduce "archaeological" downsizing, forming low-mass galaxies too early and leaving them too passive at late times, a process often discussed in the context of [[accelerated-galaxy-evolution|Accelerated galaxy evolution]] [Fontanot et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.397.1776F/abstract). While the minimal bathtub framework and typical SAMs yield highly consistent predictions for the sSFR history, both frameworks significantly underpredict the observed sSFR around cosmic noon ($z \sim 2$) [Peng et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.3643P/abstract). This tension persists even in advanced [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]]; for instance, simulated sSFRs for star-forming galaxies in the EAGLE suite are systematically lower than observed values [Furlong et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.4486F/abstract). Furthermore, the addition of self-consistent modeling of dust extinction in semi-analytic models has been shown to be necessary to accurately reproduce the clustering and ultraviolet properties of Lyman-break galaxies at high redshift [Lacey et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999elss.conf..392L/abstract). Another acute tension in sophisticated modeling suites is the difficulty of simultaneously matching the number densities of high-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies and [[high-mass-quiescent-galaxies|High-mass quiescent galaxies]] [Araya-Araya et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.542.2808A/abstract).

The stellar mass-to-halo mass relation offerings another critical point of comparison. In bathtub models, the SHMR and its scatter are predominantly a direct consequence of the mass-loading factor and feedback physics, often summarized in [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]]. Recent evaluations show that the semi-analytic [[munich-galaxy-formation-model|Munich galaxy formation model]] (L-GALAXIES) successfully captures an observational dichotomy whereby quiescent central galaxies occupy more massive halos than star-forming centrals at a fixed stellar mass. Intriguingly, sophisticated hydrodynamic suites—including [[millenniumtng|MillenniumTNG]] (Illustris and TNG) and EAGLE—fail to reproduce this specific SHMR bifurcation [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240807743W/abstract). Compounding these challenges, kinematic data of damped Ly-$\alpha$ systems reveal that standard disk-halo configurations within Cold Dark Matter models struggle to produce the observed velocity overlaps between different ionization states [Wolfe et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...545..603W/abstract).

Ultimately, the macroscopic properties of complex simulations are heavily governed by their subgrid feedback prescriptions, which mimic the parameterized sink terms of the bathtub model [Crain et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ARA&A..61..473C/abstract). In frameworks like [[galform|GALFORM]], model calibration relies strongly on matching [[global-observables-of-galaxies|Global observables of galaxies]] such as the $z=0$ galaxy stellar mass function, which restricts the viable parameter volume for feedback efficiencies [Rodrigues et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.2418R/abstract). To address some of these physical tracking limitations, recent improvements in [[simplified-cooling-model|Simplified cooling model]]s have explicitly incorporated methods that track the density and angular momentum profiles of the gaseous corona dynamically during halo growth [Hou et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475..543H/abstract). To further navigate these limitations, researchers use [[semi-empirical-galaxy-evolution|Semi-empirical galaxy evolution]] methods like abundance matching and environment-dependent quenching to test various galaxy-halo connection models against cosmological simulations [Hearin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AJ....154..190H/abstract). In this context, bathtub models remain highly relevant, serving as a physically grounded tool for extrapolating theoretical predictions to faint or high-$z$ galaxy populations that currently lie beyond the resolving power of detailed numerical frameworks [Furlanetto et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.3394F/abstract).

## Recent Advances: Spatially Resolved and Multi-Parameter Models

While foundational bathtub models are typically zero-dimensional—predicting global [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]] and the evolution of the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] by treating galaxies as a single, homogeneous gas reservoir—recent advances have extended this framework to account for spatially resolved scales and complex structural parameters. 

To explain the structural diversity of galaxies at a fixed stellar mass, modern extensions of the bathtub model incorporate secondary parameters such as galaxy radius ($R_e$) and halo concentration. Within the equilibrium bathtub framework, maintaining a constant SFR across different radii requires that more compact galaxies possess lower overall [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]]s [Lin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...93L/abstract). Moreover, exploring beyond baryonic physics, models that incorporate interactions between dark matter and gas can generate disky dark matter components that significantly improve fits to [[universal-rotation-curves|Universal Rotation Curves]], highlighting the intricate dependencies between extended halo properties and the embedded [[galactic-disc-models|Galactic Disc Models]] [Loizeau et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...920L..10L/abstract).

Beyond global structural parameters, the continuity equations of gas inflow, outflow, and star formation have recently been applied to sub-galactic patches, formalizing the "resolved self-regulator model." Direct observational evidence for local self-regulation has been detected using integral field spectroscopy. By applying the bathtub equations to localized regions, researchers can directly constrain feedback parameters on small scales [Zaragoza-Cardiel et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487L..61Z/abstract).

Finally, spatially resolved accretion models naturally link local gas regulation to the [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]] of galactic disks and the establishment of [[abundance-gradients|Abundance gradients]]. In a spatially extended bathtub model, the inner regions of a disk form early and rapidly reach a local quasi-steady state, while the outer disk forms later from continued, high-angular-momentum gas accretion. This producing a negative radial metallicity gradient that simple closed-box models fail to replicate. Furthermore, spatially resolved models tracking radial accretion and locally regulated feedback predict a universal anti-correlation between these abundance gradients and the [[stellar-density-profile|Stellar density profile]] (or disk scale length), driven by radially dependent star formation efficiencies [Prantzos et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ESASP.445...61P/abstract), [Boissier et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.325..321B/abstract). 

## Open Questions: The Redshift 2 Discrepancy and Stochasticity

Despite the foundational success of minimal bathtub models in reproducing [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] and the mean evolution of the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]], significant theoretical tensions remain. The most prominent of these is the **sSFR discrepancy** observed at cosmic noon ($z \sim 2$). 

At high redshifts ($z > 3$), where galaxy growth is dominated by continuous gaseous inflows from the cosmic web, the quasi-steady state (QSS) solution of the bathtub model naturally predicts that the sSFR should closely track the cosmological specific accretion rate. However, at $z \sim 2$, the analytical framework severely diverges from observational data [Dekel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.2071D/abstract). Complicating this issue, variations in the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] represent one of the largest systematic uncertainties in determining the precise star formation histories and stellar masses required to robustly constrain these bathtub models [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...39H/abstract).

This tension is not unique to idealized models; complex [[semi-analytic-modeling|Semi-analytic modeling]] frameworks similarly struggle with the high-redshift regime, frequently overproducing [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] with excessive star formation rates [Asquith et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.1197A/abstract). While it has been suggested that the exclusion of [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] from observational samples might introduce a selection bias that artificially inflates the empirical sSFR target, the magnitude of the discrepancy suggests a fundamental piece of physics is either missing or poorly parameterized [Dekel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.2071D/abstract). 

A second major open question concerns the fundamental assumption of equilibrium in the presence of **stochastic accretion**. The self-regulation of a bathtub model relies on the response timescale ($\tau_{eq}$) being significantly shorter than the time between discrete stochastic accretion events. High-frequency variability in dark matter and [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] challenges whether low-mass galaxies and high-redshift systems ever truly achieve a quasi-steady state, as their response timescales may exceed the age of the Universe [Peng et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.3643P/abstract).

Finally, resolving both the sSFR discrepancy and the limits of the equilibrium assumption requires a better understanding of the **circumgalactic medium** (CGM). The CGM acts as the physical buffer where stochastic inflows and feedback-driven outflows mix. Currently, models of galaxy formation exhibit significant degeneracies; vastly different subgrid feedback mechanisms can yield nearly identical final stellar masses [Crain et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ARA&A..61..473C/abstract). Consequently, future direct observations of the CGM's ionization state and density profile are critical for breaking these theoretical degeneracies and refining the sink and source terms of the bathtub framework [Singh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.3222S/abstract).
