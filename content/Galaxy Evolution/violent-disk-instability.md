Violent disk instability (VDI) is a rapid, [[internal-galactic-structure|internal dynamical process]] that dominates the structural and morphological evolution of gas-rich, high-redshift ($z \gtrsim 1$) [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]]. Unlike the slow [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] driven by weak non-axisymmetric features in the local universe [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract), or external transformations triggered by [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]], VDI is characterized by extreme, global [[gravitational-instability|Gravitational instability]]. In the early universe, galactic disks possess exceptionally high cold gas fractions, pushing the Toomre $Q$ parameter—which quantifies the balance between stabilizing rotational shear, turbulent velocity dispersion, and destabilizing self-gravity—into highly unstable, sub-critical regimes (often $Q \approx 0.2-0.3$). Unable to support themselves against their own gravity, these turbulent disks undergo rapid [[fragmentation-astrophysics|Fragmentation (astrophysics)]], transforming into [[clumpy-disks|Clumpy disks]] dominated by massive, kiloparsec-scale star-forming clumps that contain $10^8$ to $10^9 M_\odot$ of gas and stars.

Once formed, these giant clumps act as massive scattering centers that drive profound angular momentum transport across the disk. Through dynamical friction and asymmetric gravitational torques, the clumps shed their orbital angular momentum and undergo inward [[clump-migration|Clump migration]] toward the [[the-galactic-center|galactic center]]. This radial inflow serves as a primary *in situ* channel for the rapid early assembly of [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] and [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] [Immeli et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004A&A...413..547I/abstract), fundamentally challenging classical textbook paradigms that attribute the formation of massive classical bulges almost exclusively to the tidal torques of [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]] [Hernquist et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989Natur.340..687H/abstract), [Barnes et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...370L..65B/abstract). Furthermore, the extreme gas inflow rates associated with VDI funnel vast quantities of material into the central parsecs, efficiently fueling [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] and facilitating the rapid, direct-collapse growth of early [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]].

Despite its recognized importance in early galaxy assembly, several critical aspects of the VDI paradigm remain the subject of active debate. The most significant open question concerns the ultimate fate of the giant clumps: whether they survive long enough to reach the nucleus, or if they are rapidly dispersed by intense [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]]. Models assuming high local star formation efficiencies predict that clumps unbind within a few dynamical times due to radiation pressure and supernovae [Krumholz et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..112K/abstract), whereas simulations with continuous gas reaccretion suggest they can survive for hundreds of megayears to successfully build [[clump-origin-bulges|Clump-origin bulges]] [Bournaud et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...780...57B/abstract). Ongoing research aims to resolve these tensions by refining multi-component stability criteria for turbulent, gas-stellar coupled disks [Jog et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApJ...276..127J/abstract), leveraging high-resolution observations of primordial galaxies, and investigating how VDI ultimately suppresses star formation to transition early galaxies into stable, quiescent systems.

## Physical Mechanism: Instability Thresholds and Fragmentation

At high redshifts, [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]] possess exceptionally high cold gas fractions, leading to extreme [[cold-gas-surface-density|gas surface densities]]. These high densities drive the disk into a regime of violent disk instability (VDI). The local stability of a rotating disk against gravitational collapse is conventionally parameterized by the Toomre parameter, 
$$Q = \frac{\kappa \sigma}{\pi G \Sigma}$$
where $\kappa$ is the epicyclic frequency, $\sigma$ is the velocity dispersion, and $\Sigma$ is the surface density. In classic linear theory, a single-fluid disk becomes unstable when $Q < 1$. However, accurate models of [[galactic-disk-structure-and-dynamics|Galactic Disk Structure and Dynamics]] require a multi-component analysis that includes both the stellar and gaseous components, as well as the fractal nature of interstellar turbulence [Jog et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApJ...276..127J/abstract), [Romeo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407.1223R/abstract), [Agertz et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449.2156A/abstract). Incorporating the role of magnetic fields and viscosity in the linear theory of [[galactic-disc-models|disk instabilities]] further reveals that magnetic fields generally tend to reduce the non-axisymmetric responsiveness of the disk [Gammie et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..725G/abstract). In many star-forming spirals, disk instabilities are primarily driven by the self-gravity of the stellar component at kiloparsec scales, which then triggers [[fragmentation-astrophysics|fragmentation]] in the coupled molecular gas [Romeo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469..286R/abstract). Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that the stellar-to-halo mass fraction is regulated by disk gravitational instability across all morphological types, which plays a pivotal role in maintaining a quasi-universal Toomre parameter throughout various phases of galactic evolution [Romeo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.5656R/abstract).

Theoretical and observational work indicates that fragmentation and [[stellar-formation-and-protostars|gravitational collapse]] generally occur before the gas can self-shield against radiation, emphasizing that gravitational instability is the primary condition for widespread star formation [Stark et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.2323S/abstract). While nearby spiral galaxies often self-regulate to a marginal stability state near $Q \approx 1$ [Romeo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480L..23R/abstract), extreme high-redshift environments can maintain sub-critical states. Observations of primordial disks at $z > 6$ have revealed highly unstable states with $Q \approx 0.2 - 0.3$ and gas surface densities reaching $10^3 - 10^5 M_\odot \text{ pc}^{-2}$ [Fujimoto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1553F/abstract), [Forbes et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230207823F/abstract). Ultimately, macroscopic gas surface density profiles in these systems are determined by the balance between [[galaxy-accretion|cosmic accretion]], star formation, [[galaxy-outflows|galactic winds]], and the radial transport driven by gravitational instability torques [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.1552F/abstract).

When the surface density exceeds the threshold for local stability, the turbulent pressure and rotational shear are insufficient to prevent the self-gravity of the gas from inducing collapse. The disk undergoes rapid fragmentation. The characteristic mass of the collapsing fragments is governed by the turbulent Jeans mass or Toomre mass, which scales with the disk's velocity dispersion and surface density. In VDI-driven disks, the elevated velocity dispersions—sustained by feedback and gravitational torques—and the immense gas surface densities result in a characteristically large instability scale [Agertz et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449.2156A/abstract). The complex non-axisymmetric features generated by this self-gravity also require careful dynamic distinction between counter-rotating $m=1$ Jeans instabilities and rotating $m=1$ modes driven by swing amplification within the [[spiral-galaxy-dynamics|disk's resonance cavity]] [Dury et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.387....2D/abstract). 

This fragmentation process naturally produces [[clumpy-disks|Clumpy disks]] populated by massive, star-forming clumps. Unlike giant molecular clouds in the local universe, which typically have masses of $10^5 - 10^6 M_\odot$, the giant clumps formed via VDI contain $10^8 - 10^9 M_\odot$ of gas and stars [Bournaud et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..355B/abstract), [Krumholz et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..112K/abstract). These structures dominate the galaxy's ultraviolet flux and dictate its morphological appearance. Observations of both high-redshift galaxies and rare local analogs (e.g., the DYNAMO sample) have verified that the sizes of these giant clumps scale linearly with the gas fraction and the ratio of velocity dispersion to rotation velocity, precisely matching the theoretical predictions of the violent disk instability model [Fisher et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839L...5F/abstract), [Ali et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874..170A/abstract). Depending on the epoch and local conditions, these clumps can range from dense compact regions of $10-60 \text{ pc}$ in the earliest galaxies [Fujimoto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1553F/abstract) to kiloparsec-scale complexes at cosmic noon. Interestingly, similar physical principles apply across vastly different astrophysical regimes; disk fragmentation under the influence of magnetic fields and spiral-driven dynamos can also operate on much smaller [[circumstellar-disk-evolution|circumstellar scales]] to form [[giant-planet-formation|intermediate-mass planets]] while shielding them from further mass growth [Deng et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatAs...5..440D/abstract). At larger galactic radii, the onset of thermal instability—specifically the transition from warm to cold gas—frequently triggers the gravitational instability that sets the radial edges of [[outer-disks|stellar disks]] [Schaye et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...609..667S/abstract). This transition to cold gas phases can induce instability even in the far outer disks, leading to the formation of [[galactic-star-formation-distribution|double-exponential star formation profiles]] [Elmegreen et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...636..712E/abstract).

Turbulence plays a dual role in this physical mechanism. On the one hand, turbulent motions provide the primary pressure support against gravity, increasing the effective velocity dispersion $\sigma$ and establishing the large characteristic Jeans mass. On the other hand, supersonic turbulence creates local density enhancements that can act as seeds for [[gas-clumping|fragmentation]] [Hoffmann et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.1511H/abstract). Once star formation ignites within the giant clumps, [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|stellar feedback]] injects energy back into the surrounding medium, helping to drive the large-scale turbulence that maintains the disk in a state of dynamic, quasi-steady instability. The global star formation rate in these disks can be modeled as being proportional to the growth rate of the gravitational instability, linking local dynamical timescales directly to macroscopic Schmidt law behavior [Wang et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...427..759W/abstract). Crucially, stellar ultraviolet radiation and corresponding feedback act to establish a thermal equilibrium within the interstellar medium, naturally self-regulating the star formation rates according to this Schmidt law [Gerritsen et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997A&A...325..972G/abstract). This intense [[gas-clumping|Gas clumping]] allows galaxies to sustain high star formation rates without completely consuming their gas reservoirs in a single free-fall time.

## Clump Migration and Angular Momentum Transport

Once formed via VDI, massive clumps act as giant scattering centers, driving significant angular momentum transport across the disk. The inward [[clump-migration|Clump migration]] of these clumps is mediated primarily by two mechanisms: dynamical friction against the background sea of stars and dark matter, and [[asymmetric-galaxy-morphologies-and-kinematics|asymmetric gravitational torques]] exerted by the turbulent, non-axisymmetric gas disk [Immeli et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004A&A...413..547I/abstract), [Bournaud et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..355B/abstract). Through these interactions, clumps shed their orbital angular momentum and spiral toward the galactic center on timescales of a few $10^8$ years, corresponding to several disk orbital periods [Immeli et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004A&A...413..547I/abstract), [Bournaud et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..355B/abstract). During this violent phase, feedback-driven ejection of gas 'blobs' from young disks can lead to significant spin-down and the establishment of early [[galactic-disk-evolution|metallicity gradients]] [Charlton et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ApJ...346..101C/abstract).

The efficacy of this migration process hinges critically on the survival timescale of the clumps against stellar feedback (see [[stellar-feedback-and-the-debate-on-clump-lifetimes|#Stellar Feedback and the Debate on Clump Lifetimes]]). If clumps survive long enough to complete their inward migration, their coalescence serves as a primary driver of secular morphological transformation in the early universe. The coalescing clumps rapidly assemble dense central stellar mass concentrations, driving the early in-situ formation of [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] and [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] without the need for major [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]] [Immeli et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004A&A...413..547I/abstract), [Bournaud et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..355B/abstract). This internal evolutionary pathway can transform primitive, clumpy disks into bulge-dominated lenticular or spiral galaxies in less than a billion years. 

Beyond stellar mass assembly, the angular momentum transport associated with violent disk instability funnels vast quantities of gas into the inner kiloparsec. The high gas surface densities and efficient torquing in VDI-driven disks enable extreme nuclear inflow rates, providing a potent fueling mechanism for [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] and facilitating the rapid growth of [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]]. In extreme cases, massive sinking clumps may themselves form and host intermediate-mass black holes that merge upon reaching the nucleus, potentially assembling supermassive black holes that represent $>10\%$ of the host galaxy's total stellar mass at $z > 6$ [Mayer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241100670M/abstract).

## Bulge Assembly and SMBH Fueling

Violent disk instability (VDI) acts as a highly efficient mechanism for angular momentum transport, driving widespread mass inflows and facilitating rapid spheroid growth. In gas-rich, high-redshift disks, the massive star-forming clumps generated by VDI are subject to [[stellar-dynamics-and-kinematics|dynamical friction]], which causes them to lose angular momentum, undergo [[clump-migration|Clump migration]], and coalesce at the galactic center [Bournaud et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..355B/abstract), [Immeli et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004A&A...413..547I/abstract). This process allows for the assembly of massive [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] on timescales of only a few hundred million years. Observations confirm that such processes can form regularly rotating disks with massive central bulges as early as $z \simeq 5$, or roughly 1.2 billion years after the Big Bang [Lelli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...371..713L/abstract). Furthermore, isolated disk fragmentation via VDI has been shown to rapidly produce lenticular (S0) morphologies in less than a billion years, without the need for major galaxy mergers [Saha et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862L..12S/abstract).

While local [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] are traditionally thought to emerge from slow [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] driven by bars and spiral arms, cosmological simulations demonstrate that high-redshift VDI starbursts provide a primary formation channel for these structures in the early universe . Additionally, [[stellar-and-galactic-core-structure|nuclear star clusters]] in late-type bulgeless disks likely form *in situ* due to radial gas transport driven by internal disk instabilities, such as the magnetorotational instability [Milosavljević et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...605L..13M/abstract). As these [[compact-circumnuclear-disks|nuclear gas disks]] grow via infall, they eventually trigger local gravitational instabilities of their own, facilitating episodic bursts of massive star formation [Bailey et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982MNRAS.200..247B/abstract). The ultimate efficiency of this bulge-building pathway hinges heavily upon whether the giant clumps can survive intense stellar feedback long enough to complete their migration to the nucleus [Bournaud et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...780...57B/abstract), [Krumholz et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..112K/abstract). Furthermore, the high-redshift, dense disk environments produced by VDI are proposed birthplaces for metal-poor [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|globular clusters]], which are later redistributed into the [[stellar-halos|galactic halo]] [Elmegreen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...712L.184E/abstract), while early dissipative minor mergers provide a competing and complementary channel for forming metal-rich disk globular clusters alongside in-situ VDI fragmentation [Bekki et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...566..245B/abstract). To capture this holistic picture of spheroid growth, modern [[galactic-disc-models|semi-analytic models]] must incorporate two-component (gas and stars) instability recipes to accurately reproduce the observed morphology-mass relations and disk scale lengths of these evolving populations [Irodotou et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.3609I/abstract).

In addition to driving stellar mass inward, extreme global disk instabilities channel massive amounts of gas into the central parsecs, driving intense nuclear accretion and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|AGN fueling]]. When initiated by major mergers, these instabilities can trigger extreme central inflow rates [Mayer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019RPPh...82a6901M/abstract). This rapid influx provides a robust mechanism for the growth of early [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] (SMBHs). Moreover, standard hierarchical models of black hole growth must account for disk instabilities to explain the demography and population of undermassive black holes observed in late-type and pseudobulge-dominated galaxies [Shankar et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...540A..23S/abstract). It is also observed that violent relaxation occurring in the presence of a pre-existing massive black hole can produce nuclear light profiles similar to those created by slow adiabatic growth [Stiavelli et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...495L..91S/abstract). Concurrently, isolated VDI-driven clumps can sink and merge to form intermediate-mass black holes, which subsequently undergo rapid nuclear accretion to grow into SMBHs of $\sim 10^7 \ M_\odot$. Remarkably, these overmassive black holes can account for $>10\%$ of the host galaxy's stellar mass at $z > 6$, matching recent high-redshift observations of ultra-compact systems [Mayer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241100670M/abstract). 

Importantly, unlike standard [[early-galaxy-formation|direct-collapse black hole models]] that often require pristine, metal-free environments to prevent gas fragmentation, instability-driven nuclear accretion can produce SMBHs even in gas with solar metallicity. The extreme accretion rates and turbulent velocities naturally generated by the instability efficiently suppress fragmentation in the nuclear disk, allowing direct collapse scenarios to operate in more chemically evolved protogalactic environments [Mayer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019RPPh...82a6901M/abstract). At smaller nuclear scales, these accretion processes exhibit complex dynamics: thermal instabilities in relativistic slim disks can lead to dramatic observed flux changes and occultation effects in high-accretion AGN [Kawata et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PASJ...58..477K/abstract). Furthermore, disk tearing instabilities in warped accretion flows can explain quasi-periodic eruptions and extreme variability in AGN, such as those observed in GSN 069 [Raj et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...82R/abstract). Ultimately, the energy injected back into the galaxy via positive AGN feedback can trigger secondary star formation bursts that coexist with conventional negative feedback to intricately shape the scaling relations between black holes and their host bulges [Contini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..274...41C/abstract).

## Stellar Feedback and the Debate on Clump Lifetimes

High-redshift gas-rich disks frequently undergo violent disk instability, fragmenting into massive, kiloparsec-scale clumps containing $10^8 - 10^9 M_\odot$ of gas and stars. The ultimate fate of these giant clumps constitutes a major unresolved debate in galaxy evolution: do they survive long enough to migrate to the galactic center and build [[clump-origin-bulges|Clump-origin bulges]], or are they rapidly dispersed by stellar feedback?

The rapid dispersal scenario posits that intense star formation drives feedback strong enough to unbind the clumps within a few dynamical times ($\lesssim 50$ Myr). However, theoretical modeling demonstrates that the effectiveness of radiation pressure in unbinding clumps depends critically on the [[high-star-formation-efficiency|local star formation efficiency]]. If clumps convert only a few percent of their gas mass into stars per free-fall time—a rate consistent with local [[star-forming-regions-and-cores|Star-Forming Regions and Cores]]—radiation pressure alone is insufficient to disrupt them. Significant gas expulsion and rapid disruption can only occur if the clumps convert the majority of their mass into stars over a few free-fall times [Krumholz et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..112K/abstract). Even under the rapid dispersal scenario, the galactic environment maintains a steady state; there is a dynamic equilibrium between rapid cloud formation driven by Toomre instability and disruption by stellar feedback, which explains how large gas fractions can persist globally despite the short lifespans of individual molecular clouds [Mac Low et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...847L..10M/abstract).

Conversely, the long-lived clump paradigm argues that giant clumps can survive for hundreds of millions of years (roughly a galactic orbital time, $\sim 300$ Myr) [Bournaud et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...780...57B/abstract). High-resolution simulations incorporating photoionization, radiation pressure, and supernovae show that feedback successfully drives steady [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]]. The strength of these winds is often parametrized by the mass loading factor, defined as the ratio of the mass outflow rate to the star formation rate:

$$ \eta = \frac{\dot{M}_{\rm out}}{\text{SFR}} $$

In giant clumps, radiation pressure and supernovae yield mass loading factors of $\eta \gtrsim 1$, meaning that the clumps expel gas at a rate comparable to or slightly exceeding their star formation rate [Bournaud et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...780...57B/abstract). 

Despite this continuous mass loss and the concurrent tidal stripping of older stellar populations, the clumps can avoid total disruption. Because they are embedded in highly turbulent, gas-rich disks, the clumps are able to continuously reaccrete cold gas from their surroundings. This dynamic equilibrium allows them to replenish their lost mass, remaining gas-dominated and surviving long enough to undergo [[clump-migration|Clump migration]] to the central nuclear region. This migration drives [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]] and fuels the rapid growth of [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] [Bournaud et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..355B/abstract).

Observational evidence from the early universe adds further nuance to this debate. The discovery of a primordial, globally unstable disk at $z \approx 6.1$ containing at least 15 dense clumps suggests that, in some early environments, relatively weak feedback allows for prolific clump formation and extended survival [Fujimoto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1553F/abstract). Ultimately, the global distribution, size scaling, and lifetime of clumps in a turbulent disk remain highly sensitive to the exact physical prescriptions for stellar feedback. Because models utilizing different feedback efficiencies yield drastically different clump survival rates, the tension between rapid dispersal and long-term survival remains an active area of astrophysical research [Ali et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874..170A/abstract).

## VDI vs. Secular Evolution and Galaxy Mergers

Galaxy evolution is broadly governed by three distinct dynamical regimes: violent disk instability (VDI), [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]], and [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]]. While all three mechanisms drive [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]] and the growth of central mass concentrations, they operate on vastly different timescales and are triggered by completely different physical conditions.

In the local Universe, isolated disk galaxies typically evolve via secular evolution. This process is slow, quasi-adiabatic, and driven by weak, non-axisymmetric instabilities such as [[outer-bar|bars]] and spiral density waves [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). In this regime, the interstellar medium is largely self-regulated, maintaining a state of marginal gravitational stability with a Toomre parameter $Q \approx 1$. Over timescales of several gigayears, secular processes slowly redistribute angular momentum, driving moderate gas inflows that assemble [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]]. These bulges often exhibit boxy or [[peanut-formation|peanut-shaped morphologies]] as a result of the vertical buckling instabilities of central bars [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract), [Kwak et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839...24K/abstract). Indeed, it has long been recognized that bars formed during such instability are subject to vertical buckling through the fire-hose instability, which creates these peanut-shaped morphologies and reduces bar strength [Raha et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991Natur.352..411R/abstract). For low-mass and bulgeless disk galaxies, a modern two-parameter bar formation criterion involving both the Toomre $Q$ parameter and a dimensionless wavelength parameter is now used to accurately predict such internal instabilities [Jang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250907353J/abstract). Furthermore, bending instabilities (fire-hose type) acting in the vertical direction are responsible for maintaining marginal stability and generating persistent warps in outer disks [Pfenniger et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..307P/abstract). Observationally, these secular and instability-driven disk heating mechanisms across the Hubble sequence are tightly constrained by stellar kinematics, with the ratio of vertical to radial velocity dispersion ($\sigma_z/\sigma_R$) generally measured to be $0.5\text{--}0.8$ [Shapiro et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....126.2707S/abstract).

By contrast, VDI dominates in the high-redshift ($z > 1$) Universe, where galactic gas fractions are significantly higher. Rather than maintaining marginal stability, these early, turbulent disks can reach highly sub-critical states with $Q \approx 0.2 - 0.3$ [Fujimoto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1553F/abstract). This drives a rapid, non-adiabatic fragmentation of the disk into massive star-forming clumps ($10^8 - 10^9 M_\odot$). Through [[clump-migration|Clump migration]] and intense turbulence, VDI can drive massive radial inflows that assemble [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] in less than $10^9$ years [Saha et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862L..12S/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...371..713L/abstract). Notably, there is emerging tension in the literature regarding the exact origins of some local structures: numerical simulations indicate that some exponential "pseudo-bulges", traditionally assumed to be the products of slow secular evolution, may actually be the rapid relics of high-redshift VDI starbursts [Okamoto et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..718O/abstract).

VDI also contrasts sharply with external drivers of evolution, such as [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]]. Historically, hierarchical models and standard textbook paradigms have attributed the formation of classical bulges and [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|early-type galaxies]] almost entirely to the tidal torques of major mergers, which can funnel $\sim 10^9 M_\odot$ of gas into the central parsecs to fuel nuclear starbursts [Hernquist et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989Natur.340..687H/abstract), [Barnes et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...370L..65B/abstract). However, it should be noted that some dynamically "hot" or counter-rotating star-forming disk galaxies, initially thought to be in steady evolutionary phases, are often transient states directly induced by mergers and external interactions [Lu et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503..726L/abstract). In these external scenarios, counter-rotating disks can form via gas-rich dwarf mergers or adiabatic retrograde gas infall without destroying pre-existing thin primary disks [Thakar et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PASP..109..934T/abstract). In massive merger scenarios, large-scale shocks can induce star formation enhancement over a much wider distribution than predicted by simple density-dependent models [Chien et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhDT........19C/abstract). Moreover, merger-induced bars can undergo vertical buckling instabilities to form the X-shaped structures characteristic of many S0 galaxies [Mihos et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...447L..87M/abstract). Following major mergers, violent relaxation rapidly smooths isophotal irregularities, making tidal debris detectable for only a few hundred megayears at intermediate redshifts [Mihos et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...438L..75M/abstract).

Nevertheless, the VDI paradigm demonstrates that massive central spheroids can form *in situ* without any external triggering. For instance, isolated, gas-rich disks undergoing VDI can rapidly evolve into lenticular (S0) galaxies natively. This challenges the classical view that S0 galaxies are exclusively the remnants of mergers or environmental processes, such as [[intracluster-and-intragroup-phenomena|ram-pressure stripping]], operating in dense clusters [Saha et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862L..12S/abstract). To mitigate complete disk destruction, the continuous recycling of baryons through long-term stellar mass loss serves as a regulatory mechanism that supports disk survival against instability-driven destruction and lowers final bulge-to-disk ratios [Martig et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract). Furthermore, gas expelled by both severe instabilities and major mergers can be recycled in the intergalactic medium to fuel new generations of [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Tidal Dwarf Galaxies]] [Duc et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002Ap&SS.281..347D/abstract).

Ultimately, the relative importance of these three mechanisms shifts across cosmic time. VDI acts as the primary driver of rapid [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]] and bulge assembly in early, highly turbulent protogalaxies. Concurrently, major mergers dictate the assembly of the most massive ellipticals and trigger the most extreme local starbursts. As cosmic gas accretion wanes and gas fractions drop, disks settle into the stable, self-regulated $Q \approx 1$ state, allowing secular evolution to govern the late-stage morphological refinement of modern spiral galaxies.

## Historical Development of the VDI Paradigm

Even before modern computational frameworks, early theoretical foundations explored how self-gravity affected fluid disk structure, including studies of the resonant 'negative mass instability' in fluid disk models and its role in bringing disks to a marginally stable state [Lovelace et al. (1978)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1978ApJ...221...51L/abstract). Historically, the structural transformation of galaxies into bulge-dominated systems was framed by two competing frameworks: [[monolithic-collapse|Monolithic collapse]] and hierarchical assembly. Under the standard hierarchical paradigm, classical [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] and elliptical galaxies were primarily understood as the remnants of violent major mergers. In these traditional scenarios, tidal forces disrupt pre-existing [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] and funnel large amounts of gas into the nucleus, triggering intense starbursts (e.g., [Hernquist et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989Natur.340..687H/abstract), [Barnes et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...370L..65B/abstract)). Conversely, smaller, rotationally supported [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] were attributed to the slow [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]], driven by internal non-axisymmetric structures such as bars.

This strict dichotomy was challenged in the early 2000s by the advent of high-resolution cosmological simulations and 3D chemodynamical models. Simulated disks revealed that when cooling is highly efficient, high-redshift gas-rich disks become globally unstable well before a stellar bar can form [Immeli et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004A&A...413..547I/abstract). Instead of evolving secularly, these extreme disks undergo violent fragmentation into massive clumps of gas and stars ($10^8 - 10^9 M_\odot$). Gravitational torques cause these massive clumps to migrate inward over a few dynamical times, ultimately coalescing at the galactic center to rapidly form a bulge.

Theoretical predictions of violent disk instability (VDI) were soon corroborated by deep extragalactic surveys. HST observations of star-forming galaxies at $z > 1$ revealed that a large fraction possessed highly irregular, [[clumpy-galaxies|Clumpy galaxies]] morphologies dominated by giant star-forming clumps, a stark contrast to the smooth spiral arms typical of the local universe. Subsequently, studies connecting observed clump sizes to turbulent kinematics and high gas fractions using high-resolution observations of rare local analogs (the DYNAMO sample) validated predictions derived from VDI theory, explicitly linking macroscopic disk properties to local gravitational instability criteria [Fisher et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839L...5F/abstract).

As the VDI paradigm solidified, it fundamentally altered models of [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]]. It established a robust mechanism for rapid *in situ* bulge growth that bypasses the need for major mergers [Bournaud et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..355B/abstract). This theoretical shift is supported by recent submillimeter observations showing massive bulges residing in regularly rotating disks just 1.2 billion years after the Big Bang, a timescale too short for standard merger-driven or secular growth channels to operate [Lelli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...371..713L/abstract). Furthermore, JWST observations have pushed the VDI epoch even earlier, detecting primordial rotating disks at $z \approx 6$ composed of over 15 dense clumps. These early disks operate in an extreme, highly unstable regime where the Toomre parameter $Q \approx 0.2-0.3$ [Fujimoto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1553F/abstract). The subsequent evolution of the paradigm has increasingly focused on refining the role of stellar feedback and resolving whether these clumps survive long enough to build the bulge or are rapidly disrupted.

## Observational Evidence and Local Analogs

Observational confirmation of violent disk instability (VDI) relies primarily on detecting massive, star-forming clumps in gas-rich [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] at high redshifts ($z > 1$). Recent JWST results have pushed these detections into the epoch of cosmic dawn. For instance, observations of a strongly lensed, low-luminosity galaxy at $z = 6.072$ reveal a primordial rotating disk containing at least 15 dense star-forming clumps [Fujimoto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1553F/abstract). These clumps, which exhibit effective radii of $10\text{--}60\text{ pc}$, dominate approximately 70% of the galaxy's ultraviolet flux. The kinematics and cold gas emission in such early disks indicate a gravitationally unstable state characterized by extreme [[cold-gas-surface-density|gas surface densities]] ($\Sigma_{\text{gas}} \sim 10^3\text{--}10^5 M_\odot\text{ pc}^{-2}$) and highly sub-critical stability parameters [Fujimoto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1553F/abstract).

The onset of VDI is governed by the Toomre stability parameter, which for a single-fluid disk is defined as:
$$ Q = \frac{\kappa \sigma}{\pi G \Sigma} $$
where $\kappa$ is the epicyclic frequency, $\sigma$ is the velocity dispersion, and $\Sigma$ is the surface density. While theoretical models often assume disks self-regulate to $Q \approx 1$, high-redshift observations frequently yield values of $Q \approx 0.2\text{--}0.3$, suggesting that these early disks exist in violently unstable regimes [Fujimoto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1553F/abstract). Conversely, in the local universe, galaxies like M51 serve as classic examples where the Toomre $Q$ parameter robustly self-regulates around unity along the spiral arms, demonstrating stable self-regulation via disk instabilities [Hitschfeld et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...495..795H/abstract). However, localized extreme conditions still exist nearby; ALMA observations of the dense gas in NGC 7469 confirm that dense gas tracers can successfully identify highly sub-critical $Q$ regions corresponding precisely to sites of intense, active star cluster formation [Fathi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806L..34F/abstract). Furthermore, submillimeter observations of galaxies like ALESS 073.1 at $z \simeq 5$ demonstrate the rapid assembly of a massive central bulge within a regularly rotating cold gas disk only 1.2 billion years after the Big Bang [Lelli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...371..713L/abstract). This structural configuration is consistent with the rapid clump migration predicted by VDI rather than the slow [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] typically observed in the local universe.

Because high-redshift observations are often limited by spatial resolution and sensitivity, researchers rely on rare local analogs to study the detailed physics of [[clumpy-disks|Clumpy disks]]. The DYnamics of Newly Assembled Massive Objects (DYNAMO) sample consists of nearby, highly turbulent disk galaxies whose properties are closely matched to main-sequence galaxies at $z \sim 1.5\text{--}2$ [Fisher et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839L...5F/abstract). High-resolution imaging and IFU kinematics of the DYNAMO sample allow for the precise measurement of kpc-scale H-alpha clumps within these actively star-forming disks. 

Observations of these local analogs have successfully verified the clump size scaling relations predicted by VDI theory. The normalized mean sizes of the H-alpha clumps correlate linearly with both the host galaxy's cold gas fraction and its ratio of velocity dispersion to rotation velocity ($\sigma/v$) [Fisher et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839L...5F/abstract). Two-point correlation estimators applied to the DYNAMO sample further confirm that these characteristic instability scales are governed by the self-gravity of the turbulent gas, even when considering the complex, disruptive effects of stellar feedback [Ali et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874..170A/abstract). These findings provide a direct empirical link between resolved clump sizes and the theoretical framework of in-situ gravitational fragmentation, validating VDI as a primary driver of [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] and early morphological transformation. Furthermore, in dwarf galaxies such as NGC 2915 and NGC 1705, star formation occurs in extended H I disks that appear gravitationally stable until comprehensive multi-fluid models incorporating stellar and dark matter components are correctly applied [Elson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....143....1E/abstract). Beyond the DYNAMO sample, other local examples such as NGC 1068 demonstrate that stellar-driven gravitational instabilities can sometimes overcome AGN feedback to sustain vigorous starburst activity across the central kiloparsec [Romeo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.2360R/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the paradigm of violent disk instability (VDI) successfully explains the morphology and kinematics of high-redshift [[clumpy-disks|Clumpy disks]], several theoretical and observational tensions remain unresolved. A primary open question concerns the ultimate fate of massive star-forming clumps and their role in early galaxy assembly. Theoretical models sharply disagree on clump survival times: some studies argue that [[high-star-formation-efficiency|High star formation efficiency]] and subsequent stellar feedback disrupt these clumps within a few dynamical times ($\sim 50$ Myr) [Krumholz et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..112K/abstract), while others suggest that they survive for hundreds of millions of years, reaccreting gas from the turbulent disk and completing their [[clump-migration|Clump migration]] to the galactic center to build [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] [Bournaud et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...780...57B/abstract). Resolving this debate relies heavily on overcoming resolution effects in cosmological and hydrodynamic simulations, as under-resolved multi-phase interstellar medium (ISM) physics and feedback prescriptions can artificially alter clump evolution [Ali et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874..170A/abstract). Furthermore, cosmological simulations indicate that the energetic coupling of infalling gas streams can delay star formation, further complicating our understanding of clump disruption and survival [Gabor et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.437L..56G/abstract).

Another major theoretical uncertainty is the assumption of marginal stability. Traditional stability analyses assume that self-regulating feedback maintains a quasi-universal Toomre parameter of $Q \approx 1$ [Romeo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480L..23R/abstract). However, both recent analytical models and high-redshift observations challenge this universally regulated state. For instance, observations of a primordial disk at $z=6.072$ reveal a highly gravitationally unstable state with $Q \approx 0.2-0.3$ and extreme gas surface densities [Fujimoto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1553F/abstract). The mechanisms that allow galaxies to maintain such sub-critical equilibrium states without immediately exhausting their gas reservoirs require refined models of turbulent scaling laws and multi-component disk coupling [Forbes et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230207823F/abstract).

The exact transition mechanism from VDI-driven growth to quenched galaxies also remains an active area of research. While VDI is known to drive rapid central mass accumulation, it is unclear precisely when and how the resulting bulges kinematically heat the disk and stabilize the gas against further fragmentation. Massive central spheroids can increase the gas velocity dispersion, suppressing star formation and producing quiescent systems that fall below the main sequence [Gensior et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.495..199G/abstract). Yet intriguingly, recent studies have shown that massive cold gas disks residing in these early-type, nominally 'quiescent' galaxies still follow standard Kennicutt-Schmidt laws, with their star formation primarily regulated by local Toomre instabilities despite their globally quenched classification [Jiang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220803735J/abstract). Determining the relative contributions of VDI versus slow [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] and [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] across cosmic time will be crucial for mapping the full Hubble sequence evolution.

Future directions must also address the impact of non-gravitational forces on instability scales, particularly the role of magnetic fields. Magnetic fields may further complicate the fragmentation process; the cooperative action of the Parker and Jeans instabilities can facilitate the formation of large-scale H I superclouds and vertical filamentary structures [Lee et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...734..101L/abstract). 

Finally, the connection between VDI and the rapid assembly of [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] remains a promising frontier. Standard direct collapse models often require pristine gas; however, extreme gas inflows in massive compact disks could facilitate the direct collapse of early black holes even in environments with solar metallicity [Mayer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019RPPh...82a6901M/abstract), [Mayer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241100670M/abstract). Whether VDI-induced gas transport alone can fully explain the observed masses of the earliest active galactic nuclei is a critical open question for upcoming high-resolution surveys.
