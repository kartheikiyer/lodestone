## Article

**Disc regrowth** refers to the process by which a galaxy reconstitutes a rotationally supported stellar or gaseous disc following a [[galaxy-merger-dynamics-and-evolution|major merger]]. Within the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], massive galaxies grow hierarchically through a continuous sequence of [[galaxy-accretion|mass accretion]] and frequent mergers. Historically, classical models posited that major mergers violently relax collisionless stellar populations, inevitably destroying fragile [[disk-galaxies|discs]] and leaving behind early-type, spheroidal remnants. However, modern high-resolution cosmological simulations and deep observations have driven a paradigm shift: if an interaction is sufficiently gas-rich (a "[[luminous-gas-rich-disc-galaxies|wet" merger]]"), the dissipative, collisional gas can survive the disruption or rapidly re-accrete. Because this gas radiates away its shock-heated kinetic energy while conserving its angular momentum, it rapidly cools and settles into a newly formed rotational plane [Governato et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398..312G/abstract). This mechanism naturally explains the observed ubiquity of late-type spiral galaxies in the local Universe, demonstrating that the Hubble sequence is a dynamic evolutionary track rather than a static consequence of initial formation.

The continuous fueling required for successful disc regrowth relies on the delicate balance of multiple baryonic reservoirs. External [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] from the circumgalactic medium—via both pristine cold flows along the cosmic web and the delayed cooling of a shock-heated hot corona—provides the primary reservoir of raw material. This external supply is significantly bolstered by internal baryonic recycling; the steady return of gas from evolved low- and intermediate-mass stars retains the specific angular momentum of the pre-existing disc, buffering the system against complete morphological destruction and facilitating prolonged star formation [Martig et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract). As this gas accretes and cools, it typically drives an inside-out pattern of [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]], wherein galaxies progressively settle from turbulent, vertically extended states into mature thin discs embedded within older thick discs. Through this ongoing [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]], pre-existing compact spheroids (such as high-redshift "red nuggets") or merger-built cores can be effectively "cloaked" by the newly assembled disc, surviving to the present day as the central bulges of massive spirals [Hon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3410H/abstract).

Following the initial reassembly of the rotational plane, the galaxy's structural maturation is largely governed by the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]. Internal dynamical instabilities, such as [[double-barred-galaxies|stellar bars]] and [[stationary-density-wave|spiral density waves]], redistribute mass and angular momentum over billions of years, slowly building dense central [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] that are kinematically and photometrically distinct from classical merger-built bulges [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract), [Zhang et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518..613Z/abstract). While the mechanics of disc regrowth are now a cornerstone in contemporary models of [[galaxy-merger-dynamics-and-evolution|Galaxy Merger Dynamics and Evolution]], several critical open questions remain. Active research seeks to determine the exact thresholds at which [[galactic-nuclear-activity-sequence|active galactic nucleus (AGN)]] feedback permanently quenches the gas supply necessary for regrowth, the efficiency of radial migration versus satellite accretion in vertically heating thick discs, and the enduring cosmological conundrum of how giant, purely bulgeless Disk Galaxies remain so prevalent in field environments despite a universe dominated by hierarchical merging.

## Historical Paradigm Shift

Prior to the advent of high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations, models of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] viewed major collisions almost exclusively as catastrophic events for rotationally supported systems. Early descriptions of the Galaxy's collapse from an extended halo to a [[freeman-stellar-disk|flattened disc]] were documented via metal-poor subdwarf stars [Gehren et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982Msngr..27...22G/abstract). Early numerical simulations demonstrated that the violent relaxation accompanying these interactions readily disrupts the ordered kinematics of the progenitors. Phenomena such as "[[galaxy-destruction-processes|galaxy shredding]]," where a spiral's disc is captured by a massive companion, leaving the progenitor's bulge as a [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|dwarf elliptical]], emphasized the destructive nature of interactions [Thomson et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992MNRAS.257..689T/abstract). Additionally, it is recognized that radially anisotropic dark matter haloes can accelerate orbit decay and produce [[violent-disk-instability|violent interactions]] during mergers [Umiamaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250816854U/abstract). Because stellar discs are dynamically fragile, disk destruction was considered an inevitable outcome, yielding spheroidal merger remnants that closely resemble present-day elliptical galaxies. Under this classical paradigm, a galaxy's position along the Hubble sequence was interpreted as a static end-product of its merger history: late-type spirals represent systems that successfully avoided major interactions, while early-type ellipticals represent the end-products of violent collisions.

This purely destructive view of [[galaxy-merger-dynamics-and-evolution|Galaxy Merger Dynamics and Evolution]], however, encountered a significant theoretical challenge: the high merger rates predicted by hierarchical clustering seemingly contradict the overwhelming abundance of massive, disc-dominated galaxies observed in the local Universe. While spiral structure became observably more chaotic at $z > 0.5$, the overall number density of [[luminous-gas-rich-disc-galaxies|luminous discs]] has remained relatively conserved since $z = 1$ [Abraham et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..275...89A/abstract). A paradigm shift emerged as advances in simulations revealed that major mergers do not exclusively produce elliptical galaxies. Instead, under the right physical conditions, a violent merger can serve as a precursor to the reformation of a massive stellar disc. 

The critical factor determining the survival or recreation of a disc is the availability and accretion of gas. During "wet" major mergers, tidal forces funnel gas into the central regions, triggering [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|starbursts]] and fueling the growth of a central supermassive black hole. While the pre-existing stellar populations of the progenitors are violently relaxed into a central spheroidal component, any remaining or newly accreted gas that retains significant angular momentum can settle into a new rotational plane [Peschken et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1375P/abstract). As the regrown disc builds up stellar mass through subsequent *in-situ* star formation, the initial spheroidal merger remnant is gradually embedded, effectively becoming the central classical bulge of an early-type spiral or lenticular galaxy. Cosmological simulations demonstrate that a large fraction of major merger remnants can successfully undergo this [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]] and evolve back into star-forming disc galaxies, provided that AGN feedback does not permanently quench the system's gas supply [Sparre et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.3946S/abstract). This recognition of disc regrowth has fundamentally altered the understanding of [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]]. Contrary to previous interpretations, [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|secular evolution]] is now understood to drive a unidirectional structural progression from late-type, disc-dominated galaxies to early-type, bulge-dominated galaxies over cosmic time [Zhang et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518..613Z/abstract).

## Mechanisms of Gas Accretion and Cooling

Following a disruptive event, the reassembly of a rotating stellar disc requires a continuous replenishment of baryons. This disc regrowth is critically dependent on [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] from the circumgalactic medium and the broader cosmic web [Governato et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398..312G/abstract). Gas infall generally proceeds via two primary channels: direct cold gas accretion along cosmic filaments, and the gradual cooling of a shock-heated hot corona. Hierarchical [[multi-stage-formation|formation histories]] produce discs with properties governed primarily by the infalling gas supply from the [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|hot halo]] rather than specific star formation laws [Stringer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.382..641S/abstract). Furthermore, variations in star formation histories along the Hubble sequence can be regulated by a simple sequence of cold gas accretion rates [Kampakoglou et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.380..646K/abstract).

The dominant mode of accretion is strongly influenced by the host galaxy's mass and the epoch of halo virialization. In lower-mass halos and at higher redshifts, cold gas accretion allows pristine gas from the cosmic web to penetrate deep into the central regions without being shock-heated to the virial temperature. Cold accretion serves as a vital alternative to merger-driven growth, particularly in resolving the angular momentum problem in early discs [Shlosman et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book..555S/abstract). As halos grow more massive and undergo halo virialization, infalling gas is shock-heated, creating a hot coronal reservoir from which gas subsequently cools and settles into the disc [Governato et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398..312G/abstract). The critical threshold surface density ($\Sigma_c$) for [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|star formation]] is intrinsically linked to the regulation of this gas supply by halo infall [Kauffmann et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.281..475K/abstract). There is an active debate in the literature regarding the exact structural imprint of these two accretion modes. It has been hypothesized that galactic thick discs assemble rapidly through the direct accretion of cold gas, whereas thin discs form more gradually from the delayed cooling of shock-heated gas in high-mass halos [Noguchi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494L..37N/abstract). 

To rebuild an extended structure, the accreting gas must carry specific angular momentum that increases over cosmic time, naturally driving an inside-out pattern of [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]] [Mollá et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.1329M/abstract). Observations of [[structural-transformation|metallicity gradients]] in local spirals demonstrate that this growth is incompatible with gas flowing purely radially inward from the outer edges. Instead, chemical and dynamical models dictate that gas accretes directly onto the disc surface from the circumgalactic medium, rotating at a velocity approximately $20-30\%$ lower than the local circular speed [Fraternali et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181108432F/abstract). 

Observational constraints on these vast gaseous reservoirs provide a quantitative basis for the potential of extended disc fueling. In the Milky Way, high-resolution X-ray spectroscopy has identified a Warm Ionized Metal Medium (WIMM) permeating the galaxy, containing a total (disc + halo) mass $M_{\rm WIMM} \simeq 8.2 \times 10^9 M_\odot$ at a temperature $T \simeq 2900 \pm 900$ K [Nicastro et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457..676N/abstract). Extended gaseous haloes, such as the one observed in NGC 891, serve as case studies for reservoirs that lag behind the main stellar disc in rotational velocity [Fraternali et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASSP....3..271F/abstract). As gas accumulates, the atomic-to-molecular gas ratio in discs evolves over time as a function of stellar mass and gas surface density [Fu et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.2701F/abstract). 

Ultimately, the structure of the regrown disc is determined by a self-regulating dynamical equilibrium. The thermodynamic equilibrium of a relaxed disc is characterized by a lognormal surface density distribution [Marr et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Galax...8...12M/abstract). At any given galactocentric radius, the continuous supply of gas is balanced against the local star formation rate, the expulsion of mass via galactic winds, and the radial transport of gas driven by gravitational instabilities [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.1552F/abstract). This balance, however, is rarely perfectly steady. The slow timescales of secular gas accretion from the outer disc strongly contrast with the episodic, rapid mass gains triggered by merging with small companions [Combes et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.265..417C/abstract). These variable geometries and rates of gas accretion from the cosmic web can drive galaxies through long-term cycles of quenching and [[rejuvenation-episodes|Rejuvenation episodes]] on gigayear timescales [Fortun\'e et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320858F/abstract).

## The Role of Internal Stellar Mass Loss

The continuous growth and reconstruction of galactic discs rely not only on the external accretion of gas from the circumgalactic medium but also on the internal recycling of baryons. While the hierarchical assembly of galaxies frequently subjects them to disruptive interactions that drive [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]] toward spheroid-dominated systems, the long-term return of gas from evolved stellar populations provides a crucial buffering mechanism that facilitates disc survival and regrowth [Martig et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract). 

In the standard [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|baryon cycle]], newly formed stellar populations initially inject substantial energy and mass into the interstellar medium via [[puffing-astrophysics|supernova feedback]]. Over cosmological timescales, however, the bulk of the recycled gas mass is not dominated by supernovae, but rather by the slow, continuous mass loss from low- and intermediate-mass stars (e.g., via stellar winds and planetary nebulae). For a single stellar population, approximately $50\%$ of the initial stellar mass is gradually returned to the gas phase over a $10 \text{ Gyr}$ timescale [Martig et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract). 

This steady trickle of recycled gas fundamentally alters the structural evolution of massive galaxies. Cosmological simulations have historically struggled to reproduce the observed abundance of massive, late-type spiral galaxies, as frequent mergers naturally deplete angular momentum and build classical bulges. The inclusion of continuous stellar mass loss alleviates this tension. Because the gas shed by evolved stars inherently possesses the kinematic memory (specific angular momentum) of the stellar orbits from which it originated, it can readily cool and dissipate energy to re-settle into a rotationally supported thin disc. This internal gas supply fuels [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|star formation]] long after external cold flows may have been disrupted or shock-heated. 

Consequently, stellar mass loss serves as a powerful regulator of the central bulge. By continually replenishing the rotating gaseous reservoir, internal gas return promotes the extended growth of the disc while starving the central spheroid of the rapid, low-angular-momentum gas inflows required for its growth. Models demonstrate that this persistent recycling process can suppress the final bulge-to-disc ($B/T$) ratio of a massive galaxy by a factor of up to 3, directly enabling the survival of disc-dominated (Sb-Sc) morphologies in environments where pure dissipationless dynamics would otherwise yield lenticular or elliptical remnants [Martig et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract). 

While gas-rich major mergers can independently reform discs through the rapid accretion of dispersed halo gas [Governato et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398..312G/abstract), the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] driven by stellar mass loss ensures that even isolated or post-merger remnants possess a persistent, internal mechanism for disc regrowth. Independent metallicity enhancement processes in the thin and thick discs can be approximated by different infall and star formation rates acting during these secular phases [Strobel et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991A&A...247...35S/abstract). The balance between this slow baryonic return, external accretion, and active galactic nucleus (AGN) quenching ultimately dictates whether a galaxy successfully rebuilds its disc or remains a quenched spheroid.

## Post-Merger Disc Reformation in 'Wet' Mergers

Classical theories posited that [[galaxy-merger-dynamics-and-evolution|major mergers]] inevitably destroy the fragile [[freeman-stellar-disk|stellar discs]] of the progenitor galaxies. Through violent relaxation, collisionless stars are scattered, typically resulting in an early-type, spheroidal remnant. However, recent cosmological simulations have driven a paradigm shift, demonstrating that gas-rich major mergers can bypass this fate and produce disc-dominated remnants [Governato et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398..312G/abstract), [Sparre et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.3946S/abstract), [Peschken et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1375P/abstract).

The physical mechanism underlying this morphological resurrection is fundamentally divided between the collisionless stars and the collisional interstellar medium. During a wet major merger, the old stellar populations of the progenitor galaxies are kinematically heated and scattered into an ellipsoidal configuration, forming the classical bulge of the new [[post-merger-galaxies|Post-merger galaxies]]. In contrast, the gaseous component undergoes dissipative collapse. Because the gas can radiate away its shock-heated kinetic energy while conserving angular momentum, it rapidly settles into a newly reformed, rotating planar configuration [Peschken et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1375P/abstract). Disc regrowth is therefore considered a non-negligible component of the evolution of bulge-dominated galaxies in $\Lambda$CDM models [De Lucia et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.1439D/abstract). New stars subsequently form from this settled gas, assembling an extended thin disc around the merger-built central bulge.

The continuous supply and retention of gas are the critical factors determining whether a post-merger system will undergo this type of [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]]. Using the Illustris simulation, researchers have tracked the explicit origin of post-merger discs, finding that they are built gradually from young stars forming out of gas that was temporarily dispersed into the halo during the merger and later re-accreed [Peschken et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1375P/abstract). Findings from the VINTERGATAN simulation demonstrate that major mergers trigger [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|bursty star formation]] phases once coherent rotational motion outweighs turbulence [Segovia Otero et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.516.2272S/abstract). High-resolution cosmological hydrodynamical simulations, such as TNG50, further resolve the complex kinematic structures of these remnants, showing that reformed discs exhibit structural parameters (e.g., scale lengths and vertical flaring) that closely match those of observed Milky Way-like spiral galaxies [Sotillo-Ramos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3915S/abstract). Quantitative studies of specific simulated wet mergers at $z \sim 0.8$ yield highly realistic synthetic late-type galaxies by $z = 0$, possessing $i$-band bulge-to-disc ratios of $\sim 0.65$ and extended disc scale lengths on the order of $7.2 \text{ kpc}$ [Governato et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398..312G/abstract). 

Despite the success of these models, the literature acknowledges a distinct tension regarding the universality of post-merger disc reformation, specifically concerning the role of feedback. While major mergers are a viable pathway for [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]], the survival of the regrown disc is highly sensitive to AGN feedback. If the central supermassive black hole accretes efficiently during the merger, the resulting thermal and kinetic feedback can permanently quench the system by heating the halo and halting the cooling of the circumgalactic medium. Without a sufficient continuous gas accretion and cooling rate to overcome this feedback-driven outflow and heating, the galaxy is starved of the cold gas necessary to rebuild the disc, ultimately yielding a red, passive elliptical galaxy instead [Sparre et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.3946S/abstract). Consequently, the final morphological state depends on highly specific mass and environmental thresholds; early-type galaxies can be rejuvenated by "wet" mergers, particularly in lower-mass environments [Grebel et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..270..335G/abstract), while nearly $60\%$ of galaxies transitioning onto the red sequence may pass through a "[[faded-spirals|passive disk]]" phase, representing an intermediate stage of morphological evolution [Bundy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...719.1969B/abstract).

## Structural Evolution: Thin vs. Thick Discs and Pseudobulges

The regrowth and maturation of galactic discs frequently produce distinct vertical and central structural components. Vertical energy ($E_z$) is often recognized as an approximate integral of motion in defining the structure of warm stellar discs [Kuijken et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.277.1341K/abstract). Observations of distant galaxies reveal that disc settlement typically proceeds in a "downsizing" sequence, where an older, vertically extended **thick disc** forms first, followed by the accretion and settling of a younger, more metal-rich [[freeman-stellar-disk|thin disc]] [Tsukui et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240915909T/abstract), [Tsukui et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540.3493T/abstract). High-mass galaxies ($10^{9.75} - 10^{11} M_\odot$) transitioned to these double-disc systems approximately $8 \text{ Gyr}$ ago, while low-mass galaxies ($10^{9.0} - 10^{9.75} M_\odot$) underwent this transition much later, around $4 \text{ Gyr}$ ago [Tsukui et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240915909T/abstract).

The exact triggering mechanisms for this structural bifurcation remain under active debate. One theoretical framework posits that thick discs assemble directly via cold gas inflows from the cosmic web, whereas thin discs condense subsequently as shock-heated gas in [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|massive halos]] cools over time [Noguchi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494L..37N/abstract). Alternatively, the transition from bursty star formation to a settled thin disc may be dictated by the deepening of the galaxy's gravitational potential; specifically, a centrally concentrated mass profile suppresses global breathing modes to allow a fragile thin disc to persist, while a sufficiently large escape velocity ($V_{\rm esc}$) traps momentum-conserving outflows, driving the transition to smooth star formation [Hopkins et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.2241H/abstract).

Following their formation, thin discs are subject to **vertical heating** and continuous structural reshaping. Interactions with satellites and dark matter subhalos kinematically heat the stellar populations, increasing vertical thicknesses over time [Ruiz-Lara et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...586A.112R/abstract). Conversely, the growth of a central stellar disc potential can significantly destroy dark matter subhaloes on small pericentric orbits [Kelley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.4409K/abstract). In cosmological simulations, this heating often manifests as **[[puffing-astrophysics|disc flaring]]**, where the vertical scale height of the stellar disc increases radially. For Milky Way-mass analogues, outer disc scale heights are typically $1.5$ to $2$ times larger than in the inner regions [Sotillo-Ramos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3915S/abstract). Internally, non-axisymmetric features like [[spiral-galaxy-dynamics|spiral arms]] promote [[structural-transformation|radial migration]]. However, there is tension in the literature regarding whether migration alone can adequately puff up a thin disc into a thick disc; some models indicate that adiabatic cooling during outward radial migration renders this mechanism too inefficient to reproduce observed thick disc heights [Minchev et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012EPJWC..1907002M/abstract). Chemically, the superposition of older migrated populations and *in-situ* young stars causes radial metallicity gradients to flatten or even invert as a function of vertical height from the mid-plane [Miranda et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...587A..10M/abstract).

In the central regions, the structural evolution of the regrowing disc is strongly tied to the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]. Global instabilities, such as stellar bars, redistribute angular momentum and drive gas toward the nucleus, where it triggers star formation. Over billions of years, this process slowly builds dense, disky central components known as **[[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]]** [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract), [Zhang et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518..613Z/abstract). In late-type, bulgeless spirals, the presence of compact [[galaxy-nuclei-and-complex-morphologies|nuclear star clusters]] suggests that these systems experience repeated cycles of nuclear star formation and gas accumulation [Böker et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...39B/abstract). The interplay between seen and unseen mass components can also drive the development of lopsidedness and offset nuclei in galaxies like the Milky Way [Weinberg et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..231...53W/abstract). Some systems like NGC 4214, a Magellanic Irregular galaxy, demonstrate that an underlying exponential disc of old stars can exist despite a chaotic blue population [Schild et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApL....24...85S/abstract). Similarly, the blue compact dwarf NGC 2915 may host a massive, dark, or faded stellar disc revealed primarily by its HI spiral structure [Quillen et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..1217Q/abstract).

Unlike classical bulges built violently through major mergers, pseudobulges retain a memory of their disc origin, exhibiting flatter shapes, rotationally supported kinematics, and nearly exponential surface brightness profiles [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract). Although secular evolution is the canonical pathway for pseudobulge formation, a pathway for S0 formation in the field has been proposed via isolated [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]], which heats the disc and grows the bulge in under $1 \text{ Gyr}$ [Saha et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862L..12S/abstract). Furthermore, there is an observational difficulty in identifying disc truncations in face-on spirals, as stellar haloes often outshine the disc at the expected break radius [Martín-Navarro et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441.2809M/abstract). High-redshift starbursts fueled by the rapid accretion of low-angular-momentum gas may also masquerade as pseudobulges, with secular processes contributing as little as $13\% - 30\%$ to the final bulge mass [Okamoto et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..718O/abstract).

## Secular Evolution and Dynamical Stability

Once a galactic disc has successfully reformed following a major merger or rapid accretion phase, it is subject to long-term internal dynamical processes. This [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] is largely decoupled from the cosmological framework and operates over billions of years, gradually rearranging the galaxy's mass and angular momentum profiles. Secular evolution along the Hubble sequence is typically viewed as a unidirectional progression from [[disk-galaxies|late-type]] (Sm) to early-type (Sa) morphologies [Pfenniger et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ASSL..209..291P/abstract).

In the early stages of disc regrowth, particularly at high redshift, the gaseous disc is often turbulent and highly susceptible to local gravitational collapse. For example, simulations of a specific high-redshift [[luminous-gas-rich-disc-galaxies|Lyman-break galaxy]] indicate a highly unstable state with $Q \simeq 0.2$ [Kohandel et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.1250K/abstract). Classical stability criteria, such as the Efstathiou, Lake, and Negroponte (ELN) criterion, often fail to accurately distinguish between bar-stable and bar-unstable discs in semi-analytic models [Athanassoula et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.390L..69A/abstract). Such low stability thresholds can trigger [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]], leading to the formation of [[clumpy-disks|massive clumps]] that migrate inward. This can include a "violent" high-redshift mode of bulge growth, where giant gas clumps migrate inward and coalesce on short timescales [Bournaud et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..355B/abstract). Over time, $Q$-regulated star formation and gas settling govern the morphological transition of the galaxy, dictating the delayed emergence of a thin disc embedded within an older, thicker component [Tsukui et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540.3493T/abstract).

As the regrown thin disc settles and its surface density increases, it frequently becomes subject to global non-axisymmetric perturbations, most notably the [[double-barred-galaxies|bar instability]]. The lifecycle of galactic bars operates as a sequence of birth, aging, and potential self-destruction that punctuates a disc's evolution [Friedli et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..187...88F/abstract). Bar pattern speeds decrease in stellar systems but can actually increase when a significant gas component is present [Athanassoula et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002Ap&SS.281...39A/abstract). Bars act as highly efficient engines for angular momentum transport, driving cold gas toward the galactic nucleus and connecting large-scale bars to mass-transfer induced activity in the circumnuclear regions of disc galaxies [Shlosman et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994mtia.conf.....S/abstract). Two-dimensional spectroscopy has been used to investigate the kinematics of central regions to shed light on how non-circular motions in bars might drive the fueling of central black holes [Fathi et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PhDT.......188F/abstract). Notably, while Seyfert galaxies often show a higher incidence of bars, their disc structural parameters are largely identical across different activity types [Xanthopoulos et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.280....6X/abstract). Furthermore, giant low surface brightness galaxies (GLSBs) can form via bar-driven radial mixing that flattens and extends an initially compact disc [Noguchi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328..353N/abstract).

In addition to bars, transient [[spiral-galaxy-dynamics|spiral arms]] drive structural changes through spiral scattering and [[structural-transformation|radial migration]]. There is an ongoing debate over spiral pattern lifetimes, noting that numerical simulations suggest recurrent, short-lived transients rather than quasi-steady modes drive faster evolution [Sellwood et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410.1637S/abstract). Spiral scattering steadily increases the in-plane random velocities of disc stars, kinematically heating the disc over time [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). Concurrently, resonant interactions with spiral arms induce radial migration, efficiently relocating stars away from their birth radii. This radial mixing flattens metallicity gradients and plays a significant role in shaping the outer surface brightness profiles of discs, contributing to the formation of both downbending (Type-II) and upbending (Type-III) disc truncations [Ruiz-Lara et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...608A.126R/abstract).

Despite the efficiency of radial migration in redistributing mass and metals, its role in vertical disc heating remains highly debated. While scattering by massive molecular clouds can redirect in-plane kinetic energy into vertical motion [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract), N-body simulations suggest that radial migration alone is insufficient to produce the scale heights characteristic of mature thick discs. As stars migrate radially outward, they undergo adiabatic cooling, which suppresses vertical thickening [Minchev et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012EPJWC..1907002M/abstract). Consequently, the presence of a pronounced thick disc in a regrown galaxy is more likely the fossil record of early chaotic assembly or subsequent satellite accretion rather than isolated secular heating. 

Ultimately, these internal secular processes dictate the long-term fate of the regrown disc, driving a temporal evolution along the Hubble sequence. Through the continuous redistribution of mass and the fading of localized star formation, late-type, gas-rich regrown discs can progressively transform into more stable, early-type disc galaxies over a Hubble time [Zhang et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...518..613Z/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..317Z/abstract).

## Observational Evidence and Chemical Signatures

To identify galaxies currently undergoing or having recently completed disc regrowth, researchers rely on a combination of chemical, kinematic, and photometric diagnostics. Because [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]] often proceeds via the continuous accretion of gas from the circumgalactic medium, chemical abundance patterns serve as a fossil record of a galaxy's assembly. [[morphological-inner-structures|Counter-rotating discs]] and the simultaneous presence of thick and thin components are key fossil tracers of past [[galaxy-accretion|mass accretion events]] [Combes et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000bgfp.conf...99C/abstract). The accretion of metal-poor gas drives inside-out star formation, establishing distinct radial metallicity gradients [Fraternali et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181108432F/abstract). However, in galaxies that have undergone substantial mergers or interaction-driven [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]], these profiles become highly complex. Scatter in the $z \sim 1$ stellar-mass Tully-Fisher relation is partially attributed to structural asymmetries caused by minor galaxy mergers [Atkinson et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0712.1316A/abstract). 

Simulations demonstrate that radial metallicity gradients can flatten or invert as a function of height above the mid-plane; this is caused by energetic feedback and the superposition of older, radially migrated populations with younger stars formed *in situ* [Miranda et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...587A..10M/abstract). Furthermore, the age-metallicity relation (AMR) in simulated discs reveals that inverted AMR trends in the outer disc can arise from a combination of radial stellar motions and star formation triggered by accreting satellites [Ruiz-Lara et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...586A.112R/abstract). In the Milky Way, the absence of Galactic open clusters older than $9 \text{ Gyr}$ points to a potential minimum in star formation activity around $10-13 \text{ Gyr}$ ago [Carraro et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999A&A...343..825C/abstract). Moreover, super-metal-rich (SMR) stars serve as a tracer of the ultimate star generation in galactic bulges [Grenon et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989Ap&SS.156...29G/abstract).

[[puffing-astrophysics|Kinematic heating]] and the age-velocity dispersion relation (AVR) provide further measures of a galaxy's dynamical assembly. The ratio of the vertical to radial velocity dispersion, $\sigma_z / \sigma_R$, is frequently utilized to identify heating agents and assess the degree of dynamical settling in [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] [Gerssen et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000MNRAS.317..545G/abstract), [Gerssen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.2726G/abstract). Modern integral field spectroscopy allows researchers to dissect edge-on systems into mono-age populations, tracking parameters like the half-mass radius ($R_{50}$) and half-mass height ($z_{50}$) over cosmic time. For instance, observations of [[s0-ring-galaxy|lenticular galaxies]] show that while older stellar populations (formed $8-10 \text{ Gyr}$ ago) exhibit increased thicknesses indicative of past major mergers, the youngest stellar populations maintain a remarkably constant and thin vertical structure. This provides direct evidence for quiescent disc regrowth post-merger [Martig et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251119632M/abstract). To map similar extended structures in the Milky Way, astronomers use old tracers—such as red clump stars—to reveal asymmetric features and warped geometries that distinguish the undisturbed disc from historically perturbed regions [Uppal et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221008206U/abstract). Deep near-infrared imaging from the EDGES survey has also highlighted surface brightness breaks that are likely linked to extended stellar haloes rather than simple disc truncations [Staudaher et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.1995S/abstract).

Perhaps the most direct observational evidence of ongoing or recently completed disc regrowth is the detection of extended ultraviolet (XUV) discs. Observations have identified XUV emission extending well beyond traditional optical star-formation thresholds in a significant fraction of both late-type and early-type (E/S0) galaxies [Moffett et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..423..346M/abstract). In early-type galaxies, which are traditionally thought to be passive, these UV-bright outer rings indicate that the systems are actively [[disc-brightening|rebuilding a stellar disc]]. Researchers classify these into different types, with "modified Type 2" XUV discs displaying elevated star formation rates and bluer far-ultraviolet to near-ultraviolet colors ($FUV - NUV$). This signature is a hallmark of inside-out disc regrowth fueled by recent [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] or the cooling of gas dispersed during a previous interaction [Moffett et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..423..346M/abstract). Ultimately, observations of XUV discs and nested mono-age populations support the conclusion drawn from cosmological simulations that major mergers do not inevitably yield permanently quenched ellipticals, but can instead foster rejuvenated, star-forming [[post-merger-galaxies|Post-merger galaxies]] [Sparre et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.3946S/abstract).

## Disc Cloaking and the Fate of Red Nuggets

The near-absence of high-redshift "red nuggets"—quiescent, compact massive spheroids—in the local Universe is a foundational constraint on models of [[ex-situ-galaxy-growth|galaxy size evolution]] [Hon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3410H/abstract). The classical paradigm assumes that these compact objects evolve primarily through "dry" major and minor mergers, undergoing an elliptical-to-elliptical (E-to-E) [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]] to become the extended cores of present-day giant elliptical galaxies. However, recent observational and theoretical work highlights a compelling alternative evolutionary channel known as "disc cloaking," wherein a compact massive spheroid undergoes a [[disc-transformations|spheroid-to-disc transformation]] via subsequent disc regrowth [Hon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3410H/abstract).

In the disc cloaking scenario, a high-redshift red nugget accretes substantial amounts of gas—either through gas-rich ("wet") mergers or continuous cold [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]]—at redshifts $1 \lesssim z \lesssim 2$. This newly acquired gas settles into a rotational plane and forms a widespread stellar disc. Rather than being destroyed or puffing up into a giant elliptical galaxy, the original red nugget is "cloaked" by the new disc, surviving intact to the present day as the central bulge of a massive spiral or lenticular galaxy [Hon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3410H/abstract). Adiabatic core regrowth can also occur even in the most massive brightest cluster galaxies (BCGs) when driven by the adiabatic growth of their central supermassive black holes [Brockamp et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...585A.153B/abstract).

Quantitative support for this mechanism comes from detailed multicomponent photometric decompositions of local massive galaxies. In a volume-limited sample of 103 massive galaxies ($M_*/\rm M_{\odot} \gtrsim 1\times 10^{11}$) within a distance of 110 Mpc, Hon et al. found that out of 28 galaxies previously classified as pure ellipticals, 18 actually host large-scale rotational discs and 2 possess intermediate-scale discs. These objects were consequently reclassified as lenticular (S0) or ellicular (ES) galaxies [Hon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3410H/abstract). By extracting the structural properties of these central components, observers have established a lower limit for the local volume number density of compact massive spheroids of $n_{\rm c, Sph} \sim (0.17 - 1.2) \times 10^{-4} \, \rm Mpc^{-3}$ [Hon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3410H/abstract). Notably, this local abundance is within an alluring factor of a few of the peak number density of high-mass red nuggets observed at $1 \lesssim z \lesssim 2$. This suggests that a large fraction of high-redshift compact galaxies may be hiding in plain sight.

Current literature indicates that disc cloaking is likely the dominant evolutionary pathway for lower-mass red nuggets, specifically those in the stellar mass range of $1\times 10^{10} < M_*/\rm M_{\odot} \lessapprox 4 \times 10^{10}$ [Hon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3410H/abstract). This process strongly aligns with the emerging consensus that major mergers do not exclusively produce elliptical galaxies. If there is a sufficient supply of gas, post-merger remnants can systematically rebuild a rotating stellar disc [Governato et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398..312G/abstract), [Peschken et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1375P/abstract). Consequently, disc cloaking provides a unified framework that links the cosmological assembly history of early compact massive spheroids with the [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]] observed in the mature, bulge-dominated disc galaxies of the local Universe.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite recent paradigm shifts acknowledging that gas-rich major mergers can lead to the regrowth of significant stellar discs, several fundamental questions regarding the physical drivers of this process remain unresolved. 

### The Bulgeless Galaxy Conundrum
A persistent challenge in standard $\Lambda$CDM [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] is the high frequency of giant [[disk-galaxies|bulgeless galaxies]] in field environments [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..431K/abstract). Because hierarchical merging and high-redshift [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]] naturally produce substantial classical bulges, the existence of massive, pure disc galaxies—often hosting only small [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] built by the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]—remains a major open question. It is unclear how so many massive galaxies either avoided significant mergers or completely cloaked early spheroid formation through highly efficient subsequent disc regrowth [Hon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3410H/abstract).

### AGN Feedback and Quenching Mechanisms
The survival and reformation of a disc heavily depend on the balance between continuous gas accretion and energetic feedback. Simulations indicate that "wet" major mergers can yield disc-dominated remnants, provided that rapid gas cooling and cold flows continuously replenish the interstellar medium [Governato et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398..312G/abstract), [Peschken et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1375P/abstract). However, the quantitative thresholds at which AGN feedback and star formation-driven winds act as quenching mechanisms are heavily debated. If AGN feedback is excessively strong, it permanently truncates the required gas supply, starving the post-merger remnant and leaving a quenched early-type galaxy [Sparre et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.3946S/abstract). Properly balancing this feedback against the continuous gas return from stellar populations [Martig et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract) to match the observed fraction of late-type galaxies is a critical theoretical hurdle.

### Simulated Disc Realism and Vertical Structure
Achieving simulated disc realism across a diverse spectrum of environments and masses poses another challenge. [[galactic-disc-models|Galactic Disc Models]] must simultaneously reproduce realistic elliptical galaxies and disc galaxies that overlap by a factor of $>1000$ in stellar mass, yet maintain structurally distinct properties [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..431K/abstract). Furthermore, the dynamical mechanisms responsible for disc thickening during the regrowth phase are contested. While some models invoke stellar radial migration as a primary heating agent, studies suggest that this process may be too inefficient due to adiabatic cooling to reproduce the observed scale heights of thick discs [Minchev et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012EPJWC..1907002M/abstract). This tension leaves the relative contributions of radial migration, external galaxy accretion, and kinematic heating unresolved.

### Early Assembly and Universal Kinematics
The discovery of fully evolved discs and bulges in galaxies existing just $1.2$ billion years after the Big Bang strongly challenges the slow assembly paradigm [Wardlow et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...371..674W/abstract). Future work must evaluate the universal bimodality in kinematic morphology, suggesting that star formation quenching occurs separately for cold disc and hot spheroid populations [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..165W/abstract). High-redshift JWST observations are revolutionizing the empirical study of early disc assembly. Recent near-infrared data trace disc structures back $\sim 10 \text{ Gyr}$, successfully identifying both thin and thick discs at cosmological distances. These observations suggest that discs settle in a pattern of [[morphological-downsizing|Morphological downsizing]], where high-mass galaxies ($10^{9.75} - 10^{11} M_{\odot}$) transition from single thick discs to double-disc systems earlier than their lower-mass counterparts [Tsukui et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240915909T/abstract), [Tsukui et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540.3493T/abstract). Future work will need to clarify whether this structural settlement and the end of bursty star formation are primarily driven by the deepening of the global gravitational potential [Hopkins et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.2241H/abstract) or by transitions in the cosmological gas accretion mode. Resolving these questions will require synthesizing deep multi-wavelength observations with next-generation [[galaxy-merger-dynamics-and-evolution|Galaxy Merger Dynamics and Evolution]] simulations.

### Dark Matter, Feedback, and Kinematic Surveys
The role of temporal evolution in disc winds is increasingly viewed as a key regulator of angular momentum and accretion feedback across all mass scales [Castro Segura et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260102028C/abstract). To resolve these mechanisms locally, upcoming high-resolution spectroscopic surveys like 4MIDABLE-HR will map the chemical and kinematic history of the Milky Way disc using millions of stars [Bensby et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Msngr.175...35B/abstract). Furthermore, recent simulations demonstrate that regrown disc sizes exhibit a positive correlation with halo spin and an anti-correlation with dark matter concentration [Liang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240314749L/abstract). Additionally, the potential for using gravitational lensing cross-sections in disc galaxies to test Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) presents an intriguing alternative to dark matter models [Harvey-Hawes et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241117888H/abstract).
