The process of stellar mass assembly tracks the [[integrated-star-formation|Integrated star formation]] within galaxies across cosmic time, serving as a fundamental pillar in the study of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. Galaxies grow their stellar mass through two primary channels: *in-situ* star formation from the cooling of interstellar gas, and *ex-situ* [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] via [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] and minor interactions. The demographic evolution of this process is quantified by the galaxy stellar mass function (GSMF), which measures the comoving number density of galaxies as a function of their stellar mass. By mapping the GSMF and integrating the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] density across different epochs, astrophysicists can reconstruct the mass history of the Universe, linking the dark matter halo growth predicted by $\Lambda$CDM cosmology to the luminous baryonic structures observed today [Dickinson et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...587...25D/abstract). Ultimately, the observed diversity of galaxy forms arises from complex structural transformations driven by the dynamical assembly of smaller constituent systems, which remains a key area of multi-wavelength study [Ellis et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.395A...3E/abstract).

Observational censuses from deep [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]] reveal that cosmic mass assembly is a highly mass-dependent and non-linear process. The Universe's star formation rate density peaked approximately 3.5 billion years after the Big Bang ($z \sim 2$) and has exponentially declined since [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). During this time, galaxies largely evolved along the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]], a tight empirical correlation between star formation rate and stellar mass. However, the assembly history exhibits pronounced [[morphological-downsizing|Morphological downsizing]]: the most [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] formed the bulk of their stellar mass rapidly in the early Universe, whereas lower-mass systems assembled more gradually [Fontana et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...459..745F/abstract), [Pérez-González et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...675..234P/abstract). Consequently, massive systems typically undergo [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]], characterized by intense, early *in-situ* core formation—often heavily enshrouded and visible primarily as [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|Dust-Obscured Star Formation and Galaxies]]—followed by a late-time phase dominated by the *ex-situ* accretion of satellite systems.

Eventually, stellar mass assembly is halted by a combination of internal and environmental quenching mechanisms, driving galaxies out of the star-forming blue cloud and transforming them into [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]]. For massive [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]], energetic feedback from active galactic nuclei (AGN) is thought to starve the galaxy of cold gas, while [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] are subjected to environmental stripping and strangulation. Inferring the exact timelines of these processes relies heavily on [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] and spectral energy distribution (SED) modeling, which translate multi-wavelength light into physical properties like mass, age, and metallicity. This chemical enrichment acts as a fossil record, with the [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]] tracking the historical balance of gas accretion, star formation, and outflows over a galaxy's lifetime.

Despite robust constraints on late-time assembly, several critical tensions and open questions remain. A dominant systematic uncertainty in all mass derivations is the assumed [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] [Scalo et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986FCPh...11....1S/abstract), [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...39H/abstract). While traditionally treated as universal, a growing body of spectroscopic and dynamical evidence suggests the IMF may vary systematically with a galaxy's star formation rate or velocity dispersion. Furthermore, recent discoveries by the JWST in the realm of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] have revealed an unexpected abundance of extraordinarily massive galaxies at $z > 10$. These findings challenge textbook models of early halo assembly and necessitate a re-evaluation of [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|High-redshift cosmic star formation efficiencies]], the role of highly bursty star formation, and the potential biases in high-redshift SED modeling.

## Evolution of the Galaxy Stellar Mass Function (GSMF)

The Galaxy Stellar Mass Function (GSMF) tracks the comoving number density of galaxies as a function of their stellar mass, providing a direct metric of the integrated stellar mass assembly across cosmic time. Observationally derived from deep [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]], the GSMF is typically parameterized using a Schechter function, which features a power-law slope at low masses and an exponential cutoff at the high-mass end:
$$ \Phi(M) \, dM = \phi^* \left( \frac{M}{M^*} \right)^\alpha \exp \left( - \frac{M}{M^*} \right) \frac{dM}{M^*} $$
where $M$ is the stellar mass, $M^*$ is the characteristic mass cutoff, $\phi^*$ is the normalization, and $\alpha$ governs the faint-end slope. Integrating the GSMF yields the cosmic stellar mass density, which has monotonically increased from the epoch of reionization to the present day. Observations indicate that only $3\%$ to $14\%$ of today's stellar mass was present at $z \sim 2.7$ [Dickinson et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...587...25D/abstract).

At $z \lesssim 4$, the evolution of the GSMF exhibits a pronounced "downsizing" pattern. The most [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] ($M > 10^{11} M_\odot$) assembled the bulk of their stellar content rapidly before $z \sim 1.5$, whereas lower-mass systems evolved more gradually over extended timescales [Fontana et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...459..745F/abstract), [Pérez-González et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...675..234P/abstract). By $z \sim 1$, approximately $50\%$ of the local stellar mass density was already in place [Pérez-González et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...675..234P/abstract). [[cosmos-galaxy-catalogue|COSMOS galaxy catalogue]] per-type mass function results illustrate this by showing rapid massive elliptical assembly between $z=2$ and $z=1$ [Ilbert et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..399..169I/abstract). Additionally, efforts like the [[massiv-survey|MASSIV survey]] have established statistically representative samples that are crucial for investigating both the kinematic and chemical assembly of these galaxies at $z \sim 1\text{--}2$ [Contini et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...539A..91C/abstract). The transition mass scale where early-type galaxies begin to dominate the population moves to progressively higher masses at earlier cosmic epochs, further evidencing this downsizing trend [Bundy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...625..621B/abstract). While $M^*$ undergoes relatively mild evolution, the [[number-density-evolution|Number density evolution]] is heavily driven by the normalization $\phi^*$. Furthermore, since $z \sim 2$, a distinct steepening or "upturn" in the low-mass slope requires a double-Schechter function to accurately model the excess of low-mass systems, a feature found in both star-forming and [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]] [Tomczak et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783...85T/abstract). For example, [[gama-survey|GAMA survey]] luminosity functions necessitate these double-Schechter forms to account for a notable dip in red galaxy abundances faintward of $M^*$ [Loveday et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.420.1239L/abstract).

Pushing to higher redshifts ($z \sim 6-10$) using deep near-infrared data reveals a precipitous early epoch of mass assembly. The cosmic stellar mass density increased by a factor of $\sim 1000$ in the $\sim 500$ Myr between $z \sim 10$ and $z \sim 6$ [Stefanon et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922...29S/abstract). Strikingly, abundance matching suggests that the stellar-to-halo mass ratio did not change significantly over this initial $1$ Gyr of cosmic history, indicating that the buildup of stellar mass closely mirrored dark matter halo growth [Stefanon et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922...29S/abstract). Indeed, theoretical frameworks demonstrate that the primary driver of the GSMF evolution across cosmic time can be the buildup of dark matter halos alone, without necessarily requiring a redshift-dependent star formation efficiency [Tacchella et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...768L..37T/abstract). Consequently, incorporating cold gas accretion alongside an evolving bottom-light [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] can successfully reconcile the theoretical evolution of the massive end of the GSMF with observations [Kang et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...713.1301K/abstract). However, literature concerning the high-mass end at these epochs remains conflicting: there is a reported excess of massive, remarkably red galaxies at $z \approx 2.5 - 5.5$ that exceeds standard Schechter function predictions. For instance, the GMASS survey utilized a 4.5 $\mu$m selection methodology for effectively identifying massive or dust-obscured galaxies in the so-called "redshift desert" [Kurk et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..381..303K/abstract). This tension challenges current structure formation models and suggests a significant contribution from early [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|Dust-Obscured Star Formation and Galaxies]] that were historically missed by UV-selected surveys [Weaver et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...677A.184W/abstract). Indeed, hierarchical models have historically struggled to reproduce the coexisting populations of both quiescent red spheroids and dusty starbursts simultaneously at high redshift [Somerville et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005mmgf.conf..131S/abstract).

Measurements of the GSMF remain highly sensitive to systematic uncertainties, particularly mass completeness limits and cosmic variance, which acutely affect the rare, high-mass tail. Furthermore, the steeply-falling exponential cutoff is highly susceptible to asymmetric scatter in stellar mass estimates; systematic error asymmetry can artificially inflate the inferred abundance of ultra-massive galaxies at high redshift [Krishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251113708K/abstract). Conversely, recent findings from the [[massive-galaxy-catalogue|Massive Galaxy Catalogue]] reconcile the local high-mass GSMF with $z \sim 1$ observations, successfully alleviating previous concerns about a supposed lack of late-time mass growth in the most massive systems [Liepold et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971L..29L/abstract). Finally, the choice of [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models and the assumed IMF dramatically impact the derived GSMF. Recent non-parametric star formation history models (e.g., Prospector) infer older underlying stellar populations, suggesting that previous standard parametric approaches may have systematically underestimated the total mass by up to $\sim 50\%$ [Leja et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...893..111L/abstract).

## Cosmic Star Formation History and Mass Buildup

The buildup of the Universe's total stellar mass density is fundamentally tied to the time-integral of the cosmic star formation rate density (SFRD), denoted as $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$. Observations across multiple wavelengths have mapped this quantity over cosmic time. A robust consensus indicates that the global [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] peaked approximately 3.5 Gyr after the Big Bang at $z \approx 1.9-2.0$ (an epoch often termed "Cosmic Noon") and subsequently declined exponentially toward the present day with an $e$-folding timescale of 3.9 Gyr [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). In the nearby Universe, the establishment of local-volume flux-limited samples (e.g., within $d < 350 \text{ Mpc}$) has precisely mapped the local cosmography of stellar mass and [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] density [Biteau et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..256...15B/abstract). Additionally, a recalibration of 12 different SFR metrics from the [[gama-survey|GAMA survey]]/H-ATLAS surveys provides a robust local benchmark for confidently comparing SFRs across diverse cosmic epochs [Davies et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461..458D/abstract).

Assuming a universal [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], the global stellar mass density $\Omega_\star(z)$ inferred at any given epoch should broadly match the integral of all preceding star formation, minus the mass returned to the interstellar medium via stellar evolution. Observational censuses demonstrate that this mass buildup was highly non-linear. Between $z = 10$ and $z = 6$, the stellar mass density increased by a factor of $\sim 1000$, closely mirroring the rapid buildup of dark matter halos [Stefanon et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922...29S/abstract). Roughly 25% of the present-day stellar mass formed before the peak of the SFRD, and 50% was in place by $z \approx 1.3$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). In contrast, only 3-14% of today's stars were present at a lookback time corresponding to $z \sim 2.7$ [Dickinson et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...587...25D/abstract).

The rate of stellar mass assembly exhibits a strong dependence on galaxy mass, a phenomenon known as [[morphological-downsizing|Morphological downsizing]]. [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] ($\log(M_\star / M_\odot) > 11$) formed the bulk of their stars rapidly and at earlier epochs ($z > 2$), while lower-mass systems continued their assembly over longer timescales [Juneau et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...619L.135J/abstract), [Pérez-González et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...675..234P/abstract). During the peak of the SFRD at cosmic noon, mass assembly was largely governed by equilibrium growth models, where secular processes and disk instabilities within baryon-rich galaxies played a fundamental role in regulating star formation [Förster Schreiber et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ARA&A..58..661F/abstract). Deep infrared and sub-millimeter surveys reveal that [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|Dust-Obscured Star Formation and Galaxies]] played a vital role during this early epoch. Dusty starbursts dominate the massive galaxy population at $z > 2$, contribute up to 40% of the SFRD, and serve as the likely progenitors for the massive [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]] observed at lower redshifts [Martis et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..25M/abstract), [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract), [Long et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953...11L/abstract).

Historically, a factor-of-two tension existed between the integrated SFRD and the directly measured stellar mass density at intermediate redshifts. Recent advances in non-parametric spectral energy distribution (SED) modeling have largely resolved this discrepancy. By employing flexible star formation history models that can infer older underlying stellar populations, researchers recover stellar mass densities roughly 50% higher than previously measured, successfully reconciling the mass buildup with the integral of the SFRD [Leja et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...893..111L/abstract). 

However, significant tensions remain at the highest redshifts. Deep multi-wavelength surveys have identified an excess of massive, red galaxies at $z \approx 2.5-5.5$ that exceeds standard Schechter function predictions [Weaver et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...677A.184W/abstract). The literature remains conflicted on whether these extreme systems represent an intrinsic failure of standard $\Lambda$CDM models of early halo assembly, or if they are artifacts of systematic uncertainties—such as asymmetric scatter in steeply-falling mass functions and unresolved degeneracies in SED-derived stellar masses [Krishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251113708K/abstract).

## In-situ Star Formation and the Star-Forming Main Sequence

The majority of the stellar mass in the Universe is assembled via *in-situ* star formation, a process empirically bounded by a strong correlation between a galaxy's star formation rate (SFR) and its stellar mass ($M_*$). This scaling law, known as the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] (SFMS), holds across a vast range of redshifts and serves as a fundamental benchmark for models of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. Observations indicate that the SFR-$M_*$ relation is remarkably tight, with a uniform scatter of roughly $0.3$ to $0.4$ dex for star-forming galaxies across multiple cosmic epochs [Salmon et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799..183S/abstract). The relation remains linear down to low stellar masses ($M_* \sim 10^6 M_\odot$) at high redshift, maintaining its tight dispersion over orders of magnitude in mass [Iyer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...866..120I/abstract). 

The normalization of the SFMS evolves strongly with cosmic time, reflecting the global decline in cold gas availability. The specific star formation rate ($sSFR \equiv \text{SFR} / M_*$) at a fixed stellar mass decreases steadily across intermediate redshifts ($0.5 < z < 1.1$) [Feulner et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.358L...1F/abstract), reflecting a broader trend of steady decline from the peak of cosmic star formation down to the present day. While the relation is nearly linear at high redshift, observations reveal a "bend" or flattening at high stellar masses at lower redshifts ($z < 0.45$) [Thorne et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..540T/abstract). By employing the concept of Main Sequence Integration (MSI), models show that while present-day star-forming galaxies grew steadily at $z < 2$, the [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] observed at $z > 2$ are likely not the typical progenitors of local spirals [Leitner et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...745..149L/abstract). By propagating star formation histories backward in time, the redshift evolution of the SFMS can be parametrized as a function of the age of the Universe, $t_{\rm univ}$ (in Gyr):
$$ \log \text{SFR} = (0.80 \pm 0.029 - 0.017 \pm 0.010 \times t_{\rm univ}) \log M_* - (6.487 \pm 0.282 - 0.039 \pm 0.008 \times t_{\rm univ}) $$
as demonstrated by comprehensive spectral energy distribution fitting [Iyer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...866..120I/abstract).

The intrinsic dispersion of the SFMS provides crucial constraints on the physical processes regulating star formation. Recent studies track evolutionary paths using migration vectors in the $M_*$-SFR plane, noting that starburst galaxies typically originate from the main sequence and can assemble half their mass in only $\sim 350$ Myr [Arango-Toro et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241005375A/abstract). Furthermore, recent large-scale analyses reveal that the $0.3-0.4$ dex scatter correlates symmetrically with structural parameters such as the effective radius, stellar surface density, and even morphology [He et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250506868H/abstract). For example, findings from the [[gama-survey|GAMA survey]] indicate that galaxies with more spiral arms exhibit lower star-formation efficiency and lower specific star formation rates [Porter-Temple et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515.3875P/abstract). Galaxies fluctuating above and below the main sequence maintain similar fundamental properties, suggesting that the dispersion reflects a time-averaged response to stochastic oscillations in cosmic gas accretion and varied gas consumption timescales. Additionally, for massive systems, active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback begins to play a dominant role, driving rapidly declining star formation histories and contributing to the scatter at the high-mass end [Davies et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250521948D/abstract). Furthermore, results from the DEVILS survey show that sub-regions of the sSFR-$M_\star$ plane exhibit distinctly different star formation histories, necessitating new selection methodologies beyond traditional Main Sequence cuts to accurately capture this complexity [Davies et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250521947D/abstract).

While massive systems at low to intermediate redshifts tend to assemble their mass through relatively steady, [[continuous-star-formation|Continuous star formation]]—where, unless abruptly quenched, galaxies of similar masses follow relatively smooth, gradually declining star formation histories out to $z > 1$ [Noeske et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ASPC..419..298N/abstract)—the paradigm shifts significantly for [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] and systems in the early Universe. High-redshift ($z > 6$) and low-mass ($M_* < 10^{10} M_\odot$) galaxies are characterized by stochasticity and bursty star formation histories [Bauer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.434..209B/abstract), [Looser et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230602470L/abstract). In these regimes, secular exponential decline models fail to reproduce the observed distributions of sSFRs. Instead, early *in-situ* growth is dominated by a weak underlying baseline of star formation punctuated by [[short-duration-star-formation|Short-duration star formation]] bursts [Bauer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.434..209B/abstract). This bursty star formation underscores how early mass assembly was highly sensitive to localized feedback and the episodic nature of gas inflows. Ultimately, this intense *in-situ* phase lays the foundational compact cores of massive galaxies before late-time, *ex-situ* accretion begins to dominate their outer structural growth [Johansson et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..354J/abstract).

## Two-Phase Growth: In-situ vs. Ex-situ Assembly

The assembly of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] is widely understood to proceed via a [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] model, representing a paradigm shift from classical monolithic collapse theories [Johansson et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..354J/abstract). Detailed studies of structural evolution note that individual massive galaxies observed at $z > 2$ are frequently in the process of forming through [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]], serving as the direct progenitors of both local spiral disks and massive ellipticals [Conselice et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..301...47C/abstract). In the standard framework of hierarchical assembly, stellar mass buildup is characterized by an early epoch of intense, dissipational in-situ star formation, followed by a late-time phase dominated by the stochastic accretion of ex-situ stars through major and minor galaxy mergers.

**The In-Situ Phase**
During the first phase ($z \gtrsim 2$), galaxies assemble the bulk of their central mass via rapid, compact star formation. This process is fed by cold gas flows and results in the formation of a dense, massive core. Observational evidence from [[spatially-resolved-stellar-populations|Spatially resolved stellar populations]]—such as those analyzed in the CALIFA and [[phangs-survey|PHANGS survey]] programs—supports an "inside-out" growth mechanism. The inner regions of these galaxies are systematically older and more metal-rich than their outskirts, reflecting the early and rapid assembly of the galactic center before subsequent extended growth [González Delgado et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...581A.103G/abstract), [Pessa et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...673A.147P/abstract).

**The Ex-Situ Phase**
At lower redshifts ($z \lesssim 1$), as the cosmic gas supply dwindles and internal feedback mechanisms quench in-situ star formation, galaxy growth transitions to an ex-situ dominated phase. Here, mass is primarily accumulated through the accretion of existing stars from satellite galaxies. [[accreted-stellar-populations|Accreted stellar populations]] are deposited largely in the galactic outskirts, building up extended stellar halos. Spatially resolved spectroscopy reveals that the fraction of ex-situ stars increases radially; in massive early-type galaxies, the ex-situ fraction can reach $30\% \text{--} 100\%$ at two effective radii ($2R_e$). By contrast, less massive and more compact galaxies typically only reach ex-situ fractions of $5\% \text{--} 40\%$ at the same scaled radius [Davison et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.3089D/abstract). Furthermore, resolved stellar populations analyzed from MaNGA spectra in different environments help discriminate between secular internal growth and merger-driven ex-situ mass assembly [Argudo-Fernández et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019BAAA...61..201A/abstract). This establishes a direct link between early in-situ starbursts and later ex-situ accretion, where galaxies with more extended halos at a fixed mass often exhibit older stellar ages and higher [Mg/Fe] ratios [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722602Z/abstract). Insights from the [[atlas3d-survey|Atlas3D survey]] provide a complementary perspective, revealing a surprising prevalence of young stellar ages and molecular cold gas in some massive early-type galaxies, offering crucial clues to their continued late-time assembly [McDermid et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..290M/abstract). In dense environments, Balmer line evolution in early-type cluster galaxies suggests a higher [[formation-epoch|Formation epoch]] ($z_{\rm form} > 2.8$) than inferred from the Fundamental Plane alone, reflecting incredibly early in-situ growth prior to cluster virialization [Jørgensen et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AJ....145...77J/abstract). Looking forward, instruments like E-ELT/EAGLE have the potential to resolve individual stellar populations out to $20\text{ Mpc}$, providing detailed kinematics and chemical abundances essential for untangling these assembly histories [Evans et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010aoel.confE1004E/abstract).

**Mass and Environmental Dependence**
The relative importance of these two phases is strongly dependent on the total mass of the system. Semi-analytic models indicate that mergers become the dominant mass growth channel for [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]] once their host dark matter halo mass exceeds $M_{200} \sim 10^{13} M_\odot$, corresponding to a critical stellar mass of $\log(M_*/M_\odot) \gtrsim 11$ [Lee et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...836..161L/abstract). Below this threshold, secular processes and in-situ star formation remain the primary drivers of stellar mass assembly.

In the regime of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], constraining the two-phase model is observationally challenging. While hierarchical assembly predicts that dwarf galaxies also undergo [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] and mergers, the resulting tidal streams and [[stellar-substructure|Stellar substructure]] are extraordinarily faint. Recent deep-imaging surveys in the Local Volume report detectable accretion features (such as shells or streams) in less than $5\%$ of dwarf galaxies [Sakowska et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251123314S/abstract). To better constrain this across a wider range of masses, the Stellar Stream Legacy Survey (SSLS) has begun systematically mapping tidal features around nearby galaxies, serving as a direct diagnostic of ongoing hierarchical assembly [Martínez-Delgado et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...671A.141M/abstract). Additionally, the VST-SMASH survey is specifically targeting tidal streams down to a surface brightness of $\mu \sim 30\text{ mag arcsec}^{-2}$ in the Euclid footprint to map this hierarchical assembly [Tortora et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Msngr.193...31T/abstract). Broadly, capturing these faint signatures highlights the acute need for wide-field spatially resolved spectroscopy to directly link parsec-scale star formation with the low-surface brightness accretion features of extended galactic halos [Fahrion et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251214281F/abstract). Because these low surface brightness signatures are difficult to detect, the precise contribution of ex-situ assembly to the low-mass end of the galaxy stellar mass function remains ambiguous and is a subject of active debate in the literature [Davies et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260114666D/abstract).

## Stellar Population Synthesis and SED Modeling

The assembly of stellar mass across cosmic time is primarily quantified using SED fitting techniques, which translate multi-wavelength photometry and spectroscopy into physical galaxy properties such as stellar mass ($M_\star$), age, and metallicity. This process relies fundamentally on [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] (SPS) models, which integrate stellar evolutionary tracks, empirical or theoretical stellar spectral libraries, dust attenuation prescriptions, and a chosen [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] to predict the intrinsic spectral energy distribution of a composite stellar population [Conroy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...699..486C/abstract). 

The primary output of SED fitting for mass assembly studies is the stellar mass-to-light ratio, $\Upsilon_\star$, which links the intrinsic luminosity $L$ in a given passband to the total stellar mass:
$$ M_\star = \Upsilon_\star \times L $$
Because $\Upsilon_\star$ in optical bands is highly sensitive to recent star formation, near-infrared passbands (such as the $K$-band or $3.4\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) are heavily favored as robust tracers of stellar mass. In these bands, $\Upsilon_\star$ varies by a factor of 2 or less across diverse star formation histories, compared to an order of magnitude variation in the blue optical bands.

Historically, SED fitting relied on simplistic, parametric models for the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] (SFH), such as exponentially declining $\tau$-models. However, such parametric forms often fail to capture stochastic or bursty star formation episodes, which are particularly prevalent in low-mass and high-redshift [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] [Bauer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.434..209B/abstract), [Looser et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230602470L/abstract). Additionally, stochastic sampling of the IMF in low-mass star clusters can lead to unreliable SED fitting results, which particularly impacts and complicates mass derivations for dwarf systems [Anders et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...778..138A/abstract). Recent methodological advances have driven a shift toward flexible, non-parametric SFH modeling using sophisticated Bayesian codes like Prospector and Dense Basis [Iyer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...866..120I/abstract), [Leja et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...893..111L/abstract). By sampling the SFH without restrictive functional forms, non-parametric codes successfully recover older underlying stellar populations that are otherwise outshined by recent, unobscured starbursts. Consequently, Prospector-based analyses infer cosmic stellar mass densities that are up to $\sim 50\%$ higher at $z \sim 1$ than those derived from parametric methods, effectively resolving long-standing factor-of-two tensions between the integral of the cosmic star formation rate density and the directly observed stellar mass buildup [Leja et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...893..111L/abstract).

Despite these algorithmic improvements, SED fitting remains a highly degenerate inverse problem, and systematic uncertainties dominate the error budget. Uncertainties in specific phases of stellar evolution—most notably the metallicity dependence of the thermally pulsating asymptotic giant branch (TP-AGB) phase, alongside the continuing refinement of modern stellar spectral libraries—have a profound impact on the predicted spectral energy distributions of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] [Maraston et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..272M/abstract). Coupled with assumptions regarding the universality of the IMF and the geometry of dust extinction, these issues can induce errors in $M_\star$ estimates on the order of $0.3$ to $0.6$ dex at $95\%$ confidence [Conroy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...699..486C/abstract). Systematic uncertainties further compound depending on the target galaxy; literature demonstrates that different mass estimation methods can disagree by factors of 2 to 3 when applied to distinct galaxy morphological types [Kannappan et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...657L...5K/abstract). Additionally, photometric methodology plays a critical role; systematic flux losses in Petrosian magnitudes compared to Sérsic profiles can lead to significant underestimation of the stellar mass in bright, early-type galaxies [Loveday et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451.1540L/abstract). 

Furthermore, the literature demonstrates that varying assumptions between different fitting codes can lead to conflicting results for the same objects. For example, comparisons between Bagpipes and Prospector reveal that while the two codes generally agree on late-time stellar mass growth metrics (such as $t_{50}$ and $t_{90}$, the epochs at which $50\%$ and $90\%$ of the mass assembled), they can disagree significantly on the earliest formation times ($t_{10}$) [Kaushal et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..118K/abstract). Accurately capturing these early mass fractions and properly modeling [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|Dust-Obscured Star Formation and Galaxies]] remains a critical hurdle for robustly defining the mass assembly history of massive galaxies at early cosmic epochs [Whitler et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.5859W/abstract), [Krishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251113708K/abstract). Looking forward, the proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO) promises to mitigate many SED degeneracies by resolving individual stars in large galaxies beyond the Local Group, allowing astronomers to infer highly precise star formation histories extending back to the earliest cosmic epochs [Smercina et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250701960S/abstract).

## The Role of the Stellar Initial Mass Function (IMF)

The [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], which describes the birth mass distribution of stars, is a foundational parameter in studies of galaxy evolution. Because the integrated light of a galaxy is dominated by massive, short-lived stars while its mass is dominated by low-mass, long-lived stars, mapping observed luminosities to intrinsic stellar masses relies heavily on [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models. These models require the IMF as a primary input to compute the mass-to-light ratio, $\Upsilon$. Historically, mass assembly studies have assumed a universal IMF, such as the Salpeter initial mass function or the empirical distributions derived by Scalo and others [Scalo et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986FCPh...11....1S/abstract). 

However, the assumption of universality represents perhaps the greatest source of systematic uncertainty in tracing the cosmic star formation history and stellar mass buildup [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...39H/abstract). Even conservative estimates of uncertainties in the IMF slope can introduce errors of $\sim 0.4$ magnitudes in rest-frame $K$-band luminosity evolution [Conroy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...699..486C/abstract). Theoretical modeling of how star clusters populate a galaxy provides further context; investigations into the relationship between individual star cluster IMFs and the integrated, composite galaxy-wide IMF reveal that the galaxy-scale IMF is often not steeper than the individual cluster IMFs themselves [Elmegreen et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...648..572E/abstract). If the IMF varies systematically with cosmic time or environment, it dramatically alters the inferred fraction of mass assembled at high redshift.

A growing body of observational evidence challenges the textbook assumption of a universal IMF, indicating systematic IMF variations linked to the physical properties of galaxies. In massive early-type galaxies, both dynamical models and detailed absorption-line spectroscopy suggest an increasingly bottom-heavy IMF as a function of stellar velocity dispersion, $\sigma$ [Conroy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...776L..26C/abstract). In these compact, dense systems, the dynamical mass $M_{\rm dyn}$ within one effective radius is significantly higher than the stellar mass $M_*$ inferred from a standard Milky Way-like IMF. By adopting a bottom-heavy IMF enriched with low-mass stars, the mismatch between photometric and dynamical mass estimates is resolved to within $\lesssim 0.1$ dex [Conroy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...776L..26C/abstract). Incorporating these variations into semi-analytic models provides a self-consistent interpretation of mass assembly and intrinsic mass-to-light ratios that standard models utilizing a universal IMF fail to reproduce [Fontanot et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..341..124F/abstract).

Conversely, in highly active [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], evidence points toward a flatter, or top-heavy, IMF. Analysis of volume-limited samples from the [[gama-survey|GAMA survey]] demonstrates a strong dependency of the high-mass IMF slope on the [[stellar-birthrate|Stellar birthrate]] and the absolute star formation rate [Gunawardhana et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.1647G/abstract). Galaxies undergoing intense star formation appear to form a proportionally higher fraction of massive stars compared to quiescent systems. 

Despite these converging lines of evidence for a variable IMF, the literature remains in tension regarding its specific physical drivers. It is not yet unambiguously established whether IMF variations are fundamentally governed by global galaxy properties—such as velocity dispersion or integrated star formation rate—or by localized, sub-galactic interstellar medium conditions like gas density, temperature, and metallicity [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...39H/abstract). To overcome these challenges, the development of a shared framework for variable IMF studies is underway in the era of 30m-class telescopes and advanced near-infrared integral-field spectroscopy [Jerabkova et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250906886J/abstract). Resolving this debate remains a critical hurdle for accurately calibrating the stellar mass density and understanding the complete assembly history of the Universe.

## Quenching Mechanisms and the Transition to Quiescence

The accumulation of stellar mass in galaxies is eventually halted by a combination of internal and external mechanisms that suppress star formation. This transition from the star-forming "blue cloud" to the quiescent "red sequence" is a defining feature of galaxy evolution. The relative importance of stellar mass versus environment as the primary driver of this transition—often termed "mass quenching" and "environmental quenching," respectively—remains a subject of active research, though observational evidence suggests the two operate in different physical regimes.

**Internal Mechanisms and Mass Quenching**
For massive [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]], intrinsic physical processes are the primary drivers of quiescence. Empirical evidence indicates a strong dependence of star formation cessation on stellar mass, driving [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] to quench earlier and more rapidly than their low-mass counterparts—a manifestation of "downsizing" [Weaver et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...677A.184W/abstract). The favored theoretical mechanism for this is feedback from active galactic nuclei (AGN). The central supermassive black hole injects vast amounts of energy into the circumgalactic medium, preventing the cooling of hot halo gas and starving the galaxy of the fuel required for new stars [Davies et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250521948D/abstract). However, the relationship between nuclear activity and quenching is complex; observations reveal that $\sim 80\%$ of quasar host galaxies at $z \sim 0.2$ are actually star-forming systems on the main sequence, suggesting that nuclear activity is often triggered by secular processes rather than immediate quenching-inducing events [Stone et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251122453S/abstract). Moreover, the environmental neighbors of Type I quasars are similar to those of inactive galaxies, reinforcing that nuclear activity is often triggered by internal secular processes rather than immediate, externally driven quenching events [Stone et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946..116S/abstract). This massive galaxy feedback mechanism is thought to dominate in dark matter halos exceeding a characteristic mass of $\sim 10^{12} - 10^{13} h^{-1} M_\odot$. Observationally, this is supported by the tight coevolution of the cosmic star-formation rate and AGN bolometric luminosity over the past $11 \text{ Gyr}$, which maintain a flat ratio of activity across cosmic time [D'Silva et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.1448D/abstract). Spatially resolved studies further indicate that massive galaxies generally follow an "inside-out" quenching pattern, where the dense core ceases star formation before the extended disk [Ge et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24c5006G/abstract).

**Environmental Quenching**
For [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]], external environmental factors become the dominant quenching driver, particularly for systems with stellar masses below $M_* \sim 10^{10} M_\odot$ [Bravo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.4481B/abstract). When a galaxy is accreted into a larger dark matter halo, processes such as ram-pressure stripping, tidal stripping, and strangulation (the removal of the extended gas reservoir) suppress further star formation. Satellite galaxies at intermediate redshifts ($0.3 < z < 0.5$) exhibit star-formation rates suppressed by $\sim 0.5 \text{ dex}$ compared to isolated systems at fixed stellar mass, with the suppression increasing up to $\sim 1.0 \text{ dex}$ in the most massive host halos [Davies et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250720822D/abstract). Notably, environmental cessation can operate on long timescales (several Gyr) without inducing major structural transformations, occasionally producing "red spiral" galaxies where the gas has been depleted but the morphological disk remains intact [Mahajan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491..398M/abstract). Conversely, lower-mass systems statistically favor an "outside-in" quenching pattern driven by these environmental effects [Ge et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24c5006G/abstract).

**Quenching Timescales and Post-Starburst Populations**
The transition timescale ($\tau_Q$) from the blue cloud to the red sequence is not static; it has lengthened over recent cosmic time from $\tau_Q \sim 1 \text{ Gyr}$ at intermediate redshifts (a lookback time of $\sim 4 \text{ Gyr}$) to $\sim 2 \text{ Gyr}$ in the local Universe [Bravo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.4481B/abstract). Rapid quenching tracks are observationally identified through [[active-post-starburst-galaxies|Active post-starburst galaxies]] (often classified as [[e-a-galaxies|E+A galaxies]]), which lack emission lines but exhibit strong Balmer absorption (e.g., $H\delta$), indicating a starburst that ended abruptly $0.1-1 \text{ Gyr}$ ago [French et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASP..133g2001F/abstract). 

Deep spectroscopic surveys reveal that the pathways to quiescence imprint distinct signatures on stellar populations. Spectral indices such as the $4000 \text{ \AA}$ break ($D_n4000$) and $H\delta$ equivalent widths show that while star-forming galaxies exhibit properties tied almost entirely to their stellar mass, the properties of [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]] carry strong environmental dependencies [Sobral et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926..117S/abstract). At $z \sim 1$, dust-obscured star-forming galaxies are already present in sufficient numbers to serve as the direct progenitors of the massive quiescent population observed at later epochs [Long et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953...11L/abstract). Ultimately, the combination of mass-driven AGN feedback and environment-driven gas stripping populates the [[red-sequence-transition-galaxies|Red-sequence transition galaxies]] observed today. Understanding the complete transition pathways requires large, volume-representative samples (e.g., $10^6$ galaxies) to physically connect progenitor and descendant populations through the "Cosmic Middle Ages" [Gallazzi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251216998G/abstract).

## Dust-Obscured Assembly and Hidden Mass

Historically, the cosmic [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and stellar mass assembly were primarily traced using rest-frame ultraviolet (UV) selections. At the same time, detailed local observations—such as GALEX UV imaging—reveal extremely low levels of massive star formation in nearby galaxies, helping refine our understanding of differing star formation modalities [Bianchi et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007uasb.conf...65B/abstract). However, these samples systematically undercount regions of heavily obscured star formation. Galaxies experiencing intense, dust-enshrouded growth—collectively treated under [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|Dust-Obscured Star Formation and Galaxies]]—are now recognized to host a significant fraction of the universe's stellar mass assembly [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract). At early cosmic epochs, extinction corrections based solely on UV slopes are insufficient to recover this hidden mass.

To accurately reconstruct the buildup of stellar mass, the cosmic stellar mass density $\rho_*(t)$ must account for both unobscured and dust-obscured star formation rate densities ($\dot{\rho}_*$):
$$ \rho_*(t) = \int_{0}^{t} \left[ \dot{\rho}_{*,\text{UV}}(t') + \dot{\rho}_{*,\text{IR}}(t') \right] \left(1 - R(t')\right) dt' $$
where $R(t')$ represents the fraction of mass returned to the interstellar medium by evolved stars [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Observations incorporating mid- and far-infrared (FIR-selected) samples demonstrate that the role of dust-obscured star-forming galaxies (DSFGs) grows rapidly with both increasing stellar mass and redshift. At $z \gtrsim 2$, DSFGs dominate the overall galaxy population for masses $\log(M_*/M_\odot) \gtrsim 10.3$ [Martis et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..25M/abstract). Among the most [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], DSFGs are estimated to constitute between 50% and 100% of the [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] with $M_* \geq 10^{11} M_\odot$ at $z > 1$ [Long et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953...11L/abstract). In contrast, unobscured systems only dominate the population at lower mass regimes ($\log(M_*/M_\odot) \lesssim 10$). Consequently, DSFGs and their descendants are predicted to contribute 25% to 60% of the cosmic stellar mass density during the peak of cosmic star formation [Long et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953...11L/abstract). Furthermore, radio and submillimeter surveys have identified heavily obscured populations at $z > 4.5$ that were entirely missed by optical and near-infrared surveys, potentially adding up to 40% to the previously known cosmic star formation rate density [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract).

The inclusion of highly obscured populations and the application of sophisticated [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] techniques have revealed tensions in standard measurements of the galaxy stellar mass function. Deep surveys have uncovered an excess of massive, remarkably red galaxies at $2.5 \lesssim z \lesssim 5.5$ that challenge standard Schechter function parameterizations and exceed the number densities predicted by standard cosmological structure formation models [Weaver et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...677A.184W/abstract). The exact nature of this excess remains an active area of investigation, with heavy dust obscuration acting as a leading physical explanation. Moreover, resolving the degeneracies between stellar age, metallicity, and dust extinction in these obscured systems often requires non-parametric star formation histories. Such comprehensive panchromatic SED modeling corroborates findings that the true cosmic stellar mass density at early epochs may be markedly higher than inferred by simpler parametric approaches [Leja et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...893..111L/abstract).

The intense stellar mass assembly within DSFGs typically spans the period from $z \approx 6$ to $z \approx 3$. Forward-modeling of these massive, dust-obscured systems indicates that they represent a sufficient ancestral population to account for the abundance of massive [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]] observed at $z > 1$ [Long et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953...11L/abstract). Thus, the rapid, hidden growth phase in DSFGs acts as a necessary precursor bridging the gap between extreme, dust-enshrouded starbursts and the subsequent emergence of dense, quenched remnants.

## Environmental Dependence: Centrals and Satellites

The buildup of stellar mass in galaxies is strongly dictated by their position within the dark matter halo. It is standard to separate populations into [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]], which reside at or near the bottom of the halo potential well, and [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]], which orbit within the host halo. Central galaxies primarily grow through the continuous cooling and accretion of gas from the intergalactic medium, as well as through mergers with sinking satellites due to dynamical friction. In contrast, satellite galaxies are largely cut off from pristine gas accretion and are subjected to host halo environmental effects, fundamentally altering their mass assembly trajectories. Furthermore, what constitutes an "isolated" environment is highly nuanced; surveys such as GAMA have discovered "tendrils"—thin chains of galaxies rooted in larger filaments that populate cosmic voids—which fundamentally challenge traditional definitions of isolated void galaxies [Alpaslan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440L.106A/abstract).

As satellites fall into more massive halos, they experience environmental quenching mechanisms that suppress their star formation. The most prominent of these are ram-pressure stripping of the cold interstellar medium, tidal stripping of stars and dark matter, and strangulation (or starvation), wherein the galaxy's extended hot gas reservoir is completely removed upon accretion. These processes drive galaxies from the star-forming blue cloud to the red sequence. Interestingly, while the faint-end slope of the luminosity function steepens with decreasing density, the stellar mass function slope remains largely unchanged, suggesting that the environment mainly quenches star formation rather than significantly stripping stellar mass [Mercurio et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASSP...27...55M/abstract). However, morphological transformation does not necessarily occur simultaneously with star formation cessation. For instance, the existence of red spiral galaxies—which are on average $0.9$ dex more massive and reside in environments $2.6$ times denser than their blue counterparts—indicates that environment-related gas removal can operate over long timescales (several Gyr) without destroying the galactic disk [Mahajan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491..398M/abstract). Furthermore, while color and star formation activity depend strongly on environment, galaxy size remains fairly insensitive for both high- and low-mass populations [Deng et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...716..599D/abstract). Indeed, non-emission-line galaxies residing in clusters exhibit a significantly steeper faint-end slope in their stellar mass functions compared to their counterparts in field environments [Balogh et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...557..117B/abstract). Environmental effects extend beyond simple mass quenching to dynamical alterations. Results from the SAMI Galaxy Survey detect a strong anticorrelation between galaxy spin (parameterized by $\lambda_{R_e}$) and local environment density, an effect that is independent of the broader mass-environment relation [Rutherford et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...918...84R/abstract).

The local environment leaves a distinct quantitative imprint on the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] (SFMS). Observations from the [[gama-survey|GAMA survey]] reveal that for [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]], the position of the SFMS is similar to that of field galaxies and shows no significant dependence on halo mass, albeit with a larger scatter [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...618A...1W/abstract). For [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]], however, the normalization of the SFMS is systematically lower by ${\sim}0.2$ dex compared to the field [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...618A...1W/abstract), and their overall star formation rates are suppressed by ${\sim}0.5$ dex relative to isolated systems of the same stellar mass [Davies et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250720822D/abstract). This suppression is most severe in high-mass dark matter halos, where star formation rates can be reduced by up to ${\sim}1$ dex [Davies et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250720822D/abstract). Consequently, the fraction of star-forming satellite galaxies decreases dramatically with increasing halo mass, a trend that strengthens toward lower redshifts [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...618A...1W/abstract). At the center of massive halos, brightest cluster galaxy (BCG) star formation evolves similarly to massive cluster galaxies, but is generally $\sim 1$ dex lower in magnitude for COSMOS-sized clusters [Cooke et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...857..122C/abstract). At earlier epochs ($z > 0.4$), clusters actually require an additional population of dusty starburst galaxies to explain their mid-infrared luminosity functions when compared to local quiescent clusters [Muzzin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...686..966M/abstract).

The interplay between a galaxy's intrinsic stellar mass and its environment remains an active area of study. Spectroscopic surveys at $z \sim 0.6-1.0$ show that while absorption indices such as $D_n(4000)$ and $\text{H}\delta$ depend primarily on stellar mass, they also exhibit strong environmental gradients. Crucially, these environmental trends at fixed stellar mass are driven almost entirely by the increasing fraction of [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]] in dense environments, whereas the properties of the star-forming galaxies themselves remain largely independent of environment [Sobral et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926..117S/abstract). 

The efficiency of environmental quenching also depends strongly on the mass of the satellite. In the local Universe, environment significantly impacts [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] with stellar masses below $10^{10} M_\odot$, where satellites exhibit quenching timescales roughly $0.4$ Gyr shorter than those of centrals [Bravo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.4481B/abstract). In the dwarf galaxy regime, recent findings from the SAGA survey background sample (SAGAbg III) indicate that the shape of the low-mass stellar mass function is largely independent of environment, and that the quenched fraction of genuinely isolated dwarf galaxies drops to near zero ($\sim 10^{-3}$) [Kado-Fong et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250920444K/abstract). In contrast, mass return from dusty evolved stars in the Local Group may occasionally provide enough gas to rejuvenate star formation even in isolated dwarf galaxies [Mahani et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IAUS..373..264M/abstract). Furthermore, environmental quenching has become more efficient over cosmic time; at fixed stellar and halo mass, the fraction of passive satellite galaxies has increased by $10-15\%$ over the last 5 Gyr [Davies et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250720822D/abstract).

## Chemical Assembly and the Mass-Metallicity Relation

The buildup of stellar mass in galaxies is inextricably linked to their chemical enrichment. As stars evolve and die, they return heavy elements to the interstellar medium (see [[chemical-abundances-and-nucleosynthesis|Chemical Abundances and Nucleosynthesis]]), modulating the cooling efficiency of the gas and providing a fossil record of the galaxy's [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]]. This interplay establishes a tight empirical correlation between a galaxy's stellar mass and its chemical abundance, known as the mass-metallicity relation (MZR).

In the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]], the MZR is characterized by a strong correlation wherein the gas-phase metallicity—typically parameterized as the oxygen abundance, $12 + \log({\rm O/H})$—increases with stellar mass. This relation rises steeply for [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] before flattening out at a characteristic pivot mass of roughly $M_* \sim 10^{10} M_\odot$. The fact that low-mass disk galaxies have systematically lower metallicities at a given gas fraction indicates that they do not evolve as "closed boxes"; instead, their chemical evolution is heavily regulated by metal-enriched outflows and the continuous dilution of the interstellar medium by pristine gas accretion. Beyond secular evolution, there is evidence for environmental modulation of the MZR. Observations indicate gas metallicity decrements in galaxies residing within groups compared to isolated field galaxies, underscoring the complex interplay between chemical enrichment and environment . Furthermore, efforts like the [[massiv-survey|MASSIV survey]] utilize spatially-resolved chemical properties at $z \sim 1\text{--}2$ to directly probe the complex chemical assembly and kinematic evolution of early star-forming galaxies [Contini et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..277..134C/abstract).

Across cosmic time, the MZR exhibits strong redshift evolution, predominantly in its overall normalization. Observations leveraging data from the [[gama-survey|GAMA survey]] reveal that the MZR normalization drops by approximately $0.1$ dex at $M_* \sim 10^{10.5} M_\odot$ between $z=0$ and $z \sim 0.3$ [Thorne et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.6035T/abstract). This evolution becomes even more pronounced at earlier epochs. At $z > 3$, the effective oxygen yields no longer appear to scale with stellar mass, suggesting that the simple momentum- or energy-driven outflow models governing local galaxies may fail to describe the baryon cycle at high redshifts [Mannucci et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..255..106M/abstract). Furthermore, the dispersion within the MZR contains valuable temporal information: at a fixed stellar mass, galaxies with higher metallicities typically assembled the bulk of their mass over shorter timescales and prior to their peak star formation rates [Thorne et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.6035T/abstract).

The scatter in the two-dimensional MZR is strongly correlated with a galaxy's current star formation rate (SFR), giving rise to a three-dimensional surface in mass-metallicity-SFR space known as the Fundamental Metallicity Relation (FMR). In the local Universe, galaxies lying on the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] define an "equilibrium" FMR, reflecting a stable state where the timescales of gas inflows, star formation, and chemical enrichment are tightly coupled [Lara-López et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.434..451L/abstract).

However, whether the FMR represents a universally constant relationship across all redshifts remains a subject of active and significant debate. While some models propose that the FMR holds unchanged out to moderate redshifts, pushing these observational limits to cosmic noon reveals critical tensions. Careful analyses of redshift-binned samples indicate statistically significant intrinsic evolution in the mass-SFR-metallicity parameter space [Niino et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761..126N/abstract). Moreover, recent spectroscopic studies of $z \sim 2.3$ galaxies find that they are fundamentally inconsistent with the locally-defined FMR, showing no significant reduction in MZR scatter when SFR is incorporated as a third parameter [Korhonen Cuestas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...984..188K/abstract). These findings suggest that the equilibrium state governing the baryon cycle of local galaxies breaks down in the early Universe, likely driven by the highly stochastic, bursty nature of early star formation and substantially higher specific gas accretion rates.

## Open Questions: Assembly in the First Billion Years

In the first billion years of the Universe ($z > 6$), deep multi-wavelength surveys have fundamentally reshaped our understanding of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]]. Prior to the launch of JWST, Spitzer Space Telescope observations extending into the reionization epoch indicated that the cosmic stellar mass density increased by roughly a factor of $1000$ between $z \approx 10$ and $z \approx 6$, with the buildup of stellar mass closely mirroring the growth of dark matter halos [Stefanon et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922...29S/abstract). However, recent early-release JWST photometric and spectroscopic campaigns have identified candidate [[first-light-galaxies|First-light galaxies]] and exceptionally massive systems whose implied stellar masses challenge standard $\Lambda$CDM expectations.

To assemble such massive systems so early in cosmic history, theoretical models and early HST/Spitzer studies ($z \approx 8$) have long recognized that these galaxies must reside in extremely rare dark matter halos and convert their available baryons into stars with efficiencies approaching or even exceeding $100\%$ [Yan et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761..177Y/abstract), [Krishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251113708K/abstract). The required star formation efficiency, $\epsilon_{*}$, can be expressed as the ratio of the stellar mass to the available baryonic mass in the halo:
$$ \epsilon_{*} = \frac{M_{*}}{f_{b} M_{\mathrm{halo}}} $$
where $f_{b}$ is the universal baryon fraction. Observational claims of $\epsilon_{*} \to 1$ have sparked intense debate over whether these findings signal a failure of standard cosmological structure formation or highlight critical gaps in our understanding of early astrophysical processes. 

The literature currently presents several conflicting resolutions to this tension. One primary argument is that the perceived overabundance of ultra-massive galaxies is driven by observational and methodological biases. Specifically, systematic uncertainties in spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting, combined with the asymmetric scatter induced by the steeply falling high-mass tail of the halo mass function, can artificially inflate the inferred stellar masses of these high-redshift targets [Krishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251113708K/abstract).

Alternatively, the tension may be resolved by revising physical models of [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|High-redshift cosmic star formation efficiencies]] and feedback. Semi-analytic models have demonstrated that the rapid stellar mass growth observed by JWST can be naturally reproduced if early stellar feedback was significantly weaker—up to a factor of three lower—than the feedback required to regulate galaxy growth in the lower-redshift Universe [Dressler et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260201549D/abstract). Furthermore, reconciling the observed UV luminosity functions with measurements of galaxy clustering requires the implementation of a mass-dependent star formation duty cycle, with characteristic star formation timescales increasing rapidly from $z \approx 6$ to $z \approx 8$ [Chakraborty et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250307590C/abstract).

These shifting efficiencies are deeply tied to the temporal distribution of early star formation, which appears radically different from the equilibrium state seen at later epochs. Deep spectroscopy from the JADES survey indicates that galaxies at $z > 6$ possessed highly stochastic, bursty [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] tracks. This extreme burstiness leads to severe fluctuations in instantaneous star formation rates, sometimes resulting in temporary, short-duration quiescent phases. These [[mini-quenching-events|Mini-quenching events]] have been observationally confirmed, with discoveries of [[mini-quenched-galaxies|Mini-quenched galaxies]] completely halting their star formation as early as $z = 7.3$ [Looser et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230602470L/abstract).

It remains an open question whether the assembly of the first galaxies necessitates new physics, variations in the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] in low-metallicity environments, or simply highly stochastic star formation coupled with complex dust geometries. To differentiate between systematic SED biases and true deviations from theoretical models, statistically robust spectroscopic samples are required. Future and ongoing efforts, such as the JWST/NIRSpec IFS GTO programs, are specifically designed to spatially resolve gas kinematics and stellar population gradients in these early galaxies [Perna et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IAUS..373...60P/abstract), which will definitively chart stellar mass assembly during the cosmic dawn.
