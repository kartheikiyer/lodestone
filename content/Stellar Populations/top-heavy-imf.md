### Revised Full Draft


The [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] describes the empirical distribution of stellar masses formed within a single star-forming event. For decades, textbook astrophysics treated the IMF as a near-universal probability density function, typically modeled with a [[salpeter-initial-mass-function|Salpeter initial mass function]] or a Kroupa profile calibrated to the local solar neighborhood. However, a **top-heavy IMF** describes a stellar population possessing a significantly higher proportion of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] relative to low-mass dwarfs than these canonical models predict. Theoretical frameworks and mounting empirical evidence suggest that the mass spectrum of stars at birth is not a universal constant, but rather a [[variable-initial-mass-function|Variable initial mass function]] that responds sensitively to extreme physical conditions. Top-heavy distributions are systematically favored in environments characterized by low metallicities, high gas pressures, and intense star formation rates, fundamentally altering the radiative feedback, remnant mass, and nucleosynthetic yields of the resulting stellar population.

The paradigm shift away from [[non-universal-initial-mass-function|IMF universality]] accelerated with observations of extreme local starbursts, where photometric and dynamical models required an IMF heavily weighted toward high-mass stars to explain observed supernova rates [Rieke et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...412...99R/abstract) and extreme isotopic ratios in ultraluminous infrared galaxies [Brown et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...879...17B/abstract). This environment-dependent behavior is formally modeled by frameworks such as the Integrated Galactic Initial Mass Function (IGIMF) theory, which posits that a galaxy-wide IMF is the deterministic sum of all its embedded star clusters. Under this framework, the galaxy-wide IMF steepens (becomes top-light) in quiescent dwarf galaxies but becomes robustly top-heavy in starbursts exceeding $\sim 10 M_\odot \rm \, yr^{-1}$ [Weidner et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...625..754W/abstract), [Weidner et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412..979W/abstract). In the cosmological context, a top-heavy phase is essential to explain the nucleosynthetic signatures of the early Universe, including the high $[\alpha/\text{Fe}]$ abundance ratios required by the chemical evolution of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] and the unique pair-instability supernova yields expected from metal-free [[first-star-formation|First star formation]] (Population III).

Because standard extragalactic calibrations assume an invariant IMF, introducing a top-heavy mass distribution profoundly influences our interpretation of the Universe, directly impacting inferred mass-to-light ratios and the reconstruction of the cosmic [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]]. Accounting for top-heavy IMFs at high redshifts alters derived stellar masses by up to a factor of three, helping to resolve long-standing tensions in the observed evolution of the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]]. However, invoking top-heavy IMFs also introduces complex evolutionary paradoxes. Most notably, while massive early-type galaxies require an early top-heavy burst to reproduce their $\alpha$-element enhancements, detailed local kinematic studies reveal that their present-day central cores possess strongly dwarf-rich, bottom-heavy IMFs [Cappellari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.484..485C/abstract), [Martín-Navarro et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.1033M/abstract). Reconciling these distinct phases has driven the development of [[multi-stage-formation|Multi-stage formation]] models, as well as static, concordance "ski slope" IMFs that are simultaneously bottom-heavy and top-heavy [van Dokkum et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...973L..32V/abstract).

Today, constraining the high-mass end of the IMF remains a highly active frontier, particularly in the JWST era. Recent discoveries of surprisingly UV-bright galaxies at $z > 10$ have been widely interpreted as evidence for extreme top-heavy IMFs in the early Universe, though this is heavily debated against alternative explanations like low-luminosity active galactic nuclei. This article outlines the historical development of the top-heavy IMF, detailing the physical conditions that drive it in extreme environments. It reviews the diverse observational methodologies used to probe IMF variations—spanning dynamical mass modeling, gravitational lensing, and chemical abundances—and previews the critical open questions facing the field.

## Historical Development and the Universality Debate

For decades, the IMF Universality Hypothesis dominated astrophysics. Calibrated against local field stars, standard models assumed that the mass spectrum of forming stars was an invariant probability density function, typically characterized by the Salpeter slope of the [[salpeter-initial-mass-function|Salpeter initial mass function]] for masses above $1\, M_\odot$. Early challenges to this paradigm arose in Galactic chemical evolution. Early theoretical work proposed that the characteristic mass of the IMF was higher at earlier epochs due to higher gas temperatures, potentially addressing the G-dwarf problem and resolving discrepancies in cluster metal yields [Larson et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.301..569L/abstract). While a time-varying, top-heavy IMF was occasionally proposed, conventional models found that gas infall with a constant IMF provided a more consistent fit to multiple observational constraints in the Milky Way [Chiappini et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528..711C/abstract), [Chiappini et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASSL..255..565C/abstract). Nevertheless, ongoing Milky Way studies illustrate persistent complexities; recent Galactic evaluations using Cepheids actually favor a composite field-star IMF slope of $\alpha=3.2$, steeper than the canonical Salpeter value [Mor et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...599A..17M/abstract).

The first robust empirical challenges to universality emerged from studies of extreme environments. Modeling of the M82 starburst indicated that reproducing the observed supernova rates and dynamical masses required an IMF heavily weighted toward [[massive-stars|Massive stars]], effectively lacking low-mass stars below $\sim 3\, M_\odot$ [Rieke et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...412...99R/abstract), [Doane et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...419..573D/abstract). Similarly, early dynamical evidence from the young stellar discs surrounding the Milky Way's supermassive black hole, Sgr A*, demonstrated that a top-heavy IMF might be required to suppress low-mass star formation in extreme tidal environments [Nayakshin et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.366.1410N/abstract). However, counter-arguments note that the Galactic Center's luminosity and dynamical mass are also consistent with [[continuous-star-formation|Continuous star formation]] and a canonical IMF, suggesting that extreme top-heavy events may be rare even in extreme tidal environments [Löckmann et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.402..519L/abstract). Regardless, the top-heavy paradigm was supported by observations of ultra-luminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs), where extreme $^{13}\text{CO}/\text{C}^{18}\text{O}$ isotopologue ratios trace the nucleosynthetic yields uniquely produced by an overabundance of massive stars [Brown et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...879...17B/abstract).

In the cosmological context, the universality hypothesis struggled to reconcile the observed evolution of the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]]. The amplitude of the $M_*$--SFR correlation at $z \sim 2$ conflicts with local universe projections unless the IMF evolves toward a more top-heavy form at early epochs [Davé et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.385..147D/abstract). Similarly, the luminosity and color evolution of massive cluster galaxies implies a top-heavy IMF at $z > 3$ to reconcile their mass-to-light ($M/L$) ratios [van Dokkum et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674...29V/abstract). Theoretically, this shift is formalized by the integrated galaxy-wide IMF (IGIMF) framework, which posits that the galaxy-wide IMF is not a universal constant but an environment-dependent function that becomes top-heavy at high star formation rates ($>10\, M_\odot\,\text{yr}^{-1}$) [Weidner et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...625..754W/abstract), [Weidner et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3309W/abstract).

Despite these advances, the literature remains highly active and conflicted regarding massive early-type galaxies (ETGs). Spectroscopic and dynamical studies of local ETG centers unequivocally show a bottom-heavy (dwarf-rich) IMF, correlating strongly with stellar velocity dispersion [Cappellari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.484..485C/abstract), [Ferreras et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.429L..15F/abstract). However, this finding is in direct tension with their high $[\alpha/\text{Fe}]$ chemical abundances, which necessitate a top-heavy phase during their initial formation to produce the required core-collapse supernovae [De Masi et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.2217D/abstract). To resolve this dichotomy, recent models propose a static "ski slope" concordance IMF that is simultaneously bottom-heavy and top-heavy [van Dokkum et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...973L..32V/abstract). This ongoing debate underscores that the question has transitioned from whether the IMF varies, to precisely how it varies across cosmic time and environment.

## The IGIMF Theory and Environment-Dependent Star Formation

The Integrated Galactic Initial Mass Function (IGIMF) theory challenges the paradigm of a universal [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] by positing that a galaxy's overall stellar mass distribution is an environmentally dependent composite. According to this framework, stars form predominantly in embedded clusters rather than uniformly throughout a galaxy. The galaxy-wide IMF (gwIMF) must therefore be calculated as the integral of the individual stellar IMFs over the embedded cluster mass function (ECMF) [Weidner et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...625..754W/abstract), [Pflamm-Altenburg et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..440..269P/abstract). 

A core axiom of the IGIMF theory is the $m_{\rm max}$-$M_{\rm ecl}$ relation, which asserts that the mass of the most massive star ($m_{\rm max}$) in a young cluster is deterministically limited by the total mass of its parent embedded cluster ($M_{\rm ecl}$) [Weidner et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.365.1333W/abstract), [Yan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...670A.151Y/abstract). This physical constraint directly conflicts with standard models that assume stellar masses are stochastically sampled from a scale-invariant probability density function. By suppressing the formation of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] in low-mass clusters, the IGIMF naturally produces a steeper (top-light) high-mass slope for the gwIMF in galaxies that lack massive star clusters [Weidner et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.365.1333W/abstract).

Under the IGIMF framework, the upper mass limit of the ECMF is governed by the global star formation rate (SFR) of the galaxy. In dwarf galaxies or systems with low SFRs ($< 1 M_\odot \rm \, yr^{-1}$), the ECMF is dominated by low-mass clusters, leading to a gwIMF that is deficient in high-mass stars compared to the canonical cluster IMF [Jeřábková et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...620A..39J/abstract). This is corroborated by integrated light analyses of SDSS galaxies, which reveal that lower-luminosity galaxies systematically favor steeper (top-light) IMFs compared to brighter systems [Hoversten et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...675..163H/abstract). Conversely, in extreme starburst environments with SFRs exceeding $\sim 10 M_\odot \rm \, yr^{-1}$ , the formation of very massive clusters ($>10^6 M_\odot$) is enabled. In these dense, high-pressure regimes, crowding and early stellar feedback can alter the embedded cluster IMF itself, producing a top-heavy gwIMF [Weidner et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412..979W/abstract), [Weidner et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3309W/abstract). Direct evidence from the Galaxy and Mass Assembly (GAMA) survey supports this, demonstrating that galaxies with higher star formation rates produce proportionally more massive stars, resulting in flatter IMF slopes [Gunawardhana et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.1647G/abstract). Modern formulations of the IGIMF also incorporate metallicity dependence, predicting that galaxies with high metallicities ($[\rm Fe/H] > 0$) develop bottom-heavy IMFs characterized by an excess of low-mass stars, while early, metal-poor starbursts are robustly top-heavy [Jeřábková et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...620A..39J/abstract), [Yan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...629A..93Y/abstract).

This environment-dependent framework has profound consequences for interpreting observables in [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] and dwarfs alike. Because the fraction of ionizing massive stars varies with the SFR, the IGIMF theory predicts a non-linear relation between $L_{\rm H\alpha}$ luminosity and the underlying SFR. Traditional linear conversions assuming a universal IMF may therefore underestimate the SFR in dwarf galaxies by up to three orders of magnitude [Pflamm-Altenburg et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0709.1473P/abstract), [Pflamm-Altenburg et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.395..394P/abstract). Furthermore, an IGIMF that evolves with a galaxy's [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and metallicity provides a self-consistent mechanism to explain both the top-heavy conditions necessary to reproduce $[\alpha/\rm Fe]$ chemical abundance ratios at high redshift and the high dynamical mass-to-light ratios observed in the centers of local early-type galaxies [Gargiulo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.3820G/abstract), [Recchi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.4168R/abstract). 

Despite its explanatory power, the IGIMF theory remains a subject of active debate in the literature. The central tension lies in the fundamental nature of star formation: whether it is a [[deterministic-star-formation|deterministic]], self-regulated process as required by the $m_{\rm max}$-$M_{\rm ecl}$ relation, or whether the observed deficits of massive stars in low-mass environments simply reflect the statistical expectations of random sampling from a universal IMF [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...670A.151Y/abstract).

## Extreme Environments: Pop III and Starburst Galaxies

The physical conditions defining extreme star-forming environments—such as intense starbursts and the primordial universe—pose significant challenges to the paradigm of a universal [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. In these regimes, high gas pressures, elevated cosmic microwave background (CMB) temperatures, and vanishing metallicities fundamentally alter the fragmentation physics of molecular clouds, skewing the stellar mass distribution toward a [[variable-initial-mass-function|Variable initial mass function]].

In local ultraluminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs) and high-redshift starbursts, extreme SFRs are strongly correlated with a flattened IMF slope. Empirical evidence from ALMA observations of ULIRGs (such as Arp 220) reveals extremely low $^{13}\text{CO}/\text{C}^{18}\text{O}$ isotopologue ratios compared to typical star-forming disks, a chemical signature requiring an excess of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] [Brown et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...879...17B/abstract). Furthermore, the evolution of CNO isotopic ratios in submillimeter galaxies provides another powerful constraint; models require a top-heavy IMF to match observations if the thresholds for [[massive-star-formation|Massive Star Formation]] remain independent of metallicity [Romano et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.2838R/abstract). This top-heavy signature has also been detected in strongly lensed high-redshift galaxies on the main sequence, such as SDSS J0901+1814 at $z \approx 2.26$ [Guo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..136G/abstract). These observations align with IGIMF predictions for systems where the global SFR exceeds $10 \, M_\odot\,\text{yr}^{-1}$ or where local star-formation rate densities surpass $0.1 \, M_\odot\,\text{yr}^{-1}\,\text{pc}^{-3}$ [Weidner et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412..979W/abstract), [Dabringhausen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...747...72D/abstract). Furthermore, semi-analytic models of galaxy formation (e.g., GALFORM) often require a top-heavy IMF in starbursts to simultaneously reproduce the observed number counts of sub-millimeter galaxies at $850 \, \mu\text{m}$ and the $z=0$ K-band luminosity function [Lacey et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.3854L/abstract). Recent Bayesian optimization studies of the GALFORM model have robustly reinforced the necessity of a top-heavy IMF in starbursts to match these sub-millimeter counts [Elliott et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251112550E/abstract). However, the theoretical landscape remains complex; while some frameworks predict top-heavy IMFs, models based on the turbulent Jeans mass predict that extreme densities and Mach numbers in starbursts shift the characteristic mass scale downward, yielding a bottom-heavy IMF with an even steeper high-mass tail [Chabrier et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...796...75C/abstract). Some researchers additionally caution that even extreme IMF variations may be insufficient to explain the extremely hard He II ionizing flux seen in some high-redshift starbursts, suggesting alternative energy sources might be at play [Stanway et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...621A.105S/abstract).

Conditions in the early universe similarly favor altered star formation. During [[first-star-formation|First star formation]], the lack of fine-structure metal line cooling and the elevated temperature floor set by the CMB dramatically increased the Jeans mass of collapsing gas clouds. Consequently, Population III stars are theoretically expected to have formed with a top-heavy IMF. The chemical fossil records of these first stars are preserved in extremely metal-poor halo stars. For instance, the abundance pattern of the star J1010+2358 ($[\text{Fe}/\text{H}] = -2.4$) strongly suggests it may have been enriched by a pure [[pisne-models|pair-instability supernova]] (PISN) descending from a [[very-massive-stars|very massive star]] of $250\text{--}260 \, M_\odot$. However, researchers explicitly caution that the PISN contribution to J1010+2358 could be as low as 10%. Robust constraints on the Pop III IMF depend strictly on confirming its near-purity; if it is not >90% pure, no definitive constraints can be put on the progenitor mass or Pop III IMF [Koutsouridou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962L..26K/abstract). Moreover, binary population synthesis models tracking the abundance patterns of carbon-enhanced metal-poor stars point toward a distinct evolutionary transition from an early IMF dominated by massive stars to the current canonical form [Suda et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.432L..46S/abstract).

Recently, JWST has uncovered an unexpected abundance of UV-bright [[high-mass-galaxies|High-mass galaxies]] at $z > 10$. To resolve the tension between these observations and standard $\Lambda\text{CDM}$ predictions, several theoretical models have invoked an evolving, top-heavy IMF in early, low-metallicity environments [Hutter et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241000730H/abstract), [Cueto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A.138C/abstract). However, the literature remains highly conflicted regarding whether these observations definitively prove a top-heavy IMF. For example, the intense nebular emission in the $z \approx 5.9$ galaxy GS9422 was initially interpreted as requiring an IMF 10 to 30 times more top-heavy than the [[salpeter-initial-mass-function|Salpeter initial mass function]]. Yet, subsequent spectral modeling demonstrated that a composite spectrum of young, metal-poor stars and a low-luminosity Active Galactic Nucleus (AGN) can equally well reproduce the ionizing radiation without requiring an exotic IMF [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969L...5L/abstract). Disentangling the signatures of top-heavy IMFs from AGN activity and varied star-formation histories remains a major observational hurdle for high-redshift astrophysics. In contrast to these extreme high-redshift conditions, detailed star counts in nearby ultrafaint dwarf galaxies generally align with Milky Way-like IMFs today, although a handful of specific systems like Ursa Major II do suggest a more bottom-heavy slope [Filion et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967..165F/abstract).

## Chemical Evolution and Alpha-Enhancement

The elemental abundance ratio $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ serves as a fossil record of the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] of a galaxy. Because $\alpha$-elements (such as O, Mg, and Si) are predominantly synthesized in the short-lived [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] ($\gtrsim 8 M_\odot$) that explode as core-collapse supernovae, their enrichment timescale is on the order of $\sim 10^7$ years. Conversely, iron peak elements are largely produced by Type Ia supernovae, which originate from lower-mass progenitors and exhibit a broader delay-time distribution typically spanning $\sim 10^9$ years.

In the local Universe, [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]]—particularly giant ellipticals—exhibit a pronounced overabundance of $\alpha$-elements relative to iron. While standard chemical evolution models attempt to explain this through rapid, truncated star formation ("downsizing"), these models frequently fail to reproduce the steep, positive correlation between $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ and total stellar mass when assuming a universal IMF, often predicting a flat relationship instead [Fontanot et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.3812F/abstract). Additionally, the two-phase evolution of the mass-metallicity relation—which exhibits a distinct transition around $z \sim 1.5$—can be well explained by a time-dependent IMF with steeper, top-heavy slopes at early epochs [Lian et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.4000L/abstract). Furthermore, evidence from local globular clusters, based on the ratios of 'hydrostatic to explosive' $\alpha$-elements, suggests that the IMF may become systematically deficient in the highest mass stars at higher metallicities [Carretta et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260118885C/abstract).

A top-heavy IMF during the primordial starburst phase offers a robust solution to this tension. By skewing the stellar mass distribution toward higher masses, a top-heavy IMF drastically increases the ratio of core-collapse to Type Ia supernovae. Semi-analytic chemical evolution models implementing a star formation rate (SFR)-dependent [[variable-initial-mass-function|Variable initial mass function]] demonstrate that the extreme SFRs in the progenitors of massive ellipticals yield a top-heavy IMF. This efficiently pumps $\alpha$-elements into the interstellar medium before SNe Ia can significantly dilute the gas with iron, naturally reproducing the positive $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$-mass correlation [Gargiulo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.3820G/abstract), [Fontanot et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.3812F/abstract). Conversely, dwarf galaxies characterized by low SFRs are predicted to form proportionally fewer massive stars, resulting in an $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ plateau up to $0.7$ dex lower than that of the Milky Way [Recchi et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.437..994R/abstract).

The influence of a top-heavy IMF is also evident on larger scales. The intracluster medium of galaxy clusters is observed to contain approximately three times more metal mass per stellar mass than typical local galaxies. Standard IMFs struggle to produce this metal excess. However, chemical evolution models that incorporate a top-heavy IMF populate the early cluster environment with [[very-massive-stars|Very massive stars]]. The subsequent pair-instability supernovae from stars in the $140-260 M_\odot$ range naturally elevate the abundance of intermediate-mass $\alpha$-elements relative to iron, matching intracluster medium observations [Morsony et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441.2134M/abstract).

Reconciling these chemical signatures with dynamical and spectroscopic observations of nearby early-type galaxies—which suggest a dwarf-rich, bottom-heavy IMF in their present-day centers—requires complex evolutionary pathways. Chemical evolution simulations reveal that an invariant IMF cannot simultaneously reproduce both the high $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ ratios and the total metallicity of massive elliptical galaxies [Yan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...629A..93Y/abstract). Instead, the literature explores multiple pathways to resolve this. Some models heavily favor a [[multi-stage-formation|Multi-stage formation]] or time-dependent formation scenario, where an initial top-heavy phase provides the rapid metal enrichment and $\alpha$-enhancement, while a later transition produces the low-mass stars [Fontanot et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.5678F/abstract). Distinct from these time-varying models, other frameworks propose a static "concordance" or "ski slope" IMF. This configuration is not a transitioned state formed later; rather, it is a single, static shape that applies to the entire population of massive galaxies, inherently possessing both an excess of low-mass stars and a top-heavy high-mass slope simultaneously [van Dokkum et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...973L..32V/abstract).

## Observational Probes: M/L Ratios, Lensing, and Transients

Because massive stars are short-lived, inferring a top-heavy [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] in [[evolved-stars|evolved or distant populations]] relies on indirect observational proxies. These methodologies span from measuring dynamical mass-to-light ($M/L$) ratios and gravitational lensing to counting the remnants of massive stars via X-ray binaries and stellar transients.

**Mass-to-Light Ratios and Gravitational Lensing**
Dynamical $M/L$ ratios provide a foundational test for IMF variations. In environments where the observed dynamical mass significantly exceeds the stellar mass inferred from photometric [[simple-stellar-populations|Simple stellar populations]] assuming a universal IMF, a top-heavy IMF is often invoked. The excess mass is attributed to a high fraction of dark stellar remnants (black holes and neutron stars) that contribute mass but negligible light. This phenomenon is notably observed in ultra-compact dwarf galaxies (UCDs) [Dabringhausen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..440..261D/abstract). Furthermore, hydrodynamical simulations predict that this mass-to-light excess correlates strongly with a galaxy's age, metallicity, and central black hole mass [Barber et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.2515B/abstract). Conversely, in disc galaxies, adopting [[bottom-light-initial-mass-function|bottom-light IMFs]] can help match the low $M/L$ ratios required by Tully-Fisher relation simulations, though this can sometimes lead to an overproduction of metals [Portinari et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph..2427P/abstract). Beyond broadband photometry, specific spectral signatures are frequently used; for instance, the Ca II triplet feature in the near-infrared is a critical diagnostic for the low-mass end of the IMF, as its strength is highly sensitive to the IMF slope but remains largely independent of age for old stellar populations [Vazdekis et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.340.1317V/abstract).

In [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], strong gravitational lensing offers an independent, absolute measurement of the total mass within the Einstein radius. Lensing studies of the SLACS and CMASS galaxy samples have constrained the IMF mismatch parameter, $\alpha_{\rm IMF}$ (the ratio of true stellar mass to that inferred from a reference IMF). While spatially resolved spectroscopy often suggests bottom-heavy centers in massive early-type galaxies, lensing data frequently favor lighter IMFs (e.g., a [[salpeter-initial-mass-function|Salpeter initial mass function]] or Chabrier-like distribution) unless substantial dark matter fractions ($\gtrsim 25\%$) are present within the effective radius [Leier et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.3677L/abstract), [Sonnenfeld et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...630A..71S/abstract). To reconcile the low $M/L$ ratios observed at high redshift with local kinematics, recent models have proposed a static "ski slope" or concordance IMF that is simultaneously bottom-heavy and top-heavy [van Dokkum et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...973L..32V/abstract). Additionally, cosmological models suggest that dry mergers can naturally dilute the effective IMF normalization of massive galaxies over time, causing the composite IMF to appear progressively lighter as the galaxy evolves toward $z=0$ [Sonnenfeld et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2397S/abstract).

**Low-Mass X-ray Binaries (LMXBs)**
The overabundance of stellar remnants produced by a top-heavy IMF should directly enhance the formation rate of low-mass X-ray binaries. In UCDs, X-ray observations reveal LMXB frequencies up to an order of magnitude higher than expected for an invariant IMF, providing strong corroborating evidence for a top-heavy [[variable-initial-mass-function|Variable initial mass function]] in dense starbursts [Dabringhausen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...747...72D/abstract). 

However, the literature presents conflicting results when this methodology is applied to the broader population of massive early-type galaxies. Some studies find that the number of LMXBs per $K$-band luminosity is remarkably uniform across nearby early-type galaxies. This result is highly consistent with an invariant IMF and specifically challenges models that predict a systematically varying or top-heavy IMF across the mass sequence of early-type galaxies [Peacock et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...784..162P/abstract).

**Stellar Transients: Supernovae and GRBs**
Core-collapse supernovae (CCSNe) and long gamma-ray bursts (LGRBs) offer direct probes of the massive stellar population. In local starburst environments such as M82 and Arp 220, the observed high Type II supernova rates are most easily reproduced by a top-heavy IMF that is significantly depleted in low-mass stars ($m \lesssim 3 M_\odot$) [Doane et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...419..573D/abstract), [Dabringhausen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...747...72D/abstract). 

On cosmic scales, the volumetric rates of CCSNe and measurements of cosmic far-UV radiation densities are actively used to constrain the high-mass IMF slope ($\alpha$). While the diffuse supernova neutrino background (DSNB) is a proposed future independent probe (likely viable after long-duration surveys), current Bayesian inferences leveraging CCSN and UV constraints yield a cosmic-average slope of $\alpha \approx 1.8-3.2$ at $z=0$, generally consistent with the Salpeter slope ($\alpha = 2.35$), but allowing for moderate shifts depending on the adopted high-mass cutoff and evolutionary models [Aoyama et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946...69A/abstract), [Gabrielli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Univ...10..383G/abstract). Finally, in the early Universe, the chemical abundance patterns of metal-poor halo stars serve as fossil records of ancient stellar explosions. The detection of descendant stars enriched by pure pair-instability supernovae suggests a top-heavy IMF during [[first-star-formation|First star formation]], requiring [[very-massive-stars|very massive progenitors]] of $250-260 M_\odot$ [Koutsouridou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962L..26K/abstract).

## Radial Gradients and Local Variations in Galaxies

Historically, the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] was assumed to be a universal and global property of galaxies. However, recent advances using spatially resolved spectroscopy from integral field units (IFU), such as the Multi Unit Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE), have revealed that the IMF is strongly dependent on local physical conditions, frequently exhibiting significant radial gradients within individual galaxies. Spatially resolved studies demonstrate that the IMF should be regarded as a local, rather than a global, property of the [[stellar-component|Stellar component]] [Martín-Navarro et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.1033M/abstract). Findings from the CALIFA survey similarly demonstrate that the local IMF is tightly coupled to local metallicity, becoming systematically more bottom-heavy in metal-rich stellar populations [Martín-Navarro et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806L..31M/abstract).

In massive early-type galaxies (ETGs), observational evidence consistently shows that variations in the IMF are spatially segregated. The excess of low-mass stars—characterizing a bottom-heavy IMF—is typically confined to the central sub-kiloparsec regions, while the outer regions exhibit an IMF more similar to the Milky Way (e.g., a Kroupa or Chabrier IMF) [Martín-Navarro et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.1033M/abstract), [Mehrgan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..127M/abstract). Findings from the MASSIVE survey explicitly confirm a bottom-heavy IMF in the centers of the most massive ETGs, correlating robustly with both central velocity dispersion and local metallicity [Gu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932..103G/abstract). Dynamical modeling of ETGs using SINFONI and MUSE data reveals that the stellar mass-to-light ratio ($\Upsilon$) increases sharply toward the center on sub-kiloparsec scales. The IMF mass-normalization parameter relative to a Kroupa IMF, $\alpha_{\rm IMF}$, drops from centrally concentrated, Salpeter-like values of $\langle \alpha_{\rm cen} \rangle = 1.54 \pm 0.15$ to Milky Way-like values of $\langle \alpha_{\rm main} \rangle = 1.03 \pm 0.19$ at radii around 1 kpc [Mehrgan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..127M/abstract). 

Full-spectrum fitting corroborates this dynamical evidence, identifying central mass excess factors between $1.5$ and $2.5$ compared to a Kroupa IMF. In these cores, the power-law slope below $0.5 M_\odot$ varies between $1$ and $2.8$ and exhibits strongly negative radial gradients [Parikh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.7338P/abstract). Gravitational lensing constraints further support this highly concentrated variation, indicating that the galactic regions hosting a heavy IMF normalization are significantly smaller than the $5\text{--}10$ kpc scales probed by the Einstein radii of massive background lenses [Sonnenfeld et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...630A..71S/abstract).

The strength of these radial gradients correlates with the total mass and central velocity dispersion ($\sigma$) of the galaxy. While high-$\sigma$ ETGs ($\sim 300$ km/s) exhibit stark radial variations, lower-mass ETGs ($\sigma \sim 100$ km/s) tend to lack significant IMF gradients, displaying shallower, more uniform distributions throughout their volume [Martín-Navarro et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.1033M/abstract), [Zhou et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.5256Z/abstract). However, recent results from the TIMER survey for the spiral galaxy NGC 3351 demonstrate that even Milky Way-like spirals can exhibit systematic, spatially coherent IMF variations across their star-forming regions [Martín-Navarro et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...684A.110M/abstract). 

Two-dimensional IFU maps, such as those produced by the Fornax 3D project, allow researchers to investigate the physical drivers behind these local variations. The literature remains somewhat conflicted on the primary mechanism; while IMF maps generally track radial variations in [[stellar-populations-by-metallicity|Stellar Populations by Metallicity]], metallicity alone cannot fully explain the observed 2D complexity [Martín-Navarro et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...626A.124M/abstract), [Martín-Navarro et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...654A..59M/abstract). For instance, in the massive lenticular galaxy FCC 167, the IMF follows a distinct, less disk-like spatial distribution compared to the local metallicity, suggesting a coupling between the IMF and the internal orbital phase space of the galaxy [Martín-Navarro et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...626A.124M/abstract). Additionally, in relatively metal-poor stellar populations ($[\text{M/H}] \lesssim -0.1$), the slope of the IMF appears to depend on the specific star formation rate at the epoch the stars were formed [Martín-Navarro et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...654A..59M/abstract). In addition to ETGs, spatially resolved spectroscopy from the SAMI survey demonstrates that in star-forming galaxies, the high-mass IMF slope flattens significantly in regions characterized by high star formation rate surface densities [Salvador et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250108510S/abstract).

Ultimately, the dichotomy between bottom-heavy cores and standard outer envelopes aligns with scenarios of [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]], where spatial IMF gradients reflect the different physical conditions of extreme *in situ* core formation versus the later, more quiescent buildup of [[accreted-stellar-populations|Accreted stellar populations]].

## Impact on Galaxy Formation and Reionization

The assumption of a universal [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] fundamentally shapes the interpretation of extragalactic observations. Introducing a top-heavy or evolving IMF significantly alters the derived physical properties of galaxies across cosmic time, resolving several theoretical tensions while complicating the reconstruction of the cosmic star formation history.

**Cosmic Star Formation History and the Main Sequence**
The stellar mass-star formation rate relation (the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]]) exhibits an amplitude evolution from $z=0$ to $z \sim 2$ that challenges models relying on a standard, non-evolving IMF. If the IMF becomes progressively top-heavy at higher redshifts, the tension between the predicted and observed specific star formation rates can be naturally resolved [Davé et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.385..147D/abstract). Because [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] dominate the ultraviolet and H$\alpha$ luminosities used as star formation tracers, a top-heavy IMF implies that a lower total stellar mass is required to produce the observed light. Consequently, standard calibrations that assume a universal IMF likely overestimate the high-redshift [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]]. Accounting for a top-heavy or bottom-light IMF at [[formation-epoch|early epochs]] reduces the inferred volume-averaged cosmic star formation rate density at $z \approx 2-4$ by a factor of $\gtrsim 3$ [van Dokkum et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674...29V/abstract), [Ziegler et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.2471Z/abstract).

**Epoch of Reionization and Early Galaxies**
During the epoch of reionization, the ionizing photon flux was dominated by massive stars. While models incorporating a top-heavy early IMF predict significantly enhanced Lyman-continuum production, it is the intense star formation in low-mass [[early-galaxy-formation|early galaxies]]—rather than metal-free Population III stars directly—that provides the bulk ($\sim 85\%$) of the photons necessary to reionize the intergalactic medium [Mauerhofer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202647M/abstract). Recently, JWST has uncovered an unexpected abundance of extraordinarily UV-bright galaxies at $z > 10$, which has frequently been interpreted as evidence for a top-heavy IMF during early galaxy assembly [Hutter et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241000730H/abstract). 

However, the literature remains highly ambiguous regarding whether a top-heavy IMF is strictly required to explain these early systems. While a top-heavy IMF lowers the stellar mass-to-light ratio, the resulting overabundance of massive stars also produces stronger supernova feedback, which can expel gas and suppress subsequent star formation, ultimately limiting the net increase in UV-bright sources [Cueto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A.138C/abstract). Furthermore, the extreme ionizing spectra of some high-$z$ anomalies (e.g., GS9422) can alternatively be reproduced by a mixture of standard stellar populations and low-luminosity active galactic nuclei without invoking an exotic IMF [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969L...5L/abstract).

**Chemical Enrichment and Galaxy Assembly**
The assembly and chemical enrichment of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] are heavily influenced by the IMF. A top-heavy integrated galactic IMF in high-SFR environments naturally explains the observed $[\alpha/\text{Fe}]$ enhancements in massive early-type galaxies, as the overabundance of massive stars produces a higher yield of $\alpha$-elements via core-collapse supernovae prior to the delayed onset of Type Ia supernovae [Gargiulo et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.3820G/abstract). Such variations in the high-mass slope can alter the cosmic core-collapse supernova rate by a factor of $\sim 2$ relative to universal IMF predictions [Ziegler et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.2471Z/abstract). In the dwarf galaxy regime, adopting a metallicity-dependent IMF that becomes top-heavy at low $Z$ drastically increases the efficiency of both supernova and photoionization feedback. This elevated feedback can reduce the final stellar mass of a dwarf galaxy by up to a factor of 100 while maintaining a constant iron abundance, providing a self-consistent mechanism to populate the faint end of the mass-metallicity relation [Prgomet et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513.2326P/abstract). Furthermore, implementing the IGIMF framework can refine the broader baryonic Tully-Fisher relation across cosmic time, particularly for high-mass galaxies where assuming a constant $M/L$ ratio traditionally leads to substantial stellar mass underestimations [Zonoozi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250712521Z/abstract).

## Recent Advances: The JWST Era and Lensed Stars

The advent of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has revealed an unexpected overabundance of exceptionally UV-bright galaxies at redshifts $z > 10$. This discovery challenges standard theoretical models of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] and has reignited debates regarding the universality of the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] in the early Universe.

To reconcile the observed $z > 10$ UV luminosity function with standard cosmological frameworks, several models invoke a top-heavy or bottom-light IMF. Theoretical frameworks implementing an evolving IMF—one that becomes increasingly top-heavy in environments characterized by high gas densities or low gas-phase metallicities—can successfully reproduce the abundance of UV-bright galaxies at $z=5-15$ [Hutter et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241000730H/abstract), [Mauerhofer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202647M/abstract). By shifting the stellar mass distribution toward massive stars, these models lower the mass-to-light ratio and significantly boost the UV output per unit stellar mass. Similarly, analytical models of the epoch of reionization suggest that while the high-mass slope of the IMF might remain consistent with local measurements, the characteristic mass may shift to a few $M_\odot$, suppressing the formation of low-mass stars and effectively yielding a bottom-light IMF that alleviates tensions with standard cosmology [Lapi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Univ...10..141L/abstract). Additionally, top-heavy IMFs in primordial Pop III clusters are potentially critical for forming the [[smbh-seeding|black hole seeds]] observed in the early Universe [Reinoso et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320415R/abstract).

However, the literature remains explicitly conflicted on whether a top-heavy IMF is the definitive solution to the JWST $z > 10$ luminosity tension. Hydrodynamical simulations suggest that the enhanced UV luminosity produced by a top-heavy IMF is counteracted by correspondingly stronger supernova feedback, which removes gas and drives a slower overall build-up of stellar mass; thus, an evolving IMF alone may be insufficient to explain the bright source counts [Cueto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A.138C/abstract). Alternatively, other semi-analytical approaches successfully resolve the tension by either invoking a top-heavy IMF or adopting [[high-star-formation-efficiency|increasing star formation efficiencies]] at early epochs [Mauerhofer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202647M/abstract). Furthermore, specific high-redshift targets exhibit spectral features that admit alternative physical interpretations. For instance, the $z \approx 5.9$ galaxy GS9422 displays strong nebular line and UV continuum emission initially attributed to an exotic IMF that is 10 to 30 times more top-heavy than the standard [[salpeter-initial-mass-function|Salpeter initial mass function]]. Yet, recent spectral modeling demonstrates that its ionizing radiation can be equally well reproduced by a composite spectrum consisting of young, metal-poor stars and a low-luminosity active galactic nucleus, entirely avoiding the need for a top-heavy IMF [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969L...5L/abstract).

Beyond integrated galactic light, JWST and HST have introduced a powerful new probe for the IMF: individual lensed stars at cosmological distances. In strongly lensed fields, background luminous stars—such as blue supergiants—can be temporarily magnified by factors up to $10^4$ by intervening intracluster medium microlenses, causing them to appear as transient events. The detection rate of these transients is highly sensitive to the abundance of massive stars and, consequently, the high-mass end of the IMF. In an analysis of the strongly lensed "Spock" arc at $z \approx 1$, constraints derived simultaneously from the galaxy's spectral energy distribution and the transient detection rate show no clear excess of supergiants. Instead, the Bayesian likelihood analysis definitively prefers a standard [[salpeter-initial-mass-function|Salpeter initial mass function]] ($\alpha = 2.35$) over a top-heavy IMF ($\alpha = 1$) for this system [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988..178L/abstract).

Ultimately, while JWST has provided unprecedented access to the rest-frame UV and optical spectra of high-$z$ galaxies, conclusive evidence for a universally top-heavy IMF in the early Universe remains elusive, with competing interpretations of the data still under active investigation.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While empirical evidence for a variable or top-heavy [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] has grown increasingly robust in recent years, significant uncertainties remain regarding the physical mechanisms driving this variation and the consistency of different observational probes. It is currently unresolved whether the primary physical driver of IMF variability is metallicity, environmental gas pressure, velocity dispersion, or the localized star formation rate. 

One promising theoretical avenue explores **cosmic ray regulation**, which alters the thermal and chemical state of dense molecular cloud cores. Models incorporating cosmic ray heating predict an integrated galaxy-wide IMF that is simultaneously shallow at the high-mass end and steep at the low-mass end, which could uniquely explain both $\alpha$-element enrichment and the excess of low-mass stars in early-type galaxies [Fontanot et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.5678F/abstract). Concurrently, the role of **stellar feedback**—particularly photoionization, radiation pressure, and core-collapse supernovae—remains a major open question in regulating the upper mass limit of stars. High-resolution hydrodynamical simulations reveal that the choice between modeling feedback via IMF-averaged rates versus explicit sampling of individual massive stars drastically alters the star-forming environment and the efficiency of subsequent star formation [Smith et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502.5417S/abstract). Furthermore, modeling feedback from runaway stars—rather than assuming stars inject energy at a fixed birth location—can significantly increase energy and metal loading factors in galactic outflows, complicating efforts to link IMF variations directly to feedback efficiency [Steinwandel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.1408S/abstract). 

A fundamental debate also persists regarding the mathematical nature of the IMF. Standard textbook theory models the IMF as a universal probability density function from which stars are drawn via **stochastic sampling**. However, recent statistical analyses of the $m_{\rm max}-M_{\rm ecl}$ relation (the mass of the most massive star versus the mass of its host embedded cluster) reject the hypothesis of purely stochastic sampling at a $>4.5\sigma$ confidence level [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...670A.151Y/abstract). These findings suggest that star formation may instead be a strongly self-regulated, [[deterministic-star-formation|deterministic process]] where the maximum stellar mass is strictly bounded by the host cluster's mass and local environment [Weidner et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.365.1333W/abstract). Another perspective emerges from hierarchical fragmentation models, which propose that a Salpeter-like aggregate IMF slope can naturally emerge from individual clumps possessing intrinsically shallower slopes of $\alpha = 2.0$, further complicating the definition of an "underlying" IMF [Oey et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...739L..46O/abstract).

Observationally, there is ongoing tension between different measurement techniques. While spectroscopic and dynamical studies of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] frequently suggest non-universal, bottom-heavy IMFs in their central regions, strong gravitational lensing constraints often conflict with these results. Lensing models have shown that reconciling a [[salpeter-initial-mass-function|Salpeter initial mass function]] with observed lensing masses requires a significant dark matter contribution ($\gtrsim 25\%$) within the effective radius, and they firmly exclude the most extreme bottom-heavy slopes [Leier et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.3677L/abstract). Furthermore, severe **systematic uncertainties** plague spectral synthesis modeling. The inferred IMF slopes are highly degenerate with elemental abundance variations (such as [Na/Fe] or [Mg/Fe]) [Parikh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.7338P/abstract), as well as total metallicity, stellar ages, and structural parameters [Barbosa et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...645L...1B/abstract). Note that significant systematic uncertainties in derived galaxy properties (up to $0.3-0.6$ dex) arise simply from choices in the baseline IMF slope and asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stellar evolution models [Conroy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...699..486C/abstract). Similarly, the slope of the high-mass IMF and the resulting number of Type Ia supernovae remain the dominant sources of uncertainty in numerical chemical evolution predictions [Côté et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...824...82C/abstract). Theoretically, it remains challenging for cloud-scale IMF models to reproduce these extragalactic variations without predicting an unobserved, severely high scatter in the local Milky Way IMF [Guszejnov et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.4852G/abstract). Additionally, there is a theoretical challenge that hierarchical assembly and galaxy mergers may wash out local IMF variations over time. To reproduce the variations observed at $z=0$, the underlying physical relations governing the IMF at high redshifts must be significantly steeper than those currently observed [Blancato et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...845..136B/abstract).

Finally, the recent discovery of unexpectedly luminous, UV-bright galaxies at $z > 10$ by JWST has reinvigorated claims of extreme top-heavy IMFs in the early Universe. However, whether an exotic IMF is strictly required remains highly debated. For instance, the peculiar nebular emission of the $z \approx 5.9$ galaxy GS9422 was initially attributed to an IMF $10-30\times$ more top-heavy than the local neighborhood. Subsequent analyses, however, have shown that its ionizing spectrum can be equally well reproduced by a composite spectrum of young, metal-poor stars mixed with a low-luminosity active galactic nucleus [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969L...5L/abstract). Disentangling extreme [[massive-star-physics|Massive Star Physics]] from alternative high-energy sources in the early Universe remains a critical future direction for the field.
