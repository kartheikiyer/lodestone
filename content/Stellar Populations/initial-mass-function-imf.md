The Initial Mass Function (IMF) is an empirical [[probability-density-function-cosmology|Probability density function (cosmology)]] that describes the relative distribution of stellar masses formed in a single star-forming event. Functioning as a fundamental scaling relation in astrophysics, the IMF bridges the microscopic physics of star formation with the macroscopic observable properties of galaxies, governing their [[galaxy-luminosity-function|integrated luminosities]], [[luminosity-functions-and-scaling-relations|mass-to-light ratios]], and [[chemical-abundances|chemical enrichment]] trajectories. Mathematically, the IMF is conventionally parameterized to capture a steep decline at the high-mass end—first characterized by the [[salpeter-initial-mass-function|Salpeter initial mass function]] [Scalo et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986FCPh...11....1S/abstract)—and a flattening or turnover at the low-mass regime, typically modeled via a [[broken-power-law|Broken power law]] [Kroupa et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007astro.ph..3124K/abstract) or a [[log-normal-distribution|Log-normal distribution]]. These mathematical formulations form the bedrock of [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] codes [Bruzual A et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.11094B/abstract), allowing astronomers to translate the integrated spectral energy distributions of unresolved galaxies into physical quantities like [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass]] and [[global-star-formation-rate|star formation rate]].

For decades, the IMF was treated as a universal constant of nature, robustly invariant across space, time, and galactic environment [Eisenhauer et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001sgnf.conf...24E/abstract). However, high-precision integral-field spectroscopy and [[stellar-dynamics-and-kinematics|Stellar Dynamics and Kinematics]] have increasingly fractured this universality paradigm. Observations of massive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] reveal a strongly [[variable-initial-mass-function|Variable initial mass function]] that correlates with central [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|velocity dispersion]] and local [[metallicity|Metallicity]]. Specifically, the dense cores of these galaxies exhibit a "bottom-heavy" mass spectrum enriched in low-mass dwarf stars, significantly inflating their inferred stellar mass-to-light ratios relative to a standard Milky Way baseline [Cappellari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.484..485C/abstract). 

The physical mechanisms driving these variations remain a subject of intense theoretical debate, transitioning from models of purely stochastic fragmentation to those of [[deterministic-star-formation|Deterministic star formation]] regulated by local cosmic ray density, gas pressure, and radiative feedback. At the macroscopic level, the Integrated Galaxy-wide IMF (IGIMF) theory posits that a galaxy's composite IMF is dynamically linked to its [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]]. This framework predicts a [[top-heavy-imf|Top-heavy IMF]] in extreme starburst environments, naturally reproducing the observed [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]]—such as the scaling of [$\alpha$/Fe] ratios with galaxy mass—without requiring the heavily parameterized outflow rates demanded by invariant IMF models. 

This article explores the mathematical formalisms, observational constraints, and theoretical drivers of the IMF, while detailing the ongoing controversies in the field. Key open questions include severe systematic tensions between dynamically and spectroscopically derived mass limits, as well as conflicting constraints from stellar remnants like X-ray binaries. Furthermore, the launch of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has pushed IMF measurements into the epoch of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]]. Reconciling the abundance of extremely [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] at [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|high redshift]] with the bottom-heavy cores of their local descendants may require complex, time-evolving mass distributions, ensuring the IMF remains one of the most critical and heavily scrutinized variables in charting the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]].

## Mathematical Formulations and Parametric Models

The Initial Mass Function (IMF), conventionally denoted as $\Phi(m)$ or $\xi(m)$, mathematically specifies the mass spectrum of stars at [[stellar-birthrate|birth]]. It is formally defined such that $\Phi(m) dm$ represents the relative number of stars formed with initial masses in the interval $[m, m + dm]$. Traditionally, $\Phi(m)$ is treated as an empirical [[probability-density-function-cosmology|Probability density function (cosmology)]] from which individual [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar masses]] are stochastically sampled. However, the literature is currently conflicted regarding this interpretation; recent observational analyses of the maximum stellar mass versus cluster mass relation reject the stochastic sampling hypothesis at high confidence, suggesting instead that IMF sampling may be a deterministic outcome of [[deterministic-star-formation|Deterministic star formation]] [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...670A.151Y/abstract), [Kroupa et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv211210788K/abstract). 

Regardless of the underlying physics of mass assembly, several parametric models have been established to mathematically describe the shape of the IMF, primarily calibrated using star counts in local Galactic environments. Reproducing these parametric shapes purely from theoretical first principles remains challenging; high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations require sophisticated radiative feedback models to produce numerically converged solutions for the resulting IMF [Federrath et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JPhCS.837a2007F/abstract).

### The Salpeter Power Law
The earliest and simplest formulation is the [[salpeter-initial-mass-function|Salpeter initial mass function]], which describes the distribution of stars more massive than $\sim 1 M_\odot$. It takes the form of a single continuous power law:
$$ \Phi(m) \propto m^{-\alpha} $$
where the exponent is $\alpha = 2.35$ [Scalo et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986FCPh...11....1S/abstract). In observational astronomy, this is frequently expressed in terms of logarithmic mass bins ($d\log m$), yielding a [[logarithmic-slope|Logarithmic slope]] of $\Gamma = \alpha - 1 = 1.35$. Theoretical frameworks suggest that this ubiquitous slope of $\Gamma=1.35$ may be an aggregate result of hierarchical fragmentation. In this model, individual clumps have a shallower intrinsic slope of $\gamma=2$, but the imposition of upper-mass limits steeper the resulting cluster-wide mass distribution to match the Salpeter value [Oey et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...739L..46O/abstract). Modern constraints using supernova rates and UV radiation densities affirm that a Salpeter-like slope ($\alpha \approx 1.8 - 3.2$) remains a robust cosmic-average description for the high-mass tail ($m > 1 M_\odot$) [Aoyama et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946...69A/abstract). However, extrapolating this steep slope to very low masses yields an unphysical infinite [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|total mass]], necessitating models with a flattening or turnover at the low-mass end.

### The Kroupa Broken Power Law
To address the low-mass divergence and align with refined measurements of faint stars, the Kroupa IMF utilizes a [[broken-power-law|Broken power law]] parametrization [Kroupa et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007astro.ph..3124K/abstract). The function is divided into multiple mass regimes, each with its own slope $\alpha_i$:
$$ \Phi(m) \propto m^{-\alpha_i} \quad \text{for } m_{i-1} < m \leq m_i $$
To address the low-mass divergence, the Kroupa IMF is frequently approximated by a two-part broken power-law function in the stellar regime, transitioning to shallower slopes for lower-mass stars [Kroupa et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007astro.ph..3124K/abstract). More recently, chemical enrichment modeling and local Galactic field star counts have identified an additional power-law break in the IMF at approximately $1.4 M_\odot$ [Rybizki et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180208432R/abstract), demonstrating the ongoing refinement of these multi-part parameterizations. This piecewise approach successfully models the observed underabundance of low-mass stars relative to a pure Salpeter projection.

### The Chabrier Log-Normal Distribution
An alternative parametric form is the Chabrier IMF, which models the low-mass turnover as a [[log-normal-distribution|Log-normal distribution]] rather than introducing abrupt power-law breaks. At low masses ($m < 1 M_\odot$), the distribution is defined by a characteristic peak mass $m_c$ and a variance $\sigma^2$:
$$ \Phi(m) \propto \frac{1}{m} \exp \left[ - \frac{(\log m - \log m_c)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right] $$
For $m \ge 1 M_\odot$, it seamlessly transitions back into a high-mass power-law tail $\Phi(m) \propto m^{-\alpha}$. The characteristic mass scale is typically found to be $m_c \approx 0.1 M_\odot$ [Gabrielli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Univ...10..383G/abstract). 

## Historical Development and the Universality Paradigm

The concept of the Initial Mass Function (IMF) originated from early efforts to quantify the mass distribution of newly formed stars. Initial empirical constraints relied heavily on star counts within the solar neighborhood and nearby [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]]. These early stellar counts and subsequent [[stochastic-chemical-evolution-models|chemical evolution]] models in the Milky Way consistently supported a relatively invariant mass distribution, typically parameterized as a power law or a broken power law, such as the [[salpeter-initial-mass-function|Salpeter initial mass function]], or the Kroupa and Chabrier models [Scalo et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986FCPh...11....1S/abstract), [Pagel et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001PASP..113..137P/abstract). 

Observations extended to other local, low-density environments—such as the extreme outer Galaxy, local [[star-clusters|Star clusters]], and [[low-mass-galaxies|dwarf spheroidal galaxies]]—generally yielded an IMF consistent with the standard Milky Way-like form down to $\sim 0.4 M_\odot$ [Wyse et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..193..181W/abstract), [Yasui et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..225Y/abstract). Recent deep Hubble Space Telescope imaging of ultrafaint dwarf galaxies, such as Reticulum II, generally continues to support a Milky Way-like IMF down to $0.2 M_\odot$, although some specific systems like Ursa Major II display a more bottom-heavy mass distribution [Filion et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967..165F/abstract). This local consistency extends to nearby spirals; for instance, recent spatially resolved results from the TIMER survey indicate that the [[late-type-galaxies-and-stellar-populations|spiral galaxy]] NGC 3351 globally follows Milky Way-like IMF expectations despite complex, spatially coherent internal variations [Martín-Navarro et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...684A.110M/abstract). The robust observational similarity of the IMF across these resolved environments led to the long-standing universality paradigm. Under this paradigm, the IMF was treated as a fundamental constant of nature, invariant across space, time, and galactic environment [Elmegreen et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...648..572E/abstract). This assumption of a universal IMF became a cornerstone of no-evolution models and standard [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] techniques, providing a critical baseline for translating galactic luminosities and colors into physical quantities like stellar mass and [[global-star-formation-rate|star formation rates]] [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...39H/abstract), [Greggio et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011spug.book.....G/abstract).

Despite its utility as a first-order approximation, the universality paradigm has been increasingly challenged by high-precision observations of diverse extragalactic environments. The historical development of this shift began when detailed spectroscopic and dynamical studies of massive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] revealed systematic discrepancies with the predictions of a universal IMF. 

In massive early-type galaxies, gravity-sensitive absorption features (such as Na I and TiO$_2$) and spatially resolved kinematics from integral field spectroscopy (e.g., ATLAS$^\mathrm{3D}$, MUSE) indicated [[luminosity-functions-and-scaling-relations|mass-to-light ratios]] that could not be reconciled with a standard Milky Way IMF [Cappellari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.484..485C/abstract), [Ferreras et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.429L..15F/abstract). Instead, the central regions of these high-velocity dispersion galaxies exhibit a "bottom-heavy" IMF, characterized by a significant excess of low-mass stars compared to local disk galaxies [Martín-Navarro et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.1033M/abstract), [Conroy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837..166C/abstract). The steepness of this low-mass slope has been shown to strongly correlate with both the galaxy's central [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|velocity dispersion]] $\sigma$ and its local [[metallicity|Metallicity]] $[\mathrm{Z}/\mathrm{H}]$ [Cappellari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.484..485C/abstract), [Martín-Navarro et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806L..31M/abstract). Furthermore, these variations are often spatially resolved, with the bottom-heavy signature confined predominantly to the central cores (e.g., $< 0.5 R_e$) of the galaxies, while their outer regions transition back toward a shallower, more standard IMF [Martín-Navarro et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.1033M/abstract), [Martín-Navarro et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...626A.124M/abstract).

Further tension has arisen at high redshift, where inconsistencies between the observed cosmic [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and the assembly of stellar mass density challenge the assumption of a static IMF [Davé et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.385..147D/abstract). Reconciling the abundance of massive galaxies observed in the early Universe with their local descendants has led to proposals of an evolving IMF, potentially shifting toward a top-heavy form (an excess of high-mass stars) or a complex "concordance IMF" at early epochs to match luminosity densities and mass-to-light ratios [Kang et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...713.1301K/abstract), [van Dokkum et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240706281V/abstract).

## Stellar Population Synthesis and Spectroscopic Inference

The [[integrated-light-method|Integrated light method]] relies on [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] (SPS) to infer the physical properties of unresolved stellar systems. By convolving stellar evolution tracks, empirical or theoretical spectral libraries, and an assumed initial mass function (IMF), tools such as the [[bruzual-charlot-stellar-population-synthesis-models|Bruzual-Charlot stellar population synthesis models]] generate synthetic spectra that can be compared directly with observations [Bruzual A et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.11094B/abstract). 

To explicitly measure the shape of the IMF, spectroscopists target gravity-sensitive absorption features that distinguish the photometric contributions of low-mass dwarf stars from those of highly luminous, evolved giant stars. Because low-mass stars ($m \lesssim 0.3 M_\odot$) possess significantly higher surface gravities than giant stars, features such as the Na I doublet at $\lambda 8190$ and the TiO$_2$ bandhead at $\lambda 6230$ are highly sensitive to the dwarf-to-giant ratio in the stellar population [Spiniello et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753L..32S/abstract). An enhancement in the equivalent widths of these specific lines indicates a higher fractional contribution of low-mass stars, which corresponds to a "bottom-heavy" IMF.

Modern constraints increasingly rely on [[full-spectrum-fitting|Full-spectrum fitting]] rather than isolated line indices. This technique compares high signal-to-noise spectra ($S/N \gtrsim 300 \text{ \AA}^{-1}$) against comprehensive grids of [[simple-stellar-populations|Simple stellar populations]] (SSPs) generated with state-of-the-art population synthesis codes [Ferreras et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.429L..15F/abstract). The use of flexible, non-parametric IMF models applied to deep spectra of massive early-type galaxies has revealed continuous rises in the [[mass-distribution-and-functions|stellar mass distribution]] toward the hydrogen-burning limit. For instance, the core of the [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|massive elliptical galaxy]] NGC 1407 exhibits a "super-Salpeter" IMF slope [Conroy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837..166C/abstract), defined by the power-law parameterization:
$$ \frac{dn}{dm} \propto m^{-x} $$
where $x \approx 2.7$, significantly steeper than the standard Salpeter value of $x = 2.35$.

However, the spectroscopic inference of the IMF is subject to severe methodological degeneracies and physical uncertainties. The literature is currently in tension regarding whether the observed spectral variations are uniquely driven by the IMF. The prominent absorption features used to constrain the dwarf-to-giant ratio are also highly sensitive to individual [[chemical-abundances|elemental abundances]]. Specifically, variations in [$\alpha$/Fe] and [Na/Fe] can mimic the spectroscopic signatures of a bottom-heavy IMF, creating discrepancies between population synthesis estimates and orbit-based dynamical mass-to-light ($M/L$) measurements [Parikh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.7338P/abstract). 

Furthermore, assumptions about the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] play a critical role. Assuming a non-universal, velocity-dispersion-dependent IMF slope significantly alters derived star formation histories, particularly by reducing the inferred light contribution from old stellar populations in massive ellipticals [Ferré-Mateu et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431..440F/abstract). Additionally, stochastic or bursty star formation histories can produce features in the present-day mass function that may be misinterpreted as intrinsic IMF variations if a constant SFR is assumed [Elmegreen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...636..149E/abstract). 

## The Integrated Galaxy-wide Initial Mass Function (IGIMF) Theory

The Integrated Galaxy-wide Initial Mass Function (IGIMF) theory posits that the [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass distribution]] of an entire galaxy differs fundamentally from the canonical initial mass function (IMF) observed in individual star-forming regions. Because stars form predominantly in embedded [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] rather than being uniformly distributed, the galaxy-wide IMF (gwIMF) must be computed as the integral of the stellar IMFs of all individual clusters over the embedded cluster mass function (ECMF) [Weidner et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...625..754W/abstract), [Pflamm-Altenburg et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..440..269P/abstract).

Mathematically, the IGIMF framework hinges on the observation that the mass of the most massive star in a cluster, $m_{\max}$, is tightly correlated with the total mass of the cluster, $M_{\text{ecl}}$. This $m_{\max}$--$M_{\text{ecl}}$ relation challenges the traditional interpretation of the IMF as a purely stochastic probability density function. Instead, it supports a framework of [[deterministic-star-formation|Deterministic star formation]], wherein feedback from massive stars self-regulates further cloud fragmentation [Weidner et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.365.1333W/abstract), [Kroupa et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv211210788K/abstract), [Yan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...670A.151Y/abstract). Consequently, a composite population of many low-mass clusters will yield a steeper high-mass IMF slope than a single massive cluster of the same total mass, simply because the low-mass clusters physically cannot form the most massive stars [Weidner et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.365.1333W/abstract).

A direct consequence of the IGIMF theory is that the galaxy-wide IMF becomes a dynamic function of the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] (SFR). In environments with high SFRs, the formation of massive clusters is more frequent, producing a [[top-heavy-imf|top-heavy]] global IMF characterized by a flatter high-mass slope [Gunawardhana et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.1647G/abstract), [Weidner et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3309W/abstract). Conversely, in dwarf or low surface brightness galaxies with SFRs below $\sim 10^{-2} \, M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$, the IGIMF becomes "top-light," significantly suppressing the formation of massive O-stars.

Recent extensions of the IGIMF framework also incorporate [[metallicity|Metallicity]] dependencies. Models indicate that while galaxies with low metallicity ($[\text{Fe/H}] < 0$) and high SFRs are top-heavy, metal-enriched environments ($[\text{Fe/H}] > 0$) favor a "bottom-heavy" IGIMF with an excess of low-mass stars [Jeřábková et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...620A..39J/abstract). This behavior provides a physical mechanism for the systematic variations observed in the dense, metal-rich cores of massive early-type galaxies.

The application of the IGIMF to galactic chemical evolution and semi-analytic models resolves several lingering tensions in astrophysics. Traditional models assuming a universal IMF often struggle to match the $[\alpha/\text{Fe}]$ abundance ratios of massive galaxies without invoking excessively high gas outflow rates. By employing an SFR-dependent IGIMF, models naturally reproduce the observed mass-$[\alpha/\text{Fe}]$ relation, as the intense early starbursts of massive galaxies produce proportionally more [[core-collapse-supernova-rate|Type II supernovae]] progenitors [Recchi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASSP...28..151R/abstract), [Recchi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.4168R/abstract), [Fontanot et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.3812F/abstract). Furthermore, the IGIMF framework offers self-consistent corrections to the [[sfr-stellar-mass-correlation|SFR-Stellar Mass correlation]] over cosmic time, providing more accurate inferences of [[integrated-galaxy-properties|Integrated galaxy properties]] and [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass assembly histories]] than invariant IMF assumptions [Fontanot et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.3812F/abstract), [Yan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv211208141Y/abstract).

## Systematic IMF Variations in Early-Type Galaxies

The assumption of a universal initial mass function, long motivated by observations of the Solar neighborhood, has been robustly challenged in the regime of massive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] (ETGs). High-precision integral-field spectroscopy and [[stellar-dynamics-and-kinematics|Stellar Dynamics and Kinematics]] modeling have demonstrated a systematic variation of the IMF as a function of the host galaxy's physical properties. Specifically, massive ETGs exhibit a "bottom-heavy" IMF—an excess of low-mass dwarf stars compared to the Milky Way standard—that substantially increases their total [[luminosity-functions-and-scaling-relations|stellar mass-to-light ratios]] ($\Upsilon_*$) [Cappellari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.484..485C/abstract).

Evidence for these systematic variations stems primarily from two independent techniques: dynamical modeling and the analysis of gravity-sensitive absorption features in the integrated spectra of [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]]. Features such as the Na I doublet ($\lambda 8190$) and TiO bands ($\lambda 6230$) are strong in the atmospheres of low-mass dwarfs ($M < 0.3 M_\odot$) and weak in giants. Fits to these indices using [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models indicate that the IMF slope steepens systematically with central [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|velocity dispersion]] $\sigma$ and [[metallicity|Metallicity]] [Ferreras et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.429L..15F/abstract), [Martín-Navarro et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806L..31M/abstract). However, while massive ETGs show up to a factor of four variation in their stellar mass-to-light ratios, compact low-dispersion systems like [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] and the dwarf elliptical M32 exhibit only about a 50% variation.

Spatially resolved observations reveal that this IMF variation is not a global property, but is instead strongly localized. The bottom-heavy signature is generally confined to the dense inner cores ($r < 0.5 R_e$) of [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|High-mass early-type galaxies]], transitioning to shallower, Milky Way-like slopes in the outer envelopes [Martín-Navarro et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.1033M/abstract), [Feldmeier-Krause et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923...65F/abstract). Spatially resolved IMF maps of 23 [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|quiescent galaxies]] from the Fornax 3D project further reveal that IMF variations track internal metallicity structures, though the inferred IMF slopes for metal-poor populations also depend on the specific [[normalized-star-formation-rate|star formation rate]] [Martín-Navarro et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...654A..59M/abstract).

## Environmental Drivers: Density, Metallicity, and Cosmic Rays

In contrast to idealized universal models, recent theoretical and observational frameworks propose that the initial mass function (IMF) is dynamically regulated by the local interstellar environment. The thermodynamic balance of dense star-forming gas—dictated by [[metallicity|Metallicity]], cosmic ray energy density, and the available [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]]—sets the characteristic fragmentation scale and shapes the resulting mass spectrum.

Metallicity strongly influences the cooling efficiency of molecular clouds. More metal-enriched gas cools faster, which lowers the Jeans mass and favors the fragmentation of lower-mass cores. This theoretical expectation aligns with the observed "bottom-heavy" IMFs in the central, metal-rich regions of massive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] (ETGs), as discussed previously, where the low-mass IMF slope correlates tightly with local metallicity ($[Z/H]$) and central [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|velocity dispersion]] ($\sigma$) [Martín-Navarro et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806L..31M/abstract), [Feldmeier-Krause et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923...65F/abstract). 

In dense molecular clouds where optical and far-ultraviolet radiation are heavily shielded, Cosmic rays act as the fundamental thermodynamic regulators. In compact starbursts or merger environments, the cosmic ray energy density $U_{\rm CR}$ can exceed the Galactic average by factors of $\sim 10^3$. This intense cosmic-ray heating raises the minimum temperature of the molecular gas, thereby increasing the Jeans mass and suppressing the formation of low-mass stars. Consequently, cosmic-ray-dominated regions are predicted to yield a [[top-heavy-imf|Top-heavy IMF]], offering a physical mechanism for the excess of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] inferred in vigorous starbursts [Papadopoulos et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASSP...34...41P/abstract), [Fontanot et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.5678F/abstract).

On macroscopic scales, the galaxy-wide IMF (gwIMF) appears strongly dependent on the global [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] (SFR) and ambient density. Under the Integrated Galactic IMF (IGIMF) framework, galaxies with high SFRs produce proportionally more massive stars, resulting in a flatter, top-heavy high-mass slope [Gunawardhana et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.415.1647G/abstract), [Weidner et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3309W/abstract). Implementing these SFR- and metallicity-dependent IMFs into [[stochastic-chemical-evolution-models|Stochastic chemical evolution models]] and semi-analytical models reveals that top-heavy variations at high star formation rates actually have a larger impact on the overall properties of the galaxy population than the bottom-heavy variations found in massive systems [Fontanot et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442.3138F/abstract). Furthermore, the underlying assumption that stellar masses are drawn stochastically from a universal probability density function is actively challenged by the physics of [[massive-star-formation|Massive Star Formation]]. Statistical analyses of the relationship between the maximum stellar mass $m_{\rm max}$ and the embedded cluster mass $M_{\rm ecl}$ reject pure stochastic sampling at high confidence [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...670A.151Y/abstract).

## Impact on Galactic Chemical Evolution and Nucleosynthesis

The initial mass function fundamentally governs the [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] and the overall return of [[chemical-elements|Chemical elements]] to the interstellar medium (ISM). Because a star's evolutionary timeline and nucleosynthetic output are strictly dictated by its birth mass, the IMF slope determines the ratio of short-lived [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] to long-lived low-mass stars. Massive stars ($M \gtrsim 8 M_\odot$) end their lives as core-collapse supernovae, releasing large quantities of $\alpha$-elements (e.g., O, Mg, Si, Ca) on short timescales. Conversely, low- to intermediate-mass stars enrich the ISM primarily through asymptotic giant branch winds and, eventually, Type Ia supernovae, which are the dominant source of iron-peak elements. 

Observational evidence indicates that massive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] exhibit a strong enhancement in $[\alpha/\rm{Fe}]$. Under the assumption of a universal IMF, this enhancement is traditionally attributed to "downsizing"—a rapid star formation timescale that exhausts the gas supply before Type Ia supernovae can contribute significant iron. However, chemical evolution models demonstrate that the IMF itself likely plays a central role. In the IGIMF framework, galaxies with extreme star formation rates develop a top-heavy global IMF. This overabundance of massive stars in the earliest [[stellar-generations|Stellar generations]] naturally elevates the $\alpha$-element yield relative to iron, successfully reproducing the observed $[\alpha/\rm{Fe}]$-stellar mass correlation without relying solely on timescale arguments [Fontanot et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.3812F/abstract).

The choice of IMF also dictates the total metal yield, $y$, which is the mass fraction of newly synthesized heavy elements returned to the ISM per generation of stars. In a simple [[closed-box-model|Closed box model]] of chemical evolution, the metallicity of the gas relates directly to the gas mass fraction and this total metal yield. Empirical look-back models applied to hydrodynamic simulations demonstrate that metallicity increases with the ratio of stellar mass to gas mass, confirming these simple chemical evolution predictions [Kudritzki et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910...87K/abstract). Adopting a variable IMF mitigates this tension. Because dwarf galaxies with low star formation rates naturally exhibit a deficit of massive stars, their intrinsic metal yields are inherently lower [Recchi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.4168R/abstract). Further complexity arises from understanding the exact [[nucleation-fraction|Nucleation fraction]]—the efficiency with which cooling gas and newly ejected metals precipitate into successive stars or dust. 

## The IMF in Extreme Environments and at High Redshift

Although the IMF is historically treated as a universal probability density function, extreme physical conditions can drive significant deviations from the canonical [[salpeter-initial-mass-function|Salpeter initial mass function]]. In environments characterized by extraordinarily high gas densities and star formation rates (SFRs), such as those undergoing [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|Starburst Galaxy Evolution]] or major mergers, the IMF is frequently observed and theorized to be biased toward massive stars. Theoretical models suggest that extreme cosmic ray energy densities in compact starbursts increase the Jeans mass and suppress low-mass fragmentation, naturally yielding a [[top-heavy-imf|Top-heavy IMF]] [Papadopoulos et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASSP...34...41P/abstract). 

During [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] and throughout [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]], the assumption of a universal IMF introduces severe tensions between the observed [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|cosmic star formation history]] and the assembly of stellar mass. Standard invariant IMFs often overpredict the accumulation of stellar mass relative to the integral of the cosmic SFR density. To resolve this, researchers have proposed an evolving IMF that shifts toward higher characteristic masses—manifesting as a [[bottom-light-initial-mass-function|Bottom-light initial mass function]] or a top-heavy IMF—at earlier cosmic epochs [Davé et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.385..147D/abstract). Local observational evidence provides complementary support for such a shift; the high incidence of s-process enriched stars among metal-poor [[stellar-halos|halo populations]] suggests that the IMF in the early Galaxy was indeed shifted toward intermediate and higher masses [Lucatello et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...625..833L/abstract). Adopting such an SFR-dependent IMF brings the [[star-forming-main-sequence|star-forming sequence]] into better agreement with the observed mass function [Ziegler et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.2471Z/abstract).

However, the literature remains highly conflicting regarding the IMF of high-redshift [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]]. Recent ultra-deep spectroscopic observations of nine massive, [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|quiescent galaxies]] at $z \sim 0.7$ reveal gravity-sensitive absorption features indicating a distinct excess of low-mass stars. Fitting these spectra requires steep, bottom-heavy IMFs, which scale up the inferred stellar masses of these intermediate-redshift galaxies by factors of $3-4$ compared to a standard Milky Way IMF [Cheng et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260120864C/abstract). To reconcile these conflicting requirements, some authors have proposed a "concordance IMF" compromise [van Dokkum et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240706281V/abstract). Such a distribution assumes a "ski-slope" shape: it is simultaneously bottom-heavy and top-heavy, illustrating that the IMF must be treated as a dynamic variable in precision [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models.

## Dynamical vs. Spectroscopic Mass Tensions

In unresolved [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], the Initial Mass Function (IMF) is traditionally constrained through two independent techniques: full-spectrum absorption line spectroscopy and orbit-based [[stellar-dynamics-and-kinematics|Stellar Dynamics and Kinematics]]. Spectroscopic approaches rely on gravity-sensitive absorption features to isolate the flux contribution of low-mass dwarf stars from that of giants [Spiniello et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753L..32S/abstract). Conversely, dynamical models measure the total mass-to-light ratio within the half-light radius to infer the stellar mass [Cappellari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.484..485C/abstract). Both methods generally agree that the central regions of massive galaxies exhibit a "bottom-heavy" IMF—an excess of low-mass stars—that correlates strongly with the central [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|velocity dispersion]] $\sigma$ and local [[metallicity|Metallicity]]. The recognition of this [[variable-initial-mass-function|Variable initial mass function]] fundamentally alters the interpretation of [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] and [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]] across the galaxy population.

Despite this qualitative consensus, severe quantitative tensions exist between spectroscopic and dynamical mass estimates. Spectroscopic fitting often yields stellar mass-to-light ratios that systematically exceed the total dynamical ratio derived from orbit-based models, sometimes requiring the unphysical condition that the stellar mass exceeds the total dynamical mass [Parikh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.7338P/abstract). The resolution of this tension intrinsically dictates the inferred [[galaxy-mass-fractions|central dark matter fractions]] in early-type galaxies [Tortora et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022FrASS...8..197N/abstract). Furthermore, [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]] applied to composite spectra has demonstrated that approximating a galaxy's [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] with [[simple-stellar-populations|Simple stellar populations]] can bias the inferred IMF toward artificially steep slopes [Dries et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.3500D/abstract). 

Independent constraints on [[galaxy-mass-fractions|Galaxy mass fractions]] derived from low-mass X-ray binary (LMXB) populations further complicate the picture. LMXB frequencies in massive galaxies are consistent with an invariant, Milky Way-like IMF, directly contradicting the dwarf-enriched power laws inferred from optical spectroscopy [Peacock et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...784..162P/abstract). 

## Open Questions and Future Directions with JWST

Although local star counts and kinematic models have established a robust baseline for the Initial Mass Function (IMF), the physical mechanisms driving IMF variability—whether gas [[metallicity|Metallicity]], central [[global-star-formation-rate|velocity dispersion]], or the integrated star formation rate—remain a subject of intense debate [Martín-Navarro et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806L..31M/abstract). Resolving these tensions is critical for accurately modeling [[mass-evolution|Mass evolution]] and the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]].

One major unresolved issue is the universality of the high-mass end of the IMF. Constraints derived from core-collapse supernova rates yield a power-law index of $\alpha \simeq 1.8-3.2$ [Aoyama et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946...69A/abstract). Furthermore, extracting the intrinsic high-mass IMF is heavily complicated by [[stellar-multiplicity|Stellar multiplicity]]. Unresolved binary systems systematically bias photometric mass distributions and [[luminosity-functions-and-scaling-relations|luminosity functions]], requiring complex forward-modeling of the binary fraction to recover the true mass spectrum [Kroupa et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241007311K/abstract). Additionally, the formation of [[very-massive-stars|Very massive stars]] may be a deterministic and highly self-regulated process tied to the dense gas reservoir [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...670A.151Y/abstract).

The launch of JWST has pushed IMF constraints to the edge of the observable universe, immediately highlighting profound tensions in [[early-galaxy-formation|early galaxy assembly]]. JWST has unveiled a population of extremely [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] at $z > 7$ that assembled far faster than standard cosmological models predict [Cheng et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260120864C/abstract). If the IMF in these intense starbursts is top-heavy, their true stellar masses would be lower, potentially resolving the tension. However, ultra-deep spectra of massive [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|quiescent galaxies]] at $z \sim 0.7$, which are the direct descendants of these early systems, reveal bottom-heavy IMFs. This excess of low-mass stars increases their inferred stellar masses by a factor of $3-4$, severely exacerbating the theoretical tension [Cheng et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260120864C/abstract).

Breaking the degeneracies between age, metallicity, dust, and IMF shape in integrated light will require highly precise, spatially resolved spectroscopy across a broader range of cosmic epochs [Dries et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463..886D/abstract). These advances, combined with more sophisticated [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models, will ultimately determine whether the IMF is a universal constant or a dynamic, environment-dependent variable.
