## Article

**Stellar Dynamics and Kinematics** is the branch of astrophysics dedicated to studying the collective gravitational motion of stars within galaxies, [[star-clusters|star clusters]], and other dense stellar environments. At its theoretical core, the field treats large populations of stars as a macroscopic fluid flowing through [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]]. Depending on the physical density of the environment, stellar systems operate in one of two distinct dynamical regimes. In vast, extended systems like [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] and elliptical halos, the timescale for direct stellar encounters vastly exceeds the age of the Universe; such systems are governed by [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]] and are mathematically described by the [[vlasov-poisson-equation|Vlasov-Poisson equation]], wherein stars orbit within a smooth, self-consistently generated mean-field gravitational potential [Binney et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987gady.book.....B/abstract). Conversely, in dense environments such as [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] and [[galaxy-nuclei-and-complex-morphologies|Galaxy Nuclei and Complex Morphologies]], the discrete granularity of the stellar distribution cannot be ignored. Here, cumulative small-angle scatterings drive [[two-body-relaxation|Two-body relaxation]], dictating internal processes such as energy equipartition, mass segregation, and core collapse [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract).

Historically rooted in steady-state models of monolithic galactic collapse [Lindblad et al. (1959)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1959HDP....53...21L/abstract), the field has evolved into a heavily substructured, dynamic framework that synthesizes kinematic data with chemical abundances to reconstruct galactic assembly—an approach broadly termed [[chemo-dynamics|chemo-dynamics]] or galactic archaeology. This article explores how tracing stellar orbits and velocities unveils the underlying mass distributions of galactic structures. It examines the kinematics of rotationally supported disks, where [[nearly-flat-rotation-curves|Nearly flat rotation curves]] provide critical evidence for extended dark matter halos, while simultaneously exploring alternative frameworks like Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) [Sanders et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...473..117S/abstract). The text also reviews internal dynamical processes, such as the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] and radial [[stellar-migration|Stellar migration]], which continuously redistribute angular momentum and reshape galaxies over gigayear timescales. For pressure-supported systems like elliptical galaxies, the article details how discrete [[stellar-tracers|Stellar tracers]] and sophisticated orbit superposition models are utilized to map dark matter profiles and central supermassive black holes.

The modern era of stellar kinematics is characterized by a paradigm shift away from textbook assumptions of hydrostatic and dynamical equilibrium. Unprecedented astrometric data from missions like Gaia and advanced integral field unit (IFU) spectroscopy have revealed that galaxies are actively undergoing continuous phase mixing, exhibiting intricate [[stellar-substructure|Stellar substructure]] and non-equilibrium [[phase-space-dynamics|Phase Space Dynamics]] such as [[moving-groups|moving groups]] and phase spirals. Finally, this article reviews the persistent open questions at the frontier of the field. These include debates over the physical mechanisms driving variations in the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] across different galactic environments, the computational challenge of disentangling the precise timing of external satellite perturbations from internal secular resonances, and the ongoing search for kinematic signatures that can definitively distinguish between triaxial dark matter halos and modified theories of gravity.

## Foundations of Stellar Kinetic Theory

The statistical mechanics of large stellar populations is formalized through stellar kinetic theory, a mathematical framework sharing deep foundational analogies with the kinetic theory of plasmas [Hamilton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhPl...31l0901H/abstract). Unlike terrestrial fluid dynamics, where local atomic collisions rapidly enforce thermodynamic equilibrium, stellar systems frequently exhibit mean free paths that vastly exceed the physical dimensions of the system. Consequently, the macroscopic behavior and structural stability of these systems are dictated by the distribution of orbits in [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]].

For massive, extended systems such as elliptical galaxies and galactic disks, the timescale for direct gravitational encounters is orders of magnitude longer than the age of the Universe. Such systems operate entirely in the regime of [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]] [Binney et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987gady.book.....B/abstract). The state of the system is described by a phase-space distribution function $f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v}, t)$, which specifies the number density of stars in a differential volume element. By Liouville's theorem, the flow of this stellar "fluid" is incompressible in phase space. In the mean-field approximation, the continuous evolution of $f$ is governed by the collisionless Boltzmann equation, commonly known as the [[vlasov-poisson-equation|Vlasov-Poisson equation]]. This physical framework dictates that the motion of stars is guided by a smooth gravitational potential that is self-consistently generated by the broader mass distribution via the Poisson equation. This coupled framework underpins the construction of equilibrium distribution functions for stationary galaxy models. Alternatively, exploring maximum entropy frameworks, thermodynamic models of disk galaxies treat these systems as microcanonical ensembles, successfully yielding lognormal surface density distributions that naturally match observed rotation curves [Marr et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Galax...8...12M/abstract). However, standard textbook models often assume steady-state equilibrium, a premise successfully challenged by modern kinematic surveys. Current evidence demonstrates that galactic disks are frequently in a state of non-equilibrium [[phase-space-dynamics|Phase Space Dynamics]], exhibiting coherent spiral density waves and phase-mixing phenomena resulting from recent dynamical perturbations [Frankel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240707149F/abstract). 

Conversely, in dense environments such as [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] and galactic nuclei, the discrete granularity of the gravitational field cannot be ignored. In these systems, cumulative small-angle scatterings alter individual stellar trajectories over time, a mechanism known as [[two-body-relaxation|Two-body relaxation]]. The efficiency of this process is parameterized by the [[relaxation-timescale|Relaxation timescale]], which directly depends on the dynamical time and the total number of stars in the system. Because the relaxation timescale in globular clusters is often shorter than the Hubble time, these systems act as natural laboratories for collisional dynamics, driving phenomena such as mass segregation, core collapse, and the preferential evaporation of stars into tidal tails [Meylan et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000chun.proc...76M/abstract).

Chandrasekhar’s early formulation of dynamical friction remains a cornerstone for understanding these collisional energy exchanges [Heggie et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011BASI...39...69H/abstract). To accurately model the long-term structural evolution of these collisional systems, the strictly collisionless Vlasov formulation must be augmented with encounter terms, typically yielding a Fokker-Planck equation that captures the slow diffusion of stars through phase space [Merritt et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006RPPh...69.2513M/abstract). 

These fundamental collisionless and collisional principles extend beyond isolated systems to complex environmental interactions. For example, the "tidal stirring" model demonstrates how interactions with a massive host galaxy can transform rotationally supported progenitor dwarf disks into prolate dwarf spheroidal galaxies, yielding varying kinematic profiles depending entirely on the observer's line-of-sight orientation [Łokas et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...708.1032L/abstract). For smaller-scale stellar structures, determining dynamics requires specialized formalisms; describing the kinematic evolution of stellar associations, for instance, heavily relies on the application of Galactic epicycle theory and advanced numerical approaches like the corrected convergent point method [Makarov et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.352.1199M/abstract). Together, these regimes of kinetic theory form the essential physical basis for all broader models of [[stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|Stellar and Galactic Dynamics]].

## Historical Evolution of Galactic Dynamical Models

The historical evolution of galactic cosmogony is characterized by a profound shift from steady-state, monolithic collapse models to dynamic, heavily substructured paradigms driven by hierarchical galaxy formation. In the mid-20th century, kinematic data of the Milky Way largely supported the classical Eggen, Lynden-Bell, and Sandage (ELS) model, which posited that galaxies formed via a rapid, dissipative collapse of a singular protogalactic cloud [Gilmore et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ARA&A..27..555G/abstract), [Schweizer et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998giis.conf..105S/abstract). Early observational efforts to map global galactic kinematics, such as the [[lick-astrographic-survey|Lick astrographic survey]], utilized proper motions measured against background galaxies to constrain these early models of uniform rotation and collapse [Vasilevskis et al. (1973)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1973VA.....15..145V/abstract). 

Under the monolithic framework, stellar kinematics were assumed to be smooth and well-mixed. Theoretical treatments of [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]] approximated the galactic potential as a smooth function, and the [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]] distribution function was treated as largely time-independent. For an equilibrium system, this implies that the stellar distribution satisfies the steady-state [[vlasov-poisson-equation|Vlasov-Poisson equation]], maintaining an invariant form without explicit dependence on time.

However, this steady-state picture was progressively dismantled by accumulating kinematic and chemical evidence. The observation of distinct sub-populations in the [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] of the Milky Way and Andromeda revealed varying metallicities and rotation vectors, suggesting that the halos of massive galaxies were assembled piece-meal from multiple distinct progenitors over extended timescales [Rodgers et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApJ...283L...5R/abstract), [Li et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260105380L/abstract). During these [[galaxy-merger-physics|merging events]], rapid fluctuations in the gravitational potential induce violent relaxation—a process that partially erases the initial kinematic memory of the merging bodies while scattering stars into extended halos and tidal tails [Barnes et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998giis.conf..275B/abstract).

The advent of massive, high-precision surveys (such as SDSS and Gaia) definitively established hierarchical galaxy formation as the dominant driver of galactic architecture, revealing that galaxies are not in a dynamical steady state [Ivezić et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..251I/abstract). Modern kinematic modeling relies heavily on orbital actions $\mathbf{J} = (J_r, L_z, J_z)$, which serve as adiabatic invariants under slow potential changes but are disrupted by discrete merger events and secular [[stellar-migration|Stellar migration]] [Beane et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...867...31B/abstract). By mapping these actions alongside chemical abundances, astronomers have uncovered surviving kinematic fossils, shifting the field toward [[phase-space-dynamics|Phase Space Dynamics]] models that emphasize continuous phase mixing.

Despite a strong consensus around hierarchical assembly, tensions remain regarding the precise origins of specific kinematic features. Prominent non-equilibrium structures in the solar neighborhood indicate ongoing phase mixing from past perturbations, definitively dismantling the assumption of a static disk [Frankel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240707149F/abstract). However, the literature remains actively engaged in determining whether such spiral structures in phase space are the exclusive signatures of external satellite impacts or if they can also be generated internally by secular bar resonances. Consequently, contemporary dynamical models must continually balance the roles of slow secular evolution and violent hierarchical accretion to accurately reproduce the complex kinematics observed in modern surveys.

## Dynamics of Collisional Systems: Globular and Open Clusters

Unlike the broader galaxy, which is largely governed by [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]], dense [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] operate in a distinctly collisional regime. In these environments, the [[relaxation-timescale|Relaxation timescale]]—the time required for [[two-body-relaxation|Two-body relaxation]] to significantly alter a star's initial orbit—is often shorter than the age of the Universe [Meylan et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000chun.proc...76M/abstract). Consequently, both open and [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] serve as fundamental astrophysical laboratories for observing non-equilibrium dynamical processes, including stellar collisions, mass segregation, core collapse, and tidal disruption.

As a cluster evolves, continuous multi-body encounters drive the system toward a state of energy equipartition. Through this process, massive stars systematically lose kinetic energy and sink toward the bottom of the potential well, a phenomenon known as mass segregation. Conversely, lower-mass stars gain kinetic energy, populating the extended halo or escaping the cluster entirely. If unabated, this mechanism drives the core to a gravothermal catastrophe, culminating in core collapse. However, the collapse is eventually arrested by the presence of binary stars. The energy emitted through the dynamical hardening of these binaries—where tight binaries become more tightly bound during close encounters—injects kinetic energy back into the system, balancing the energy expended in expanding the cluster halo [Lynden-Bell et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..228...99L/abstract). Furthermore, the role of numerical simulations has proven essential in uncovering the diverse formation conditions and subsequent environmental impacts on the kinematics of young star clusters [Olczak et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASPC..453..241O/abstract). 

The internal kinematics of these systems are intricately linked to their external environments. [[tidally-limited-star-clusters|Tidally limited star clusters]] continuously lose mass due to the host galaxy's tidal field, a process that couples with two-body relaxation to accelerate their structural evolution. Detailed models further emphasize that cluster disruption rates and radial migration are intimately coupled to the host galaxy's underlying tidal field and overall star formation history [Kruijssen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.1339K/abstract). In general, the long-term structural evolution of entire globular cluster systems is governed by a combination of physical processes, including steady evaporation, impulsive tidal shocks, and dynamical friction [Ostriker et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988IAUS..126..271O/abstract). 

Historically, it was assumed that the long-term relaxation of a cluster would efficiently erase its initial angular momentum. However, both observations and $N$-body simulations reveal that many Galactic globular clusters retain a measurable degree of systemic [[stellar-rotation|Stellar rotation]]. Over tens of relaxation times, two-body relaxation and the escape of stars redistribute ordered rotational kinetic energy from the inner to the outer regions, resulting in an overall decline in the amplitude of the rotation curve over time [Tiongco et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469..683T/abstract). Despite this gradual loss of angular momentum and mass, the ratio of rotational velocity to velocity dispersion, $V/\sigma$, can remain non-negligible well into the cluster's evolutionary history [Tiongco et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469..683T/abstract).

Beyond individual star clusters, these collisional and dynamical principles provide a foundation for understanding broader structures. Scaling these concepts up to massive galaxy clusters, observers utilize the orbits and spatial distributions of member galaxies to determine internal mass profiles and evaluate the overall dynamical states of these immense systems [Biviano et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10053B/abstract). Within such dense cluster environments, the evolution of [[stellar-populations|stellar populations]] in member galaxies also exhibits distinct dependencies on galaxy mass and local density, visibly incorporating evolutionary phenomena like the Butcher-Oemler effect [Poggianti et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003IAUJD..10E..14P/abstract).

The bulk kinematics of cluster populations also provide a fossil record of galactic assembly. For instance, the system of globular clusters in the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy]] (M31) exhibits distinct kinematic subpopulations that are tightly correlated with their accretion history. Lower-metallicity clusters and those associated with stellar substructures show rapid, aligned rotation that is kinematically distinct from the higher-metallicity clusters, which tend to corotate with the galactic disk [Li et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260105380L/abstract). Broader comparisons of globular cluster system kinematics across diverse spiral and elliptical galaxy halos further reveal distinct rotational signatures and mass profiles that reliably differentiate these discrete host environments [Romanowsky et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..9251R/abstract). Furthermore, the internal kinematics of globular clusters provide unique constraints for testing alternative theories of gravity. For instance, globular cluster velocity dispersions can be used to distinguish between Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) and Modified Gravity (MOG), as MOG distinctly predicts that the velocity dispersion should remain independent of the cluster's distance from the Galactic center [Moffat et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...680.1158M/abstract).

## Kinematics of Spiral Disks and Rotation Curves

The kinematics of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] are fundamentally characterized by their [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]] profile, $V_{\rm rot}(R)$, which describes the circular motion of stars and cold gas around the galactic center. In massive spirals, $V_{\rm rot}(R)$ typically rises rapidly at small galactocentric radii and subsequently becomes constant over the majority of the disk. This results in the well-documented phenomenon of [[nearly-flat-rotation-curves|Nearly flat rotation curves]]. Because the velocity remains constant in the outer regions, the enclosed dynamical mass must scale linearly with radius:
$$M(R) \propto R$$
Since the surface brightness of the stellar disk declines exponentially, the enclosed luminous mass converges quickly, revealing a severe mass discrepancy at large radii. For example, in NGC 3198, the farthest measured kinematic point implies an enclosed mass of $1.5 \times 10^{11} M_\odot$, roughly four times larger than the visible stellar mass. In the standard cosmological paradigm, this discrepancy is resolved by embedding the [[stellar-component|Stellar component]] within an extended, triaxial dark matter halo.

Historically, pioneering efforts such as Tully's mass model for M51 utilized multiple Gaussian components to mathematically capture and describe the distinct kinematics of the galactic disk and its nuclear lens [Tully et al. (1974)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1974ApJS...27..437T/abstract). Today, the physical origin of this mass discrepancy remains a subject of intense debate. Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) presents a prominent alternative to dark matter by altering the laws of gravity or inertia in the limit of low acceleration. This theoretical framework naturally yields the [[milgrom-relation|Milgrom relation]], which connects the baryonic mass of a galaxy to its asymptotic rotation velocity. MOND has successfully reproduced the detailed shapes and amplitudes of extended rotation curves across diverse samples of spiral galaxies, using primarily the stellar mass-to-light ratio as a single free parameter [Sanders et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...473..117S/abstract). For high-surface-brightness early-type disks with asymptotic velocities reaching $250 - 350 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, MOND models fit the observational data well, yielding mass-to-light ratios that are consistent with [[maximum-disks|Maximum disks]], wherein the baryonic matter entirely dominates the central potential without the need for a finely tuned dark halo [Sanders et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.379..702S/abstract). To further evaluate the validity of MOND against dark matter models, researchers employ 3D kinematic data, testing the dynamical self-consistency of disks by jointly analyzing their rotational velocities and vertical velocity dispersions [Stubbs et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.12067S/abstract).

Beyond MOND, alternative theories challenge the dark matter paradigm without explicitly modifying Newtonian gravity. Certain studies propose that the perceived mass discrepancies giving rise to flat rotation curves could alternatively stem from out-of-equilibrium mass distributions [Benhaiem et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...851...19B/abstract) or from fictitious forces acting within non-inertial reference frames [Gomel et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Galax...9...34G/abstract). Moreover, it has been suggested that the constant group velocity of nonlinear density waves can mathematically support flat rotation curves, relying on solutions to the nonlinear Schrödinger equation rather than standard exponential approximations for surface mass density [Vukcevic et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....161..118V/abstract). 

Measuring disk kinematics in the extreme outer regions, where surface brightness drops orders of magnitude below the sky background, requires advanced observational techniques such as integral field unit (IFU) spectroscopy and multislit mask designs (e.g., the SuperSKiMS technique on DEIMOS) [Pastorello et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PASA...33...35P/abstract). Furthermore, campaigns leveraging specialized facilities like SALT, utilizing its Prime Focus Imaging Spectrograph (PFIS), are designed to accurately measure the velocity ellipsoids of stars and gas to probe whether underlying dark matter halos are lopsided or elliptical rather than perfectly axisymmetric [Bershady et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph..3478B/abstract). These extended kinematic profiles are essential not only for mass modeling but for constraining global disk stability. Purely rotationally supported disks are susceptible to violent global instabilities, such as bar modes and bending waves. A disk's structural integrity against these perturbations relies on a delicate balance between the velocity dispersion (pressure) of its stars and the stabilizing presence of a background potential—whether provided by a massive dark halo or a modified gravitational field.

## Secular Evolution and Stellar Migration

In [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], dynamical evolution is not solely dictated by violent, hierarchical assembly. Over gigayear timescales, these systems undergo [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]—slow, internal restructuring driven by non-axisymmetric global instabilities such as bars, spiral arms, and bending modes. These features facilitate the continuous redistribution of mass and angular momentum, fundamentally altering the structural and kinematic profiles of the disk. Beyond planar structures, comprehensive reviews of disk instabilities highlight that warps and bending waves play a crucial role in the long-term, three-dimensional dynamical evolution of galactic disks [Sellwood et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013pss5.book..923S/abstract).

A classic signature of secular evolution is "disk heating," wherein the velocity dispersion of a stellar population increases with its age. In the Milky Way, transient spiral arms are considered the fundamental drivers of this heating, with scattering by giant molecular clouds playing a crucial moderating role [Binney et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..230...63B/abstract). However, the universality of this [[age-velocity-dispersion-relation|Age-velocity dispersion relation]] is currently a subject of active debate in the literature. While massive spirals exhibit clear heating trends, kinematic surveys of the low-mass spiral M33 reveal a relatively low and constant stellar velocity dispersion of $\sim 16 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ across all stellar ages. The youngest stars in M33 are dynamically as hot as the oldest, a result that challenges current predictions from simulated low-mass galactic analogs [Quirk et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AJ....163..166Q/abstract).

In tandem with heating, spiral arms induce [[stellar-migration|Stellar migration]] (specifically, radial migration)—a process where stars undergo significant changes in their guiding radii while preserving nearly circular orbits. Resonant scattering by transient spiral waves allows stars to traverse large galactocentric distances over their lifetimes [Roškar et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...684L..79R/abstract). This mechanism represents a major departure from classical textbook models, which generally assume that stars remain near their birth radii. 

Radial migration elegantly resolves several historical anomalies in Galactic archaeology. Notably, it provides a physical mechanism for the unexpected flatness and broad scatter of the age-metallicity relation in the solar neighborhood, as well as the relative dearth of metal-poor stars (the classic G-dwarf problem) [Roškar et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...684L..79R/abstract). Because of the efficiency of this radial mixing, a star's current kinematic properties are largely decoupled from its birth environment. Consequently, dynamical actions—such as the radial action $J_r$, the vertical action $J_z$, and the $z$-component of angular momentum $L_z$—exhibit only weak gradients with stellar age. Due to the large intrinsic dispersion of actions at any given age, they serve as poor indicators for the ages of individual stars [Beane et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...867...31B/abstract).

Finally, the central stellar bar is a dominant engine for secular evolution. Bars redistribute angular momentum by transferring it outward to the surrounding disk and dark matter halo, while simultaneously driving metal-poor gas inward to fuel nuclear star formation and altering global stellar trajectories [Binney et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013NewAR..57...29B/abstract). Because these structures dictate the long-term dynamical fate of the disk, researchers have developed specialized methods for estimating bar ages directly from stellar kinematics, providing vital empirical constraints on the timescales of this secular restructuring [Gadotti et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..527G/abstract). Furthermore, detailed studies of ringed barred spiral galaxies utilize the morphology and kinematics of rings to link specific orbital resonances directly with observed galactic structures, enabling researchers to derive robust bar mass-to-light ratios [Buta et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....121..225B/abstract). Together, these secular processes demonstrate that disk galaxies are not in a static equilibrium, but are complex, continually evolving dynamical machines.

## Collisionless Dynamics in Elliptical Galaxies

Elliptical galaxies are quintessential examples of [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]], where the overall macroscopic structure and dynamical evolution are dictated by the system's [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]] distribution function. Unlike rotation-supported [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], ellipticals are primarily pressure-supported. Modeling their internal kinematics is crucial for constraining radial mass profiles—including supermassive black holes and dark matter halos—and for determining their orbital geometries.

A fundamental challenge in mapping the gravitational potentials of ellipticals at large galactocentric radii is the absence of extended cold gas. Consequently, dynamicists must rely on discrete [[stellar-tracers|Stellar tracers]], such as planetary nebulae (PNe) and globular clusters (GCs), which remain detectable in regions where the integrated surface brightness of starlight is orders of magnitude fainter than the night sky [Brodie et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..317..190B/abstract). Kinematic measurements of PNe extending out to $6-8$ effective radii ($R_e$) have proven critical in tracing the outer mass distributions of luminous ellipticals [Tian et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.1092T/abstract).

Extracting mass profiles from these tracers typically requires [[dynamical-modeling-of-stellar-systems|Dynamical modeling of stellar systems]], often employing Jeans modeling or Schwarzschild orbit superposition. A persistent analytical challenge in these models is the mass-anisotropy degeneracy. A steeply rising central velocity dispersion profile $\sigma(r)$ could indicate the presence of a supermassive black hole, but it can equally be produced by a radially anisotropic orbital distribution rather than an [[isotropic-velocity-dispersion|Isotropic velocity dispersion]] [Romanowsky et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...553..722R/abstract). To break this degeneracy, models must incorporate the full line-of-sight velocity distribution (LOSVD), commonly parameterized by the higher-order Gauss-Hermite moments $h_3$ and $h_4$. Detailed orbit-based modeling of the giant elliptical M87, using stellar kinematics combined with discrete velocities from over 200 GCs, conclusively ruled out a constant mass-to-light ratio and demonstrated that the dark halo density falls off more slowly than $\rho \propto r^{-2}$ [Romanowsky et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...553..722R/abstract). Beyond local measurements, deriving central velocity dispersions for elliptical galaxies at intermediate redshifts (e.g., $z=0.18$) has established early critical constraints on the cosmological evolution of scaling laws like the Faber-Jackson relation [Franx et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PASP..105.1058F/abstract).

The orbital structure in these pressure-supported systems is exceedingly rich, supporting complex families of box and tube orbits not found in thin disks. $N$-body simulations reveal that ellipticals frequently settle into non-axisymmetric configurations. Triaxiality is a common outcome; for instance, in models exploring Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), triaxial configurations can be driven by the external tidal fields of galaxy clusters due to the theory's unique external field effect [Wu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844..130W/abstract). Additionally, massive slow rotators are found to be rare and typically reside at the density peaks of galaxy clusters; these systems lack significant rotation and serve as fossil relics of violent hierarchical cluster formation [Graham et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191106103G/abstract). Furthermore, the preservation of long-lasting kinematic groups or "fossils" in the stellar halo strongly favors triaxial dark matter potentials, as such phase-space structures tend to phase-mix away rapidly in purely spherical or axisymmetric geometries [Rojas-Niño et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...805...29R/abstract).

Despite advancements in mapping triaxial halos, the literature remains explicitly conflicted regarding the physical nature of the underlying potential. While the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm successfully utilizes triaxial dark matter halos to explain extended kinematics, alternative frameworks such as Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) have also been successfully fitted to the PNe dispersion profiles of ellipticals like NGC 821, NGC 3379, and NGC 4494 without requiring any dark matter [Tian et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.1092T/abstract). However, some theorists argue that MOND struggles to support the longevity of halo kinematic fossils, suggesting that robust detections of these substructures could eventually rule out modified gravity theories in favor of particulate, triaxial dark matter halos [Rojas-Niño et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...805...29R/abstract).

## Dynamics of Galactic Nuclei and Supermassive Black Holes

The central parsecs of galaxies harbor extreme dynamical environments dominated by the steep gravitational potentials of supermassive black holes (SMBHs). The kinematics of these regions provide critical astrophysical laboratories for testing both [[stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|Stellar and Galactic Dynamics]] and stellar physics under strong gravity [Alexander et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55...17A/abstract). The definitive textbook on these environments provides a comprehensive review of dynamical processes in galactic nuclei, specifically detailing the evolution of supermassive black hole binaries and the intricacies of cusp relaxation [Merritt et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013degn.book.....M/abstract).

In the Milky Way, high-resolution astrometry of [[s-stars-galactic-center|S-stars (Galactic Center)]]—which pass within 100 astronomical units ($1.5 \times 10^{15}$ cm) of the central object—provides unambiguous dynamical evidence for an SMBH, constraining its mass to approximately $2 \times 10^6 M_\odot$. Beyond the Local Group, identifying the gravitational signature of an SMBH relies on detecting a steep central rise in the projected velocity dispersion, $\sigma_p(R)$. However, interpretations of these kinematic profiles are frequently limited by the mass-anisotropy degeneracy. A radially anisotropic stellar velocity distribution can mimic the signature of a central massive object without requiring a true central rise in the physical mass-to-light ratio. This literature tension is explicitly highlighted when comparing different galaxy types. For example, while orbit superposition models and higher-order moments of the line-of-sight velocity distribution conclusively rule out a constant mass-to-light ratio in M87 (pointing to an SMBH of $\sim 3 \times 10^9 M_\odot$), searches for $\sim 10^9 M_\odot$ "dead quasars" in nearby luminous spirals yield ambiguous results. In systems such as NGC 7331, simple models lacking a central black hole and assuming a constant mass-to-light ratio fit the observational data naturally, while models attempting to include a massive central black hole require highly anisotropic velocity distributions to hide its kinematic signature—and even then, models with a $10^9 M_\odot$ black hole fail entirely to fit the data [Bower et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...402...76B/abstract).

The dense environments surrounding SMBHs are frequently populated by nuclear star clusters (NSCs), whose kinematics retain the imprint of their host galaxy's evolutionary history. Spatially resolved, parsec-scale kinematic measurements reveal a morphological dichotomy in NSC formation mechanisms. NSCs in late-type galaxies are largely rotation-dominated, pointing to an *in situ* formation scenario driven by cold gas funneled into the center. Conversely, early-type galaxies generally host slowly rotating NSCs with lower ellipticities, though exceptions exist where early-types display disk-like accretion processes more typical of spirals [Pinna et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...921....8P/abstract).

Within these [[dense-stellar-cusps|Dense stellar cusps]], the stellar phase-space distribution is continually reshaped by two-body relaxation and close stellar encounters. Loss cone theory details the complex dynamical mechanisms that deflect stars onto highly eccentric orbits intercepting the SMBH [Merritt et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006RPPh...69.2513M/abstract). These encounters precipitate energetic tidal disruptions and dictate the long-term secular evolution of the nucleus [Alexander et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55...17A/abstract). Furthermore, the broader structures of [[galaxy-nuclei-and-complex-morphologies|Galaxy Nuclei and Complex Morphologies]] are heavily influenced by hierarchical assembly. Self-consistent $N$-body simulations demonstrate that the infall and coalescence of binary SMBHs during [[galaxy-merger-physics|major galaxy mergers]] efficiently scour the central regions, fundamentally altering the stellar kinematics and flattening the density profiles of the surviving nuclear cusps [Merritt et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..228..155M/abstract).

## Chemo-Dynamics and Galactic Archaeology

The synthesis of high-resolution spectroscopy and astrometry to reconstruct the evolutionary tracks and assembly histories of galaxies is broadly termed galactic archaeology. Contrary to classical models of monolithic collapse, large-scale kinematic surveys (e.g., SDSS, Gaia) reveal that galaxies like the Milky Way and Andromeda are complex, non-equilibrium systems shaped by hierarchical assembly and continuous merging [Ivezić et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..251I/abstract), [Wyse et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.10025W/abstract).

In galactic disks, the textbook assumption that stars remain near their birth radii has been largely overturned. Resonant scattering by transient spiral arms induces significant [[stellar-migration|Stellar migration]] across large galactocentric distances while preserving roughly circular orbits [Roškar et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...684L..79R/abstract). As discussed previously, this radial migration successfully accounts for the unexpected flatness and broad scatter in the age-metallicity relation of the solar neighborhood. However, the exact efficiency of this migration and whether internal [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] can entirely account for the chemo-dynamical differences between thin and thick disks—or if external minor mergers are strictly required—remains an area of active debate [Combes et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..271..119C/abstract).

Because spatial structures dissipate over time as systems undergo phase mixing, galactic archaeologists rely heavily on conserved or slowly varying quantities in [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]]. While orbital actions serve as useful integrals of motion, their large intrinsic dispersions limit their utility as individual stellar age indicators [Beane et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...867...31B/abstract). Instead, intrinsic chemical abundances act as robust stellar labels that remain invariant over a star's lifetime. These labels are crucial for identifying [[stellar-streams-and-moving-groups|Stellar Streams and Moving Groups]] that represent the tidal debris of [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]].

The combination of kinematics and chemistry has illuminated recent phase-space substructures, definitively demonstrating that the Milky Way is not in a dynamical steady state. Analysis of spiral abundance patterns using labels like $[\mathrm{Mg/Fe}]$ has helped date historical perturbations to the Milky Way disk [Frankel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240707149F/abstract), [Frankel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...987...81F/abstract). Nevertheless, determining the precise identity of the perturber remains computationally challenging, and some researchers argue that internal non-axisymmetric features might mimic these signatures without requiring a violent external perturbation. 

Extragalactic [[chemo-dynamics|Chemo-dynamics]] extends these principles to our neighbors and the distant universe. For instance, spatially-resolved kinematic studies of massive galaxies at $z \sim 2$ from the SINS and zC-SINF surveys have revealed vital insights into early-stage growth and star formation properties on kiloparsec scales [Förster Schreiber et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Msngr.145...39F/abstract). In the Local Group, detailed studies of dwarf galaxies have shown how kinematic and abundance patterns vary both spatially and temporally across different dwarf types, reflecting their diverse star-formation histories [Tolstoy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ARA&A..47..371T/abstract). Modern efforts to unveil the chrono-chemo-kinematical properties of stellar streams and these dwarf galaxies include massive spectroscopic campaigns like the 4DWARFS survey [Skúladóttir et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Msngr.190...19S/abstract). In the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda galaxy]] (M31), surveys mapping the spatial kinematics of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] and resolved giant branch stars have uncovered a stellar halo metallicity gradient extending out to $100\mathrm{\ kpc}$. Subpopulations of these clusters exhibit distinct kinematic alignments and rotational properties, indicative of discrete accretion epochs during M31's assembly [Gilbert et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..317..134G/abstract), [Li et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260105380L/abstract). In the broader universe, integral field spectroscopy from surveys like MaNGA has demonstrated how stellar age and metallicity gradients tightly correlate with galaxy rotation and total-density slopes. This is particularly evident in green valley massive disks, where chemo-dynamical modeling tracks the progression of galaxies from actively star-forming to quenched states [Lu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.1022L/abstract).

Ultimately, precision age determinations remain a major hurdle in reconstructing [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]]. While asteroseismology from missions like Kepler has provided unprecedented age constraints for solar-type stars, reconciling these empirical ages with classical synthetic population models highlights significant tensions [van Saders et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872..128V/abstract), [Chaplin et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...332..213C/abstract). This indicates that the mapping between a star's kinematics, metallicity, and true age is highly complex, and efforts to fully decode the chemo-dynamical fossil record are ongoing.

## Modern Astrometrics and Phase-Space Substructure

Traditional analytical models of [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]] heavily rely on the assumption that galaxies exist in a steady-state hydrostatic or dynamical equilibrium. However, the unprecedented precision of modern astrometric surveys—particularly the Hipparcos and Gaia missions—has overturned this textbook picture, revealing that the Milky Way is actively undergoing phase mixing and is characterized by pervasive [[stellar-substructure|Stellar substructure]] [Ivezić et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..251I/abstract), [Binney et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..230...63B/abstract). Early tomographic mapping from surveys like SDSS established fundamental kinematic baselines, revealing how disk rotational velocity systematically decreases, and velocity dispersion increases, with distance from the Galactic plane [Bond et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...716....1B/abstract). Building on this, astronomers combining precise proper motions, parallaxes, and radial velocities can now map the full [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]] of millions of stars, exposing intricate kinematic features that cannot be described by smooth, time-independent potentials [Smith et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..420..113S/abstract), [La Fortune et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200810445L/abstract).

The hallmark of this non-equilibrium state in the local volume is the "Gaia snail," a distinct spiral pattern observed in the $Z$-$V_Z$ (vertical position versus vertical velocity) phase-space plane. When a galactic disk undergoes a perturbation, stellar orbits are disturbed and subsequently phase-mix over time, winding into a tight spiral in the vertical phase-space density. Characterizing the exact morphology of the Gaia snail is computationally challenging due to the complex spatial selection functions inherent to observational surveys. To circumvent this, researchers utilize chemical labeling. Because intrinsic stellar abundances remain largely constant over a star's lifetime, any perturbation that raises a spiral wave in phase-space density will induce a corresponding wave in the distribution of stellar labels. Analyzing these "iron snails" using tracers like the $\text{[Mg/Fe]}$ ratio has successfully dated the primary dynamical perturbation responsible for the local snail to roughly $400 \text{ Myr}$ ago [Frankel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240707149F/abstract), [Frankel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...987...81F/abstract).

In addition to vertical phase spirals, local [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]] contains numerous [[moving-groups|Moving groups]] and kinematically detected streams. In the stellar halo, the remnants of accreted satellite galaxies produce over-densities in velocity space that remain coherent far longer than their spatial clustering. To quantify these features, kinematic data are frequently transformed into action-angle coordinates, which serve as adiabatic invariants for the unperturbed potential and are powerful tools for isolating distinct accreted populations or identifying resonant trapping by the Galactic bar and spiral arms [Smith et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..420..113S/abstract). Beyond the Local Group, massive integral field surveys have identified macroscopic phase-space anomalies, such as the 120 counter-rotating stellar disk galaxies discovered in the MaNGA survey. These "Stellar CoRGI" systems exhibit stellar counter-rotation, providing stark kinematic evidence for discrete disk assembly via gas accretion from recent mergers or cosmic filaments [Gasymov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250402925G/abstract).

Despite clear evidence of ongoing phase mixing, determining the precise timing and identity of the perturbers remains a subject of active debate. The prevailing consensus attributes large-scale [[phase-space-dynamics|Phase Space Dynamics]] features like the Gaia snail to discrete external events, such as the recent pericentric passages of the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy. However, the literature is not entirely settled; some theoretical models and interpretations suggest that similar kinematic signatures could alternatively arise from internal secular processes or nonlinear density waves. Future integration of large-scale spectroscopic data with exquisite astrometry will be crucial to disentangle these non-equilibrium signatures and reconstruct the complete assembly history of the Galaxy. To meet this challenge, alongside new frameworks like "cogsworth" which self-consistently integrate rapid binary stellar evolution with complex galactic dynamics simulations [Wagg et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJS..276...16W/abstract), researchers rely heavily on modern computational tools such as GalPot for finding the gravitational potentials associated with diverse axisymmetric density profiles and precisely integrating stellar orbits [McMillan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ascl.soft11006M/abstract).

## Open Questions: The IMF and Dark Matter Nature

Determining the true dynamical mass of systems governed by [[stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|Stellar and Galactic Dynamics]] requires precisely decoupling the visible baryonic mass from the dark sector. This decomposition fundamentally relies on the mass-to-light ratio ($M/L$), which serves as the crucial nexus connecting two of the most pressing debates in modern astrophysics: the universality of the stellar mass spectrum and the physical nature of dark matter. 

For decades, [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models assumed a universal [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], such as the [[salpeter-initial-mass-function|Salpeter]] or Kroupa distributions, across all galactic environments. However, recent spatially resolved kinematics and spectral fitting from surveys like ATLAS3D and MUSE have provided compelling evidence for a [[non-universal-initial-mass-function|non-universal initial mass function]] [Cappellari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.484..485C/abstract), [Parikh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.7338P/abstract). Specifically, massive early-type galaxies exhibit a "bottom-heavy" IMF enriched with low-mass stars in their central regions, leading to a factor of up to three increase in the inferred stellar mass compared to Milky Way-like templates [Cappellari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.484..485C/abstract). Radial gradients in the IMF slope have also been detected, with central mass excess factors ranging between 1.5 and 2.5 [Parikh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.7338P/abstract). Furthermore, understanding the ratio of accreted versus *in situ* stellar mass in these early-type galaxies is vital, as it strongly correlates with their structural assembly and directly impacts the interpretation of derived dynamical mass estimates [McDermid et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..290M/abstract). Nonetheless, the literature remains conflicted on the underlying physical drivers of this IMF variation. A significant tension exists between synthetic population models and observational data due to calibration ambiguities in spectroscopic lines (such as NaI), which can yield higher $M/L$ ratios than those derived from orbit-based dynamical models [Parikh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.7338P/abstract).

This uncertainty in the stellar $M/L$ ratio directly impacts the search for kinematic signatures of dark matter. Kinematic data from the outer halos of galaxies—traced by planetary nebulae and [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]]—conclusively rule out constant $M/L$ models in massive ellipticals like M87 [Romanowsky et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...553..722R/abstract). Yet, the physical origin of the mass discrepancy remains highly contested. Some studies demonstrate that Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) can accurately reproduce the flat rotation curves and extended [[galaxy-kinematics|Galaxy kinematics]] of rotationally supported early-type discs and spirals without invoking a dark matter halo [Sanders et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...473..117S/abstract), [Sanders et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.379..702S/abstract). Additionally, MOND has also been successfully applied to map the extended kinematics and planetary nebulae dispersion profiles of massive elliptical galaxies [Tian et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.1092T/abstract). Conversely, other dynamical simulations highlight that MOND fails to reproduce certain persistent phase-space substructures. These studies suggest that the observed kinematic groups and long-lasting orbital resonances in the stellar halo may only emerge and survive within **triaxial dark matter halos**, providing a potential avenue to rule out modified gravity [Rojas-Niño et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...805...29R/abstract).

Complicating this debate is the realization that galaxies are rarely in perfect dynamical equilibrium. Continual perturbations from satellite interactions induce phase mixing in the [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]], creating transient spiral waves and vertical ripples that can persist for hundreds of millions of years [Frankel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240707149F/abstract). The ubiquitous presence of such non-equilibrium structures makes it exceedingly difficult to cleanly invert the [[vlasov-poisson-equation|Vlasov-Poisson equation]] to solve for the steady-state gravitational potential. Furthermore, recent theoretical propositions argue that anomalous [[velocity-statistics-and-kinematics|Velocity Statistics and Kinematics]] responsible for global flat rotation curves might also be generated by non-equilibrium mass distributions [Benhaiem et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...851...19B/abstract) or fictitious forces arising from non-inertial reference frames [Gomel et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Galax...9...34G/abstract), as well as by the constant group velocity of non-linear density waves in [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] [Vukcevic et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....161..118V/abstract). Distinguishing whether flat rotation curves and global mass discrepancies are the product of triaxial dark matter halos, modified gravity, or out-of-equilibrium dynamical responses remains a critical open frontier in galactic astrophysics.
