**Galactic archaeology** is the study of a galaxy's formation and evolutionary history by treating long-lived stars as "fossils" whose precise chemical abundances and phase-space kinematics preserve a record of their birth environments. Operating as a form of [[near-field-cosmology|Near-field cosmology]], the field uses detailed, star-by-star observations of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] and other nearby galaxies to test theoretical frameworks of galaxy assembly [Freeman et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ARA&A..40..487F/abstract), [Wyse et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.10025W/abstract). By analyzing these [[stellar-tracers|Stellar tracers]], astronomers can piece together how massive galaxies evolved from early monotonic collapse scenarios to the modern cosmological paradigm of hierarchical growth, in which galaxies are built over cosmic time through the continuous accretion and merger of smaller [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|satellite systems]] [Gilmore et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989epg..conf..172G/abstract), [Majewski et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASA...21..197M/abstract).

The discipline is observationally driven by the synergy between space-based astrometry, such as the *Gaia* mission, and massive ground-based spectroscopic campaigns like the [[galah-survey|GALAH survey]] and APOGEE [Allende Prieto et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AN....329.1018A/abstract). These facilities provide the high-dimensional data required for [[chemo-dynamics|chemo-dynamical]] mapping. Researchers utilize [[strong-chemical-tagging|Strong chemical tagging]]—treating elemental abundance ratios as distinct "barcodes" inherited from natal gas clouds—alongside conserved integrals of motion to distinguish between [[in-situ-formation|In-situ formation]] and [[accreted-stellar-populations|Accreted stellar populations]] [Wyse et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995PASP..107..785W/abstract). This methodology has allowed astronomers to identify the remnants of major ancient collisions, such as the [[gaia-enceladus|Gaia Enceladus]] merger, and to trace the role of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] as surviving witnesses to the Galaxy's primordial building blocks.

Beyond identifying structural components, galactic archaeology integrates asteroseismology and chemical clocks to determine precise stellar ages, enabling the reconstruction of a continuous, absolute [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]]. These archaeological principles are increasingly being applied to the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] and the broader [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|Local Volume and Nearby Galaxy Studies]], providing a comparative context for the Milky Way's evolution. Furthermore, understanding the spatial and temporal distribution of chemical enrichment allows researchers to define the [[galactic-habitable-zone|Galactic Habitable Zone]], linking galactic assembly directly to [[astrobiology|Astrobiology]] and the environmental prerequisites for complex life.

Despite rapid observational advances, the field faces several critical open questions regarding its foundational assumptions. The standard premise that accreted debris clusters tightly in phase-space is actively challenged by simulations showing severe dynamical overlap with kinematically heated *in situ* populations, complicating the reliable identification of distinct [[stellar-substructure|Stellar substructure]]. Similarly, the immutability of chemical fingerprints is challenged by post-formation phenomena like planetary engulfment, which can alter surface abundances. Finally, theoretical uncertainties in stellar evolution models and the effects of binary mass transfer—which can mimic deceptively "young" ages in $\alpha$-rich stars—present ongoing hurdles. Resolving these tensions is a primary objective for the next generation of causal machine learning frameworks and extremely large telescopes.

## Foundations of Galactic Archaeology

Galactic archaeology operates on the premise that long-lived stars act as *stellar fossils*, preserving the physical and chemical conditions of their birth environments [Wyse et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.10025W/abstract), [Kobayashi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230207255K/abstract). Interpreting this fossil record in the Local Group is essential for understanding the physics of typical galaxies across the universe [Gilmore et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003RMxAC..17..149G/abstract). The discipline functions as a form of [[near-field-cosmology|Near-field cosmology]], using detailed, star-by-star observations of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] and other Local Group galaxies to test theoretical frameworks of galaxy formation. This approach complements direct look-back observations of the high-redshift universe [Freeman et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ARA&A..40..487F/abstract), [Frebel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ARA&A..53..631F/abstract). By disentangling the spatial distribution, kinematics, chemical composition, and ages of [[stellar-populations|stellar populations]], the field aims to reconstruct a continuous [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and detail the structural assembly of galaxies.

A cornerstone of this reconstruction relies on the analysis of *phase-space kinematics*. In the standard cosmological paradigm, the stellar halos of massive galaxies are largely built through hierarchical growth via the continuous merging and accretion of smaller [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|satellite systems]] [Majewski et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASA...21..197M/abstract), [Horta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240416939H/abstract). High-precision 6D kinematic data allow researchers to identify the disrupted remnants of [[accreted-dwarf-galaxy-origin|accreted dwarf galaxies]] as coherent substructures in integral-of-motion space [Deason et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NewAR..9901706D/abstract). Furthermore, the concept of *galactoseismology* has emerged as a methodology to infer the characteristics of the Galactic potential and substructure by analyzing observed perturbations in the Galaxy's gas or stellar disk [Chakrabarti et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..321..108C/abstract). For instance, an intuitive 3D map using classical Cepheids has revealed that the stellar disk actively follows the gas disk's warped and flared structure out to distances of $20$ kpc [Chen et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NatAs...3..320C/abstract). Within the disk itself, kinematic and chemical properties establish the thick disk as a distinct component of the Milky Way, providing vital clues to its unique star formation history [Eswar Reddy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0911.0570E/abstract).

Alongside kinematics, *chemical abundances* serve as critical diagnostic tools, effectively functioning as "barcodes" that trace the chemical enrichment history of the interstellar medium [Buder et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240919858B/abstract). By measuring the ratios of various elements—such as $\alpha$-elements, iron-peak, and neutron-capture elements—spectroscopic campaigns like the [[galah-survey|GALAH survey]] and APOGEE facilitate the grouping of stars into [[chemo-dynamics|chemo-dynamical]] populations that share common origins [Allende Prieto et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AN....329.1018A/abstract), [Martell et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150700079M/abstract). At the extremes of this chemical record, abundance patterns in the most metal-poor stars reveal the nature of the first stars and supernovae in the early universe [Frebel et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AN....331..474F/abstract). By carefully studying various types of metal-poor stars in the Milky Way system, researchers can effectively reconstruct the specific element production histories that occurred prior to their birth [Frebel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241115415F/abstract). Crucially, the accuracy of these chemical enrichment models is highly dependent on input stellar physics, specifically the theoretical nucleosynthesis yields and binary physics applied in the modeling [Kobayashi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250620436K/abstract). However, the interpretation of local metallicity distributions must account for the substantial role of *radial migration*, which has the potential to significantly blur the chemical records of galactic disks over time [Prantzos et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1101.2108P/abstract).

Finally, translating chemo-dynamical structures into an evolutionary timeline requires precise stellar age-dating. Asteroseismology of giant stars provides highly accurate evolutionary clocks, allowing the measurement of stellar masses and, consequently, ages with theoretical precisions of $10\%$ [Miglio et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AN....338..644M/abstract). While these observational pillars provide a robust framework, ongoing research actively interrogates the limits of these foundational assumptions, investigating phenomena that blur dynamical coherence, alter surface chemistry, and complicate theoretical age inferences.

## Historical Development and the Hierarchical Framework

The spatial distribution, kinematics, ages, and chemical abundances of long-lived stars serve as a fossil record of a galaxy's evolution [Wyse et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.10025W/abstract), [Freeman et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ARA&A..40..487F/abstract). By studying these [[stellar-tracers|Stellar tracers]], galactic archaeology seeks to reconstruct the sequence of events that built the [[milky-way|Milky Way]]. Historically, the use of horizontal branch stars proved vital as early tracers of halo density profiles and kinematic variations across the Galaxy, helping to lay the groundwork to map these extended structures [Layden et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..136...14L/abstract).

Early models of galaxy formation posited that the Galaxy formed via a rapid, monotonic collapse of a massive proto-galactic gas cloud [Gilmore et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989epg..conf..172G/abstract). While this scenario provides a reasonable description for the rapid formation of the inner regions of the halo, subsequent observations revealed that the outer halo must have fragmented and collapsed in a much more chaotic manner [Sarajedini et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PASP..109.1321S/abstract). This realization marked a fundamental transition toward the modern framework of hierarchical assembly, wherein massive galaxies grow continuously through the ingestion of smaller building blocks over cosmic time. 

In the hierarchical paradigm, the Galactic halo is predominantly composed of debris from [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]]. Tracers such as giant stars and [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] reveal a halo networked with extended, coherent substructures [Majewski et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASA...21..197M/abstract). Modern cosmological simulations indicate that the assembly of a Milky Way-mass system involved the accretion of approximately 100 progenitor galaxies with stellar masses $M_{\text{star}} \gtrsim 10^5 M_\odot$, with the main progenitor emerging at $z \sim 3-4$ (roughly $11.6-12.2$ Gyr ago) [Santistevan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497..747S/abstract). Precision age-dating of specific events confirms that the most significant major mergers occurred early in the Galaxy's history. Furthermore, the formation of the thick disk approximately $10$ Gyr ago provides a vital temporal benchmark, frequently interpreted as a possible limit to the last major, significant accretion event the Galaxy experienced [Gilmore et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..228..225G/abstract). For example, asteroseismology of indigenous halo stars kinematically heated by the [[gaia-enceladus|Gaia Enceladus]] collision has constrained the earliest possible epoch of the merger to $\sim 11.6$ Gyr ago [Chaplin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..382C/abstract).

## Observational Foundations: Missions and Surveys

The transition of Galactic archaeology into a precision, data-driven science has been fundamentally enabled by the synergy between space-based astrometry and massive ground-based spectroscopic surveys. Together, these facilities provide the high-dimensional data—spanning 6D phase space and multi-element chemical abundances—required for detailed [[chemo-dynamics|chemo-dynamical]] mapping of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] [Wyse et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASPC..507...13W/abstract), [Ness et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190705422N/abstract). These multidimensional views of dynamics and stellar populations are poised to revolutionize constraints on both dark matter and galaxy formation [Sanderson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019BAAS...51c.347S/abstract). To maximize the scientific return of astrometric measurements, ground-based Gaia follow-up surveys must meet strict instrumental requirements for precise radial velocity and elemental abundance determination [Feltzing et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150608642F/abstract).

The ESA Gaia mission stands as the cornerstone of this observational revolution. By delivering unprecedented astrometric precision, Gaia has provided parallaxes and proper motions for over a billion stars. This phase-space information enables researchers to reconstruct stellar orbits, identify kinematically coherent substructures, and quantitatively characterize past hierarchical assembly events, such as the accretion of [[gaia-enceladus|Gaia Enceladus]] [Chaplin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..382C/abstract), [Deason et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NewAR..9901706D/abstract). The paradigm shift brought by Gaia has moved the field from localized, qualitative descriptions to rigorous, star-by-star dynamical modeling across the Galaxy [Wyse et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..330..136W/abstract). Additionally, the use of old tracers like RR Lyrae stars has provided a critical three-dimensional view of the Galactic bulge and its chemical composition, bridging astrometry and standard candle distance determinations [Kunder et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018SSRv..214...90K/abstract).

While Gaia provides exceptional astrometry, mapping the [[chemical-abundances-and-nucleosynthesis|Chemical Abundances and Nucleosynthesis]] of stellar populations requires highly multiplexed ground-based spectroscopy. An early pioneer in this domain was the Radial Velocity Experiment (RAVE), which collected over $500,000$ medium-resolution spectra for nearby stars. RAVE yielded foundational chemo-kinematic relations and demonstrated the power of large-scale automated stellar parameterization [Boeche et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..298..292B/abstract), [Zwitter et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151206725Z/abstract).

To pierce the heavy dust obscuration of the Galactic plane and bulge, the Apache Point Observatory Galactic Evolution Experiment (APOGEE) operates in the near-infrared $H$-band. With a high spectral resolution of $R \sim 20,000$, APOGEE has mapped detailed elemental abundances and precise radial velocities (accurate to $\lesssim 0.5$ km s$^{-1}$) for hundreds of thousands of giant stars across all Galactic environments [Majewski et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AAS...21113208M/abstract), [Allende Prieto et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AN....329.1018A/abstract). This capability has facilitated star-by-star archaeology of the inner Galaxy that was previously inaccessible to optical surveys [Zasowski et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AJ....146...81Z/abstract). Complementing this infrared perspective, the Spitzer Space Telescope played a key role in mapping star formation within the outer, lower-metallicity disk and reconstructing the three-dimensional distribution of stars in the Sagittarius and Perseus spiral arms [Majewski et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007sptz.prop40791M/abstract).

In the optical regime, the [[galah-survey|GALAH survey]] (Galactic Archaeology with HERMES) represents a massive effort to obtain high-resolution ($R \sim 28,000$) spectra for approximately one million stars, building on the HERMES project's core goal of using high-resolution spectroscopy to reconstruct the original star-forming aggregates that built the Galaxy's disk and halo [Freeman et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gama.conf..319F/abstract). GALAH DR4 provides abundances for up to 32 elements—including critical markers like C, N, O, and various neutron-capture elements—enabling sophisticated chemical tagging to group stars into co-natal birth clusters even after they have dynamically dispersed [Martell et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150700079M/abstract), [Buder et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240919858B/abstract). Looking forward, future facilities like 4MOST will study the earliest phases of chemical enrichment via high-resolution samples of over 1.5 million stars [Christlieb et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Msngr.175...26C/abstract). To address specific current gaps in these studies, targeted campaigns like the MINCE project provide complementary high-resolution spectroscopy of intermediate metal-poor stars, focusing particularly on mapping neutron-capture element enrichment [Cescutti et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...668A.168C/abstract).

The integration of these diverse datasets is increasingly dependent on machine learning and population synthesis tools, such as the [[besan-on-galaxy-model|Besançon Galaxy Model]], to handle complex selection functions and extract reliable stellar parameters [Kordopatis et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...535A.106K/abstract), [Lagarde et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...601A..27L/abstract). However, the literature notes ongoing challenges in automated analysis: spectroscopic degeneracies—particularly between effective temperature ($T_{\rm eff}$) and surface gravity ($\log g$) in metal-poor stars—can still bias distance and kinematic estimates, highlighting the persistent need for rigorous cross-calibration between astrometric and spectroscopic data pipelines [Kordopatis et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...535A.106K/abstract).

## Methodology: Chemical Tagging and Phase-Space Clustering

Reconstructing the assembly of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] requires disentangling its diverse stellar components. The identification of [[co-natal-stars|Co-natal stars]] and [[accreted-stellar-populations|Accreted stellar populations]] relies heavily on [[chemo-dynamics|Chemo-dynamics]], a methodology that bridges high-resolution spectroscopy with precision astrometry to track the origins of [[stellar-tracers|Stellar tracers]].

**Chemical Tagging**
The principle of chemical tagging posits that stellar atmospheric abundances, which are inherited from natal gas clouds, serve as highly dimensional "barcodes" or fingerprints [Buder et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240919858B/abstract). Beyond stellar atmospheres, meteorites and presolar grains provide highly localized, high-precision information about the Galaxy's isotopic and chemical evolution over time [Nittler et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006mess.book..127N/abstract). High-resolution spectroscopic campaigns measure numerous elements across hundreds of thousands of stars. By utilizing machine learning algorithms like Principal Component Analysis and Extreme Deconvolution on these abundance spaces, researchers can distinguish accreted debris from the [[in-situ-formation|In-situ formation]] record without applying kinematic priors [Buckley et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240718823B/abstract). Furthermore, borrowing from biology, phylogenetic trees have been constructed from chemical abundances to map evolutionary relationships, such as establishing the thick disk as an ancestral population to the thin disk [Jackson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502...32J/abstract). 

In the disk, chemical tagging reveals complex spatial structures. Researchers often construct "mono-abundance populations" (MAPs)—groupings of stars with nearly identical elemental abundances—as a utility for dynamical modeling and constraining the Milky Way's formation history [Rix et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&ARv..21...61R/abstract). For instance, APOGEE data shows that azimuthal metallicity variations closely correlate with stellar age; older MAPs exhibit the largest deviations from standard radial gradients, suggesting that transient spiral arms are not the sole drivers of chemical substructure in the Galactic disk [Hackshaw et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240518120H/abstract). However, the foundational assumption of [[strong-chemical-tagging|Strong chemical tagging]]—that chemical signatures are strictly immutable fossils—is a subject of active debate. High-precision studies of open clusters reveal that post-formation events, such as planetary engulfment, can induce chemical anomalies at the $\sim 5\%$ level, complicating its use as a pristine tracer of birth environments [Spina et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...863..179S/abstract).

**Integrals of Motion and Action Space**
Complementing chemical fingerprints, phase-space clustering analyzes stellar orbits. Because the dynamical relaxation time of the Galactic halo is long, debris from cannibalized satellites is expected to retain dynamical coherence in the space of conserved integrals of motion. These include orbital energy $E$, the vertical component of angular momentum $L_z$, and action space variables such as the radial action $J_R$ and vertical action $J_z$. Mapping stars in $E-L_z$ or action space has been instrumental in identifying the remnants of massive mergers like [[gaia-enceladus|Gaia Enceladus]] [Deason et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NewAR..9901706D/abstract). Beyond the halo, chemo-dynamical $N$-body simulations are employed to reconstruct the sequence of formation for the Galactic bar and bulge, establishing a self-consistent link between the Galaxy's central morphology and its complex chemical gradients [Athanassoula et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..334...65A/abstract).

Despite its widespread use, the assumption that accreted populations, including [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]], maintain strict dynamical coherence is actively contested. Dissipationless $N$-body simulations demonstrate that for typical major merger mass ratios (e.g., $1:10$), accreted populations severely overlap with "kinematically heated" in-situ populations in kinematic space, blurring the boundaries of specific accretion events [Pagnini et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...673A..86P/abstract). Furthermore, rigid kinematic selection criteria in integrals-of-motion space can systematically bias progenitor reconstructions; for instance, strict kinematic cuts frequently miss the metal-rich, tightly bound cores of accreted galaxies [Buder et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251011284B/abstract).

**Latent Causal Discovery**
To overcome the degeneracies inherent in clustering techniques, the field is turning toward advanced causal inference frameworks. Recently, Rank-based Latent Causal Discovery (RLCD) has been applied to galactic datasets. By analyzing just a few observable properties (e.g., age, metallicity, and present-day orbital parameters), RLCD algorithms can recover latent variables that map directly to physical initial conditions, such as the unobservable birth radius and guiding radius of migrated stars [Jin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250700134J/abstract). This data-driven extraction of causal relationships represents a vital step toward robustly disentangling the complex history of the Milky Way.

## Reconstructing the Milky Way’s Merger History

The reconstruction of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]]'s mass assembly relies on the principle of hierarchical galaxy formation, wherein the stellar halo is populated heavily by debris from the tidal disruption of [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]] [Majewski et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASA...21..197M/abstract). By identifying the remnants of these satellite accretion events, astronomers can piece together a chronological timeline of the Galaxy's growth. High-precision astrometry has been transformative in this effort; for example, hierarchical clustering of proper motions among halo RR Lyrae variables has revealed at least 16 unique [[stellar-substructure|Stellar substructure]] groupings in the Milky Way environment [Cook et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513.2509C/abstract). Furthermore, 3D two-point correlation function measurements of RR Lyrae stars suggest that the Milky Way halo acquired most of its mass approximately $9-10$ Gyr ago and has been largely quiescent since that epoch [Lancaster et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.2556L/abstract).

The most prominent of these accretion events is the Gaia-Enceladus (or Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus) merger, a major collision that fundamentally shaped the Galaxy's halo and thick disk. The timing of this merger has been tightly constrained using asteroseismology. Observations of the naked-eye, metal-poor star $\nu$ Indi—an *in situ* star that was kinematically heated by the collision—yield an age of $11.0 \pm 0.7$ (stat) $\pm 0.8$ (sys) Gyr [Chaplin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..382C/abstract). This measurement implies that the earliest the Gaia-Enceladus merger could have begun was $11.6$ Gyr ago (at $68\%$ confidence) or $13.2$ Gyr ago (at $95\%$ confidence) [Chaplin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..382C/abstract). The merger was gas-rich ("wet") and is associated with a distinct, younger population of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] aged $10-11$ Gyr that formed as a direct result of the starburst triggered by the accretion event [Valenzuela et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...687A.104V/abstract).

Recent advances have also focused on the very heart of the Milky Way. Detailed 3D mapping of inner-Galaxy metal-poor giants has successfully linked the formation of the "proto-Galaxy" population to early episodes of high-redshift gas compaction and dynamic "blue-nugget" phases, offering a direct view into the earliest stages of the Galaxy's structural assembly [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251017693S/abstract). In these innermost regions, the fossil record of massive star formation episodes in the Galactic Center—including present-day massive young star clusters—provides a continuous link between past accretion activity and ongoing structural evolution [Figer et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009msfp.book...40F/abstract). Indeed, the Galactic Center serves as a critical laboratory for studying secular processes and connecting local star formation details directly to extragalactic observations [Ginsburg et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019BAAS...51c.220G/abstract). Differentiating between early *ex situ* (accreted) stellar populations and the *in situ* populations formed within the Galaxy requires multi-dimensional chemo-dynamical analysis. Spectroscopic datasets from the [[galah-survey|GALAH survey]] indicate that while $\alpha$-elements like Mg are traditionally used for separation, elements such as Ba, Al, Cu, and Sc are critical for distinguishing accreted stars from the disk, while elements like Ba, Y, Eu, and Zn help differentiate them from the in-situ halo [Buckley et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240718823B/abstract).

However, the literature exhibits significant tension regarding the reliability of dynamically tagging accreted populations. It is a standard assumption in Galactic archaeology that accreted [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] and stellar debris retain dynamical coherence, clustering tightly in integral-of-motion space. As previously noted, recent $N$-body simulations explicitly challenge this, demonstrating severe phase-space overlap between accreted components and kinematically heated *in situ* populations [Pagnini et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...673A..86P/abstract). Furthermore, cosmological simulations suggest that applying strict cuts in integral-of-motion space selectively omits the chemically enriched cores of merging satellite galaxies, which become more tightly bound to the Milky Way potential than the metal-poor outskirts [Buder et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251011284B/abstract). Because cosmological models indicate that a typical Milky Way-mass system was built from approximately $100$ progenitor galaxies with $M_{\rm star} \gtrsim 10^5 M_\odot$, surviving satellite galaxies represent only a highly incomplete census of the Galaxy's total accretion history [Santistevan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497..747S/abstract).

## Chrono-Chemo-Kinematics: The Role of Age-Dating

Galactic archaeology has transitioned from static structural models to a dynamic, time-resolved framework known as chrono-chemo-kinematics. Establishing a definitive timeline for the [[milky-way|Milky Way]]'s assembly requires precise [[galaxy-stellar-age|stellar ages]], which are notoriously difficult to measure from traditional photometry or spectroscopy alone [Casagrande et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AN....337..889C/abstract).

The most robust absolute age determinations currently come from asteroseismology. By measuring the global oscillation frequencies of giant stars—specifically the large frequency separation $\Delta \nu$ and the frequency of maximum acoustic power $\nu_{\max}$—astronomers can accurately determine stellar masses [Stello et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835...83S/abstract). Because the lifetime of a giant star is dominated by its main-sequence phase, its mass serves as a highly reliable proxy for its age [Miglio et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.429..423M/abstract). Data from missions like Kepler, K2, and CoRoT have provided precise stellar properties for thousands of dwarfs and giants, significantly contributing to reconstructed timelines of Milky Way evolution [Silva Aguirre et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AN....337..823S/abstract). Building upon these capabilities, future observatories such as the PLATO mission are expected to reach theoretical asteroseismic age precisions of $\sim 10\%$ [Miglio et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AN....338..644M/abstract), further transforming ubiquitous red giants into galactic chronometers capable of probing distant regions of the disk and halo [Basu et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020FrASS...7...44B/abstract).

To extend age-dating beyond limited asteroseismic spatial footprints, researchers utilize chemical clocks—age-sensitive abundance ratios such as [C/N], which is altered by deep mixing along the giant branch [Lagarde et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...601A..27L/abstract), [Viscasillas Vázquez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251213145V/abstract). Other spectroscopic features, including the shape of H-$\alpha$ lines, have also been established as direct empirical tracers of stellar mass and age, capable of achieving age precisions around $10\%-15\%$ [Bergemann et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...594A.120B/abstract). Additionally, specific brief stages in stellar evolution, such as the subgiant phase, offer precise means of dating stars—acting as a stellar clock—to pinpoint dynamical processes in Galaxy formation [Beers et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Natur.603..580B/abstract). Recently, machine learning frameworks have been trained on high-fidelity asteroseismic and chemical datasets to infer ages for millions of stars. For example, the SIDRA algorithm leverages Gaia spectrophotometry to predict ages with residuals as low as $\sim 0.064$ dex for giant stars around $10 \text{ Gyr}$ old [Almannaei et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241209040A/abstract). 

Despite these advances, the literature presents significant tensions regarding age accuracy. The choice of theoretical stellar evolutionary models heavily influences age inference; different model grids can yield age discrepancies exceeding $80\%$ for red giants without known masses, particularly at non-solar metallicities [Morales et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986..229M/abstract). Furthermore, the discovery of apparently "young" $\alpha$-rich stars has sparked ongoing debate. While initially interpreted as evidence of recent, unusual star formation in the disk, subsequent kinematic and chemical analyses indicate these objects are likely the products of binary evolution, stellar mergers, or mass accretion. These processes mimic higher masses and artificially lower their asteroseismic ages, meaning up to $10\%$ of low-$\alpha$ field stars may also have underestimated ages [Grisoni et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.111G/abstract).

When accurate ages are successfully integrated with chemical and 6D kinematic data, they provide a stringent timeline for galactic evolution. For instance, determining the age dependence of metallicity gradients in the Galactic disc through combined astrometry and asteroseismology provides critical constraints for understanding the evolution of the Milky Way disc at high-redshift [Casagrande et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..330..206C/abstract). A prime example of precise dating is for early accretion events: asteroseismology of the naked-eye star $\nu$ Indi constrained the earliest possible epoch of the [[gaia-enceladus|Gaia Enceladus]] merger to no earlier than $\sim 11.6$ Gyr ago [Chaplin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..382C/abstract). By mapping such age-dated populations across the Galaxy, chrono-chemo-kinematics is steadily reconstructing the [[milky-way|Milky Way]]'s [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and hierarchical assembly.

## Globular Clusters as Witnesses of Early Assembly

Globular clusters (GCs) are among the oldest gravitationally bound systems in the [[milky-way|Milky Way]], making them essential probes of the Galaxy's nascent phases and its subsequent hierarchical assembly. As surviving remnants of both [[in-situ-formation|In-situ formation]] and [[ex-situ-galaxy-growth|Ex-situ galaxy growth]], the [[galactic-globular-cluster-system|Galactic globular cluster system]] offers a robust fossil record for tracing the timeline of early merger events and the chemical evolution of the young Galaxy. Detailed abundance studies show that different $\alpha$-elements in globular clusters trace distinct processes of Milky Way chemical evolution along a chronological timeline [Dias et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Msngr.165...19D/abstract).

Traditionally, GCs have been approximated as simple stellar populations formed in a single burst. However, high-resolution spectroscopy and precision photometry have revealed the ubiquitous presence of multiple [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] within individual clusters. While the exact formation mechanism of these multiple populations remains unresolved, comparative studies between Galactic and [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]] suggest that the host galaxy environment plays only a minor role in their origin [Vanaraj et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507..282V/abstract).

In the context of the Galactic bulge, certain massive, complex clusters are now interpreted as the surviving nuclei or remnants of primordial building blocks rather than standard GCs. A premier example is Terzan 5, which exhibits at least two distinct stellar populations separated by different iron contents and ages [Ferraro et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..483F/abstract). The identification of field stars like SOS1—a second-generation star exhibiting enhancements in nitrogen, sodium, aluminum, and $s$-process cerium, dynamically stripped from Terzan 5 by the Galactic bar approximately $350$ Myr ago—further corroborates the hypothesis that Terzan 5 is the remnant of a massive primordial fragment that contributed to the early assembly of the bulge [Souza et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108096S/abstract).

Beyond the inner Galaxy, the age and kinematic distributions of halo GCs serve as chronometers for early accretion events. The age distribution of Milky Way GCs is broadly bimodal. Older GCs ($12-13$ Gyr) are typically associated with rapid, in-situ collapse or the initial accretion of massive satellites, whereas slightly younger populations ($10-11$ Gyr) trace gas-rich ("wet") mergers, most notably the [[gaia-enceladus|Gaia Enceladus]] (or Gaia-Sausage) event [Valenzuela et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...687A.104V/abstract). This bimodal age structure aligns with a formation scenario wherein the inner halo formed monotonically on timescales $\Delta t < 1$ Gyr, while the outer halo assembled more chaotically over several gigayears [Sarajedini et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PASP..109.1321S/abstract).

Despite their utility, the use of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] to reconstruct specific merger histories remains challenging. The standard paradigm assumes that GCs accreted from a single progenitor satellite will cluster tightly in integral-of-motion space. However, $N$-body simulations demonstrate that accreted GCs can experience severe phase-space mixing, significantly overlapping with "kinematically heated" in-situ clusters [Pagnini et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...673A..86P/abstract). This dynamical dispersion highlights the necessity of combining kinematics with rigorous [[chemical-abundances-and-nucleosynthesis|Chemical Abundances and Nucleosynthesis]] to accurately untangle the Galaxy's early assembly.

## Extragalactic Archaeology and the Local Volume

Extragalactic archaeology applies the principles of galactic archaeology to external galaxies, bridging the detailed "near-field" fossil record with the high-redshift universe [Freeman et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ARA&A..40..487F/abstract), [Frebel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ARA&A..53..631F/abstract). In this context, the galactic neighborhood serves as a local laboratory for studying mass-energy-chemical cycles and the fossil record of galaxy assembly [Wang et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011nlaw.confI...5W/abstract). By extending our view to the [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|Local Volume and Nearby Galaxy Studies]], astronomers can directly test whether the hierarchical assembly history of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] is typical of other galaxies with similar masses. To provide "far-field" context, look-back observations of massive submillimetre mergers at redshifts of $z \sim 2.3$ illustrate the rapid assembly of massive elliptical galaxies, a process that [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] aims to trace locally through fossil remnants [Fu et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.498..338F/abstract). A foundational review of the cosmic star formation history established that starbirth probably peaked at redshifts between $1$ and $2$ (roughly $10$ billion years ago) at rates $10$ times higher than today [Madau et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997AIPC..393..481M/abstract). Locally, this is corroborated by analyzing the "fossil record" of nearly 100,000 nearby galaxies, which similarly indicates that the bulk of cosmic star formation peaked roughly 5 billion years ago before steadily declining [Heavens et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Natur.428..625H/abstract). Furthermore, extragalactic archaeology can also be applied to trace the signatures of past nuclear activity (AGN lifecycles) using multi-wavelength optical and radio observations [Morganti et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017NatAs...1..596M/abstract).

The [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] (M31) provides the closest comparative laboratory for these studies. Observations of resolved stars in M31 have revealed an extended, metal-poor stellar halo following a power-law density profile out to $r = 160~{\rm kpc}$, which is dynamically and chemically distinct from its inner $r^{1/4}$ bulge [Guhathakurta et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..2366G/abstract). This extended debris network confirms that M31 grew via hierarchical accretion. Cosmological frameworks, such as the FIRE simulations, indicate that typical Local Group galaxies formed their main progenitors at redshifts $z \sim 3-4$ (roughly $11.6-12.2~{\rm Gyr}$ ago) and accreted approximately 100 dwarf satellites with stellar masses $M_{\rm star} \gtrsim 10^5~M_\odot$ over their evolutionary histories [Santistevan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497..747S/abstract). Advanced models, such as the StAGE semi-empirical framework, explicitly use stellar archaeology to link these local quiescent galaxies directly back to their high-redshift dusty star-forming progenitors [Bosi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...984..117B/abstract).

Because resolving individual faint stars becomes observationally expensive beyond the Local Group, [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]] serve as vital archaeological tracers. The age distributions of globular clusters in M31 and other nearby galaxies (such as NGC 1407 and NGC 3115) exhibit multimodal peaks that coincide with active, early gas-rich ("wet") merger phases [Valenzuela et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...687A.104V/abstract). To further this goal, astronomers have proposed future large-scale spectroscopic facilities that will utilize high-resolution ($R \sim 20,000$) integrated-light spectroscopy of these massive clusters, which will allow for the chemical tagging of distinct accretion events across the Local Volume [Beasley et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260105849B/abstract). Furthermore, comparative studies of ancient clusters in the Large Magellanic Cloud (e.g., NGC 1786 and NGC 1898) and the Milky Way indicate that the host galaxy environment plays only a minor role in the formation of internal multiple [[stellar-populations|stellar populations]] [Vanaraj et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507..282V/abstract).

Deep, wide-field imaging has also begun to map low-surface-brightness tidal features and diffuse stellar halos in nearby galaxy groups. Recent Euclid Early Release Observations of the Dorado group have successfully constrained the mass assembly of massive ellipticals and lenticulars (e.g., NGC 1549 and NGC 1553) through the morphology of their diffuse structures and globular cluster density profiles [Urbano et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...700A.104U/abstract). 

Looking ahead, next-generation observatories will revolutionize [[near-field-cosmology|Near-field cosmology]]. The proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory aims to resolve [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] down to the oldest main-sequence turnoff within distances of $\sim 5~{\rm Mpc}$, and down to the red clump out to $\sim 20~{\rm Mpc}$. Achieving this will require a diffraction-limited spatial resolution of $\leqslant 0.015''$ [Smercina et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250701960S/abstract). Simultaneously, extremely large ground-based instruments like ELT-HARMONI will enable detailed absorption-line spectroscopy of dense, resolved stellar fields in more distant Local Volume targets, such as the giant elliptical Centaurus A [Gonzalez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181004422G/abstract).

## Astrobiological Implications: The Galactic Habitable Zone

The intersection of [[astrobiology|Astrobiology]] and galactic archaeology provides a quantitative basis for identifying regions in the Universe capable of supporting complex life. This effort centers on defining the [[galactic-habitable-zone|Galactic Habitable Zone]] (GHZ), a spatial and temporal parameter space constrained by the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and chemical enrichment of a host galaxy. To anchor these models, researchers must consider the host galaxy's underlying parameters, such as the [[milky-way|Milky Way]]'s calculated star formation rate, which is observationally estimated at approximately $1.7~M_\odot$ per year [Robitaille et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010HiA....15..799R/abstract).

For complex life to emerge and survive, a galactic environment must satisfy four fundamental prerequisites: the presence of long-lived host stars, sufficient heavy elements to form terrestrial planets, adequate time for biological evolution, and an environment largely free of life-extinguishing supernovae [Lineweaver et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Sci...303...59L/abstract). By mapping the age and metallicity distributions of stellar populations, galactic archaeologists can trace the evolution of these conditions over cosmic time.

In the Milky Way, models integrating these chemical and dynamic constraints identify the GHZ as an annular region located $7-9$ kpc from the Galactic center, which gradually widens over time [Lineweaver et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Sci...303...59L/abstract). The optimal window for the formation of stars within this zone occurred between $8$ and $4$ billion years ago. Consequently, the archaeological record suggests that approximately $75\%$ of the stars currently residing in the Milky Way's GHZ are older than the Sun [Lineweaver et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Sci...303...59L/abstract).

Beyond the Milky Way, these principles are generalized into a broader cosmobiological framework to evaluate habitability across diverse extragalactic environments. The astrobiological potential of a galaxy is heavily governed by the metallicity-habitability relation, which links total stellar mass, total metal mass, and the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]]. Initial studies utilizing this fundamental metallicity relation argued that metal-rich, massive giant elliptical galaxies—specifically those at least twice as massive as the Milky Way with a significantly lower star formation rate—offer the most stable and enriched environments, making them the most probable "cradles of life" in the cosmos [Dayal et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...810L...2D/abstract).

However, the literature characterizing the optimal sites for habitability remains ambiguous and actively debated. Recent high-resolution cosmological simulations have revisited the distribution of habitability, challenging the strict preference for massive quiescent galaxies. These models suggest that metal-rich dwarf galaxies—often the compact remnants of more massive systems subjected to tidal stripping—also possess significant astrobiological potential [Mitrašinović et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250511048M/abstract). Although dense or dynamic environments were traditionally considered too hazardous for long-term habitability due to stellar encounters and frequent supernovae, recent analyses suggest that tidal structures and dynamic galactic interactions might actually enhance the distribution of biological material rather than simply suppressing it [Mitrašinović et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250511048M/abstract). Thus, the exact galactic environments most favored for complex life remain an open question, inextricably linked to our evolving understanding of galaxy assembly and chemical enrichment.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite the transformative impact of the *Gaia* mission and massive spectroscopic surveys, fundamental assumptions in Galactic archaeology remain under active debate. As the field transitions from data collection to detailed dynamical modeling, it faces significant theoretical, methodological, and observational challenges.

A primary bottleneck is the theoretical uncertainty in stellar age-dating. While asteroseismology provides highly precise measurements, the choice of underlying stellar evolution models can induce systematic age discrepancies exceeding $80\%$, particularly for red giant branch stars at non-solar metallicities [Morales et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986..229M/abstract). The timeline of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] is further complicated by the presence of "young" $\alpha$-rich stars. Recent studies suggest that many of these stars are not genuinely young products of anomalous star formation, but rather the results of binary mass transfer or mergers that mimic young asteroseismic ages [Grisoni et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.111G/abstract). Furthermore, theoretical models face the challenge of matching high-precision data. Cosmological simulations increasingly use observed stellar gradients to investigate the role of mechanical supernova feedback in shaping the inside-out growth of galaxies, a process whose exact regulatory mechanisms remain an open question [Ibrahim et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250111209I/abstract).

Foundational assumptions regarding [[chemo-dynamics|Chemo-dynamics]] and substructure recovery are also being scrutinized. Reconstructions of hierarchical assembly often assume that accreted [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] and satellite debris retain dynamical coherence in kinematic space. However, as previously established, $N$-body simulations demonstrate that accreted systems can severely overlap with kinematically heated [[in-situ-formation|In-situ formation]] populations, casting doubt on specific accretion histories derived purely from phase-space clustering [Pagnini et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...673A..86P/abstract). Chemically, the premise that stellar atmospheres act as immutable fossil records has been challenged by evidence that post-formation surface alterations, such as planetary engulfment, can induce abundance anomalies [Spina et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...863..179S/abstract), complicating [[strong-chemical-tagging|Strong chemical tagging]] efforts.

To overcome these degeneracies, the field is increasingly turning to Machine learning and advanced statistical frameworks. Neural networks are now routinely deployed to infer stellar ages directly from survey spectrophotometry [Almannaei et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241209040A/abstract). More fundamentally, causal discovery algorithms, such as Rank-based Latent Causal Discovery (RLCD), are being used to disentangle the complex effects of [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|galactic migration]] and stellar feedback. By analyzing present-day observable properties, these models can infer unobservable latent variables—such as a star's true birth radius—in a purely data-driven manner [Jin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250700134J/abstract).

In the coming decades, next-generation observatories will push the boundaries of the field into the Local Volume. The upcoming Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST) will play a crucial role, providing the deepest wide-angle view of the southern sky for studying galaxy evolution and generating unprecedented statistical samples of [[stellar-populations|stellar populations]] [Robertson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NatRP...1..450R/abstract). Additionally, 30- to 40-meter class facilities, including the Extremely Large Telescope (ELT), will provide the high-resolution faint-star spectroscopy necessary to resolve dense nuclear regions [Gonzalez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181004422G/abstract). However, current ELT designs lack the necessary wide-field survey capabilities. To address this gap, a dedicated, new northern 30- to 40-meter telescope with wide-field multiplexed capabilities has been proposed specifically to enable the chemodynamical mapping of distant halo streams, allowing researchers to measure gravitational perturbations from dark matter subhalos [Anguiano et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251214789A/abstract). Looking further ahead, the proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory aims to panchromatically resolve the main bodies of galaxies out to distances of $\sim 50$ Mpc. By extending precise stellar astrophysics to cosmological volumes, it will allow astronomers to reconstruct the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and chemical evolution of galaxies across the entire Hubble sequence [Smercina et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250701960S/abstract).
