**Frequentist inference** is a foundational framework within [[statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Statistical methods in astrophysics]] that treats probability strictly as the long-run frequency of observable events under hypothetical repeated trials. Unlike Bayesian inference, which models physical parameters as random variables described by prior probability distributions, the frequentist paradigm considers parameters to be fixed but unknown constants. Consequently, statistical constraints rely entirely on the likelihood of the observed data rather than external priors. The cornerstone of this approach is [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] (MLE), which identifies the parameter values that maximize the probability of the observed dataset. This is routinely paired with [[null-hypothesis-testing|Null hypothesis testing]] to rigorously quantify the statistical significance of new transient events, periodicities, or signal detections via p-values and confidence intervals. Historically, these techniques were instrumental in early evaluations of spatial point processes, originally used to determine whether the large-scale distribution of galaxies deviated from uniform randomness [de Vaucouleurs et al. (1971)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1971PASP...83..113D/abstract).

In modern observational astrophysics, frequentist techniques are widely applied across a variety of regimes, from calibrating scaling relations and characterizing multivariate galaxy populations [Saslaw et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999diga.book.....S/abstract) to probing the cosmic large-scale structure. A primary objective within this framework is the construction of robust [[statistical-estimators-in-astronomy|Statistical estimators in astronomy]] that minimize variance and observational bias, such as the minimum-variance Landy-Szalay estimator for the [[n-point-correlation-function|N-point correlation function]] [Landy et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...412...64L/abstract). When physical models contain high-dimensional nuisance parameters, frequentist approaches typically employ the profile likelihood—maximizing the likelihood over these uninteresting variables—as a direct alternative to Bayesian marginalization. This profiling technique has proven highly effective in precision cosmology, yielding constraints on fundamental values like the Hubble constant that are remarkably consistent with prior-heavy Bayesian models [Barua et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250211998B/abstract).

Despite its robust mathematical foundations, the application of frequentist inference faces significant challenges in the era of Stage-IV cosmological surveys. Standard implementations often rely heavily on the [[chi-squared-test|Chi-squared test]] and the assumption that observables follow [[multivariate-gaussian-distributions|Multivariate Gaussian distributions]]. However, these assumptions frequently break down on highly non-linear spatial scales or in the presence of complex, heteroscedastic measurement noise. This article explores the mathematical evolution of frequentist testing, detailing the derivation of non-Gaussian likelihoods, corrections for finite-volume biases, and the use of linear shrinkage techniques to regularize ill-conditioned precision matrices. Finally, the article surveys ongoing open questions in the field, including the integration of frequentist error correction with likelihood-free neural network inference, the computational scalability of profile likelihoods compared to high-dimensional Bayesian field-level reconstructions [Simon-Onfroy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250420130S/abstract), and specific regimes—such as astrometric parallax estimation—where the fundamental absence of structural prior information can induce severe measurement biases.

## Foundations of Frequentist Inference in Astrophysics

In astrophysics, the frequentist framework treats probability strictly as the long-run frequency of repeatable events. Under this paradigm, physical parameters are viewed as fixed, unknown constants rather than random variables. Consequently, frequentist inference does not employ prior probability distributions, distinguishing it fundamentally from Bayesian approaches that map parameter uncertainty using priors. 

The cornerstone of frequentist parameter estimation is [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] (MLE). Given an observational dataset $D$ and a physical model parameterized by $\theta$, the likelihood function $\mathcal{L}(\theta | D) = P(D | \theta)$ quantifies the probability of observing the data given a specific set of parameters. The frequentist estimator $\hat{\theta}$ is defined as the value that maximizes $\mathcal{L}(\theta | D)$. While parameter estimation often relies on [[gaussian-statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Gaussian Statistical Methods in Astrophysics]] and the [[chi-squared-test|Chi-squared test]], the maximum likelihood framework can be rigorously generalized to non-Gaussian distributions. This generalization is critical for analyzing structures like the cosmic microwave background and large-scale structure, where initial perturbations may exhibit primordial non-Gaussianity [Amendola et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApL&C..33...63A/abstract). This rigorous mathematical framing is critical for confronting 21st-century statistical and computational challenges in astrophysics, such as detecting extremely faint signals buried in non-stationary noise and developing sophisticated machine learning algorithms to process multi-petabyte mega-datasets [Feigelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AnRSA...8..493F/abstract).

In constructing [[statistical-estimators-in-astronomy|Statistical estimators in astronomy]], frequentists aim for robust estimators with minimal variance and bias. For example, in galaxy clustering, optimized estimators are required to approach theoretical Poisson variance limits and mitigate survey edge effects [Landy et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...412...64L/abstract). However, care must be taken with non-linear combinations of direct observables, which can suffer from significant volume-dependent biases—often related to the integral constraint—when normalized by estimated mean counts in a cell [Hui et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...519..622H/abstract).

To handle nuisance parameters, frequentist methods typically utilize the profile likelihood, which maximizes the likelihood over the nuisance dimensions rather than integrating over them as in Bayesian marginalization. The literature demonstrates that these two approaches often yield highly consistent results. For instance, frequentist profile likelihood constraints on the Hubble constant are found to agree closely with Bayesian marginalization [Barua et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250211998B/abstract). Similar cross-method consistency has been found when placing upper limits on the local density of ultralight scalar-field dark matter using pulsar timing array data [Porayko et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98j2002P/abstract).

Despite these successes, the absence of prior probability distributions in purely frequentist inference can induce severe tensions in specific observational regimes. A well-documented conflict arises in distance estimation from stellar parallaxes. When fractional parallax errors exceed approximately $20\%$, a standard frequentist MLE approach (which mathematically mirrors an uninformative uniform prior in distance) produces estimates with unacceptably large bias and variance, and fundamentally struggles to accommodate non-positive observed parallaxes [Bailer-Jones et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PASP..127..994B/abstract). In such cases, the literature explicitly notes that the classical frequentist treatment diverges from Bayesian methods, and that incorporating structural prior information (e.g., an asymptotically decreasing density at infinity) is often unavoidable to produce physically meaningful distances [Hendry et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995VA.....39..297H/abstract), [Bailer-Jones et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PASP..127..994B/abstract). The impact of these foundational assumptions extends across the discipline. For instance, in fitting galaxy rotation curves to test whether Newton's constant or the universal acceleration scale varies from galaxy to galaxy, the specific choice of Bayesian priors can lead to starkly different physical conclusions compared to a pure frequentist likelihood analysis, explicitly highlighting the profound role that parameter degeneracies play in shaping astrophysical inference [Li et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646L..13L/abstract).

## Historical Evolution of Statistical Testing

Historically, the application of frequentist inference in astrophysics was heavily concentrated on spatial point processes, specifically to determine whether the large-scale distribution of galaxies deviated from a uniform random Poisson distribution [de Vaucouleurs et al. (1971)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1971PASP...83..113D/abstract). As observational catalogs expanded, the focus shifted from simple [[null-hypothesis-testing|Null hypothesis testing]] of randomness toward the quantitative measurement of clustering via the [[n-point-correlation-function|N-point correlation function]]. A landmark in frequentist [[statistical-estimators-in-astronomy|Statistical estimators in astronomy]] was the introduction of the Landy-Szalay estimator for the two-point correlation function, which brought the measurement variance close to the theoretical Poisson limit while mitigating survey edge effects [Landy et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...412...64L/abstract). Parallel to spatial correlation estimators, the historical and contemporary use of hierarchical clustering has been fundamental for classifying sample populations and identifying diverse sub-structures, ranging from asteroids to massive galaxy clusters [Yu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&C....4100662Y/abstract). Moreover, as observational capabilities advanced, so too did techniques for parsing the cosmic web; researchers increasingly employed information theory decision schemes to optimally compare and choose classifiers for cosmic web structure types, such as voids and clusters [Leclercq et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JCAP...08..027L/abstract). However, frequentist point estimates for higher-order clustering statistics, such as variance and skewness, were subsequently shown to suffer from inherent finite-volume biases, most notably the integral constraint [Hui et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...519..622H/abstract).

With the advent of precision cosmology, the focus of large-scale structure studies shifted to Fourier-space power spectrum analysis, building upon the pedagogical foundations of power spectrum estimation—specifically the Feldman-Kaiser-Peacock (FKP) method, which optimized Fourier density field measurements in flux-limited surveys [Hamilton et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009LNP...665..415H/abstract). The traditional frequentist approach assumes that the distribution of power spectrum measurements follows [[multivariate-gaussian-distributions|Multivariate Gaussian distributions]], a choice historically justified by the Central Limit Theorem given the vast number of density modes. Under this assumption, inference predominantly relies on the standard [[chi-squared-test|Chi-squared test]] and [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]].

However, there is an explicit consensus in the modern literature that this standard Gaussian likelihood assumption breaks down on large physical scales (where the number of modes is small) and on highly non-linear scales. Assuming a Gaussian likelihood for non-Gaussian distributed data can severely bias cosmological parameter estimation; for instance, measurements of primordial non-Gaussianity ($f_{\rm NL}$) can be artificially underestimated if the true skewed posterior of the power spectrum is ignored [Kalus et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2573K/abstract). To correct for this, both historical and contemporary methods have attempted to generalize maximum likelihood techniques for non-Gaussian data [Amendola et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApL&C..33...63A/abstract) and developed analytical data transformations to enforce an approximately Gaussian likelihood, combined with variance-correlation decompositions of the covariance matrix [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..951W/abstract). 

The evolution of frequentist testing is also tightly coupled to the computational challenge of covariance matrix estimation. Modern Stage-IV galaxy surveys require immense precision matrices (the inverse of the covariance matrix). Because these are typically estimated from a finite number of mock catalogs, standard sample estimators become ill-conditioned and noisy. To address this, frequentist shrinkage estimators have been introduced to apply linear shrinkage to the empirical correlation matrix, yielding non-singular matrices that drastically improve parameter inference at low sample sizes [Looijmans et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240213783L/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609118E/abstract).

While Bayesian frameworks have largely dominated recent cosmological parameter estimation, frequentist methods continue to evolve as a robust alternative. Methodological tensions often center on the treatment of nuisance parameters and prior information—for example, the necessity of priors to avoid severely biased distance estimates from non-positive astrometric parallaxes [Bailer-Jones et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PASP..127..994B/abstract). Nevertheless, modern frequentist techniques utilizing profile likelihoods offer highly competitive results, validating them as a rigorous and complementary tool in modern precision astrophysics [Barua et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250211998B/abstract).

## Maximum Likelihood Estimation and Parameter Characterization

[[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] (MLE) serves as a foundational frequentist framework in astrophysics for parameterizing physical models from observed data. In standard applications, the goal is to identify the parameter vector $\theta$ that maximizes the likelihood function $\mathcal{L}(\theta | D)$ for a given dataset $D$. While analytic likelihoods in cosmology are traditionally assumed to be Gaussian or $\chi^2$-distributed, the literature emphasizes that these assumptions often break down on large spatial scales or in the non-linear regime. Utilizing strictly Gaussian likelihoods in these regimes can severely underestimate errors or bias the extraction of parameters such as the primordial non-Gaussianity $f_{\rm NL}$ [Kalus et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2573K/abstract). Consequently, generalized non-Gaussian likelihood functions have been developed to mitigate these biases in cosmic microwave background and large-scale structure analyses [Amendola et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApL&C..33...63A/abstract).

A prominent application of MLE is the statistical characterization of galaxy populations, which are formally described by a multivariate distribution function [Saslaw et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999diga.book.....S/abstract). Reconstructing intrinsic galaxy mass functions from flux-limited surveys requires rigorous corrections for observational uncertainties, such as the Eddington bias, and complex survey selection functions. To tackle these challenges within a frequentist paradigm, the Modified Maximum Likelihood (MML) estimator was developed. The MML approach provides an unbinned method to recover generative distribution functions, accounting natively for small-number statistics and non-detections. Crucially, MML achieves this recovery many orders of magnitude faster than exact [[hierarchical-bayesian-model|Hierarchical Bayesian Model]] computations while yielding identical theoretical accuracy for structural distributions [Obreschkow et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5500O/abstract). While frequentist approaches have traditionally required physical stacking to evaluate the spatial structure of faint astronomical systems, Bayesian alternatives have been developed to robustly bypass this. For example, the Population Profile Estimator (PoPE) leverages a population-based Bayesian inference approach to simultaneously model both the average spatial structure and the population variance directly from noisy datasets without stacking [Farahi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....161...30F/abstract). Similarly, evaluating the morphology of open clusters is frequently tackled via Bayesian frameworks like ASteCA, which infers projected ellipticities and rotation angles from Gaia data [Pera et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024BAAA...65...94P/abstract). At extreme scales, frequentist and Bayesian MLE variants drive multi-million object classification and parameter inference, as exemplified by the Apsis system deployed in Gaia Data Release 3, which robustly infers astrophysical parameters by integrating immense spectral and photometric datasets [Creevey et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..26C/abstract).

MLE is also extensively used to calibrate astrophysical scaling relations, such as those linking galaxy cluster masses to observable properties like X-ray luminosity and temperature. Frequentist optimization of these scaling relations requires likelihood functions that systematically incorporate intrinsic astrophysical scatter and correlated measurement errors across multiple response variables. Extensions of Gibbs sampling for multivariate linear regression have provided powerful methods for calibrating these complex scaling relations between galaxy cluster mass and gas properties [Mantz et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457.1279M/abstract). Conversely, modern approaches increasingly incorporate the use of machine learning to directly combine multi-wavelength cluster observables for mass estimation, completely bypassing traditional scaling-law likelihoods [Cohn et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.1575C/abstract). In contrast to pure frequentist likelihood frameworks, multi-messenger astrophysics and compact binary coalescence studies standardly deploy Bayesian hierarchical population inference tools like ICAROGW to systematically handle noisy, heterogeneous, and incomplete observation catalogs, enabling the robust parameterization of underlying astrophysical population properties [Mastrogiovanni et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230517973M/abstract). Meanwhile, to bridge the gap between strict point estimation and complex model selection, variational inference has rapidly emerged as a faster computational alternative to classical Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods for diverse problems, including exoplanet orbital modeling and cluster mass estimation [Gunapati et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASA...39....1G/abstract). Furthermore, for dynamical cluster masses, simulation-based inference (SBI) utilizing 3D convolutional neural networks provides another modern avenue, directly quantifying uncertainties from projected phase-space distributions [Kodi Ramanah et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.4080K/abstract).

When models contain a large number of uninteresting variables, frequentist inference typically employs the profile likelihood to eliminate them, serving as the classical counterpart to Bayesian marginalization. Recent comparative studies in cosmography demonstrate that profile likelihoods offer a highly robust alternative, producing remarkably consistent confidence intervals without the need for priors [Barua et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250211998B/abstract), [Barua et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025Ap&SS.370...62B/abstract). 

Despite the power of MLE and its variants, practitioners must carefully account for non-linear estimation biases inherent to frequentist summary statistics. For example, deriving the skewness of the galaxy distribution $S_3 = \bar{\xi}_3 / \bar{\xi}_2^2$ by taking the ratio of independently unbiased estimators for the third moment ($\bar{\xi}_3$) and the variance ($\bar{\xi}_2$) does not inherently produce an unbiased estimator for $S_3$. This division by estimated mean counts introduces volume-dependent systematic errors in survey data, demonstrating that even formally optimized frequentist point estimators must be strictly validated against survey geometry and selection effects [Hui et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...519..622H/abstract).

## Hypothesis Testing and Statistical Significance

In frequentist inference, the evaluation of astrophysical signals—such as transient events, weak emission lines, or periodicities—relies fundamentally on [[null-hypothesis-testing|Null hypothesis testing]]. Under this framework, researchers compute p-values to quantify the probability of obtaining an observation at least as extreme as the one measured, assuming that the null hypothesis (typically modeling pure background noise or standard physics) is true. When a p-value falls below a pre-determined significance threshold, the null hypothesis is rejected in favor of a signal detection.

When constructing parameter constraints, frequentist methods determine statistical significance using confidence intervals rather than Bayesian credible intervals. A $1-\alpha$ confidence interval is defined such that, under hypothetical repeated sampling, the calculated interval will contain the true parameter value a fraction $1-\alpha$ of the time. To evaluate confidence intervals in the presence of complex observational systematics, frequentist approaches often replace Bayesian marginalization with the profile likelihood, an extension of [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]]. The profile likelihood $L_p$ for a parameter of interest $\theta$ is derived by maximizing the full likelihood $\mathcal{L}$ over all nuisance parameters $\eta$:
$$ L_p(\theta) = \max_{\eta} \mathcal{L}(\theta, \eta | \mathbf{x}) $$
This technique has been robustly validated against modern cosmological measurements, demonstrating that frequentist confidence intervals can agree with Bayesian credible intervals to within fractions of a standard deviation [Barua et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250211998B/abstract), [Barua et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025Ap&SS.370...62B/abstract).

Frequentist significance tests are particularly critical in searches for periodicities, where establishing rigorous upper limits in the event of a non-detection is required. In the search for ultralight scalar-field ("fuzzy") dark matter using data from the Parkes Pulsar Timing Array, researchers tested for periodic nanohertz variations in pulse arrival times. Because no statistically significant periodicity was detected, frequentist inference was employed to establish a 95% confidence upper limit on the local dark matter density, constraining it to $\le 6$ GeV cm$^{-3}$ for boson masses $m \le 10^{-23}$ eV [Porayko et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98j2002P/abstract). Historical analyses have similarly relied on likelihood ratio tests to evaluate the statistical significance of periodic quantizations in double-galaxy redshifts against monotone-decreasing, continuous distribution functions [Cocke et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...393...59C/abstract).

The accuracy of computed p-values and confidence intervals is strictly contingent upon the validity of the underlying distributional assumptions. Traditional significance testing in astronomy frequently relies on the [[chi-squared-test|Chi-squared test]], which implicitly assumes that observational errors are Gaussian. However, the literature indicates that non-Gaussianity is prevalent in many astrophysical domains, such as the nonlinear regimes of large-scale structure and cosmic microwave background fluctuations. In these environments, the standard $\chi^2$ distribution fails to accurately capture the probability in the distribution's tails. Generalized likelihood frameworks that accommodate non-Gaussian distributed data must be utilized to correctly map the boundaries of confidence intervals; failing to adjust for these deviations can fundamentally bias claims of statistical significance [Amendola et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApL&C..33...63A/abstract). Furthermore, when inferred parameter distributions (whether derived through frequentist profile likelihoods or Bayesian posteriors) must be rigorously validated against true underlying constraints, multidimensional Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests can be utilized. These tests formally quantify whether high-dimensional astrophysical parameter estimates—such as those describing galaxy cluster properties—statistically deviate from the true distributions, providing a robust, non-parametric validation of statistical constraints [Harrison et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451.2610H/abstract).

## Frequentist Estimators for Large-Scale Structure

In cosmological surveys, analyzing the spatial distribution of galaxies relies heavily on the construction of robust frequentist estimators. The fundamental statistic used to quantify this clustering is the [[n-point-correlation-function|N-point correlation function]], which measures the excess probability of finding $N$-tuplets of galaxies at specific spatial separations relative to a random, unclustered Poisson distribution. 

For the two-point angular correlation function, $w(\theta)$, early estimators such as the simple ratio of data-data ($DD$) to random-random ($RR$) pair counts, or data-data to data-random ($DR$) counts, were found to suffer from sub-optimal variance and non-negligible biases. To resolve this, Landy & Szalay [Landy et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...412...64L/abstract) introduced a minimum-variance estimator that has since become the standard in large-scale structure studies. The Landy-Szalay estimator is defined as:
$$ \hat{w}(\theta) = \frac{DD(\theta) - 2DR(\theta) + RR(\theta)}{RR(\theta)} $$
where $DD$, $DR$, and $RR$ represent the normalized pair counts in a given angular separation bin $\theta$. By explicitly cross-correlating the observed catalog with a synthetic random catalog that shares the same survey geometry and selection effects, this estimator removes first-order boundary effects and suppresses the variance to a nearly Poissonian level [Landy et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...412...64L/abstract). While the Landy-Szalay point estimator remains a canonical frequentist tool, it contrasts significantly with joint Bayesian methods that simultaneously infer density fields, power spectra, and luminosity-dependent biases from the same datasets [Jasche et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...779...15J/abstract). Similarly, to handle complicated survey geometries and masks, hierarchical Bayesian modeling has been developed as a direct alternative to frequentist shear power spectrum estimators [Alsing et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.4452A/abstract). Pushing beyond statistical summary points entirely, algorithms such as BORG (Bayesian Origin Reconstruction from Galaxies) offer a deep-inference alternative to frequentist spatial statistics, enabling the physical probabilistic cartography and reconstruction of initial conditions alongside late-time large-scale structures [Leclercq et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151202242L/abstract).

Another key application of two-point spatial statistics is clustering-based redshift estimation. This data-driven frequentist technique leverages [[cross-correlation-analysis|Cross-correlation analysis]] to infer the unknown redshift distributions of target photometric sources by measuring their clustering amplitude against a well-characterized reference sample of spectroscopic redshifts [Rahman et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.3500R/abstract). 

Despite the mathematical optimality of estimators like Landy-Szalay, volume-averaged measurements of variance and skewness derived from such correlation functions are inherently subject to estimation biases. Because the true universal mean density of galaxies is unknown, it must be estimated from the survey sample itself. This introduces a systematic negative offset known as the integral constraint [Hui et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...519..622H/abstract). This estimation bias is highly volume-dependent; consequently, estimators applied to surveys of different sizes can yield systematically discrepant measurements of variance and skewness if these finite-volume effects are not carefully analytically corrected [Hui et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...519..622H/abstract).

As traditional two-point analyses reach their theoretical limits, extracting additional cosmological information requires moving toward higher-order and topological estimators. The 'Beyond-2pt' community data challenge benchmarks these emerging techniques, testing the statistical power of non-standard frequentist approaches like density-split clustering and void statistics to extract information inaccessible to the canonical two-point correlation function [Beyond-2pt Collaboration et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240502252B/abstract). Beyond simple catalog division, researchers have also introduced soft clustering assignments as a method for quantifying model uncertainty and enabling fully differentiable correlation functions in large-scale structure catalogs, significantly enriching the estimator landscape [Berman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250406174B/abstract). 

Moving beyond two-point statistics, evaluating the exact [[n-point-correlation-function|N-point correlation function]] natively scales as $\mathcal{O}(n^N)$ for a sample of $n$ galaxies. For Stage-IV surveys containing hundreds of millions of objects, direct point-counting for $N \ge 3$ is computationally prohibitive. However, modern frequentist computational frameworks have bypassed this bottleneck by expanding the spatial statistics into separable angular bases. This mathematical reformulation allows the components of the $N$-point correlation functions to be estimated in $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ time for any dimension, enabling rigorous higher-order clustering analysis [Philcox et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PNAS..11911366P/abstract).

Finally, evaluating the statistical significance of these frequentist estimators requires the precise determination of the covariance matrix and its inverse, the precision matrix. Because empirical covariance matrices computed from a limited number of mock catalogs are often overly noisy and ill-conditioned, directly inverting them amplifies noise. To stabilize the precision matrix, contemporary analyses employ linear shrinkage estimators, which pull the empirical correlation matrix toward a theoretical Gaussian target [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609118E/abstract) or an empirical target constructed from the sample estimate [Looijmans et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240213783L/abstract). This shrinkage ensures the precision matrix is strictly non-singular and unbiased, which is a critical requirement for accurate frequentist parameter inference in modern large-scale structure pipelines [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609118E/abstract).

## Covariance and Precision Matrix Estimation

In frequentist parameter inference, constructing a robust likelihood requires an accurate covariance matrix and its inverse, the precision matrix. For Stage-IV high-precision cosmological surveys (e.g., DESI and Euclid), covariance matrices must rigorously capture observational shot noise and intrinsic sample variance. However, estimating a high-dimensional covariance matrix from a limited sample size $n$—such as a finite suite of mock simulations or internal data resampling—presents a major statistical challenge. When $n$ is not strictly much larger than the dimension of the data vector, the empirical sample covariance matrix becomes noisy and ill-conditioned. This results in a highly unstable precision matrix upon inversion, which can artificially distort frequentist confidence intervals and limit the survey's statistical power [Looijmans et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.537...21L/abstract).

To regularize ill-conditioned covariance matrices, modern [[statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Statistical methods in astrophysics]] increasingly rely on shrinkage estimation. These techniques construct a stabilized estimator by combining an empirical sample covariance with a well-behaved target matrix, which can be derived analytically or stochastically. Methods such as the non-linear NERCOME estimator, or direct linear shrinkage applied to the precision matrix itself, significantly improve the conditioning of the matrix at low $n$ [Looijmans et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.537...21L/abstract). By shrinking the empirical estimate toward a structured target, these [[statistical-estimators-in-astronomy|Statistical estimators in astronomy]] guarantee a non-singular and strictly invertible matrix. 

Data-driven internal estimation techniques, such as jackknife resampling, offer an alternative to generating thousands of computationally expensive forward simulations. However, standard delete-1 jackknife estimators tend to be biased high. To mitigate this, the DICES (Debiased Internal Covariance Estimation with Shrinkage) framework applies linear shrinkage to the empirical correlation matrix (rather than the covariance directly) toward a Gaussian prediction [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609118E/abstract). When combined with a delete-2 jackknife bias correction applied to the diagonal elements, DICES yields relative error improvements of $33\%$ for the covariance and $48\%$ for the correlation structure compared to standard jackknife estimates [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609118E/abstract).

Beyond sample size limitations, the inherent cosmology dependence of the covariance matrix must be modeled to prevent biased parameter inference [Fumagalli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...652A..21F/abstract), [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..951W/abstract). Analytical covariance models, which incorporate both shot noise and sample variance, have been shown to reproduce simulation variances to within $10\%$ for observables like cluster number counts, emphasizing the necessity of using a full, cosmology-dependent covariance in the Gaussian likelihood [Fumagalli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...652A..21F/abstract). 

Finally, estimating variance directly from observational data is complicated by finite-volume effects. For clustering statistics, naïve pair-count estimators exhibits variances significantly larger than pure Poisson noise, requiring optimized estimators to suppress this variance [Landy et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...412...64L/abstract). Furthermore, spatial smoothing and boundaries introduce an estimation bias equivalent to the integral constraint, which systematically biases sample variance and skewness measurements downward if uncorrected [Hui et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...519..622H/abstract). Applying variance-correlation decompositions allows researchers to accurately capture these cosmological and volume-dependent effects while preserving an approximately Gaussian likelihood [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..951W/abstract).

## Profile Likelihood and Nuisance Parameter Handling

In astronomical data analysis, models frequently contain both parameters of primary physical interest and a set of "nuisance parameters" that must be constrained or integrated out to accurately compute uncertainties. While Bayesian frameworks inherently deal with nuisance parameters by integrating over them (marginalization), frequentist methods typically address them through the construction of a profile likelihood. This approach has gained traction as a robust complement to Bayesian techniques, allowing researchers to evaluate parameter constraints without specifying prior distributions.

Let a model be described by a likelihood function $L(\theta, \eta)$, where $\theta$ represents the parameters of interest and $\eta$ denotes the nuisance parameters. The profile likelihood, $L_p(\theta)$, is defined by maximizing the likelihood function over the nuisance parameters for every fixed value of $\theta$:
$$L_p(\theta) = \sup_{\eta} L(\theta, \eta)$$
By replacing $\eta$ with its conditional maximum likelihood estimate $\hat{\eta}(\theta)$, the dimensionality of the inference problem is reduced to the parameter of interest. The resulting profile likelihood can then be used to construct confidence intervals via the likelihood ratio, leveraging standard asymptotic theorems in [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]]. 

When the number of nuisance parameters becomes overwhelmingly large, directly profiling them over a likelihood surface can become computationally intractable. In explicitly Bayesian frameworks, marginalization over massive nuisance spaces can be accelerated using linear compression techniques, such as mode projection, which systematically compresses the dimensions based on a sampled prior distribution. This technique was specifically developed for dealing with highly uncertain, binned redshift distributions in weak lensing analyses [Bernstein et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250600758B/abstract). Because frequentist profile likelihoods strictly maximize the data likelihood without utilizing prior probability distributions, prior-dependent compression techniques like mode projection cannot mathematically be applied to profiling. Instead, frequentists must rely on alternative data-driven dimension reductions or highly optimized mathematical minimizers.

Recent cosmological analyses have demonstrated that profile likelihoods can serve as a highly effective alternative to Bayesian marginalization, often yielding highly consistent quantitative results. A prominent proof-of-principle application is the determination of the Hubble constant ($H_0$) from the Megamaser Cosmology Project [Barua et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250211998B/abstract). The original Bayesian analysis of this dataset marginalized over six nuisance parameters—corresponding to the systemic velocities of the megamaser galaxies—to infer a value of $H_0 = 73.9 \pm 3.0 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$. Reanalyzing the exact same data using a frequentist profile likelihood to dispense with these nuisance parameters yields $H_0 = 73.5^{+3.0}_{-2.9} \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$. This frequentist estimate agrees with the Bayesian result to within $0.2\sigma$, producing nearly identical confidence intervals and validating the robustness of the inference against the choice of statistical paradigm [Barua et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250211998B/abstract). 

Similar methodological cross-checks have been applied in the search for ultralight scalar-field dark matter using pulsar timing arrays. When constraining the local dark matter density of low-mass bosons ($m \leq 10^{-23} \text{ eV}$), independent frequentist and Bayesian treatments of the timing residuals yield consistent upper limits, bounding the density below $6 \text{ GeV cm}^{-3}$ at $95\%$ confidence [Porayko et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98j2002P/abstract).

However, the literature notes that frequentist and Bayesian treatments of nuisance parameters and uncertainties are not universally interchangeable, particularly when observational uncertainties are large or subject to complex selection effects. A notable tension arises in distance estimation from astrometric parallaxes. When the fractional parallax error exceeds roughly 20%, classical frequentist treatments relying on uniform or uninformative priors can produce significantly biased distance estimates with large variances, particularly in the presence of non-positive parallaxes [Bailer-Jones et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PASP..127..994B/abstract). In such regimes, the injection of physically motivated prior information via Bayesian hierarchical models is often required to regularize the inference. Consequently, while the profile likelihood represents a powerful tool for [[null-hypothesis-testing|Null hypothesis testing]] and parameter estimation in well-constrained domains like megamaser cosmology, care must be taken when the data lack sufficient resolving power to strongly peak the likelihood surface.

## Challenges in Non-Gaussian and Heteroscedastic Data

Classical [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] and the [[chi-squared-test|Chi-squared test]] conventionally assume that observational data follow a [[multivariate-gaussian-distributions|Multivariate Gaussian distributions]]. While analytically convenient, this assumption is frequently violated in modern [[statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Statistical methods in astrophysics]], where researchers encounter strongly non-Gaussian likelihoods, heteroscedastic measurement errors, and ill-conditioned covariance matrices.

In the analysis of the cosmic large-scale structure, the multivariate normal assumption inherently fails on large physical scales where the number of overdensity modes is low, as well as on small, highly non-linear scales [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract), [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..951W/abstract). Applying standard Gaussian likelihoods to power spectrum fitting can lead to severe systematic biases in cosmological parameter estimation. For example, assuming a Gaussian posterior with a fixed covariance matrix underestimates the local primordial non-Gaussianity parameter $f_{\rm NL}$ by $\Delta f_{\rm NL} \approx 19.1$ for datasets equivalent to BOSS DR9 [Kalus et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2573K/abstract). To circumvent these biases, researchers have generalized maximum likelihood estimators to explicitly accommodate non-Gaussian functional forms [Amendola et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApL&C..33...63A/abstract) and applied data transformations to yield approximately Gaussian likelihoods alongside variance-correlation decompositions [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..951W/abstract).

Observational realities introduce further statistical challenges through heteroscedastic errors and complex selection functions. Astronomical surveys are subject to fluctuating sky conditions and varying telescope sensitivities, producing out-of-distribution measurement errors that strictly violate the assumptions of stationary noise models. To correctly model these complex response distributions characterized by multi-modality or heteroscedastic noise, researchers have explored non-parametric [[conditional-density-estimation|Conditional density estimation]] frameworks (e.g., FlexCode) as robust alternatives to traditional high-dimensional regression models [Izbicki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170408095I/abstract). In emerging simulation-based inference paradigms, processing data with noise properties absent from the training simulations can strongly bias results. Methodologies such as SBI++ mitigate this limitation by utilizing Monte Carlo techniques to marginalize over missing data and approximate out-of-distribution errors using nearby data realizations [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221103747W/abstract), [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952L..10W/abstract). Furthermore, direct non-linear combinations of observables intrinsically suffer from volume-dependent estimation biases, necessitating precise analytical corrections [Hui et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...519..622H/abstract). In Stage-IV surveys like LSST, these statistical challenges are compounded by severe large-scale structure systematics; for example, unmitigated photometric redshift biases and spectroscopic calibration biases can substantially degrade the accuracy of cosmological parameter estimation, requiring rigorous forward modeling and robust likelihood formulations [Awan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240914265A/abstract). To tackle biases arising from non-representative training sets, techniques such as stratified learning have been introduced, utilizing propensity score conditioning to correct for covariate shift across data populations [Autenrieth et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210611211A/abstract). Additionally, ensuring the reliability of complex inferences often requires conditional calibration; methods like Cal-PIT (Probability Integral Transform) have been developed to enforce conditionally calibrated predictive distributions in critical applications such as galaxy redshift estimation [Dey et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220514568D/abstract).

For Stage-IV spectroscopic surveys, estimating the precision matrix presents a critical computational bottleneck. Shrinkage estimators address this by combining the empirical estimate with a well-behaved analytical or stochastic target, ensuring the resulting precision matrix remains non-singular and strictly invertible without introducing significant bias [Looijmans et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240213783L/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609118E/abstract).

Finally, when frequentist tools struggle with continuous, spatially distributed signals that exhibit complex non-Gaussianities—such as log-normal cosmological density fields distorted by Poissonian shot noise and survey masks—researchers increasingly turn to Bayesian frameworks like Information Field Theory (IFT). By performing non-linear signal inference through the minimization of the Gibbs free energy, IFT constructs optimized estimators that bypass the limitations of rigid summary statistics. This methodology allows for the robust renormalization of the response of incomplete galaxy surveys, enabling the direct reconstruction of underlying initial density perturbations from challenging, heteroscedastic datasets [Enßlin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvD..80j5005E/abstract), [Enßlin et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvE..82e1112E/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The advent of Next-generation surveys (such as Euclid, DESI, and LSST) has introduced unprecedented computational and statistical challenges, driving rapid evolution in the application of frequentist inference in astrophysics. As the volume of data grows, traditional likelihood-based methods are being rigorously tested and expanded, revealing several open questions and prompting the integration of machine learning into standard [[statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Statistical methods in astrophysics]].

**Integration with Simulation-Based Inference**
A major paradigm shift is the move toward Simulation-based inference (SBI), also known as likelihood-free inference. Because standard Gaussian approximations—such as the [[multivariate-gaussian-distributions|Multivariate Gaussian distributions]] assumed in a standard [[chi-squared-test|Chi-squared test]]—are insufficient to describe large-scale structure clustering on non-linear scales, analytical likelihoods are often biased [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract). SBI and [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]] bypass explicit likelihoods by utilizing forward generative models and neural networks, matching the accuracy of traditional Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods with a $>10^4$ increase in computational speed [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952L..10W/abstract). A key example of this evolution is the SIMBIG framework, which utilizes high-fidelity forward models and machine learning-based inference to extract non-linear cosmological information that is typically lost in standard large-scale structure analyses [Hahn et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PNAS..12018810H/abstract). Furthermore, the application of neural data compression within these SBI frameworks has proven highly transformative; for instance, applying neural compression to weak-lensing map statistics in the Dark Energy Survey (DES) Year 3 data improved dark energy parameter precision by more than a factor of two compared to standard two-point power spectrum analyses [Jeffrey et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240302314J/abstract). Expanding on this, researchers are developing hybrid machine learning frameworks that integrate field-level extraction directly with SBI. For example, the Shear-to-Cosmology paradigm maps weak lensing shear fields directly to cosmological parameters, fundamentally bypassing intermediate convergence reconstructions and the associated summary statistic bottlenecks [Ding et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251122851D/abstract).

However, a highly debated open question is how SBI frameworks should handle "out-of-distribution" (OOD) measurement errors. When observational data features noise properties, missing data, or varying telescope conditions not present in the training simulations, standard neural network estimators can produce severely biased results. The literature is currently conflicting on the optimal solution: some researchers advocate for modified SBI methodologies (such as the SBI++ framework) that natively marginalize over missing data using nearby training realizations [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221103747W/abstract), [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952L..10W/abstract), while others maintain that neural net approximation errors must be explicitly propagated through formal Bayesian error correction methods to debias parameter inferences [Grandón et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022OJAp....5E..12G/abstract). As these probabilistic neural networks become central to astrophysical inference, accurate uncertainty calibration remains paramount. Exploring advanced optimization strategies, such as utilizing second-order optimizers like natural gradient descent (e.g., the iVON algorithm), holds significant potential to improve uncertainty calibration in complex classification tasks, such as characterizing radio galaxies [Mohan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251113224M/abstract). To address the heavy computational burden of high-dimensional inference, new algorithmic approaches like the $\text{EL}_2\text{O}$ method (expectation optimization of $L_2$ distance) are being explored as faster, noise-free alternatives to stochastic Kullback-Leibler (KL) minimization [Seljak et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190104454S/abstract).

**Covariance Matrix Estimation in Stage-IV Surveys**
Another critical challenge is the scaling of covariance matrix estimation. For Stage-IV surveys, the dimension of the data vector routinely approaches the number of available mock simulations. Under these conditions, the standard empirical sample covariance matrix becomes mathematically ill-conditioned. Its inverse—the precision matrix required for evaluating the likelihood—becomes overly noisy and singular. To resolve this, modern research in [[statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Statistical methods in astrophysics]] is heavily focused on developing sophisticated shrinkage estimators. These techniques apply linear shrinkage toward a Gaussian target, ensuring that the precision matrix remains invertible and robust without relying on computationally prohibitive numbers of simulations [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609118E/abstract), [Looijmans et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.537...21L/abstract).

**Mitigation of Systematic Errors**
As statistical uncertainties shrink with increasing survey volumes, parameter estimation is increasingly dominated by Systematic errors. Frequently used theoretical simplifications, such as the Limber approximation or the neglect of lensing magnification, have been shown to induce severe systematic biases in cosmological constraints, particularly for the primordial non-Gaussianity parameter $f_{\rm NL}$ [Bernal et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...10..017B/abstract). Furthermore, subtle observational effects like observer motion (the "Kaiser-Rocket" effect) can induce a clustering signal capable of biasing $f_{\rm NL}$ constraints by $>5\sigma$ in idealized full-sky surveys if unmitigated [Bahr-Kalus et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...11..027B/abstract). 

**Profile Likelihoods in High Dimensions**
Finally, there is a renewed research interest in utilizing frequentist profile [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] to handle high-dimensional nuisance parameters as an alternative to Bayesian marginalization. Recent comparative studies have demonstrated that profile likelihoods can effectively dispense with nuisance parameters without requiring priors, yielding parameter estimates that are highly consistent with Bayesian models [Barua et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250211998B/abstract), [Barua et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025Ap&SS.370...62B/abstract). Whether these frequentist profiling techniques can be computationally scaled to compete with the million-dimensional field-level inferences currently reliant on advanced Hamiltonian sampling remains an open frontier in cosmology [Simon-Onfroy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250420130S/abstract). Concurrently, the community is moving toward fully probabilistic descriptions of catalogs, driving large-scale MCMC applications for massive parametric galaxy modeling in surveys like LSST [Buchanan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230910321B/abstract). Indeed, the potential of extracting more cosmological information from the density field is substantial; joint field-level forward models (such as the LEFTfield framework) have demonstrated the capacity to extract significantly more information than classical summary statistics, achieving Baryon Acoustic Oscillation (BAO) scale measurements up to 1.6 times more precise than standard power-spectrum reconstruction on the exact same datasets [Babić et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240701524B/abstract).
