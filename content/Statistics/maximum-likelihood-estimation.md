## Article

**Maximum likelihood estimation** (MLE) is a foundational statistical framework used extensively in astrophysics for rigorous [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] and [[model-fitting|Model fitting]]. At its core, MLE identifies the set of physical parameters, $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, that makes the observed data most probable, by globally maximizing the likelihood function, $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta} | \mathbf{D}) \propto P(\mathbf{D} | \boldsymbol{\theta})$, which quantifies the probability of observing a given astronomical dataset $\mathbf{D}$ under a specific theoretical model. In many applications—particularly within [[cosmological-inference|Cosmological inference]]—the likelihood is approximated as a [[multivariate-gaussian-distributions|multivariate Gaussian distribution]] governed by a theoretical covariance matrix. Under this pure Gaussian assumption, maximizing the likelihood is mathematically equivalent to minimizing a [[chi-squared-test|Chi-squared test]] statistic or [[merit-function|Merit function]]. By systematically exploring the peak and curvature of this probability surface, astronomers can constrain fundamental physical quantities ranging from the expansion rate of the Universe to the complex star formation histories of individual galaxies.

The application of MLE spans virtually all domains of modern astrophysics. In the study of galaxy populations, MLE provides the standard methodology for deriving the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and empirical scaling relations, naturally correcting for [[sample-selection-biases|Sample selection biases]] (such as Malmquist and Eddington bias) across flux-limited surveys. In large-scale structure and precision cosmology, it is the primary mechanism for reconstructing the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] and extracting cosmological parameters from the cosmic microwave background (CMB) and massive [[galaxy-redshift-survey|galaxy redshift surveys]]. This relies heavily on foundational data compression and eigenmode extraction techniques established in the late 1990s and early 2000s [Gorski et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...464L..11G/abstract), [Tegmark et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...480...22T/abstract), [Tegmark et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhRvD..66j3508T/abstract). Furthermore, MLE has continuously evolved in galaxy cluster abundance studies, transitioning from simple [[poisson-statistics|Poisson statistics]] to complex compound likelihoods that explicitly account for large-scale [[sample-variance|Sample variance]] and super-sample covariance.

Despite its ubiquity, standard analytical MLE faces significant theoretical and computational challenges in the era of Stage-IV surveys. Current methodological tensions include the breakdown of textbook Gaussian likelihood approximations on non-linear scales and the computational bottleneck of estimating and inverting high-dimensional, noisy covariance matrices. To address these limitations, analytical pipelines are adopting advanced shrinkage estimators and shifting towards [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]] to handle complex, interdependent systematic errors like photometric redshift uncertainties. Finally, in regimes where the true likelihood function is analytically intractable or computationally prohibitive, astrophysicists are increasingly bypassing explicit likelihood evaluations entirely. Utilizing neural [[density-estimation-techniques|density estimation]], [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]], and [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]], researchers are now performing robust, "likelihood-free" parameter extraction directly from forward-modeled mock catalogs.

## Introduction and Fundamental Principles

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) represents a rigorous and foundational statistical framework for [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] in astrophysics. It operates by defining a likelihood function, $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta} | \mathbf{D}) \propto P(\mathbf{D} | \boldsymbol{\theta})$, which quantifies the probability of obtaining the observed astronomical data $\mathbf{D}$ given a specific set of physical model parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. The MLE framework seeks the parameter vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ that globally maximizes this function, providing a self-consistent method for interpreting observations ranging from the cosmic microwave background (CMB) to [[galaxy-redshift-survey|galaxy redshift surveys]].

In the context of the [[probability-density-function-cosmology|Probability density function (cosmology)]], the likelihood is frequently modeled as a [[multivariate-gaussian-distributions|multivariate Gaussian]]. This reflects the assumption that the central limit theorem applies to the highly averaged data modes inherent in the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]]. Under a purely Gaussian assumption, maximizing the likelihood is mathematically equivalent to minimizing a [[chi-squared-test|Chi-squared test]] statistic or [[merit-function|Merit function]]. The curvature of the log-likelihood surface at its peak is routinely utilized in [[fisher-matrix-analysis|Fisher matrix analysis]] to forecast parameter uncertainties and evaluate expected degeneracies. However, exact likelihood evaluations have demonstrated that strict Fisher matrix approximations can sometimes overestimate the true errors on cosmological parameters, particularly when small correlated errors (such as those from foregrounds) are present [Efstathiou et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.304...75E/abstract).

While the Gaussian likelihood approximation is ubiquitous, the literature highlights significant tensions regarding its universal validity. Studies have shown that for large-scale structure and cosmic shear data, the Gaussian approximation breaks down on the largest scales (low $k$) and in regimes dominated by non-linear clustering. Incorrectly assuming a Gaussian likelihood in these regimes can significantly bias estimates of primordial non-Gaussianity ($f_{\rm NL}$) and artificially shrink credible regions, necessitating more complex [[log-normal-distribution|log-normal]] or modified data transformations [Hartlap et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...504..689H/abstract), [Sun et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777...75S/abstract), [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..951W/abstract).

Similar complexities arise in cluster abundance cosmology. The detection and counting of massive galaxy clusters fundamentally follow [[poisson-statistics|Poisson statistics]]. However, standard Poisson likelihoods consistently underestimate parameter errors because they fail to account for [[sample-variance|sample covariance]] and super-sample covariance (SSC) induced by large-scale density fluctuations across the survey volume. For unbiased inference in Stage-IV surveys, researchers agree that full covariance matrices must be integrated into a Gaussian or Gauss-Poisson Compound likelihood framework [Hu et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvD..73f7301H/abstract), [Fumagalli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...652A..21F/abstract), [Payerne et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532..381P/abstract).

Historically, exact MLE required the inversion of dense data covariance matrices, an $\mathcal{O}(N_d^3)$ computational operation that becomes prohibitive for modern multi-megapixel datasets. This limitation has driven the development of optimal hybrid estimators—applying exact maximum likelihood methods at low multipoles while utilizing pseudo-$C_l$ approximations at high multipoles [Efstathiou et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.349..603E/abstract). Furthermore, when the true likelihood function is analytically intractable or computationally unfeasible, the field is increasingly shifting toward [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] and [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]], which bypass explicit likelihood evaluations entirely by forward-modeling the physical data generation process [Ishida et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&C....13....1I/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract).

## Historical Development and Evolution

The application of maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) in astrophysics has undergone a profound evolution, transitioning from simple demographic counting to the complex, high-dimensional parameter inference required by modern precision cosmology. 

In its earliest applications, [[frequentist-inference|Frequentist inference]] via MLE was primarily utilized to determine demographic properties of astronomical sources, such as estimating the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and characterizing galaxy counts. Traditionally, astronomers relied on the classical [[v-vmax-test|V/Vmax test]] ($1/V_{\max}$) to estimate luminosity functions in flux-limited samples. However, this method implicitly assumes a uniform spatial distribution. Researchers demonstrated that maximum likelihood estimators could [[non-parametric-methods-in-astrophysics|non-parametrically]] eliminate observational cutoff biases [Nicoll et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980A&A....82L...3N/abstract), formally generalizing the $1/V_{\max}$ approach to properly account for radial density fluctuations induced by large-scale structure [Cole et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416..739C/abstract). 

As cosmic surveys expanded in the 1990s, the focus of MLE expanded to the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] and galaxy clustering. Early geometric interpretations of [[two-point-correlation-function|two-point estimators]] revealed fundamental integral constraint biases in common statistics—such as those of Landy-Szalay and Hamilton—prompting a more rigorous likelihood-based treatment of clustering data [Kerscher et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999A&A...343..333K/abstract). The introduction of the Feldman-Kaiser-Peacock method provided an optimal minimum-variance weighting scheme for estimating the power spectrum in the presence of varying survey selection functions and shot noise [Hamilton et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..3603H/abstract), [Smith et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.1266S/abstract). Despite these theoretical advances, evaluating an exact multivariate Gaussian likelihood for large datasets requires the inversion of an $N_d \times N_d$ covariance matrix. For massive CMB maps and extensive [[galaxy-redshift-survey|redshift surveys]], brute-force MLE quickly became computationally prohibitive. 

To overcome these computational bottlenecks, cosmologists pioneered data compression techniques, most notably Karhunen-Loève compression, to isolate the highest signal-to-noise eigenmodes of the data [Tegmark et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...480...22T/abstract). This mathematical foundation paved the way for optimal [[quadratic-estimators|Quadratic estimators]] and hybrid approaches. For example, to efficiently manage disparate survey regimes, hybrid estimators were developed independently for both galaxy redshift surveys—using Fourier and quadratic matrix methods [Tegmark et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...499..555T/abstract)—and for Planck-scale CMB datasets, which transition from exact maximum likelihood computations at large scales (low multipoles) to faster, pseudo-$C_\ell$ approximations at smaller scales [Efstathiou et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.349..603E/abstract). For large-scale datasets, alternative exact evaluation schemes have also been proposed, such as utilizing the renormalization group (RG) method as an incremental integration approach to efficiently compute likelihood functions in linear time [McDonald et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvD..99d3538M/abstract).

Over the last two decades, the field has increasingly recognized the limitations of pure frequentist maximum likelihood methods when dealing with complex, interdependent systematic errors. Consequently, there has been a paradigm shift towards [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]]. For example, in deriving luminosity functions or stellar masses, a [[hierarchical-bayesian-model|Hierarchical Bayesian Model]] can simultaneously fit population-level parameters and individual object properties, naturally correcting for Eddington bias and observational uncertainties where classical MLE is known to provide inconsistent estimates [Szalai-Gindl et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&C....25..247S/abstract). 

Today, as astrophysics enters the era of Stage-IV surveys (such as Euclid and LSST), standard analytical likelihoods are often insufficient. The textbook assumption of a multivariate Gaussian likelihood fails on both the largest scales (low $k$) and on small, non-linear scales, potentially biasing critical parameters such as primordial non-Gaussianity ($f_{\rm NL}$) [Sun et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777...75S/abstract), [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..951W/abstract). Where the true likelihood function is intractable, unknown, or computationally exorbitant, the evolution of MLE has led to likelihood-free inference techniques. Methods such as [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]] (ABC) and [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] (SBI) leverage forward generative models and neural networks to extract [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] constraints directly from mock catalogs, bypassing the need to evaluate an explicit likelihood equation altogether [Ishida et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&C....13....1I/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract), [Lehman et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108957L/abstract).

## Galaxy Populations and Scaling Relations

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is the standard statistical framework for deriving the demographic properties and empirical scaling laws of galaxy populations. Because observational samples are fundamentally limited by flux thresholds, simple empirical estimators like the [[v-vmax-test|V/Vmax test]] are often inadequate for precision studies. MLE rigorously accounts for the survey [[selection-function|Selection function]] and underlying measurement uncertainties to yield unbiased population parameters.

**Luminosity and Mass Functions**
Constructing the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] (LF) or stellar mass function requires disentangling intrinsic galaxy abundances from distance-dependent selection effects. When the functional form of the LF is assumed to be known—most commonly a single or double [[schechter-function|Schechter function]]—parametric [[model-fitting|MLE fits]] the function's parameters by maximizing the probability of observing a specific set of galaxy luminosities given the survey flux limits [Weigel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.2150W/abstract). Conversely, non-parametric approaches such as [[step-wise-maximum-likelihood|Step-wise maximum likelihood]] (SWML) discretize the luminosity distribution into discrete magnitude bins $\phi_k$. The algorithm treats the amplitude of each bin as a free parameter to be maximized without assuming an overarching functional form. Furthermore, specialized maximum likelihood fits of the Planetary Nebula Luminosity Function (PNLF) across various Hubble types have historically established it as a highly robust empirical distance indicator [Jacoby et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ASPC....4...42J/abstract).

Modern extensions of MLE are highly focused on mitigating Eddington bias, which arises when photometric noise scatters numerous faint galaxies into brighter magnitude bins. Modified maximum likelihood (MML) methods natively account for this bias, non-detections, and complex large-scale structure variations without requiring binning [Obreschkow et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5500O/abstract). However, the literature presents an active methodological tension: studies show that in the presence of severe photometric noise and flux-limited thresholds, standard MLE can provide statistically inconsistent parameter estimates. This has prompted a shift toward full [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]] models to correctly propagate population-level uncertainties simultaneously with individual object measurements [Szalai-Gindl et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&C....25..247S/abstract).

**Scaling Relations and Photometry**
Scaling relations tightly couple the kinematic, structural, and photometric properties of galaxies. [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] via MLE is particularly suited to fitting these relations because it can explicitly model the [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] across multi-dimensional selection boundaries. For early-type galaxies, the Fundamental Plane relates physical size, surface brightness, and velocity dispersion. By formulating the likelihood as a [[multivariate-gaussian-distributions|multivariate Gaussian distribution]] incorporating observational errors and censoring, MLE yields unbiased structural parameters and isolates the true intrinsic scatter of the plane [Magoulas et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..262..376M/abstract).

Similarly, MLE has expanded the utility of the Tully-Fisher relation (TFR) for disk galaxies. Traditionally, the TFR requires precise galaxy inclinations to correct projected rotational velocities. However, specialized maximum likelihood formulations can recover the zero-point, slope, and scatter of the TFR even when inclination data are entirely absent. By marginalizing over a random $\sin i$ prior distribution, this inclination-free MLE recovers the relation with statistical errors only $\sim 1.5$ times larger than models utilizing perfect inclination measurements [Obreschkow et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777..140O/abstract). However, one must be cautious; recent studies have highlighted that severe parameter degeneracies and specific choices of priors in Bayesian techniques can sometimes lead to false inferences, such as erroneously concluding that Newton's constant varies from galaxy to galaxy when fitting rotation curves . In localized studies, MLE has long been applied to the radial velocities and distances of satellite galaxies to systematically determine the total mass and density profile of the Milky Way [Kulessa et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992MNRAS.255..105K/abstract).

At the level of observational derivation, likelihood estimation fundamentally underpins precision photometry and distance calculations. For modern high-resolution imaging, such as that from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), tools like `forcepho` leverage likelihood gradients for the simultaneous generative modeling of complex galaxy fluxes and shapes across multiple bands [Baldwin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ascl.soft10006B/abstract). Moreover, optimization algorithms structured around maximum likelihood evaluations have been successfully designed to extract precise local distances by identifying demographic cutoffs, such as locating the Tip of the Red Giant Branch (TRGB) [Makarov et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....132.2729M/abstract). In the spatial domain, novel Bayesian approaches such as the Population Profile Estimator (PoPE) utilize [[non-parametric-gaussian-process-regression|Gaussian processes]] to directly model the average spatial structure and population variance of astronomical systems without the need for traditional data stacking [Farahi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....161...30F/abstract). Furthermore, when calibrating complex semi-analytic models of galaxy formation, locating the maximum likelihood regions in high-dimensional parameter spaces is notoriously difficult; researchers have successfully deployed fast self-learning algorithms like Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) to robustly navigate these spaces [Ruiz et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...801..139R/abstract).

**Spectral Energy Distributions**
To infer underlying physical properties—such as stellar mass, metallicity, and star formation history—MLE is routinely applied to broad-band photometry and spectroscopy via Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) techniques. Algorithms like STECKMAP non-parametrically recover both stellar content and kinematics by maximizing a regularized likelihood function [Ocvirk et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.365...74O/abstract). Despite the historical prevalence of MLE and MAP in standard population synthesis codes—such as the widely used [[bagpipes-code|BAGPIPES]] or [[magphys|MAGPHYS]] package, which acts as a key Bayesian tool for deriving fundamental galaxy parameters including stellar mass, star formation rates, and dust activity by interpreting SEDs from the UV to the far-infrared [da Cunha et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ascl.soft06010D/abstract)—the field is currently experiencing a rapid paradigm shift. Recent machine learning approaches (e.g., neural density estimation and parametric t-SNE) have been shown to drastically outperform traditional SED maximum likelihood fitting in both speed ($\sim 3000\times$ faster) and accuracy, reducing systematic mass biases from $\sim 0.286$ dex to $\sim 0.029$ dex in simulated mock catalogs [Asadi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220544A/abstract).

## Large-Scale Structure and Power Spectrum Estimation

In the study of cosmic large-scale structure, maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) serves as a foundational framework for inferring the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] $P(k)$ and the [[angular-power-spectrum|Angular power spectrum]] $C_\ell$ from [[galaxy-redshift-survey|galaxy redshift surveys]] and cosmic microwave background (CMB) maps. The fundamental observable is typically a high-dimensional data vector $\mathbf{x}$ representing the overdensity field or map pixels, from which cosmological parameters are extracted by maximizing the likelihood of a given theoretical model.

A standard approach in these analyses is to assume a [[multivariate-gaussian-distributions|multivariate Gaussian likelihood]] for the data vector, given a theoretical covariance matrix $\mathbf{C}(\theta)$ dependent on cosmological parameters $\theta$:
$$ \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}|\theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|2\pi \mathbf{C}(\theta)|}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{C}^{-1}(\theta) \mathbf{x} \right) $$
To extract cosmological information deep into the mildly non-linear regime without relying on strict, information-destroying binary scale cuts ($k_{\rm max}$), modern frameworks like the CLASS-PT code incorporate a modeled "theoretical error" directly into the likelihood covariance evaluation [Chudaykin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220406591C/abstract). However, the broader literature reveals significant tension regarding the overall adequacy of the Gaussian assumption. While efficient and often justified by the central limit theorem on scales where many modes are sampled, assuming a Gaussian likelihood for the power spectrum can significantly bias the estimation of the primordial non-Gaussianity parameter $f_{\rm NL}$ [Sun et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777...75S/abstract). Similarly, the true cosmic shear likelihood exhibits marked non-Gaussianity that can artificially tighten [[credible-interval|credible intervals]] and shift constraints on parameters such as $\sigma_8$ [Hartlap et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...504..689H/abstract).

To obtain optimal, minimum-variance estimates of the power spectrum in the presence of incomplete sky coverage and variable noise, the [[quadratic-maximum-likelihood|Quadratic Maximum Likelihood]] (QML) estimator is widely employed. The consensus in the literature supports the use of hybrid estimators that apply exact QML at low multipoles and fast, pseudo-$C_\ell$ methods at high multipoles to bypass the otherwise prohibitive $\mathcal{O}(N_d^3)$ scaling of exact covariance inversions [Efstathiou et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.349..603E/abstract). Comprehensive joint likelihood analyses are essential to fully harness these measurements in Stage-IV surveys; frameworks such as COSMOLIKE simultaneously combine galaxy clustering, cosmic shear, and magnification signals using rigorous non-Gaussian covariance modeling [Eifler et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440.1379E/abstract). Additionally, advanced autodifferentiable likelihood pipelines are now being deployed to reconstruct the cosmic radial velocity field via the cross-correlation of CMB and large-scale structure induced by the kinetic Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect [Kvasiuk et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109h3515K/abstract). These exact maximum likelihood estimators for kinetic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich velocity reconstruction have been shown to significantly outperform traditional [[quadratic-estimators|Quadratic estimators]], particularly in high-resolution and low-noise regimes [Contreras et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.107b3521C/abstract). Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that various kSZ statistics are mathematically equivalent to the optimal [[three-point-correlation-function|bispectrum estimator]], substantially improving sensitivity to large-scale primordial density fluctuations when integrated into a unified likelihood framework [Smith et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181013423S/abstract).

MLE is utilized to break fundamental degeneracies in the cosmological parameter space. By rigorously modeling the redshift-space covariance matrix and tracking the anisotropic clustering signal, likelihood methods can effectively break the degeneracy between the linear galaxy bias $b$ and the matter density parameter $\Omega_m$ [Susperregi et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...546...85S/abstract). Furthermore, extending standard [[fisher-matrix-analysis|Fisher matrix analysis]] to evaluate the likelihood surface using non-linear second-order terms has been shown to significantly increase the extracted information content and further break parameter degeneracies [Taylor et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1027T/abstract). When theoretical models involve continuous unknown systematic functions—such as scale-dependent galaxy bias or intrinsic alignments—researchers utilize path integral marginalization within the likelihood to formally integrate out these functional uncertainties [Kitching et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410.1677K/abstract). To handle the high dimensionality of these unbinned analyses, Karhunen-Loève eigenmode expansions are often employed to compress the data vector while retaining maximal Fisher information [Tegmark et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...480...22T/abstract).

Beyond two-point statistics, the spatial geometry of the universe is characterized by the [[n-point-correlation-function|N-point correlation function]]. Historically, computational scaling of $\mathcal{O}(n^N)$ restricted the use of higher-order statistics in rigorous likelihood analyses. However, recent algorithmic advancements utilizing separable angular bases allow the estimation of $N$-point statistics in $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ time, where $n$ is the number of particles [Philcox et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PNAS..11911366P/abstract). The integration of these higher-order estimators into an MLE framework requires highly accurate covariance matrices. Because sample covariances estimated from mock catalogs can be overwhelmingly noisy, the development of [[analytical-covariance-matrix|analytic Gaussian covariance matrices]] for arbitrary $N$-point functions has provided a crucial, stable precision matrix for modern likelihood evaluations .

## Galaxy Cluster Abundance and Mass Inference

The abundance of galaxy clusters as a function of mass and redshift is a sensitive probe of the growth of structure and the expansion history of the Universe. At the detection phase, the Adaptive Matched Filter (AMF) algorithm serves as a standard statistical signal processing method, creating likelihood maps to efficiently identify and extract clusters from [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]] [Kepner et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph..4304K/abstract). Once clusters are detected, extracting robust masses presents further challenges. While core regions are often well-modeled, researchers utilize the 'caustic' technique—relying purely on galaxy positions and redshifts—as a powerful likelihood-based approach for [[mass-estimators|mass estimation]] in the non-equilibrium outskirts of galaxy clusters [Diaferio et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.309..610D/abstract).

In modern [[cosmological-inference|Cosmological inference]], maximum likelihood estimation is used to extract fundamental parameters (such as $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$) by fitting theoretical [[peak-mass-function|halo mass functions]] to observed cluster counts. Because the true halo mass is not a direct observable, the likelihood function must jointly constrain the cosmology and the parameters governing the [[observable-mass-scaling-relations|Observable-mass scaling relations]] [Hu et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvD..73f7301H/abstract). Beyond standard cosmological constraints, properly quantifying the correct likelihood is essential for evaluating the "rareness" of extreme galaxy clusters. Rigorous likelihood analyses have demonstrated that many supposedly anomalous massive clusters are entirely consistent with the expected statistical fluctuations of the standard $\Lambda$CDM model, resolving claimed tensions in the literature [Hotchkiss et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011JCAP...07..004H/abstract).

Historically, cluster abundance analyses have relied on [[poisson-statistics|Poisson statistics]]. For a survey divided into discrete bins of a mass proxy and redshift, the standard binned Poisson log-likelihood is given by:
$$ \ln \mathcal{L}_{\rm Poisson} = \sum_i \left( N_i \ln \mu_i - \mu_i - \ln N_i! \right) $$
where $N_i$ is the observed number of clusters in the $i$-th bin and $\mu_i$ is the theoretically expected mean. 

While the Poisson likelihood accurately captures shot noise, it implicitly assumes that clusters are independent draws from a universal background density. In reality, large-scale density fluctuations across the finite survey volume modulate the local mean density of clusters. This effect, known as super-sample covariance (SSC) or [[sample-variance|Sample variance]], introduces correlated noise across different mass and redshift bins [Hu et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvD..73f7301H/abstract). 

For next-generation Stage-IV surveys like Euclid and the Rubin Observatory LSST—which are expected to deliver catalogs of tens of thousands to millions of clusters—the pure Poisson likelihood is inadequate and consistently underestimates parameter errors [Payerne et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.6223P/abstract). To achieve unbiased [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]], the likelihood must incorporate the full covariance structure of the cluster counts. A [[multivariate-gaussian-distributions|multivariate Gaussian likelihood]] is often employed for this purpose:
$$ \ln \mathcal{L}_{\rm Gauss} = -\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{N} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^T \mathbf{C}^{-1} (\mathbf{N} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) - \frac{1}{2} \ln |\mathbf{C}| $$
where $\mathbf{C}$ is the covariance matrix combining both shot noise and SSC. Studies using simulated dark matter halo catalogs have shown that the Gaussian approximation provides highly robust constraints over a large range of binning configurations [Payerne et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.6223P/abstract). Furthermore, for high-precision data, an [[analytical-covariance-matrix|Analytical covariance matrix]] is often used, and its cosmology-dependence must be updated dynamically during likelihood maximization to prevent biased posteriors [Fumagalli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...652A..21F/abstract).

To preserve the advantages of unbinned analyses—which avoid information loss from arbitrary bin definitions—hybrid formulations such as the Gauss-Poisson Compound (GPC) likelihood have been developed. The GPC model operates on infinitesimal mass bins while explicitly capturing the large-scale SSC. For synthetic catalogs of $\sim 10^5$ clusters, incorporating SSC into an unbinned likelihood increases the dispersion of the parameter [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] by approximately 20% compared to a naive Poisson likelihood [Payerne et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532..381P/abstract). Furthermore, when combining cosmological constraints from cluster counts and galaxy clustering, researchers have successfully employed Gram-Charlier series to analytically compute joint non-Gaussian likelihoods that capture the cross-correlation between the two probes [Lacasa et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..306..216L/abstract).

Evaluating these complex likelihoods requires sophisticated computational tools. Modern packages like `cosmocnc` utilize backward convolutional approaches to rapidly evaluate unbinned and binned likelihoods, cleanly marginalizing over measurement uncertainties, scatter in [[mass-estimators|Mass estimators]], and unconfirmed survey detections [Zubeldia et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240309589Z/abstract). Looking ahead, machine learning is increasingly being explored to extract cluster masses directly from multi-wavelength data without assuming standard functional scaling relations, potentially modifying how observable constraints are fed into the likelihood [Cohn et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.1575C/abstract).

## Covariance and Precision Matrix Modeling

In maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) applied to large-scale structure and cosmic microwave background datasets, the formulation of the likelihood heavily relies on the covariance matrix $C$ of the observables. For a standard Gaussian likelihood, the log-likelihood or [[merit-function|Merit function]] scales directly with the precision matrix, $\Psi = C^{-1}$. Accurate modeling of $C$ and $\Psi$ is critical, as any inaccuracies or noise in the covariance matrix directly propagate into biases in [[cosmological-inference|Cosmological inference]] and the artificial tightening or broadening of [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]].

**Sampling Noise and Shrinkage Estimation**
In the analysis of modern [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Galaxy Surveys and Catalogs]], the covariance matrix is typically estimated numerically from a finite ensemble of $N$-body simulations or mock catalogs. Because the size of the data vector $n$ often approaches the number of independent simulations, the empirical sample covariance matrix becomes noisy and ill-conditioned. Direct inversion of this matrix yields a biased estimate of the precision matrix, severely skewing [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] [Fumagalli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...652A..21F/abstract). 

To stabilize the precision matrix without requiring computationally prohibitive numbers of simulations, recent analyses have increasingly adopted shrinkage estimation techniques. Shrinkage estimators optimally combine the noisy empirical sample covariance with a well-behaved, low-dimensional target matrix. For instance, the NERCOME (Nonparametric Eigenvalue-Regularized Covariance Matrix Estimator) algorithm applies a non-linear shrinkage that significantly reduces sampling noise in the parameter posteriors when the number of mock simulations is limited [Looijmans et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240213783L/abstract), [Looijmans et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.537...21L/abstract). However, while NERCOME improves the stability of the precision matrix, it can lead to a systematic overestimation of parameter error bars if not properly calibrated. This has led some pipelines to favor rapid approximate mock generators (e.g., the COVMOS method) to simply increase the number of realizations to unbiased levels ($O(10^4)$) [Gouyou Beauchamps et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230605988G/abstract). Another recent data-driven approach is DICES (Debiased Internal Covariance Estimation with Shrinkage), which generates non-singular, unbiased jackknife covariances directly from the survey data by applying linear shrinkage toward a Gaussian prediction, effectively bypassing the need for massive simulation suites [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609118E/abstract). Beyond stabilizing the precision matrix, the choice of sampling algorithms (e.g., PolyChord versus MultiNest) fundamentally impacts the calculated Bayesian evidence and the robustness of parameter [[credible-interval|credible intervals]] in Stage-III and IV surveys like the Dark Energy Survey (DES), necessitating rigorous sampler validation within the inference pipeline [Lemos et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1184L/abstract).

**Analytical Covariance Matrices**
As an alternative to simulation-derived matrices, analysts may compute an [[analytical-covariance-matrix|Analytical covariance matrix]]. Analytical matrices bypass sampling noise entirely and can be formulated to cover higher-order statistics; for example, fully analytic Gaussian covariance matrices have been successfully derived for arbitrary [[n-point-correlation-function|N-point correlation functions]] by projecting the terms onto isotropic basis functions . Additionally, analytical covariance models have recently been developed for galaxy peculiar velocity correlations, rigorously accounting for selection functions and redshift-space distortions in cosmological likelihood analyses [Blake et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527..501B/abstract). However, pure Gaussian approximations of the covariance matrix fail to capture off-diagonal mode-coupling induced by non-linear gravitational evolution. Regardless of the estimation method, neglecting these non-Gaussian covariance terms strictly biases parameter estimates and central values on small physical scales, typically at $k > 0.2 \, h/\text{Mpc}$ [Gouyou Beauchamps et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230605988G/abstract).

**Cosmology-Dependent vs. Fixed Covariance**
A prevailing tension in precision matrix modeling is whether the covariance matrix should be updated dynamically as a function of the cosmological parameters during inference, or kept fixed at a fiducial cosmology. Several studies demonstrate that an uncalibrated, fixed covariance matrix fails to account for the parameter dependence of the data variance, which can bias Stage-IV survey constraints [Fumagalli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...652A..21F/abstract). To address this, variance-correlation decompositions have been proposed to isolate and update the cosmology-dependent variance while maintaining a fixed correlation matrix [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..951W/abstract). 

Conversely, other analyses argue that the assumption of a parameter-independent covariance matrix remains an exceptionally good approximation for most realistic scenarios. These studies assert that the information gain from modeling the parameter dependence of the covariance matrix is negligible compared to standard statistical uncertainties, and that keeping it fixed avoids artificially complex likelihood surfaces [Kodwani et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019OJAp....2E...3K/abstract). The literature currently advises treating the cosmology dependence of the covariance matrix on a case-by-case basis depending on the specific survey volume and the required precision.

## Treatment of Systematic Errors and Biases

Effective [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] via maximum likelihood must explicitly model the measurement process to prevent observational artifacts from masquerading as physical signals. If uncorrected, these [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|systematics]] lead to highly precise but fundamentally biased results.

A foundational challenge in flux-limited surveys is the [[selection-function|Selection function]], which imposes a hard or probabilistic truncation on the observable data. This truncation is the root of Malmquist bias, wherein intrinsically luminous objects are artificially overrepresented at large distances. In a Bayesian or maximum likelihood framework, Malmquist corrections are implemented rigorously by including the survey selection criteria directly into the normalization of the likelihood function [Hendry et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995VA.....39..297H/abstract). Historically, [[non-parametric-methods-in-astrophysics|non-parametric]] maximum likelihood estimators have been successfully deployed to eliminate magnitude cut-off biases without requiring *a priori* assumptions about the underlying [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] [Nicoll et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980A&A....82L...3N/abstract).

Similarly, measurement noise operating on a steeply falling intrinsic distribution (such as a [[peak-mass-function|halo mass function]] or luminosity function) preferentially scatters a larger number of faint, low-mass objects into higher observational bins than vice versa. This phenomenon, known as Eddington bias, artificially inflates the high-end tail of the inferred distribution. Modern implementations overcome this by using modified maximum likelihood (MML) methods or [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]] to simultaneously model the underlying generative distributions and the object-level observational uncertainties [Obreschkow et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5500O/abstract). By treating the true physical quantities as latent variables and marginalizing over them, these approaches naturally decouple population statistics from measurement noise. In the spatial domain, finite survey boundaries introduce the integral constraint, a nonlinear [[estimation-bias|Estimation bias]] that systematically suppresses the measured variance and skewness of galaxy distributions if not formally accounted for in the statistical estimator [Hui et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...519..622H/abstract). Systematic variations in time-domain astronomy require similarly tailored approaches. For instance, estimating astronomical time delays between gravitationally lensed stochastic light curves is heavily dependent on state-space models and profile likelihood approximations to accurately account for measurement gaps and micro-lensing noise [Tak et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160201462T/abstract).

In [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]], distance uncertainties constitute one of the most critical systematic errors. The practice of utilizing single-point estimates for a photometric redshift ($z_{\rm phot}$) systematically degrades cosmological constraints. Instead, incorporating the full [[probability-density-function-cosmology|posterior probability density function]], $p(z)$, into the likelihood is strictly necessary to mitigate systematic biases in dark energy parameter estimation [Wittman et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...700L.174W/abstract). Algorithms utilizing Bayesian photometric redshift estimation—such as the [[photo-z-code|PHOTO-z codes]]—effectively break broad-band color degeneracies by applying empirical priors on the galaxy type mix and redshift distributions, achieving robust accuracies (e.g., an rms error of $\Delta z / (1+z_{\rm spec}) \approx 0.08$ at $z < 6$) [Benítez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ascl.soft08011B/abstract).

Translating photometric data into robust cosmological constraints is nonetheless fraught with [[sample-selection-biases|Sample selection biases]], largely because the spectroscopic datasets used to train photo-$z$ models are heavily biased toward the brightest, rarest galaxies [Izbicki et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160401339I/abstract). Misclassification of redshifts scatters galaxies between tomographic bins, acting as a contaminant in clustering signals. This contamination can be managed via "decontamination" formalisms that correct the [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] using observed cross-correlations among samples [Awan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...890...78A/abstract). To circumvent the limitations of SED-based priors entirely, clustering-based redshift inference provides a powerful, data-driven technique to accurately estimate the true redshift distribution of galaxies by leveraging spatial cross-correlations with known reference objects [Rahman et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.3500R/abstract). Furthermore, astronomical datasets frequently require discrete binary corrections, such as population classification or masking. Recent methodologies have demonstrated that analytic marginalization over vast numbers of binary data configurations can be mathematically mapped to the Ising model, permitting exact likelihood evaluations without combinatorial explosion [Högås et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251021912H/abstract). Alternatively, uncertainties in the overall redshift distribution $N(z)$ can be analytically marginalized over by modifying the data covariance matrix, which automatically downweights the modes most sensitive to $N(z)$ variations [Hadzhiyska et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...10..056H/abstract). 

Despite algorithmic improvements in likelihood formulations, the literature demonstrates that spectroscopic calibration biases and unmodeled photo-$z$ scatter remain the dominant systematics in near-future Stage-IV surveys (such as those conducted by the Rubin Observatory). If the error model fails to fully capture these redshift systematics, they will continue to severely bias inferred cosmological parameters [Awan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240914265A/abstract). To actively combat these issues, upcoming Stage-IV missions have developed rigorous mitigation strategies. For instance, the Euclid spectroscopic galaxy sample controls angular systematics by employing extensively calibrated random catalogs and sophisticated detection models for specific emission lines, directly embedding these instrumental artifacts into the selection function prior to any likelihood evaluation [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251120856E/abstract).

## Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) and Spectral Analysis

In the context of extragalactic astrophysics, traditional maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) techniques are frequently extended into a Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) framework to solve highly ill-posed inverse problems. A prominent example is the non-parametric reconstruction of star formation histories (SFHs), stellar metallicities, and internal kinematics from galaxy spectra using the [[integrated-light-method|Integrated light method]].

The observed spectrum of a galaxy, $S(\lambda)$, can be modeled as a linear combination of Single Stellar Population (SSP) templates, convolved with the line-of-sight velocity distribution (LOSVD), $\mathcal{L}(v)$, and modified by a dust attenuation law. The theoretical model spectrum $M(\lambda)$ is typically expressed as:
$$ M(\lambda) = \int \int \psi(t, Z) \, \left[ S_{\mathrm{SSP}}(\lambda, t, Z) \ast \mathcal{L}(v) \right] dt \, dZ $$
where $\psi(t, Z)$ represents the joint age-metallicity distribution of the stellar populations. Directly maximizing the likelihood of this model with respect to $\psi(t,Z)$ and $\mathcal{L}(v)$ using a standard [[least-squares-method|Least squares method]] leads to severe degeneracies, as the problem is highly underdetermined and susceptible to noise. 

To break these degeneracies, algorithms employ a MAP approach by incorporating physically motivated [[priors-in-astrophysics|Priors in astrophysics]]. In the MAP framework, the objective is to maximize the posterior probability, which is mathematically equivalent to minimizing a penalized [[merit-function|Merit function]]:
$$ \mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{x}) = - \ln \mathcal{L}(\mathrm{data} \mid \mathbf{x}) + \alpha P(\mathbf{x}) $$
where $\mathbf{x}$ encapsulates the parameters of $\psi(t, Z)$ and $\mathcal{L}(v)$, $\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{data} \mid \mathbf{x})$ is the standard likelihood function, and $P(\mathbf{x})$ is a penalty function representing the prior. The hyperparameter $\alpha$ controls the strength of the regularization.

This formalism is the foundation of the STECKMAP (STEllar Content and Kinematics via Maximum A Posteriori likelihood) algorithm [Ocvirk et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.365...74O/abstract), [Ocvirk et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1108.4631O/abstract). STECKMAP utilizes [[full-spectrum-fitting|Full-spectrum fitting]] to simultaneously recover the kinematic properties and the stellar content of a galaxy without assuming a parametric functional form for the SFH or the LOSVD. Instead, it relies on prior conditions such as positivity ($\psi(t,Z) \ge 0$) and strict smoothness criteria, which are optimized via generalized cross-validation to prevent over-fitting the noise [Ocvirk et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.365...74O/abstract). Similarly, methods incorporating data compression algorithms have successfully applied likelihood surface exploration across the entire spectral range to reconstruct the "fossil record" of galaxies. These studies demonstrate that [[full-spectrum-fitting|Full-spectrum fitting]] significantly mitigates age-metallicity degeneracies compared to narrow-band spectral index analyses [Panter et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.343.1145P/abstract). These techniques remain under active software development; modern frameworks like the Population Synthesis Toolkit (PST) Python library facilitate this by efficiently forward-modeling the composite spectra required for such robust likelihood and MAP evaluations [Corcho-Caballero et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250322802C/abstract).

Beyond individual galaxies, MAP techniques are applied to intergalactic medium tomography. The Maximum A Posteriori Ly-$\alpha$ Estimator (MAPLE), for example, employs a Wiener-filter approach to jointly reconstruct the 3D power spectrum and covariance of the Lyman-alpha forest [Horowitz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240317294H/abstract). Moreover, MAP and Bayesian modeling are extensively used for broadband spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting to derive physical parameters such as dust mass, star formation rates, and stellar masses. Widely used codes such as [[magphys|MAGPHYS]] [da Cunha et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ascl.soft06010D/abstract) and iSEDfit [Moustakas et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ascl.soft08029M/abstract) rely on this approach. Rather than merely identifying the single MAP estimate, these tools increasingly map the full [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] of the physical parameters. Evaluating the marginalized posteriors naturally yields robust limits on [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] and handles non-Gaussian degeneracies between parameters, such as the widely known degeneracy between dust attenuation and stellar age. In large surveys, embedding these likelihood-based SED and spectral fitting techniques within a [[hierarchical-bayesian-model|Hierarchical Bayesian Model]] allows for the population-level extraction of the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] while intrinsically correcting for selection effects and observational systematics, such as Eddington bias [Szalai-Gindl et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&C....25..247S/abstract), [Obreschkow et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5500O/abstract).

## Recent Advances: Machine Learning and Simulation-Based Inference

Standard approaches to cosmological [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] often assume a Gaussian or chi-squared functional form for the likelihood. However, in highly non-linear regimes or when modeling complex observational systematics, the true likelihood function is often analytically intractable. This limitation has driven the development of 'likelihood-free' inference techniques, notably [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]] (ABC) and [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] (SBI). Rather than evaluating an explicit likelihood, these methods rely on forward generative models of the data and a distance metric to compare synthetic catalogs with observations. For example, ABC has been successfully employed to constrain the parameters of the halo occupation distribution without assuming a Gaussian pseudo-likelihood, yielding consistent results while properly capturing non-Gaussianities in the data [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract). Similarly, Population Monte Carlo ABC frameworks have been implemented to estimate posteriors from galaxy cluster number counts directly from simulations [Ishida et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&C....13....1I/abstract).

Recent advances have dramatically accelerated SBI through the use of neural [[density-estimation-techniques|density estimation]]. Deep learning architectures are increasingly used to learn the mapping from data to parameters, bypassing the need for an explicit functional form. Concurrently, variational inference is gaining traction as a dramatically faster alternative to Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods for high-dimensional estimation problems, such as determining exoplanet orbital parameters and characterizing weak gravitational lensing [Gunapati et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASA...39....1G/abstract). In deep learning paradigms, neural networks automatically compress complex galaxy catalogs to identify optimal [[summary-statistics|Summary statistics]] associated with non-linear modes in the range $k = 5 - 30\ h/\mathrm{Mpc}$, preserving cosmological information directly from simulation models [Lehman et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108957L/abstract). A novel non-parametric example within these pseudo-likelihood frameworks is the use of [[knn-distances|k-nearest neighbor cumulative distribution functions]] (kNN-CDFs) to efficiently identify spatial cross-correlations between IceCube high-energy neutrino samples and known galaxy catalogs [Zhou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...979..194Z/abstract). Furthermore, neural network regression can be coupled with importance [[nested-sampling|Nested sampling]] (as implemented in the NAUTILUS code) to construct highly efficient proposal distributions. At the level of generative modeling, Normalizing flows have proven highly effective for representing complex, high-dimensional probability distributions. In the context of upsampling hydrodynamical simulations to create realistic mock stellar catalogs, tools like GALAXYFLOW utilize normalizing flows to estimate and sample from the underlying stellar phase-space density. This method has been shown to outperform traditional [[adaptive-kernel-density-estimation|Adaptive kernel density estimation]] in recovering the true density of star particles [Lim et al. Despite these successes, the transition from explicit analytical likelihoods to machine learning-driven estimators presents ongoing challenges. While neural network emulators can drastically accelerate the evaluation of the joint likelihood—such as in a [[3x2pt-analysis|3x2pt analysis]] combining cosmic shear and galaxy clustering for Euclid-scale data [Manrique-Yus et al. ## Open Questions and Future Directions

As observational astrophysics transitions into the era of Stage-IV surveys (such as those conducted by the Rubin Observatory and Euclid), maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) faces significant theoretical and computational hurdles. Future surveys demand unprecedented precision, pushing traditional Gaussian and Poisson likelihood models to their breaking points.

**Non-Gaussianity and Likelihood Approximations**
The standard assumption of a multivariate Gaussian likelihood for the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] and large-scale structure is a convenient approximation driven by the central limit theorem. However, this approximation breaks down on the largest observable scales (low $k$) and in regimes dominated by non-linear clustering [Sun et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777...75S/abstract), [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..951W/abstract). Assuming a purely Gaussian likelihood in these regimes can severely bias constraints on primordial Non-Gaussianity ($f_{\rm NL}$) [Sun et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777...75S/abstract) and artificially reduce the size of credible regions [Hartlap et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...504..689H/abstract). Consequently, there is an active debate regarding whether Gaussian approximations remain sufficient for future multi-probe analyses, or if more accurate, non-Gaussian data transformations and exact likelihoods must be widely adopted [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..951W/abstract). Conversely, some studies demonstrate that for specific applications like binned cluster abundance, the standard Gaussian likelihood actually remains highly robust without the need for complex compound distributions [Payerne et al. To fully maximize information extraction from weak lensing galaxy surveys in this regime, recent work explores integrating [[quadratic-maximum-likelihood|Quadratic Maximum Likelihood]] (QML) estimators with rigorous theoretical uncertainty modeling, directly addressing the complex systematics inherent in Stage-IV data [Maraio et al. **Cluster Abundance and Super-Sample Covariance**
In galaxy cluster cosmology, traditional parameter inference relies on a [[poisson-statistics|Poisson likelihood]] to model cluster counts. However, standard Poisson models consistently underestimate parameter errors because they fail to account for density fluctuations across the finite survey volume, an effect known as super-sample covariance (SSC) [Hu et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvD..73f7301H/abstract), [Fumagalli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...652A..21F/abstract). Developing computationally efficient, unbinned likelihoods that naturally incorporate SSC remains a critical open question. Recent hybrid approaches, such as the Gauss-Poisson Compound (GPC) likelihood, show promise in merging the benefits of unbinned cluster analysis with rigorous [[sample-variance|SSC corrections]] [Payerne et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532..381P/abstract).

**Covariance Matrix Estimation**
Stable likelihood evaluation requires the precise inversion of a covariance matrix. Directly inverting a noisy sample covariance matrix—often estimated from a finite suite of expensive simulations—leads to a biased precision matrix and degraded cosmological constraints [Fumagalli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...652A..21F/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609118E/abstract). To mitigate this, future pipelines are increasingly relying on shrinkage estimators (such as the NERCOME algorithm) to ensure the precision matrix remains non-singular and unbiased [Looijmans et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240213783L/abstract). Furthermore, the literature remains conflicted on whether the likelihood must dynamically model the cosmological parameter dependence of the covariance matrix. Some studies argue this dependence is essential for unbiased Stage-IV inference [Fumagalli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...652A..21F/abstract), while others demonstrate that a fixed, parameter-independent [[analytical-covariance-matrix|Analytical covariance matrix]] is an exceptionally good approximation that avoids unnecessary computational overhead [Kodwani et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019OJAp....2E...3K/abstract).

**Simulation-Based and Multi-Messenger Inference**
When analytical likelihoods become entirely intractable due to complex observational systematics or highly non-linear physics, the field is pivoting toward [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] (SBI) and [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]] [Ishida et al. For instance, multiplying the likelihoods of electromagnetic (EM) time-domain surveys with pulsar timing array (PTA) datasets provides dramatically tighter constraints on the masses and orbital inclinations of supermassive black hole binaries compared to analyzing the data streams independently [Charisi et al. To manage the extreme computational costs of these multi-probe joint likelihoods, future efforts are focusing on deep-learning-assisted [[nested-sampling|sampling algorithms]] (such as NAUTILUS) to accelerate [[posterior-distributions|posterior estimation]] by orders of magnitude, provided they can remain coupled to deterministic physical models to avoid becoming opaque "black boxes" [Manrique-Yus et al.
