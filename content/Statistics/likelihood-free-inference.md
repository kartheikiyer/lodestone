**Likelihood-free inference** (LFI) is a statistical framework designed to perform Bayesian parameter estimation in complex physical systems where the likelihood function, $P(\text{data} \mid \text{parameters})$, is analytically intractable. In traditional cosmological and astrophysical analyses, researchers often rely on simplified, Gaussian likelihood approximations to constrain parameters using summary statistics. However, these analytic approximations break down when analyzing complex, non-linear physical processes, leading to systematically biased parameter estimates. 

LFI bypasses this bottleneck by relying on a computationally expensive but physically accurate *forward generative model*. By mapping fundamental physical parameters directly to synthetic realizations of the data (mock observables) and comparing them to real observations, LFI allows researchers to implicitly sample the posterior distribution without ever evaluating a closed-form likelihood equation [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract).

Historically dominated by Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC), the field has recently undergone a paradigm shift toward [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] (SBI) powered by deep learning. Modern LFI pipelines utilize state-of-the-art architectures to compress complex data and learn the probability density of the posterior directly from high-fidelity training simulations. Core methodologies like [[neural-posterior-estimation|Neural posterior estimation]] (NPE) "amortize" the inference cost, allowing networks to evaluate new observations in fractions of a second. 

Despite its theoretical optimality and transformative gains in precision, the widespread adoption of LFI faces significant open challenges. The most notable is *model misspecification*—neural density estimators can be highly brittle when exposed to out-of-distribution (OOD) data. Overcoming this "sim-to-real" gap through robust domain generalization, improving the interpretability of neural estimators, and scaling these computationally heavy forward models to the massive data volumes of upcoming Stage-IV surveys remain the primary frontiers of likelihood-free research.

## Introduction to Likelihood-Free Inference in Astrophysics

Traditional Bayesian parameter inference in cosmology and astrophysics relies on the explicit evaluation of a likelihood function, $P(\text{data} \mid \text{parameters})$. However, as modern surveys probe cosmic structure down to non-linear scales (typically $k \gtrsim 0.2\, h/\text{Mpc}$), gravitational collapse and complex baryonic physics induce highly non-Gaussian covariances. In this regime, standard analytic approximations (such as the Gaussian $\chi^2$ likelihood for two-point statistics) break down, and sampling errors in non-Gaussian covariance matrices can propagate directly into systematically biased cosmological parameter estimates and underestimated uncertainties [Blot et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.458.4462B/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract), [Gouyou Beauchamps et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230605988G/abstract). 

LFI addresses this bottleneck by providing a statistical framework to constrain physical parameters when dealing with an intractable likelihood. Instead of evaluating an analytic equation, LFI—often executed within the broader paradigm of [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]]—relies on a *forward generative model*. This model mathematically encapsulates the fundamental physics and observational systematics, mapping input parameters (e.g., $\Omega_m, \sigma_8$) directly to synthetic realizations of the data [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...04..010H/abstract). By comparing the observed data to these high-fidelity mock catalogs or maps, researchers can estimate the posterior distribution without explicitly evaluating the likelihood function, a process also referred to as *Implicit Likelihood Inference* [Jo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...67J/abstract).

A primary advantage of LFI is its ability to rigorously propagate highly complex, non-linear observational systematics. Effects such as survey masks, fiber collisions, shape measurement bias, and non-Gaussian shape noise can be applied directly to the synthetic realizations, bypassing the need for analytic approximations [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...04..010H/abstract), [Jeffrey et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240302314J/abstract). Because LFI can safely extract non-Gaussian information from highly non-linear scales, it yields dramatically tighter constraints than traditional methods. Recent applications to spectroscopic galaxy surveys have demonstrated that constraints on structure growth parameters like $\sigma_8$ and $S_8$ can be improved by factors of ~1.27 to 2.65 over standard power spectrum analyses [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221100723H/abstract), [Lemos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231015256L/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1457H/abstract). The computational burden of generating sufficient simulations is increasingly managed using machine learning techniques like [[neural-posterior-estimation|Neural posterior estimation]], which amortizes the inference cost to provide rapid posterior evaluation.

## Historical Evolution: From ABC to Neural Density Estimation

Even before the modern deep learning era, the theoretical foundations of non-linear signal inference were being laid out using frameworks like Information Field Theory, which explored estimating complex fields through the principle of minimal Gibbs free energy [Enßlin et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvE..82e1112E/abstract). Meanwhile, early efforts to bypass explicit likelihood evaluations for parameter estimation relied on Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC). Instead of calculating the probability of the data given a model, ABC generates forward-simulated mock data and accepts parameters if the distance between the simulated and observed summary statistics falls below a specified tolerance. To overcome the prohibitively low acceptance rates of standard rejection-sampling ABC in high dimensions, astrophysicists adopted adaptive schemes such as Population Monte Carlo (PMC) importance sampling. Frameworks like COSMOABC demonstrated the viability of PMC-ABC for inferring cosmological parameters from cluster counts [Ishida et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&C....13....1I/abstract), while other implementations successfully constrained halo occupation distribution (HOD) parameters without making analytic assumptions about the likelihood [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract).

Despite these successes, ABC suffers heavily from the curse of dimensionality. The requirement of manually crafting distance metrics and the computational cost of forward-simulating during the sampling phase proved intractable for the data volumes of Stage-IV surveys. Consequently, the field experienced a paradigm shift toward [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] (SBI) powered by Neural Density Estimation. 

Instead of relying on distance thresholds, modern SBI uses machine learning architectures—such as normalizing flows and convolutional neural networks—to learn the probability density of the posterior or likelihood directly from a training set of forward simulations. This transition unlocked previously inaccessible non-Gaussian information embedded in non-linear clustering scales. By exploiting neural data compression and forward modeling, modern SBI frameworks have rapidly outpaced the constraining power of traditional analytic analyses [Lemos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231015256L/abstract). 

## Core Methodologies: NPE, NRE, and Normalizing Flows

Modern [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] relies heavily on deep learning architectures to bypass intractable analytic likelihoods. The core algorithmic variants in this domain leverage neural networks to construct highly accurate, implicit likelihoods or direct posterior distributions from simulated mock data.

### Neural Posterior Estimation and Normalizing Flows
[[neural-posterior-estimation|Neural posterior estimation]] (NPE) frames the inference problem as a direct density estimation task. By training a neural network on forward-modeled mock observations and their corresponding ground-truth parameters, NPE yields a surrogate posterior. In astrophysical applications, this is most frequently implemented using normalizing flows. Normalizing flows apply a sequence of invertible, differentiable transformations to a simple base probability distribution (such as a multivariate Gaussian) to map it onto complex, high-dimensional, and strongly non-Gaussian posteriors.

A major advantage of NPE is that it provides "amortized" inference: once the normalizing flow is trained globally over the prior parameter space, the computational cost of evaluating new observational data is negligible. In spectral energy distribution (SED) modeling, amortized NPE accelerates parameter estimation by factors of $10^3$ to $10^5$, reducing inference times from $\sim 100$ CPU hours using standard Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to approximately 1 second per galaxy [Alsing et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..249....5A/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract), [Khullar et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MLS&T...3dLT04K/abstract). 

### Neural Ratio Estimation (NRE)
An alternative to modeling the posterior directly is Neural Ratio Estimation (NRE). Rather than estimating a probability density, NRE trains a binary classifier to distinguish between joint samples of data and parameters versus marginal (uncorrelated) samples. The classifier's output asymptotically converges to the likelihood-to-evidence ratio. This ratio can then be integrated with standard MCMC samplers to explore the posterior without ever evaluating an explicit likelihood function. For upcoming Stage-IV photometric surveys, variants such as Marginal Neural Ratio Estimation (MNRE) have demonstrated computational speedups of $40$ to $60\times$ over classical MCMC methods while maintaining high accuracy in high-dimensional cosmological models [Abellán et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240314750A/abstract).

### Truncated Marginal Neural Ratio Estimation
In domains requiring extreme hierarchical marginalization over thousands of nuisance parameters, researchers employ Truncated marginal neural ratio estimation (TMNRE). TMNRE iteratively focuses the training data generation on regions of parameter space that are highly relevant to the specific observation, effectively "truncating" the prior to improve sampling efficiency. This approach is uniquely suited for strong gravitational lensing. For instance, TMNRE allows for the simultaneous constraint of the warm dark matter (WDM) halo mass function cut-off scale by implicitly marginalizing over the vast population of undetectable, sub-threshold dark matter perturbers across multiple lensed images [Anau Montel et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.2746A/abstract). Similar TMNRE implementations have also enabled population-level inferences of Type Ia supernova light curves by effectively marginalizing over complex dust distributions, redshift evolution, and instrumental noise [Karchev et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.3881K/abstract).

## Simulation Suites and Forward Modeling Frameworks

In [[simulation-based-inference|SBI]], the need for an explicit analytic likelihood is bypassed entirely by utilizing high-fidelity forward models. The success of this approach is critically dependent on the accuracy of the simulations used to generate the training data. 

For large-scale structure (LSS) analyses, $N$-body simulation suites serve as the primary engine for forward modeling. A prominent example is the SimBIG framework, which utilizes the high-resolution Quijote simulations coupled with flexible halo occupation distribution models to analyze galaxy clustering [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...04..010H/abstract). A major advantage of SimBIG is its ability to naturally incorporate detailed survey realism—such as angular masking, fiber collisions, and non-Gaussian noise—directly into the mock catalogs. By properly modeling these effects, SimBIG enables the extraction of cosmological information from nonlinear scales ($k > 0.25 \, h/\mathrm{Mpc}$) that are inaccessible to standard analytic perturbation theory [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221100723H/abstract). Another flagship suite is the MillenniumTNG project, which combines IllustrisTNG baryonic physics with extremely large-volume simulations to provide high-precision clustering predictions and halo statistics across vast cosmic scales [Hernández-Aguayo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2556H/abstract).

To model smaller scales where baryonic effects become dominant, SBI frameworks rely on comprehensive hydrodynamic simulation suites. The CAMELS project provides thousands of state-of-the-art simulations that sample a vast parameter space of both cosmology and subgrid astrophysics, such as supernova and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract). To generate diverse training sets for machine learning models, CAMELS incorporates multiple distinct galaxy formation codes, including IllustrisTNG, SIMBA, and Astrid [de Santi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952...69D/abstract), [Shao et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956..149S/abstract). To optimize the generation of these computationally heavy datasets, frameworks employ techniques like Bayesian optimization to iteratively select optimal training points for emulators, significantly reducing the number of expensive simulations required for structures like the Lyman-alpha forest [Rogers et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...02..031R/abstract). 

To further accelerate this process, generative machine learning models are being deployed. For instance, U-net architectures have been used to computationally "paint" high-fidelity hydrodynamic baryonic properties directly onto computationally cheap dark matter-only $N$-body simulations [Chadayammuri et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.2812C/abstract). Similarly, the CHARM framework utilizes neural spline flows to rapidly generate high-fidelity mock halo catalogs directly from dark matter density fields, significantly reducing the computational overhead of generating training data [Pandey et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240909124P/abstract). Furthermore, semi-analytic models (SAMs) are increasingly utilized as computationally efficient alternatives to full hydrodynamic simulations, allowing researchers to train robust LFI models that generalize effectively across disparate subgrid physics prescriptions [de Santi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251210222D/abstract). To rigorously evaluate these diverse forward modeling approaches, community initiatives such as the "Beyond-2pt" mock data challenge benchmark various SBI and field-level inference pipelines to ensure unbiased parameter recovery prior to their application on observational data [Beyond-2pt Collaboration et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240502252B/abstract).

## Field-Level Inference and Beyond Summary Statistics

Cosmological analyses have traditionally relied on $n$-point summary statistics, such as the power spectrum and bispectrum, to compress the large-scale structure of the Universe. However, on non-linear scales ($k \gtrsim 0.2\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$), gravitational evolution and baryonic feedback induce significant non-Gaussianities. To bypass this bottleneck, recent approaches leverage LFI to extract information directly from 3D density fields, pixelated maps, or discrete point clouds. Beyond standard $n$-point statistics, alternative summary metrics such as the $N$-probability density function ($N$-pdf) of the galaxy number density have also been demonstrated as an effective tool for the optimal Bayesian estimation of galaxy bias and clustering amplitude [Arnalte-Mur et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JCAP...03..005A/abstract).

### Forward-Modeled Field-Level Inference
Field-level inference (FLI) seeks to optimally extract cosmological information by explicitly forward-modeling the entire matter or tracer field and marginalizing over the initial conditions, bias parameters, and noise [Schmidt et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...01..042S/abstract). Historically, frameworks like the BORG algorithm laid the groundwork for explicitly extracting spatial information by deriving initial conditions and creating physical, probabilistic reconstructions of the local large-scale structure [Leclercq et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151202242L/abstract). Another notable advancement is the COSMIC BIRTH algorithm, which facilitates the efficient Bayesian inference and reconstruction of evolving primordial density fields directly from light-cone galaxy surveys using Gibbs-sampling [Kitaura et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502.3456K/abstract). Building upon this foundation, the Manticore-Local project leverages the BORG algorithm to produce a suite of Bayesian constrained simulations, effectively creating a "digital twin" of our local cosmic neighborhood for highly precise field-level analyses [McAlpine et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540..716M/abstract). 

Modern FLI pipelines often employ effective field theory (EFT)-based forward models such as LEFTfield to capture non-linear modes that are otherwise lost. Explicitly sampling the initial conditions up to $k_{\mathrm{max}} \sim 0.1\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$ has been shown to yield a factor of $3.5$ to $5.2$ improvement on $\sigma_8$ constraints compared to combined power spectrum and bispectrum analyses [Nguyen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240303220N/abstract). Furthermore, field-level reconstruction of the baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) scale reduces errors by $30-50\%$ relative to standard post-reconstruction techniques [Babić et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...11..066B/abstract). 

### Convolutional Neural Networks and Wavelet Scattering
Machine learning architectures provide a computationally efficient alternative for field-level compression. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) can process voxelized 3D density fields or 2D weak lensing maps to learn optimal, low-dimensional data summaries. In the SimBIG framework, applying a CNN with stochastic weight averaging to the BOSS CMASS galaxy field produced constraints on $\sigma_8$ that were $2.65\times$ tighter than standard analytic power spectrum analyses [Lemos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109h3536L/abstract). Within the SimBIG framework, researchers have also demonstrated the power of marked power spectra, which weight galaxies by their local density to efficiently capture higher-order clustering information that is otherwise missed by standard two-point statistics [Massara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.112h3507M/abstract).

As a mathematically interpretable alternative to CNNs, the Wavelet Scattering Transform (WST) extracts hierarchical, non-Gaussian features using fixed wavelet filters. While the WST significantly outperforms the power spectrum in constraining $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$, it is highly sensitive to model misspecification at small scales. Variations in subgrid baryonic physics or observational systematics can push WST coefficients out-of-distribution (OOD), requiring aggressive scale cuts (e.g., $k < 0.3\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$) or specialized OOD mitigation techniques to ensure robust inference [Régaldo-Saint Blancard et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109h3535R/abstract), [Pierre et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250703086P/abstract). Similar wavelet-based approaches, such as Wavelet Phase Harmonics (WPH), have been leveraged as robust summary statistics for constructing non-Gaussian generative models of large-scale reionization maps [Lin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221014273L/abstract). Beyond standard filtering, topological data analysis such as persistent homology applied to spherical convergence maps can extract non-Gaussian structural information entirely differently, yielding cosmological constraints significantly tighter than standard two-point statistics [Prat et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250613439P/abstract). Alternative topological approaches, such as $k$-th nearest neighbour (kNN) statistics, provide highly robust data summaries capable of safely extracting cosmological information from non-linear scales down to $3 \, h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Yuan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.1993Y/abstract).

### Graph Neural Networks and Point Clouds
Voxelizing a sparse galaxy catalog inevitably discards small-scale structural information. To preserve this, recent frameworks treat galaxy surveys natively as 3D point clouds, applying Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) or E(3)-invariant point-cloud networks. GNNs naturally handle irregular spatial distributions by constructing graphs where nodes represent galaxies (with attributes like mass or velocity) and edges represent spatial proximity. When applied to mock catalogs from the CAMELS hydrodynamic simulations, GNNs processing just $\sim 1000$ galaxies in small $(25\,h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc})^3$ volumes can constrain $\Omega_m$ to $\sim 12\%$ precision [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract), [de Santi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952...69D/abstract). In the domain of weak lensing and time-delay cosmography, Bayesian graph neural networks have also been developed to hierarchically estimate the lensing convergence directly from line-of-sight photometric catalog measurements [Park et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953..178P/abstract). Architectures designed to enforce rotational, translational, and permutation invariance further improve the ability to extract fundamental physical relations directly from phase-space coordinates without intermediate graph construction [Chatterjee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513119C/abstract).

## Differentiable Programming and Hamiltonian SBI

As the dimensionality of astrophysical models increases, traditional sampling algorithms like random-walk Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) suffer from exponentially decreasing acceptance rates. Gradient-based sampling techniques, primarily Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) and the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS), circumvent this by using Hamiltonian dynamics to propose distant, high-probability parameter states. However, HMC requires the exact gradient of the log-posterior with respect to the model parameters, $\nabla_{\theta} \log p(x|\theta)p(\theta)$ [Jasche et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407...29J/abstract).

The advent of differentiable programming has revolutionized this paradigm. By employing automatic differentiation (autodiff) frameworks such as JAX, researchers can now propagate exact gradients through complex numerical forward models. A foundational example is `JAX-cosmo`, an end-to-end differentiable cosmology library that implements background evolution and linear/non-linear power spectra [Campagne et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..15C/abstract). By making the forward model natively differentiable, `JAX-cosmo` enables HMC sampling over cosmological parameters without relying on analytic likelihood approximations.

When full-physics simulators contain fundamentally non-differentiable operations or remain too computationally expensive, the [[simulation-based-inference|SBI]] framework leverages neural networks to act as differentiable surrogates. Emulators constructed using deep learning are inherently differentiable, facilitating a hybrid "Hamiltonian SBI" approach. For instance, the `CosmoPower-JAX` framework trains neural emulators on cosmological power spectra. By executing these differentiable emulators on GPUs and sampling via HMC, a joint cosmic shear and galaxy clustering analysis spanning $157$ model parameters can converge in just 3 days—a speed-up of $\mathcal{O}(10^3)$ over standard techniques [Piras et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..20P/abstract). Similarly, `PyBird-JAX` accelerates one-loop effective field theory of large-scale structure (EFTofLSS) predictions to $0.2 \text{ ms}$ on a GPU, allowing gradient-based searches and MCMC chains to converge in minutes without accuracy loss [Reeves et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250720990R/abstract). 

When numerical simulators remain strictly non-differentiable and neural emulators are impractical, novel frameworks such as "Learning to Optimize" (LULO) enable gradient-free field-level inference by iteratively updating cosmic initial conditions using machine learning, bridging the gap between exact simulators and high-dimensional optimization [Doeser et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.542.1403D/abstract).

The integration of differentiable programming into forward models extends to other domains:
*   **Galaxy-Halo Connection:** Differentiable stochastic halo occupation distribution (HOD) models allow for well-defined gradients through discrete mock catalogs using continuous relaxation, yielding an $8\times$ improvement in HMC convergence efficiency [Horowitz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.2473H/abstract).
*   **Strong Lensing:** End-to-end differentiable strong lensing pipelines unite CNNs for source galaxy light profiles with exact gravitational lensing physics. This enables the simultaneous, gradient-based optimization of nearly $100$ lens and source parameters [Chianese et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496..381C/abstract).
*   **Stellar Population Synthesis:** The `DSPS` library implements differentiable stellar population synthesis, calculating exact gradients for galaxy SEDs with respect to star formation histories, metallicity, and dust attenuation. `DSPS` provides GPU accelerations of factors of $300-400$ [Hearin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1741H/abstract).

## Applications in Cosmology and Galaxy Evolution

By bypassing analytically intractable likelihoods, LFI methods allow researchers to directly forward-model complex, non-linear physical processes across multiple scales of astrophysics. While Bayesian methods dominate, frequentist alternatives also play a role in complex parameter spaces. For example, profile likelihood techniques provide an effective frequentist alternative to Bayesian marginalization for handling nuisance parameters when determining the Hubble constant ($H_0$) from Megamaser Cosmology Project measurements [Barua et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250211998B/abstract).

### Cosmological Parameter Inference
Standard cosmological inference from large-scale structure typically relies on two-point statistics and Gaussian likelihood approximations, discarding non-linear information and introducing biases at small scales [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract), [Gouyou Beauchamps et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230605988G/abstract). [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] circumvents these limitations by utilizing $N$-body and hydrodynamic simulations to implicitly model the likelihood. By extracting non-Gaussian information from non-linear scales using neural data compression, SBI yields significantly tighter constraints on $\Lambda$CDM parameters (up to 2.65 times tighter than traditional power spectrum analyses) [Lemos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231015256L/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1457H/abstract). Furthermore, applying deep-learning analysis to combined probes—such as joint cosmic shear and galaxy clustering—can effectively break pervasive degeneracies between clustering amplitude, matter density, and intrinsic galaxy alignments [Kacprzak et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvX..12c1029K/abstract). In the domain of weak lensing, neural emulators trained on the integrated shear 3-point correlation function have provided a highly computationally efficient mechanism for higher-order cosmic shear analyses [Gong et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...07..040G/abstract).

### Galaxy-Halo Connection
Connecting the cosmological dark matter distribution to observable galaxies requires modeling the galaxy-halo connection. Early applications of likelihood-free inference demonstrated the ability to constrain HOD parameters without assuming analytically tractable likelihoods [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract). Recent approaches have utilized machine learning to bridge the galaxy-halo connection in both directions. Graph neural networks have been used to infer baryonic properties directly from dark matter subhalo kinematics [Wu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230612327W/abstract), while normalizing flows have successfully inferred host dark matter halo masses from galaxy photometry and morphology [Hahn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968...90H/abstract). Furthermore, neural networks can effectively extract precise halo and subhalo mass information from local environment measures, such as galaxy counts within specific apertures [Bowden et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..37B/abstract).

### Stellar Population Synthesis
At the scale of individual galaxies, deriving physical properties such as stellar mass, metallicity, and star formation history via SED fitting is traditionally bottlenecked by the computational cost of high-dimensional MCMC sampling. Amortized [[neural-posterior-estimation|Neural posterior estimation]] and neural emulators have revolutionized this domain, providing computational speedups of $10^3$ to $10^5$ and reducing the inference time per galaxy to a fraction of a second [Alsing et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..249....5A/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract). Nonetheless, researchers caution that emulation uncertainties can propagate into posterior biases, and the ability of neural nets to accurately fit rare objects depends critically on the chosen training distribution [Mathews et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954..132M/abstract).

### 21-cm Power Spectrum and Reionization
LFI is increasingly critical for interpreting the 21-cm power spectrum from the Epoch of Reionization (EoR). The complex astrophysics of early ultraviolet and X-ray sources produces highly non-Gaussian 21-cm signals. While the 1D 21-cm power spectrum likelihood can be well described by a Gaussian, standard approaches that estimate the covariance at a single point in parameter space lead to biased and overconstrained posteriors [Prelogović et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.4239P/abstract). Using semi-numerical simulations like 21CMFAST, SBI pipelines employing neural density estimators can jointly infer cosmological parameters and galaxy emission properties. Emulators trained on these simulations reduce computational costs by a factor of $10^4$ while maintaining percent-level accuracy [Breitman et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.9833B/abstract), [Greig et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.2502G/abstract).

### Lensing, Transients, and the Cosmic Microwave Background
SBI is also expanding into new realms of multi-messenger and transient astrophysics. For compact binary coalescence events detected via gravitational waves, LFI employing normalizing flows allows researchers to infer the Hubble constant directly from the data, bypassing the need for an explicit analytic likelihood [Stachurski et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109l3547S/abstract). However, consistent Bayesian frameworks for these "dark siren" gravitational-wave cosmology measurements must carefully model and incorporate survey selection effects to avoid severely biased $H_0$ estimates [Gair et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....166...22G/abstract). Similarly, SBI is applied to Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) to robustly constrain the dispersion measure-redshift relation while modeling complex host galaxy contributions [Konar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241007084K/abstract). In the study of the cosmic microwave background (CMB), SBI utilizing neural likelihood models has been deployed to extract cluster mass constraints from CMB lensing distortions, providing a computationally efficient alternative to mathematically exact but intractable likelihood methods [Baxter et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...09..002B/abstract). In the search for dark matter substructure, SBI has been applied to astrometric lensing datasets, demonstrating a much greater sensitivity to subhalo populations than traditional two-point power spectrum methods [Mishra-Sharma et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MLS&T...3aLT03M/abstract).

## Systematics, Robustness, and Out-of-Distribution Data

A primary advantage of LFI over traditional analytic likelihoods is its capacity to natively incorporate **observational systematics**. Instead of relying on simplified data covariance matrices, forward models propagate complex effects directly into the mock data. Frameworks explicitly forward-model sky masks, fiber collisions, non-Gaussian shape noise, intrinsic galaxy alignments, and photometric redshift uncertainties [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...04..010H/abstract), [Jeffrey et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240302314J/abstract). Furthermore, algorithms like SELFI allow researchers to diagnose the impact of survey masks and selection functions on the inferred initial power spectrum prior to running the full cosmological parameter estimation [Hoellinger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204443H/abstract).

Despite these advantages, a fundamental vulnerability of LFI is **model misspecification**, frequently referred to as the "sim-to-real" gap. The literature cautions against the brittleness of [[neural-posterior-estimation|Neural posterior estimation]] and similar techniques when applied to data outside their training domain. Detailed sensitivity analyses highlight this vulnerability, demonstrating that while [[simulation-based-inference|SBI]] for galaxy clustering is generally robust to changes in the macroscopic gravity model (e.g., full $N$-body versus approximate particle-mesh simulations), it is highly sensitive to the chosen halo-finder and the complexity of the underlying HOD models used for training [Modi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230915071M/abstract). Studies similarly demonstrate that networks trained on one hydrodynamic simulation suite frequently fail or produce biased constraints when applied to another due to differing baryonic feedback or active galactic nucleus (AGN) prescriptions. For example, a neural network trained to infer cluster masses from X-ray profiles in the Magneticum simulation suffered a 40% performance drop when evaluated on an out-of-domain IllustrisTNG test set [Ntampaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251009748N/abstract). Similar brittleness is commonly observed in field-level and galaxy property inferences [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929..132V/abstract), [Chawak et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969..105C/abstract), [Gluck et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.52710038G/abstract).

To bridge this gap, researchers increasingly employ **domain generalization** and adaptation techniques. One approach uses semantic alignment loss to map fast, approximate lightcone simulations and high-fidelity $N$-body simulations into a shared latent space, improving the accuracy of the network when applied to real survey data [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975...38L/abstract). Methods adapted from causal inference, such as stratifying training data based on propensity scores, have also been shown to mitigate covariate shift [Autenrieth et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210611211A/abstract). Conversely, some works suggest that carefully selected physical features—such as combining 3D galaxy positions with radial velocities—can naturally yield robust inferences that extrapolate successfully across disparate subgrid models without explicit adaptation algorithms [de Santi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952...69D/abstract), [Shao et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956..149S/abstract).

Real-world astronomical surveys also suffer from heterogeneous coverage and variable noise levels that routinely push observations out-of-distribution (OOD) relative to a static training set. The SBI++ methodology addresses this by utilizing Monte Carlo marginalization over nearby data realizations in the training set to handle missing data and OOD measurement errors, successfully expanding the applicability of SBI to realistic datasets [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221103747W/abstract), [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952L..10W/abstract). Other OOD mitigation strategies include deep compact clustering algorithms that explicitly flag and discard inferences if the observed data diverges from the training distribution [Tregidga et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251109660T/abstract), as well as transformation techniques that dynamically project OOD summary statistics back into the training support to preserve constraining power [Pierre et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250703086P/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While [[simulation-based-inference|SBI]] has demonstrated the ability to extract highly constraining non-Gaussian information from astrophysical data, its deployment for precision cosmology faces several unresolved challenges. 

### Scalability and Stage-IV Surveys
The transition from summary statistics to field-level inference entails sampling million-dimensional latent spaces. Scaling these approaches to the massive volumes demanded by Stage-IV surveys (such as Euclid, LSST, and DESI) remains a formidable computational hurdle [Simon-Onfroy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250420130S/abstract). To bypass the prohibitive cost of running full-physics simulations during inference, researchers are developing differentiable forward models and neural emulators. While these surrogates have achieved massive computational speedups [Alsing et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..249....5A/abstract), ensuring that they maintain percent-level accuracy across vast parameter spaces is an ongoing challenge. 

### Multi-Modal and Hybrid Approaches
To maximize the extraction of cosmological information, researchers are beginning to merge disparate modeling paradigms. The Hybrid SBI (HySBI) framework exemplifies this by combining simulation-based inference on small, non-linear scales with traditional analytic perturbation theory on large, linear scales, ensuring optimal constraints across all physical regimes [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250513591Z/abstract). Concurrently, the emergence of multi-modal foundation models for cosmology promises to unify diverse data streams. By learning mappings between fundamental simulation features (such as halo mass and star formation history) and diverse observables (including photometry and spectroscopy), these foundation models aim to create flexible, generalized inference engines capable of interpreting heterogeneous datasets from upcoming surveys [Xia et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251007684X/abstract).

### Cross-Simulation Validation
Because deep learning models can be highly brittle when exposed to out-of-domain subgrid physics prescriptions, cross-simulation validation has become a mandatory diagnostic tool for LFI pipelines. While techniques like domain adaptation and feature selection show promise (as discussed in the *Systematics* section), achieving true domain generalization without manual tuning or relying on specific, highly curated observables remains an open computational question. 

### Interpretability and Uncertainty Calibration
When deep learning models extract cosmological information directly from non-linear fields, verifying exactly which physical scales or features drive the constraints is difficult. Recent work has attempted to identify and interpret the optimal summary statistics learned by these networks, mapping them to underlying simulation parameters across specific physical modes (e.g., $k \sim 5 - 30 \, h/\mathrm{Mpc}$) [Lehman et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108957L/abstract), but widespread adoption of interpretable architectures is still lacking. To move beyond simple parameter estimation towards understanding underlying physical mechanisms, causal discovery algorithms (e.g., the Peter-Clark algorithm) are being introduced to cosmology to help identify the primary physical drivers of processes such as supermassive black hole and galaxy co-evolution [Pasquato et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231115160P/abstract).

Additionally, Bayesian deep learning approaches in astrophysics frequently suffer from the "cold posterior effect," where predictive posteriors are overconfident and must be heuristically down-weighted to achieve proper uncertainty calibration. This effect is hypothesized to stem from likelihood misspecification or the over-curation of simulated training data [Mohan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv211111654M/abstract), [Mohan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.3722M/abstract). Ensuring statistically rigorous, perfectly calibrated posteriors for high-stakes cosmological parameter estimation is a primary objective for the next generation of likelihood-free inference tools.
