**Neural posterior estimation (NPE)** is a powerful computational framework within [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] (SBI) and [[likelihood-free-inference|Likelihood-free inference]] (LFI) that utilizes deep learning to approximate Bayesian probability distributions. In traditional astrophysical parameter estimation, algorithms like Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) require the explicit evaluation of a likelihood function $\mathcal{L}(x|\theta)$, which is often analytically intractable or computationally prohibitive for complex physical systems. NPE bypasses this requirement by training deep generative architectures—such as normalizing flows or denoising diffusion models—directly on datasets of mock observables and physical parameters $(\theta_i, x_i)$ generated by forward-modeling simulators. Acting as highly flexible [[conditioned-models|Conditioned models]], these networks frame the inference problem as a density estimation task. The neural network parameters $\phi$ are optimized to maximize the expected log-likelihood of the simulated parameter values given their corresponding mock observables:
$$ \max_{\phi} \mathbb{E}_{(\theta, x) \sim p(\theta, x)} [\log q_\phi(\theta|x)] $$
Learning the multidimensional, often non-Gaussian posterior distribution $q_\phi(\theta|x) \approx p(\theta|x)$ directly from the data represents a fundamental paradigm shift from likelihood-based stochastic sampling methods.

A defining advantage of NPE is *amortized inference*. While the upfront cost of training the neural density estimator on millions of simulations is high, evaluating the posterior for any new, unseen observation takes only fractions of a second. The literature consistently demonstrates that NPE achieves computational speedup factors ranging from $10^3$ to $10^6$ over traditional samplers, enabling population-level Bayesian inference on massive datasets. Consequently, NPE has unlocked new analytical capabilities across diverse astrophysical domains. In extragalactic astronomy, it permits rapid spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting for billion-galaxy catalogs, reducing inference time from over 100 CPU hours to approximately one second per galaxy [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract), [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167...16L/abstract), [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110640H/abstract). In cosmology, frameworks like SimBIG utilize NPE to extract matter density and clustering amplitudes from non-linear, small-scale galaxy clustering statistics where analytical perturbation theory fails [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...04..010H/abstract). Furthermore, NPE facilitates the implicit marginalization over thousands of latent variables, making it a premier tool for characterizing sub-arcsecond dark matter substructure in strong gravitational lensing [Coogan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201007032C/abstract), [Anau Montel et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.2746A/abstract) and performing probabilistic field-level reconstructions of the underlying dark matter distribution from observed galaxies [Ono et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..174O/abstract).

Despite its transformative speed and flexibility, the widespread adoption of NPE faces several critical challenges, primarily centered on the "simulation-to-observation gap." Because NPE relies entirely on the fidelity of its training data, unmodeled observational noise or inaccuracies in subgrid astrophysical models can lead to severe systematic biases. A major open question in the field is how to robustly mitigate *domain shift*—where models trained on one hydrodynamical simulation suite fail to generalize to data governed by different physical prescriptions. As astronomers prepare for Stage IV observatories like Euclid and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST), current research is intensely focused on scaling these techniques to high-dimensional ($100+$ parameter) spaces, diagnosing model misspecification prior to inference, and utilizing advanced phenomenological tools like [[galsbi|GalSBI]] to build highly robust, unified cosmological foundation models.

## Historical Evolution: From Regression to Generative Posteriors

The application of neural networks to cosmological parameter estimation traces its origins to early efforts to bypass the computational expense of exact likelihood evaluations. A foundational example is the work of Phillips (2001), who demonstrated that machine learning could be used as a rapid tool for analyzing large, correlated data sets such as cosmic microwave background (CMB) maps and galaxy redshift surveys [Phillips et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12359P/abstract). By training a "committee" of neural networks to distinguish between Monte Carlo simulations generated at fixed cosmological parameters, this approach enabled accurate parameter interpolation to intermediate regions of parameter space. This early methodology provided unbiased parameter estimates with precision comparable to traditional maximum-likelihood algorithms, but at a fraction of the computational cost—scaling as $\mathcal{O}(N^{3/2})$ for CMB anisotropies [Phillips et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12359P/abstract). However, these early architectures primarily functioned as sophisticated regression tools rather than modern, exact probabilistic density estimators.

Throughout the 2000s and 2010s, rigorous Bayesian parameter estimation in astrophysics relied almost exclusively on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) and nested sampling techniques. While these methods successfully mapped non-Gaussian posteriors, they required explicit, analytically tractable likelihood functions and millions of forward-model evaluations. As physical models grew in complexity—incorporating highly non-linear hydrodynamical physics, stellar population synthesis, and complex observational systematics—the computational cost of standard stochastic sampling became a severe bottleneck. Routine inference tasks began requiring thousands of CPU hours per object [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract). This limitation catalyzed the transition towards [[likelihood-free-inference|Likelihood-free inference]] and the development of modern neural posterior estimation (NPE).

The modern era of NPE represents a paradigm shift from deterministic point estimation to direct probability density estimation. Contemporary deep learning architectures—such as normalizing flows and diffusion models—are trained directly on forward-modeled simulations to approximate the marginal posterior distribution $p(\theta | x)$. These generative architectures capture the full, non-Gaussian probability density functions of high-dimensional parameter spaces. Within the broader framework of [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]], flow-based NPE has been shown to systematically outperform standard Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs). For example, while BNNs often exhibit systematic biases when fitting complex source-light parameters in strong gravitational lensing, NPE yields highly precise and well-calibrated posteriors [Poh et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221105836P/abstract). By explicitly learning the posterior distribution, modern generative [[conditioned-models|Conditioned models]] have solved the scaling limitations of early network 'committees', enabling population-level Bayesian inference on catalogs containing billions of galaxies [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167...16L/abstract).

## Generative Architectures and Amortization

Neural posterior estimation (NPE) fundamentally relies on the principle of *amortized inference*. In traditional Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) or nested sampling algorithms, estimating posterior distributions requires evaluating the likelihood function millions of times for every new observation. Amortized Neural Posterior Estimation (ANPE) shifts this computational burden entirely to an upfront training phase. Once trained on a diverse dataset of forward-modeled simulations, the neural density estimator can evaluate the posterior distribution for any new, unseen observation in a fraction of a second. This is a key enabler for [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] (SBI) and [[likelihood-free-inference|Likelihood-free inference]], routinely delivering speed-up factors ranging from $10^3$ to $10^6$ over traditional samplers while maintaining comparable accuracy [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract), [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167...16L/abstract), [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110640H/abstract).

**Normalizing Flows and Frameworks**  
The workhorse architecture for NPE is the normalizing flow. A normalizing flow constructs a complex, non-Gaussian target density by applying a sequence of differentiable, invertible transformations to a simple base distribution (typically a multivariate standard Gaussian). In the context of ANPE, the flow is parameterized by neural networks that act as [[conditioned-models|Conditioned models]], adapting the transformations based on the input observational data. Normalizing flows have proven exceptionally successful at scaling to large datasets. For example, the SEDFLOW framework uses ANPE to infer 12-parameter galaxy spectral energy distribution (SED) posteriors in roughly $1\text{ s}$ per galaxy, compared to $>100$ CPU hours for MCMC [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract). Similarly, architectures like PopSED utilize normalizing flows to bypass individual galaxy fitting entirely, estimating population-level distributions $10^5$ to $10^6$ times faster than classical hierarchical modeling [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167...16L/abstract). To streamline these developments across diverse fields, comprehensive software frameworks such as LtU-ILI have been introduced, providing an all-in-one pipeline for rapid neural architecture implementation and validation in simulation-based inference tasks [Ho et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..54H/abstract). Furthermore, specialized tools such as GalaxyFlow utilize normalizing flows to upsample star particles from hydrodynamical simulations into individual stars, achieving smooth phase-space densities comparable to Gaia observations [Lim et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.tmp.1660L/abstract).

**Conditional Autoencoders and Geometric Learning**  
For high-dimensional structured data, such as raw images or pixel-level photometry, conditional autoencoders (including variational autoencoders, or VAEs) serve as a powerful generative bridge. These models compress complex input data into a lower-dimensional latent space via an encoder network, while a decoder network reconstructs the data or maps it to physical parameters. Conditional autoencoders can rapidly map galaxy images to semantically meaningful variables (e.g., shape and brightness) with regression errors nearing the Cramér-Rao bound [Yin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASP..134d4502Y/abstract). Recently, the Minuet architecture introduced a low-dimensional diffusion autoencoder capable of compressing multi-band galaxy images into a compact 5D latent space while maintaining semantic interpretability for downstream redshift and mass inference [Gagliano et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251204145G/abstract). When used alongside NPE, autoencoders effectively compress the data vector into summary statistics before the density estimator constructs the final posterior. Additionally, applying techniques like dataset distillation condenses complex galaxy morphological knowledge into highly compact synthetic datasets, aiding neural network interpretation and training efficiency [Guan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231117967G/abstract). Specialized geometric deep learning models are also expanding the expressive power of these representations; for instance, E(3) equivariant Graph Neural Networks are being utilized to jointly model Euclidean galaxy properties and their SO(3) orientations, offering a highly effective approach for galaxy intrinsic alignment studies [Jagvaral et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240918761J/abstract).

**Denoising Diffusion Models**  
Recently, denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) have emerged as highly expressive alternatives to standard normalizing flows. Diffusion models learn to iteratively denoise a sample drawn from a prior distribution, progressively recovering structure conditioned on the observed data. They are particularly well-suited for high-dimensional, highly multimodal, and non-Gaussian posteriors. In astrophysics, diffusion models have been successfully applied as data-driven priors and generative models for strong-lensing source reconstruction [Karchev et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221104365K/abstract) and for the probabilistic deconvolution of point spread functions (PSFs) in galaxy imaging [Xue et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230711122X/abstract). By conditioning the diffusion process on noisy observables, these models sample from the joint posterior of the source distribution given the observation, naturally capturing greater sample diversity than classical VAEs [Xue et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230711122X/abstract). They are also actively deployed for field-level reconstruction, effectively "debiasing" observed galaxy density fields to infer the underlying dark matter distribution [Ono et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..174O/abstract).

## Stellar Population Synthesis and SED Fitting

The physical characterization of galaxies from broadband photometry and spectroscopy relies heavily on comparing observations to Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) models generated via Stellar population synthesis (SPS). Traditional Bayesian approaches to SED fitting rely on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) or nested sampling algorithms to explore high-dimensional spaces of stellar mass, metallicity, dust attenuation, and star formation history (SFH). However, evaluating complex SPS forward models typically requires $10$ to $100$ CPU hours per galaxy [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract). As upcoming Stage-IV surveys such as the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) are expected to observe millions to billions of galaxies, traditional likelihood-based sampling has become computationally intractable.

Neural Posterior Estimation (NPE) and other forms of [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] offer a highly scalable alternative by amortizing the cost of inference. Once trained on a synthetic catalog of SPS parameters and their corresponding noisy observables, normalizing flows or diffusion models can produce full, multivariate posterior distributions in fractions of a second. For instance, the SEDFLOW framework generates 12-parameter posteriors from optical photometry and redshift in $\sim 1\text{ s}$ per galaxy, matching MCMC accuracy while accelerating the inference by several orders of magnitude [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract). Similarly, algorithms like Synference apply NPE to 14-band HST and JWST photometry, achieving a $\sim 1700\times$ speedup over traditional nested sampling by processing $18$ galaxies per second per CPU [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110640H/abstract). In the spectroscopic regime, frameworks such as DIGS leverage NPE to analyze noisy galaxy spectra, conducting inference on $\sim 10^5$ spectra in the computational time required for a single traditional MCMC fit [Khullar et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MLS&T...3dLT04K/abstract).

The raw speed of amortized NPE is enabling novel analyses that were previously unfeasible. For the DESI PROVABGS catalog, which aims to characterize $>10^{7}$ galaxies, neural emulators of the SPS model achieve $\ll 1\%$ error margins and a $100\times$ acceleration compared to standard evaluation, ensuring the computational viability of the catalog [Kwon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...23K/abstract). Beyond integrated photometry, NPE has been extended to pixel-level SED fitting of high-resolution JWST images. By evaluating posteriors at a speed of $10^{-4}\text{ s}$ per pixel, spatially resolved maps of stellar population properties can be constructed for millions of pixels across thousands of galaxies in roughly a single CPU-day. This circumvents the traditional limitations of outshining and unresolved substructure, revealing spatially resolved mass growth and assembly histories [Iglesias-Navarro et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250604336I/abstract). In parallel, conditional variational autoencoders (VAEs) and normalizing flows are being used to infer spatially resolved 2D maps of ex-situ stellar mass fractions from Integral Field Unit observables like age and metallicity [Angeloudi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241024069A/abstract). Furthermore, Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) frameworks are now integrating pixel-level morphology with photometry to successfully break longstanding dust-age degeneracies in galaxy property inference [Yunus et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251205078Y/abstract). Similarly, the ERGO-ML framework employs conditional Invertible Neural Networks (cINNs) to directly infer unobservable galaxy assembly and merger histories from integral observables [Eisert et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.2199E/abstract).

NPE also facilitates hierarchical and population-level inference, where the objective is to characterize the distribution of galaxy properties directly rather than aggregating individual object fits. Frameworks like PopSED and tools built around the [[galsbi|GalSBI]] model utilize neural density estimation to infer stellar mass and Photometric redshift distributions $10^5$ to $10^6$ times faster than traditional SED modeling [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167...16L/abstract), [Fischbacher et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241208722F/abstract). Correspondingly, the PZFlow package enables the probabilistic forward modeling of galaxy catalogs to robustly validate photometric redshift posteriors [Crenshaw et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168...80C/abstract). Furthermore, models employing score-based diffusion over SPS parameter spaces can yield highly accurate photometric redshifts with minimal bias ($\sim 10^{-4}$) and a low outlier rate [Thorp et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240619437T/abstract). The accuracy of these models is proving highly competitive; comparative analyses of machine learning algorithms like Pt-SNE against traditional SED-fitting codes such as LePhare demonstrate that ML approaches can reduce errors in stellar mass estimation by up to $50\%$ for low-mass galaxies, while also significantly reducing overall bias [Asadi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220544A/abstract).

Despite these successes, the literature notes important tensions regarding emulator training and network architectures. The choice of the training set's prior distribution heavily influences the model's ability to fit rare or out-of-distribution objects. If the neural emulator's approximation uncertainties exceed the observational noise, it can result in catastrophic outliers and systematic errors in predicted colors [Mathews et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954..132M/abstract). Ensuring robust generalization to unique galaxy populations remains an active area of refinement for machine learning approaches to SED modeling.

## Large-Scale Structure and Galaxy Clustering

The spatial distribution of galaxies and dark matter across the cosmic web contains immense cosmological information, much of which resides in the non-linear regime. Traditional likelihood-based inference typically relies on analytical perturbation theory, which safely applies only to linear or quasi-linear scales ($k \lesssim 0.2\, h/\mathrm{Mpc}$). Neural posterior estimation (NPE), operating within the broader framework of [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] (SBI) or [[likelihood-free-inference|Likelihood-free inference]], bypasses these intractable analytical likelihoods by training neural density estimators on forward-modeled mock catalogs. 

The success of NPE relies fundamentally on the physical fidelity of these mocks. Phenomenological forward-modeling tools, such as [[galsbi|GalSBI]], are often employed to render realistic galaxy populations and their photometric properties [Fischbacher et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241208722F/abstract). To accelerate the generation of these realistic datasets, methods like CHARM utilize multi-stage neural spline flows to create mock halo catalogs directly from large-scale dark matter density fields, enabling SBI with highly efficient forward models [Pandey et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240909124P/abstract). Similarly, diffusion models are increasingly used to bridge the gap between dark matter simulations and high-fidelity galaxy distributions, significantly outperforming traditional halo occupation distribution (HOD) methods in generating realistic, probabilistic mock catalogs [Sether et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241205131S/abstract). A prominent implementation of NPE in large-scale structure is the SimBIG framework, which is designed to forward-model the galaxy distribution to match surveys like the SDSS-III BOSS CMASS sample, effectively capturing complex observational systematics like fiber collisions and survey masking [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...04..010H/abstract). By employing NPE, SimBIG can extract unbiased constraints on cosmological parameters—such as the matter density $\Omega_m$ and clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$—using conventional power spectrum multipoles pushed deep into the non-linear regime, up to $k_{\mathrm{max}} = 0.5\, h/\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...04..010H/abstract). Normalizing flows are also being utilized to model prior distributions for effective field theory (EFT) galaxy bias parameters directly from HOD mock catalogs, an approach that has notably improved full-shape constraints on single-field primordial non-Gaussianity in BOSS data [Ivanov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240213310I/abstract).

Furthermore, NPE naturally accommodates higher-order, non-Gaussian summary statistics whose likelihoods cannot be written down analytically. For instance, combining NPE with the wavelet scattering transform (WST) captures morphological information beyond two-point statistics, yielding seemingly improved constraints over standard perturbation theory approaches [Régaldo-Saint Blancard et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109h3535R/abstract). Quantifiable improvements are observed using marked power spectra, which weight the galaxy field by local density to upweight underdense or overdense regions; deploying NPE on these statistics yields up to a $1.2\times$ tightening in $\sigma_8$ constraints [Massara et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240404228M/abstract). Furthermore, NPE-based emulators are being extended to cosmic void statistics—such as the void size function and density profiles—which offer complementary cosmological information that is potentially more robust to baryonic effects [Lehman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205262L/abstract).

Pushing beyond summary statistics entirely, NPE and deep learning models facilitate field-level inference, where the raw 3D spatial positions and velocities of galaxies are analyzed directly. Using Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) operating on point clouds, field-level approaches can infer $\Omega_m$ with $12{-}13\%$ accuracy from the coordinates of just $\sim 1000$ galaxies in a $(25\, h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc})^3$ volume [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract). At larger scales, Bayesian field-level frameworks (e.g., BORG) sample the high-dimensional posterior of the primordial initial conditions to provide physically consistent, voxel-by-voxel realizations of the local large-scale structure [McAlpine et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540..716M/abstract). Additionally, the SELFI algorithm functions as a likelihood-free method to infer the primordial matter power spectrum directly from black-box numerical simulations [Leclercq et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.4237L/abstract). For weak lensing data, frameworks like DeepMass deploy U-Net architectures to reconstruct high-resolution dark matter maps directly from shear fields, significantly outperforming standard Wiener filtering [Jeffrey et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.5023J/abstract). Expanding on this, score-based generative models have been successfully applied to Bayesian weak lensing mass-mapping, demonstrating superior performance in reconstructing highly non-Gaussian dark matter distributions in fields like COSMOS compared to standard filtering techniques [Remy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...672A..51R/abstract).

Despite these successes, the literature highlights significant unresolved tensions regarding model misspecification and domain shift (the "sim-to-real" gap). Because NPE assumes the forward model perfectly represents the target data, inaccuracies in the simulation's subgrid astrophysics can severely bias the inferred parameters. For example, neural networks trained on one set of hydrodynamical prescriptions often fail to generalize to mock data produced by simulations run with different subgrid physics than those used for training [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract). Additionally, studies applying the WST to the BOSS data have noted that observational predictions can vary significantly depending on the specific normalizing flow architecture used, revealing a vulnerability to unmodeled observational imprints and architectural biases [Régaldo-Saint Blancard et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109h3535R/abstract). Addressing these systematic misspecifications remains an active area of research required to unlock the full potential of NPE for Stage-IV galaxy surveys.

## Strong Lensing and Dark Matter Substructure

Strong gravitational lensing offers a unique probe of small-scale dark matter structure, as sub-arcsecond perturbations in lensing arcs can reveal low-mass subhalos ($<10^{10} M_\odot$) even if they lack luminous baryonic counterparts [Wagner-Carena et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942...75W/abstract). However, inferring the properties of these sub-threshold perturbers is statistically challenging. The exact likelihood of the observed images is typically intractable because it requires marginalizing over an immense, high-dimensional latent space of all possible stochastic subhalo realizations along the line of sight [Coogan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201007032C/abstract), [Anau Montel et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.2746A/abstract).

To overcome this bottleneck, [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] (SBI) techniques—specifically Neural Posterior Estimation (NPE) and Truncated Marginal Neural Ratio Estimation (TMNRE)—are increasingly utilized. These [[likelihood-free-inference|Likelihood-free inference]] methods permit the implicit marginalization over thousands of unobserved subhalos. For example, as a proof-of-concept demonstrated on simulated mock lensing data, TMNRE has been successfully applied to constrain the warm dark matter (WDM) halo mass function cut-off scale by analyzing multiple lensing images simultaneously [Anau Montel et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.2746A/abstract). By training neural density estimators on large, forward-modeled datasets that incorporate realistic observational effects, such as point spread functions and noise, researchers can reliably recover the subhalo mass function (SHMF) across populations of hundreds of lenses [Wagner-Carena et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942...75W/abstract). Beyond traditional photometric images, these techniques are also applied to astrometric datasets; for instance, neural likelihood-ratio estimation has been employed to search for dark matter-induced lensing signatures in astrometry, demonstrating enhanced sensitivity over standard two-point correlation statistics [Mishra-Sharma et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MLS&T...3aLT03M/abstract).

Beyond substructure, NPE facilitates rapid, population-level inference of macroscopic lens parameters. For survey-scale data, such as mock images modeled after the Dark Energy Survey (DES), NPE has been shown to robustly recover 12-parameter lens mass models [Poh et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221105836P/abstract), [Poh et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...05..053P/abstract). Studies systematically comparing NPE to standard Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs) highlight that NPE yields posteriors that are consistently more accurate, precise, and better calibrated. In contrast, standard BNN implementations often exhibit systematic biases, particularly in source-light model parameters [Poh et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221105836P/abstract), [Poh et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...05..053P/abstract). Applying sequential NPE to real samples, such as the STRIDES strongly lensed quasars, allows individual lens models to be folded into a hierarchical Bayesian framework to derive population-level constraints. This approach recently constrained the population mean of the power-law elliptical mass distribution slope to $\gamma_{\rm lens} = 2.13 \pm 0.06$ [Erickson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241010123E/abstract). In parallel, models such as Bayesian Graph Neural Networks (BGNN) have been applied to estimate external convergence from photometric catalogs, aiding in precision Hubble constant inference from time-delay strong lensing [Park et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953..178P/abstract).

Accurately characterizing the lens mass relies heavily on the morphological source reconstruction of the background galaxy. Mis-specifying the source profile can artificially mimic or obscure the subtle signals of dark matter substructure. To address this, NPE has been combined with differentiable probabilistic programming, allowing for the simultaneous gradient-based optimization and posterior sampling of $\sim 100$ joint source and lens parameters within a single coherent model [Chianese et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496..381C/abstract). Furthermore, recent approaches replace restrictive parametric source models with data-driven priors. Using denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) as generative priors, it is possible to reconstruct complex, non-parametric source morphologies that remain consistent with both the noisy lensing observation and the learned distribution of realistic unlensed galaxies [Karchev et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221104365K/abstract).

## Characterizing Galaxy Clusters and High-Mass Probes

Galaxy clusters are the most massive gravitationally bound structures in the Universe, making them sensitive probes of the late-time growth of structure. However, inferring cosmological parameters from cluster surveys requires accurate mass calibration, a task historically plagued by complex projection effects, the presence of interloper galaxies, and non-equilibrium dynamics in the intracluster medium. Neural posterior estimation (NPE) and broader [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] techniques offer a compelling alternative to traditional analytical models, enabling the direct extraction of cluster masses and cosmological parameters from rich, high-dimensional observables.

**Dynamical Mass Estimation**  
In the optical regime, NPE is extensively used for dynamical mass estimation by leveraging the 3D projected phase-space distributions of cluster member galaxies. Using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on mock catalogs, it is possible to process the line-of-sight velocities and projected radial distances of galaxies to infer dynamical masses without explicitly identifying and removing interlopers [Kodi Ramanah et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.4080K/abstract). Recent architectures incorporating Deep Sets and conditional normalizing flows effectively model these arbitrary-sized point clouds, yielding residual corrections to the $M-\sigma$ relation with significantly reduced scatter compared to traditional virial estimators [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250720378W/abstract). Furthermore, approximate Bayesian CNNs can produce well-calibrated log-normal dynamical mass posteriors, recovering $68\%$ and $90\%$ confidence intervals to within $1\%$ of their true values—a critical requirement for accurately propagating mass uncertainties into cosmological constraints [Ho et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908..204H/abstract). Generally, NPE approaches significantly reduce scatter compared to traditional dynamical mass estimators, providing more robust cluster mass distributions [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250720378W/abstract).

**X-ray and Sunyaev-Zel'dovich Effect Observations**  
Beyond optical dynamics, NPE facilitates the analysis of multi-wavelength cluster photometry, such as X-ray surface brightness and the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect. Score-based generative models and diffusion models have been trained to ingest mock SZ and X-ray maps as conditional inputs, successfully reconstructing the underlying projected gas and dark matter density profiles to within $5\%$ accuracy [Hsu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241002857H/abstract). Furthermore, NPE permits [[likelihood-free-inference|Likelihood-free inference]] directly from survey observables, bypassing the intermediate step of estimating individual cluster masses. For instance, neural density estimation applied directly to X-ray count rates, hardness ratios, and redshifts can constrain the matter density $\Omega_m$ and the clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$ while avoiding the biases inherent to highly scattered scaling relations [Kosiba et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240906001K/abstract), [Kosiba et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A..46K/abstract). 

**Cluster Number Counts**  
At the population level, the abundance of galaxy clusters as a function of mass and redshift provides stringent constraints on structure formation. NPE methods seamlessly integrate cluster number counts into cosmological pipelines without relying on analytically intractable or computationally expensive likelihoods [Reza et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240920507R/abstract). Validations against simulated data for the upcoming Simons Observatory and real data from the Planck MMF3 cluster sample demonstrate that [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] yields posterior parameter constraints within $0.2\sigma$ of those derived from exact explicit likelihoods [Zubeldia et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250410230Z/abstract).

**Challenges in Generalization**  
Despite these successes, the sensitivity of neural density estimators to the training distribution poses a significant challenge. If the forward model is misspecified, the resulting inference can be severely biased. The literature highlights a major, unresolved tension regarding "domain shift" across different hydrodynamical simulation suites; for example, X-ray mass estimators trained on the Magneticum simulations exhibit up to a $40\%$ degradation in performance when evaluated on IllustrisTNG mocks due to differences in subgrid astrophysical feedback prescriptions [Ntampaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251009748N/abstract). Consequently, robustly adapting NPE models to bridge the simulation-to-reality gap via domain adaptation remains an active and critical priority for the robust analysis of next-generation cluster surveys.

## Differentiable Programming and Neural Emulators

Traditional Bayesian parameter estimation in cosmology relies on evaluating complex, computationally expensive forward models or likelihood functions across millions of sampling steps. For next-generation high-dimensional surveys, standard Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) and nested sampling routines can require thousands of CPU hours. The integration of neural network emulators and differentiable programming has emerged as a solution to this computational bottleneck, yielding speed-up factors ranging from $10^3$ to $10^6$ while maintaining accuracy consistent with traditional samplers [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract), [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110640H/abstract).

A primary driver of this acceleration is the replacement of costly physical simulations—such as Boltzmann solvers or perturbation theory integrations—with highly accurate neural emulators. For instance, the CosmoPower suite emulates matter, cosmic microwave background (CMB), and weak lensing power spectra, providing acceleration factors of up to $\mathcal{O}(10^4)$ over standard Boltzmann codes [Spurio Mancini et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.1771S/abstract). Furthermore, attention-based neural network emulators, specifically transformers, have been shown to reach sub-percent accuracy for predicting CMB temperature and polarization power spectra, fulfilling the stringent precision requirements of next-generation observatories like the Simons Observatory [Zhu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522574Z/abstract). Similarly, Transformer-based emulators have been developed to model high-dimensional multi-probe data vectors for the Rubin Observatory, providing high-accuracy predictions of cosmic shear and clustering while forecasting the growth-geometry split [Zhong et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240217716Z/abstract). These emulators recover cosmological constraints with sub-percent precision, maintaining errors well beneath the expected shot noise or cosmic variance of Stage-IV surveys. Similar surrogate models have been developed for Lagrangian perturbation theory (LPT), enabling the evaluation of real and redshift-space galaxy power spectra in approximately one millisecond [DeRose et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...04..056D/abstract). Such mapping techniques are continually advancing; for instance, the COMET emulator package has been extended to include the effects of massive neutrinos in galaxy clustering predictions, achieving sub-$0.01\%$ accuracy [Pezzotta et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250316160P/abstract).

The true power of these emulators is unlocked when they are integrated into end-to-end differentiable programming frameworks, largely driven by the JAX ecosystem. Automatic differentiation allows researchers to compute exact, analytical gradients of the likelihood or the emulated observables with respect to the input cosmological parameters. The JAX-COSMO library exemplifies this approach, providing a fully differentiable, GPU-accelerated cosmology pipeline that computes background evolution and power spectra [Campagne et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..15C/abstract). Because every step of the computation graph is differentiable, JAX-COSMO permits the exact, tuning-free calculation of Fisher matrices and seamlessly interfaces with gradient-based probabilistic programming languages.

Access to exact gradients enables the use of advanced sampling algorithms like Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) and the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS), which scale exceptionally well to high-dimensional parameter spaces where standard random-walk MCMC algorithms fail. By combining differentiable emulators with these samplers, frameworks like CosmoPower-JAX can perform joint cosmic shear and galaxy clustering analyses (so-called $3\times2$pt analyses) over highly complex spaces. In simulated Stage IV survey configurations with up to 157 model parameters, CosmoPower-JAX achieves converged posterior contours in mere days—a process that would conventionally require years of compute time using standard nested sampling [Piras et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..20P/abstract).

The paradigm of differentiable programming extends beyond summary statistics into field-level and pixel-level analysis, often complementing [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] and [[likelihood-free-inference|Likelihood-free inference]] pipelines. In the context of strong gravitational lensing, differentiable probabilistic programming allows convolutional neural networks (CNNs) generating source light profiles to be directly combined with exact physical lensing operators. This enables the simultaneous, gradient-based optimization of nearly $100$ lens and source parameters [Chianese et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496..381C/abstract), and can be extended to implicitly marginalize over stochastic latent variables like dark matter substructure [Coogan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201007032C/abstract). Ultimately, the shift toward fully differentiable, emulator-backed pipelines allows cosmologists to bypass intractable likelihoods and scale their Bayesian inference to the massive data volumes expected in the coming decade.

## Robustness, Validation, and Domain Adaptation

A fundamental limitation of Neural Posterior Estimation (NPE) and broader [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] (or [[likelihood-free-inference|Likelihood-free inference]]) frameworks is their reliance on the fidelity of the underlying forward model. When applied to observational data, these methods are uniquely susceptible to the "simulation-to-observation gap." Model misspecification—arising from an incomplete physical treatment of baryonic feedback, unmodeled observational noise, or incorrect survey selection functions—can lead to catastrophic failures in inference. If left undiagnosed prior to inference, even subtle misspecifications in galaxy bias or survey masks can induce parameter biases exceeding $2\sigma$ in the $(\Omega_m, \sigma_8)$ plane [Hoellinger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204443H/abstract).

A common manifestation of this challenge is *domain shift*, where a neural network trained on one cosmological hydrodynamical simulation suite systematically underperforms when applied to data governed by different subgrid physics. For example, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) that successfully infer cosmological parameters from one hydrodynamical simulation often fail to generalize to data from simulations run with different subgrid physics than those used during training [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract). This lack of robustness represents a major hurdle for extracting non-linear, small-scale information from galaxy clustering using deep learning [Régaldo-Saint Blancard et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109h3535R/abstract).

To mitigate the impact of subgrid physics uncertainties, one dominant strategy is to explicitly marginalize over massive, multi-parameter simulation ensembles. The CAMELS simulation suite was specifically designed for this purpose, providing thousands of realizations that simultaneously vary cosmology and astrophysical feedback parameters. While training models across the broad prior spaces of CAMELS or similar simulation arrays like the DREAMS project allows marginalization over astrophysical parameters within a specific modeling framework, this approach does not inherently confer robustness when tested on data governed by completely different subgrid physical prescriptions [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract), [Lin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..170L/abstract), [Rose et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240500766R/abstract). An alternative but related application of [[likelihood-free-inference|implicit likelihood inference (ILI)]] and emulators involves directly calibrating these complex hydrodynamical simulation parameters (e.g., stellar wind and black hole feedback efficiencies) against observed summary statistics like star formation rate densities and stellar mass functions [Jo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...67J/abstract). By doing so, simulations can be tuned to better reproduce reality before they are used as forward models for cosmological inference.

An alternative algorithmic strategy is *Domain adaptation*. Rather than relying strictly on parameter marginalization, domain-generalized neural networks attempt to align the latent representations of data originating from different distributions. Techniques such as minimizing the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) across different simulation suites force the network to extract only domain-invariant features. This approach has been shown to enhance cross-dataset robustness, reducing the $\chi^2$ error of out-of-domain predictions by nearly an order of magnitude [Roncoli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231101588R/abstract). Similar domain alignment loss functions have been utilized to bridge the gap between approximate light-cone mocks and computationally expensive N-body simulations [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240902256L/abstract). However, the overall efficacy of domain adaptation remains an active debate. In some specialized tasks, such as estimating cluster masses from X-ray profiles, deep networks engineered for domain adaptation still underperform compared to simple empirical scaling relations when confronted with out-of-domain test sets, highlighting the persistent danger of hidden biases [Ntampaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251009748N/abstract).

Finally, verifying the reliability of the inferred distributions requires rigorous coverage testing. Techniques such as Simulation-based calibration (SBC) are routinely employed to guarantee that the amortized posteriors are statistically well-calibrated—meaning that the true parameters fall within the $68\%$ credible interval exactly $68\%$ of the time across the prior volume. Performing both Bayesian validation and frequentist calibration tests is a standard and necessary requirement to ensure that NPE pipelines do not yield artificially tight, overconfident posteriors when applied to real astronomical data [Karchev et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.3881K/abstract), [Régaldo-Saint Blancard et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109h3535R/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While Neural Posterior Estimation (NPE) and broader [[simulation-based-inference|Simulation-based inference]] frameworks have demonstrated vast computational speedups over traditional Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods, scaling these techniques to the demands of 'Stage IV' cosmological surveys remains a primary challenge. Upcoming observatories such as the Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST) and the Euclid mission will require joint cosmological and astrophysical inference across parameter spaces with $100+$ dimensions. Performing a robust Multi-probe analysis—combining weak lensing, galaxy clustering, and cluster abundances—will severely test the dimensionality limits of current density estimators.

A critical open question in scaling NPE is how to overcome the "Sim-to-Real" gap, or domain shift. Neural networks trained on one suite of hydrodynamical simulations frequently fail to generalize to data produced by simulations run with different subgrid physical prescriptions [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract). The literature reflects an active debate regarding the optimal solution. Some researchers advocate using domain adaptation networks to mathematically align the latent representations of different simulations and observations [Roncoli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231101588R/abstract), [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240902256L/abstract). Alternatively, other groups investigate marginalizing over massive, parameterized suites of hydrodynamical simulations—such as the CAMELS or DREAMS projects—to span broad astrophysical feedback scenarios, though literature explicitly cautions that these models can still fail to generalize to entirely different subgrid physical frameworks [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract), [Lin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..170L/abstract), [Rose et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240500766R/abstract).

For Stage-IV surveys, successfully incorporating complex, unmodeled survey systematics into the forward model is strictly necessary. Studies indicate that neglecting variable survey depth, point spread function (PSF) variations, and complex window functions can bias the inferred amplitude of structure, $S_8$, by up to $5\%$ [Karim et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525..311K/abstract), [von Wietersheim-Kramsta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240415402V/abstract). Recently, NPE has been extended to handle spatially varying covariates directly, such as sky backgrounds and point spread functions, during the construction of probabilistic astronomical image catalogs [Patel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250300156P/abstract). A related challenge is determining how to optimally diagnose and mitigate systematic model misspecification prior to the extraction of cosmological parameters. Recent efforts have sought to use the inferred initial matter power spectrum as a field-level diagnostic tool to flag subtle theoretical or observational misspecifications before unblinding [Hoellinger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204443H/abstract).

Moving forward, the integration of diverse datasets into unified cosmological foundation models will likely rely heavily on Hierarchical Bayesian modeling. For example, population-level inference approaches, such as those implemented via [[galsbi|GalSBI]] or PopSED, shift the objective from fitting individual galaxies to rapidly recovering the overarching parameter distribution of billion-galaxy catalogs [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167...16L/abstract), [Fischbacher et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241208722F/abstract). Furthermore, NPE is demonstrating significant utility in multi-messenger and transient cosmology. Normalizing flows have been utilized to perform cosmological inference of the Hubble constant from gravitational wave events in seconds [Stachurski et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109l3547S/abstract). Similarly, simulation-based inference has been applied to Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) to robustly constrain the dispersion measure-redshift relation alongside host galaxy parameters [Konar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241007084K/abstract).

Finally, the optimal neural architecture for these highly complex tasks remains debated within the [[likelihood-free-inference|Likelihood-free inference]] community. While NPE via normalizing flows systematically outperforms Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs) in posterior calibration and precision for tasks like strong lensing [Poh et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221105836P/abstract), [Poh et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...05..053P/abstract), emerging research suggests that diffusion and score-based [[conditioned-models|Conditioned models]] may offer superior flexibility. These generative models are increasingly favored for handling the highly non-Gaussian, multimodal posteriors expected from next-generation multi-probe datasets [Leung et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240715703L/abstract), [Hsu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241002857H/abstract). Furthermore, improving the uncertainty calibration of these probabilistic networks remains a priority, prompting investigations into second-order optimization methods like natural gradient descent (e.g., the iVON algorithm) to enhance the robustness of variational inference based classification of radio galaxies [Mohan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251113224M/abstract).
