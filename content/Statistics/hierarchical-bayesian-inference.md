In astrophysics, **hierarchical Bayesian inference** is a rigorous statistical framework used to characterize population-level distributions by simultaneously modeling the physical properties of individual objects and their associated observational uncertainties. As modern [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]] produce increasingly massive [[large-galaxy-catalogs|Large galaxy catalogs]], traditional frequentist techniques—such as data stacking and standard [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]]—often fall short, systematically underestimating population variance and introducing parameter biases. The Hierarchical Bayesian Model resolves this by treating the true, unobserved properties of individual sources as latent variables drawn from a shared, overarching demographic distribution governed by hyperparameters. This mathematically unified approach correctly propagates complex, non-Gaussian measurement noise and survey [[selection-function|Selection function]] effects up to the population level, allowing astrophysicists to cleanly deconvolve instrumental dispersion from genuine astrophysical variations. Foundational literature and implementations (e.g., [Hobson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009bmc..book.....H/abstract), [Jasche et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407...29J/abstract)) have firmly established this multi-level methodology as the standard paradigm for modern demographic analysis.

The adoption of hierarchical frameworks has driven major revisions across [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], particularly in the characterization of [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]] and [[mass-distribution-and-functions|Mass Distribution and Functions]]. By rigorously accounting for heteroscedastic errors, Eddington bias, and survey incompleteness, hierarchical inference accurately recovers the true [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] of fundamental correlations, such as the Tully-Fisher or Kennicutt-Schmidt relations, which classical least-squares regression systematically underestimates. The methodology has also advanced [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]]; it has been demonstrated to successfully recover complex variations in the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] from composite mock spectra, resolving biases that plague traditional single-population fits [Dries et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.3500D/abstract). Furthermore, at the cosmological scale, joint hierarchical inference is deployed to reconstruct three-dimensional [[galaxy-density-fields|Galaxy density fields]] and photometric [[redshift-distribution|Redshift distribution]] estimates. This field-level inference effectively mitigates systematic biases in power spectrum recovery that previously plagued the analysis of large-scale structure [Jasche et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...779...15J/abstract).

Despite its statistical power, scaling hierarchical Bayesian models to accommodate the billions of objects expected in Stage IV surveys presents immense computational challenges. This article traces the field's historical evolution from frequentist point-estimation to full Bayesian forward modeling, detailing the mathematical treatment of observational noise and [[sample-selection-biases|Sample selection biases]]. It further explores how modern likelihood-free algorithms and machine learning—such as normalizing flows, Simulation-Based Inference, and Bayesian Graph Neural Networks—are currently being integrated to accelerate population-level [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]]. Finally, the article outlines the open questions facing the discipline, notably the critical risk of model mis-specification. In the era of high-precision cosmology, overly rigid priors or incorrect galaxy-dark matter bias models can propagate significant [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]] into weak lensing constraints and $H_0$ measurements, underscoring the ongoing need for robust model selection techniques.

## Introduction to Hierarchical Bayesian Frameworks

In astrophysics, observing populations—such as galaxies from [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]] like the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]]—requires statistical frameworks capable of disentangling intrinsic population variances from observational noise. Hierarchical Bayesian inference, commonly referred to as *multilevel modeling*, provides a mathematically rigorous approach to this problem. Unlike classical frequentist stacking or [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] applied to point-estimators, which systematically underestimate [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] and suffer from Eddington bias [Stone et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912...41S/abstract), [Obreschkow et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5500O/abstract), hierarchical models simultaneously infer the properties of individual objects and the demographic characteristics of the overall population [Leistedt et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.4258L/abstract).

The core of the hierarchical framework rests on treating the unobserved physical properties of individual objects as *latent variables*. Let a dataset consist of independent observations $D = \{d_1, d_2, \dots, d_N\}$ for $N$ astronomical objects. Each object $i$ is described by a set of latent parameters $\theta_i$ (e.g., stellar mass, redshift, or kinematic properties). Rather than assigning an independent, fixed prior to each $\theta_i$, the hierarchical model assumes these parameters are drawn from a shared underlying population distribution, $P(\theta_i | \alpha)$, which is governed by a set of hyperparameters $\alpha$. Additionally, the emergence of [[functional-data-analysis|Functional data analysis]] (FDA) as a framework for modeling populations of functions, such as light curves and spectra, has allowed astronomers to naturally handle functional data that is sparsely and irregularly sampled [Loredo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240814466L/abstract).

To close the Bayesian hierarchy, one must specify a prior distribution over the hyperparameters themselves, known as *hyperpriors*, denoted $P(\alpha)$. By applying Bayes' theorem, the joint *posterior distribution* of both the individual latent variables and the population hyperparameters is given by:

$$ P(\{\theta_i\}, \alpha | \{d_i\}) \propto P(\alpha) \prod_{i=1}^N P(d_i | \theta_i) P(\theta_i | \alpha) $$

In this structure, $P(d_i | \theta_i)$ is the individual likelihood function, which encapsulates the measurement noise, the [[selection-function|Selection function]], and specific instrumental effects for object $i$.

By sampling the full joint posterior distribution—often using Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithms or, increasingly, variational inference to handle high-dimensional spaces [Gunapati et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASA...39....1G/abstract)—researchers can naturally propagate non-Gaussian uncertainties from individual measurements up to the population level [Hahn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963...56H/abstract). The population distribution $P(\theta_i | \alpha)$ acts as an adaptive, data-driven prior that mathematically links the objects, pooling information to constrain noisy individual observations. 

This mathematical formulation is particularly critical for recovering the functional forms and variances of [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]] [Shetty et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.430..288S/abstract). For example, when evaluating relationships between galaxy luminosity and rotational velocity, classical least-squares regression systematically biases results due to complex heteroscedasticity and non-linear error propagation [Stone et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912...41S/abstract). Hierarchical models explicitly model these covariant uncertainties, providing a robust framework capable of separating genuine physical scatter from observational dispersion [de Souza et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.1928D/abstract). Furthermore, the hierarchical structure is instrumental in reconstructing photometric [[redshift-distribution|Redshift distribution]] estimates, where it enables the combination of photometric fluxes with spatial clustering cross-correlations while rigorously maintaining the probability distributions of the unobserved true redshifts [Sánchez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.2801S/abstract).

## Historical Evolution from Stacking to Hierarchical Inference

The historical development of population statistics in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] is characterized by a fundamental shift in how observational uncertainties are propagated. With the advent of massive [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]] such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey and the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]], the astronomical community required scalable methods to characterize the distributions and correlations of [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]]. Early methodologies relied heavily on [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] (MLE) and standard $\chi^2$ minimization to derive point estimates for individual objects. These point estimates were then aggregated to construct empirical distributions, such as the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] or demographic scaling laws.

To compensate for low signal-to-noise ratios in individual observations, researchers widely adopted frequentist stacking—co-adding spectra, photometry, or images across pre-defined bins of an observable proxy to extract a mean signal. While computationally efficient, frequentist stacking and sequential point-estimation systematically discard the non-Gaussian shape of individual posteriors and their covariant uncertainties. This information loss leads to severe statistical artifacts, most notably parameter bias. Similarly, using fixed point estimates to populate histograms inherently ignores heteroscedastic measurement errors, leading to Eddington bias—where objects with large uncertainties scatter preferentially into sparsely populated regions, artificially inflating the high-mass or high-luminosity tails of distribution functions [Obreschkow et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5500O/abstract).

The adoption of the Hierarchical Bayesian Model (HBM) framework resolved these tensions by restructuring the inference problem. Instead of treating individual parameter extraction and population-level demographic fits as disjointed steps, hierarchical inference models the data-generating process simultaneously at multiple levels, cleanly isolating intrinsic variance from observational noise—a distinction that first-order classical methods fail to make [Stone et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912...41S/abstract).

The practical superiority of this approach over historical methods has been quantitatively demonstrated across multiple subfields. In their analysis of luminous red galaxies, Shu et al. explicitly highlighted the advantage of hierarchical Bayesian inference over the frequentist stacking of noisy spectra. By utilizing the full velocity-dispersion likelihood function for each galaxy, they avoided the systematic parameter bias introduced by stacking and successfully detected the redshift evolution of the [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|Galaxy velocity dispersion function]] [Shu et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....143...90S/abstract). 

Furthermore, hierarchical approaches have rendered the arbitrary binning of data obsolete. Likelihood estimators adapted for HBMs natively accommodate small-number statistics, asymmetric errors, and non-detections, allowing for the continuous, unbinned recovery of fundamental distributions like the stellar mass function [Obreschkow et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5500O/abstract). By treating individual measurements as probability distributions rather than absolute truths, the transition to hierarchical inference has allowed extragalactic astrophysics to fully exploit deep survey data without the precision-limiting compromises of the stacking era.

## Treatment of Selection Effects and Observational Noise

Astronomical data are inevitably compromised by observational noise and systematic sample truncation. Traditional analysis methods often rely on extracting point estimates—such as maximum likelihood values for the properties of individual objects—which are subsequently binned or stacked. This approach struggles to properly account for [[sample-selection-biases|Sample selection biases]] and complex measurement uncertainties, frequently leading to distorted demographic inferences [Leistedt et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.4258L/abstract). Hierarchical Bayesian inference (HBI) overcomes these limitations by treating the true physical properties of individual sources as latent variables. By simultaneously inferring individual properties and the global population hyper-parameters, HBI rigorously propagates the full posterior of each object into the final demographic result.

A critical component of modeling survey data is the [[selection-function|Selection function]], $S(\mathbf{x})$, defined as the probability that a target with given intrinsic properties and spatial location is included in the sample. For flux-limited surveys, intrinsically brighter galaxies are systematically overrepresented at larger distances, a manifestation of Malmquist bias. Historically, astronomers applied ad hoc [[completeness-corrections|Completeness corrections]] or constructed [[volume-limited-samples|Volume-limited samples]] by discarding faint or distant galaxies. In a hierarchical framework, $S(\mathbf{x})$ is explicitly integrated into the generative forward model. The population likelihood evaluates the probability of observing the data given both the underlying physical distribution and the selection criteria, allowing the accurate recovery of the true [[mass-distribution-and-functions|Mass Distribution and Functions]] or [[luminosity-functions-and-scaling-relations|Luminosity Functions and Scaling Relations]] without unnecessarily discarding data. Fast approximate techniques, such as modified maximum likelihood estimators, have also been developed to emulate this hierarchical treatment of complex selection functions natively [Obreschkow et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5500O/abstract). 

Measurement errors do not merely blur astrophysical distributions; they systematically alter their shape. Because lower-mass or fainter objects are exponentially more numerous in the universe, symmetric measurement errors preferentially scatter more objects upward than downward. As mentioned, this Eddington bias artificially flattens the faint-end slope of the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and inflates the apparent abundance of rare, massive systems. By using the modeled population distribution as an adaptive prior, HBI effectively deconvolves the observational noise from the true distribution. It mitigates Eddington bias by naturally pulling noisy, low signal-to-noise measurements back toward the population mean.

Furthermore, hierarchical models readily incorporate non-detections, upper limits, and zero-inflated data, which are otherwise highly problematic. By explicitly modeling the detection probability alongside the parameter distribution, HBI frameworks yield unbiased estimates of population variance even in regimes dominated by upper limits or sparse counts, avoiding the statistical pitfalls that plague standard frequentist regression [de Souza et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.1928D/abstract). This capability is heavily utilized in studies of extended sources; for instance, HBI is uniquely suited to fit individual galaxy cluster pressure profiles while simultaneously estimating the population-averaged profile and its intrinsic heterogeneity [Castagna et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250907095C/abstract). Similarly, utilizing clustering-based redshift inference on photometric catalogs within this framework has enabled the mapping of the $z \sim 0$ galaxy luminosity function down to $\sim 10^6 L_\odot$ (the globular cluster scale), properly accounting for complex faint-end noise and completeness limits [Karademir et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.5467K/abstract).

## Reconstructing Galaxy Scaling Relations and Intrinsic Scatter

The accurate characterization of [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]] is frequently limited by the challenge of disentangling true astrophysical variance from observational noise. Classical frequentist methods, such as ordinary least squares or bisector fits, systematically underestimate the [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] of scaling relations because they fail to properly account for heteroscedastic measurement errors and non-linear error propagation [Stone et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912...41S/abstract), [Shetty et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.430..288S/abstract). 

Hierarchical Bayesian models overcome these limitations by simultaneously inferring the properties of individual galaxies alongside the hyper-parameters governing the population distribution. This approach allows for the rigorous incorporation of each object's full posterior distribution and the correlated uncertainties within the measurement covariance matrix. A prominent application of this framework is the determination of structural scaling laws for late-type galaxies. By modeling variables such as rotational velocity, physical size, and stellar mass, Bayesian estimates of intrinsic scatter have been shown to be approximately 25% more accurate than first-order classical methods, which routinely underestimate the true physical variance [Stone et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912...41S/abstract). Furthermore, to properly handle spatial structures in low signal-to-noise ensembles, frameworks such as the Population Profile Estimator (PoPE) use Gaussian processes to infer average spatial profiles and the underlying population variance, ensuring intrinsic scatter is preserved even when individual profiles cannot be tightly constrained [Farahi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....161...30F/abstract). 

At smaller scales, Bayesian object detection methods have proven critical for identifying localized secondary [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] and kinematic substructure within complex systems like the Ursa Minor dwarf spheroidal galaxy [Pace et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442.1718P/abstract). Similarly, within Galactic astronomy, the application of Bayesian inference inside tools like ASteCA allows researchers to robustly estimate open cluster morphology and ellipticities, far outperforming classical frequentist spatial fits [Pera et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024BAAA...65...94P/abstract).

Beyond continuous structural parameters, hierarchical inference is critical for modeling discrete or bounded observables. In the study of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] (GCs), Bayesian negative binomial and lognormal hurdle models successfully accommodate the non-negative integer nature of the data and the prevalence of dwarf galaxies lacking any GCs [de Souza et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.1928D/abstract), [Berek et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...955...22B/abstract). This framework yields unbiased estimates of the intrinsic scatter (e.g., $0.59_{-0.21}^{+0.30}$ dex for dwarf populations) [Berek et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...955...22B/abstract). While comparative baseline logistic regression initially suggested a 50% probability threshold for a dwarf galaxy to host a GC population at a stellar mass of $\log(M_*/M_\odot) = 6.8$ [Eadie et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926..162E/abstract), the full application of the hierarchical Bayesian lognormal hurdle model refined this threshold to $\log(M_*/M_\odot) = 6.996$ [Berek et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...955...22B/abstract). Moreover, properly modeling this intrinsic scatter reconciled the Milky Way's previously anomalous GC count within global scaling relations [de Souza et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.1928D/abstract).

Finally, hierarchical modeling provides a rigorous mechanism for testing the universality of fundamental laws. While standard galaxy formation theories frequently treat the Kennicutt-Schmidt (KS) star-formation relation as a fixed scaling law, hierarchical regression applied on a galaxy-by-galaxy basis reveals significant intrinsic variation in the KS parameters [Shetty et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.430..288S/abstract). In several local spiral galaxies, the inferred slopes are strictly sublinear—with a linear slope being explicitly excluded at the $2\sigma$ level specifically for M51—indicating that the physical relationship between gas surface density and star formation rate explicitly differs across individual systems.

## Stellar Populations and IMF Reconstruction

Hierarchical Bayesian inference provides a rigorous statistical framework for [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] (SPS), addressing the notorious degeneracies between age, metallicity, and dust attenuation that limit [[traditional-sed-fitting-techniques|Traditional SED fitting techniques]]. By fitting integrated broadband photometry and spectroscopy, hierarchical models allow for the consistent propagation of uncertainties to infer complex physical parameters, including the stellar mass and star formation history (SFH). Advanced codes like Prospector utilize ensemble Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling with flexible, non-parametric SFH priors to combine multi-wavelength data and robustly constrain the linear combination of stellar population components [Johnson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ascl.soft05025J/abstract). Due to the varying systematic assumptions underlying these spectral fits, Bayesian model comparison is increasingly important for discriminating between different panchromatic SED modeling recipes and accurately characterizing their uncertainties [Han et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..341..143H/abstract). Additionally, advanced forward modeling frameworks that explicitly connect intrinsic galaxy evolution physics directly with SED models can now recover true cosmological [[redshift-distribution|Redshift distribution]] estimates without the need for unrepresentative spectroscopic calibration data [Alsing et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..264...29A/abstract).

The adoption of hierarchical Bayesian modeling has led to significant revisions in our understanding of galaxy evolution. Standard textbook models relying on simplified UV and infrared calibrations have traditionally depicted a relatively young, star-forming universe. However, applying a 14-parameter Bayesian physical model to 3D-HST survey photometry revealed that inferred stellar masses are systematically $0.1$ to $0.3$ dex larger, and star formation rates are $0.1$ to $1$ dex lower, than those derived from classical indicators [Leja et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877..140L/abstract). These shifts are primarily driven by properly marginalizing over the emission from older stellar populations ($t > 100$ Myr). Consequently, Bayesian inference lowers the observed cosmic star formation rate density by $\sim 0.2$ dex, suggesting an older, more quiescent universe than previously established [Leja et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877..140L/abstract).

Hierarchical inference has also been instrumental in testing the universality of the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. While standard galaxy formation models typically treat the IMF as a fixed scaling law, Bayesian reconstructions from integrated light can trace underlying systematic variations across [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]]. By using a parametrized IMF prior to regulate direct inference, it has been demonstrated that classical fits assuming a single stellar population (SSP) artificially bias IMF slope estimates to higher values [Dries et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463..886D/abstract). When composite stellar populations (CSPs) are instead modeled hierarchically, the Bayesian evidence increases significantly, and the inferred IMF slopes converge to their true values. When applied to mock spectra generated with an IMF that varies with stellar velocity dispersion, these hierarchical models successfully reconstruct the true parameters, demonstrating their ability to test the universality of the IMF in future surveys [Dries et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.3500D/abstract).

A major bottleneck for Bayesian Stellar Population Synthesis is computational expense; analyzing millions of galaxies individually to map population demographics is often intractable. Recent machine learning approaches have accelerated this process by shifting inference directly to the population level. For instance, neural density estimation using normalizing flows can recover population-level demographics orders of magnitude faster than [[traditional-sed-fitting-techniques|Traditional SED fitting techniques]] [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167...16L/abstract). Similarly, hierarchical population inference using the PROVABGS framework accurately reconstructs the probabilistic [[mass-distribution-and-functions|Mass Distribution and Functions]] of surveys by statistically propagating individual mass uncertainties while simultaneously modeling complex selection effects [Hahn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963...56H/abstract). On an even grander scale, automated Bayesian inference systems, such as the Apsis pipeline developed for Gaia Data Release 3, demonstrate the feasibility of simultaneously classifying and inferring astrophysical parameters for billions of individual sources in massive surveys [Creevey et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..26C/abstract).

## Large-Scale Structure and Joint Redshift Inference

Hierarchical Bayesian inference (HBI) has driven a paradigm shift in the analysis of [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]], transitioning the field from relying solely on summary statistics—such as the two-point correlation function—to performing full field-level inference. In this framework, researchers simultaneously reconstruct the three-dimensional [[galaxy-density-fields|Galaxy density fields]], cosmological power spectra, and the redshift probability distributions of galaxy samples while rigorously propagating observational uncertainties.

**Density field reconstruction and power spectrum inference**

The mapping of the cosmic large-scale structure from discrete galaxy counts requires treating complex [[selection-function|Selection function]] geometries, noise, and non-linear gravitational evolution. HBI approaches this by modeling the galaxy distribution as a point process sampling an underlying continuous density field. Because the density posterior is highly non-Gaussian and the parameter space can span millions of dimensions, algorithms frequently employ Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) sampling to explore the lognormal-Poissonian density posterior efficiently [Jasche et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407...29J/abstract). Beyond the continuous density field itself, the Blackwell-Rao estimator (based on the Bayesian chain rule) can be strategically employed to connect the inferred three-dimensional density fields directly to dark matter halo detection probabilities [Merson et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.1340M/abstract). Furthermore, the identification of bound structures from these fields has been revolutionized by Bayesian cluster finders, which maximize filter likelihoods based on both luminosity and density to accurately determine cluster position and richness in datasets like the CFHTLS Archive Research Survey [Ascaso et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.420.1167A/abstract).

A major advantage of this joint inference is the ability to properly treat the degeneracies between the cosmological power spectrum and luminosity-dependent [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|Galaxy Bias and Clustering Statistics]]. Standard frequentist estimators often ignore the anti-correlation between bias amplitudes and power spectrum measurements, which can introduce systematic errors of up to $\sim 20\%$ across $k$-space [Jasche et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...779...15J/abstract). To accurately model these statistics despite complex survey masks, hierarchical models apply Gibbs sampling to jointly sample [[3x2pt-analysis|3x2pt analysis]] maps and tomographic power spectra [Alsing et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.4452A/abstract). For weak lensing applications, the hierarchical probabilistic inference of cosmic shear is further refined by marginalizing over intrinsic galaxy properties, utilizing flexible conditional probability distributions to learn morphological characteristics and properly propagate shape uncertainties [Schneider et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...807...87S/abstract). In the strong lensing regime, Bayesian inference is also vital for the inversion of strong gravitational lenses, allowing researchers to plausibly reconstruct complex lens mass distributions alongside source surface brightness profiles while propagating degeneracies [Brewer et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...637..608B/abstract). Similarly, the ARES framework extends joint modeling by self-consistently sampling unknown coefficients for foreground and target contamination, such as dust extinction, alongside the cosmological power spectrum [Jasche et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...606A..37J/abstract). Furthermore, recent advancements have introduced differentiable forward models, such as the HICOBIAN "fuzzy" cosmic-web model. This approach integrates non-linear, non-local, and stochastic biasing into the field-level inference to self-consistently recover initial conditions and late-time filamentary structures [Rosselló et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603969R/abstract). To rigorously validate these advanced methodologies, initiatives like the 'Beyond-2pt' mock data challenge explicitly benchmark field-level inference and higher-order statistics—such as voids and density-split clustering—against traditional two-point estimators, ensuring the robust extraction of additional cosmological information [ThBeyond-2pt Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990...99B/abstract).

**Joint Redshift Inference**

Accurately characterizing the [[redshift-distribution|Redshift distribution]], $N(z)$, of photometric samples is critical for cosmological parameter estimation, particularly for weak lensing analyses. Traditional photometric redshift methods are highly susceptible to biases from unrepresentative spectroscopic training sets. Early foundational work in this domain was established by tools like the BPZ code, a Bayesian photometric redshift estimator that uses empirical prior probabilities and mathematical marginalization to dramatically improve photometric redshift reliability over simple [[template-fitting|Template fitting]] [Benítez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ascl.soft08011B/abstract). Modern HBI approaches further mitigate training biases by fusing photometric color information with spatial clustering cross-correlations against a reference spectroscopic sample—a technique known as clustering redshifts [Ménard et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1303.4722M/abstract), [Sánchez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.2801S/abstract). 

By utilizing a hierarchical model, clustering-based redshift estimation allows for a data-driven derivation of $N(z)$ that overcomes gaps in the spectroscopic prior coverage and does not rely exclusively on the assumed spectral energy distributions of the sources [Sánchez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.2801S/abstract). Extensive validation of clustering-based redshift inference against spectroscopic samples is essential to ensure prediction accuracy and properly control for limitations introduced by uncertain galaxy bias [Rahman et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.3500R/abstract). The HBI framework computes full posterior distributions for both individual galaxy redshifts and the population-level $N(z)$, passing these uncertainties forward into later cosmological analyses [Leistedt et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.4258L/abstract). Additionally, to handle these statistical distance measures properly in clustering statistics, a rigorous decontamination formalism has been introduced for angular correlation functions. This technique corrects for sample contamination using the observed cross-correlations inherent in probabilistic distance measurements, ensuring structure clustering signals remain unbiased [Awan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...890...78A/abstract).

However, the literature indicates an ongoing challenge: the accuracy of clustering redshifts is fundamentally limited by uncertainties in the galaxy-dark matter bias. Mis-specifying the redshift-dependent bias model can cause biases in the redshift prediction that subsequently propagate into $10\%$ to $30\%$ systematic errors in the weak lensing convergence power spectrum [Rau et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.4768R/abstract). Bayesian model selection techniques are therefore actively employed alongside HBI to detect and penalize such systematic modeling errors.

## Machine Learning and Simulation-Based Inference

In the era of large-scale [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]], the high dimensionality of hierarchical Bayesian models often renders traditional Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling computationally intractable. To bypass these bottlenecks, the astrophysical community has increasingly integrated machine learning and simulation-based inference techniques into Bayesian workflows, enabling accelerated population-level [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]].

When explicit likelihood functions (which are often incorrectly assumed to be Gaussian) are unavailable or computationally prohibitive to evaluate, Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) offers a likelihood-free alternative. ABC relies on forward-generative models and distance metrics to compare mock data with observations. For example, ABC has successfully constrained halo occupation distribution (HOD) parameters by matching [[summary-statistics|Summary statistics]] such as the two-point correlation function and the [[group-multiplicity-function|Group multiplicity function]], bypassing the need for an explicit likelihood [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract). Frameworks like COSMOABC implement Population Monte Carlo ABC to perform parameter inference on systems such as galaxy cluster number counts [Ishida et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&C....13....1I/abstract). Similarly, modern simulation-based inference (SBI) pipelines, such as GalSBI, constrain phenomenological galaxy population models directly from photometric observables by comparing simulated outputs to real data [Fischbacher et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241208722F/abstract). To cohesively analyze multiple cosmic probes simultaneously, advanced architectures such as the AUM (Abundance, Un-lensed and Multi-lensed) framework provide a unified modeling scheme that directly conducts cosmological simulation-based inference by seamlessly combining galaxy clustering, galaxy abundance, and galaxy-galaxy lensing signals [More et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ascl.soft08002M/abstract). In the realm of large-scale structure, redshift-weighted power spectrum estimators and simulation-based tools are continuously refined to optimize constraints on primordial non-Gaussianity ($f_{\mathrm{NL}}$) directly from galaxy surveys without the efficiency loss associated with arbitrary redshift binning [Mueller et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.4160M/abstract).

To further accelerate inference, variational inference recasts Bayesian updating as an optimization problem. It approximates the exact posterior $P(\theta|x)$ with a tractable parameterized distribution $Q(\theta)$ by minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence:
$$ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \parallel P) = \int Q(\theta) \log \frac{Q(\theta)}{P(\theta|x)} d\theta $$
This approach is highly parallelizable and yields parameter constraints—such as cluster masses derived from weak lensing—that are competitive with MCMC but at a fraction of the computational cost [Gunapati et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASA...39....1G/abstract). For structural decomposition across massive galaxy catalogs, the community is moving toward JAX-accelerated Bayesian packages, such as `pysersic`, to rapidly determine galaxy properties like Sérsic profiles natively via Bayesian inference [Pasha et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JOSS....8.5703P/abstract). The demand for high-performance computing has additionally spurred the development of specialized libraries like `Gravity.jl`, a fast Julia-based tool designed specifically for hierarchical lens modeling and scalable Bayesian inference on cosmological parameters [Lombardi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A.346L/abstract).

Advanced neural density estimation leverages normalizing flows to directly model population distributions. The PopSED framework, for instance, trains normalizing flows to minimize the Wasserstein distance between synthetic photometry and observed data. This allows for the reliable recovery of population-level redshift and stellar mass distributions $10^5$ to $10^6$ times faster than [[traditional-sed-fitting-techniques|Traditional SED fitting techniques]] [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167...16L/abstract). In the realm of Bayesian image deconvolution, researchers are similarly leveraging advanced generative architectures; diffusion model priors and score-based likelihoods are actively used to recover intricate structural details in faint galaxies with unprecedented fidelity, an application referred to as finding "echoes in the noise" [Adam et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....169..254A/abstract). Neural networks like Self-Organizing Maps (SOMBI) can also be directly combined with Bayesian inference to automatically identify parameter clusters and complex multi-dimensional relations in massive, unstructured cosmological data sets [Frank et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...595A..75F/abstract). Additionally, when predicting parameters such as multi-wavelength cluster mass, model-agnostic Importance Permutation values are successfully deployed alongside machine learning to systematically determine which specific observables most significantly contribute to the accuracy of the overall mass estimation [Cohn et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.1575C/abstract).

For highly structured spatial data, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) provide a powerful tool for hierarchical inference. By representing galaxies along a line of sight as nodes in a graph, Bayesian Graph Neural Networks (BGNNs) can accurately estimate the probability distribution of weak lensing convergence ($\kappa$). When embedded in a hierarchical model for strong gravitational time-delay cosmography, BGNNs efficiently ingest all available sightline information, outperforming standard galaxy number count matching and reducing the systematic contribution to the $H_0$ error budget to well under 1% [Park et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953..178P/abstract). Moving beyond traditional optical survey observables, simulation-based and hierarchical techniques are actively expanding; recent studies, for instance, have demonstrated the first applications of HBI to analytical toy models of the LISA massive black hole binary population to successfully characterize [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]], as well as using hierarchical Bayesian models to infer the delay time distribution of supermassive black hole binary mergers by elegantly combining gravitational wave detections with galaxy merger rate distributions [Fang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.5120F/abstract).

Despite these immense computational speedups, there remains an inherent tension: neural networks are approximative by nature and can introduce biases if their errors are not carefully tracked. To preserve the statistical rigor of hierarchical models, explicit Bayesian error propagation is required. By capturing a network's approximation errors during its validation phase, inferences can be mathematically debiased. This calibration ensures that neural-network-based parameter estimation propagates uncertainties correctly, allowing it to serve as a robust surrogate for exact theoretical likelihoods without corrupting the final posterior [Grandón et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022OJAp....5E..12G/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions for Stage IV Surveys

As observational cosmology enters the era of Stage IV [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]], hierarchical Bayesian models face unprecedented challenges. As highlighted in modern astrostatistics reviews, the overarching 21st-century statistical challenge remains the mathematically robust treatment of heteroscedastic measurement errors and the modeling of complex, non-Gaussian probability distributions across increasingly massive astrophysical populations [Feigelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AnRSA...8..493F/abstract). While providing an optimal statistical framework for population analysis, these models must simultaneously address the demanding requirements of **computational scalability** and the rigorous mitigation of **systematic errors** when applied to [[large-galaxy-catalogs|Large galaxy catalogs]] containing billions of objects.

Standard Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms struggle with the millions of dimensions required for full-survey forward modeling and 3D field-level inference. To bypass these bottlenecks, the field is increasingly transitioning toward gradient-assisted sampling, such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, driven by highly optimized and differentiable forward models [Rosselló et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603969R/abstract). In parallel, likelihood-free methods like Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) and simulation-based inference provide vital scalable alternatives for extracting cosmological parameters when exact likelihoods are mathematically intractable [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract), [Ishida et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&C....13....1I/abstract).

Machine learning architectures are also being rapidly integrated into Bayesian pipelines to accelerate inference. Variational inference [Gunapati et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASA...39....1G/abstract), normalizing flows for spectral [[template-fitting|Template fitting]] [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167...16L/abstract), and Bayesian Graph Neural Networks for weak lensing convergence [Park et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953..178P/abstract) can reduce computational costs by orders of magnitude. However, because neural networks are approximative by nature, a critical open question is how to execute robust Bayesian error propagation through these emulators. Failure to account for a neural network's intrinsic approximation errors can severely bias the resulting cosmological parameter inference [Grandón et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022OJAp....5E..12G/abstract).

In the high-precision regime of Stage IV surveys, statistical uncertainties will plummet, making **model mis-specification** a dominant source of systematic error. Hierarchical models are notoriously sensitive to their assumed functional forms and parameterized priors. For example, failing to correctly specify the redshift-dependent galaxy-dark matter bias model can propagate relative errors of $10\%$ to $30\%$ directly into the weak lensing convergence power spectrum [Rau et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.4768R/abstract). Similarly, unmodeled parameter degeneracies and mis-specified error models in individual galaxy rotation curves can falsely mimic variations in fundamental physical constants, serving as a cautionary tale against using overly rigid or simplistic priors [Li et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646L..13L/abstract). Addressing these risks necessitates objective Bayesian model selection to detect and penalize modeling biases before they corrupt downstream constraints. This ongoing effort relies on rigorous statistical validation techniques, such as multidimensional Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests, to verify that the inferred posteriors are truthful, unbiased representations of actual constraints across multi-parameter spaces [Harrison et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451.2610H/abstract). Furthermore, when facing complex nuisance parameters, researchers must carefully select their estimation methods; for example, analyses mapping $H_0$ in megamaser systems frequently compare formal Bayesian marginalization against frequentist profile likelihoods to determine the most statistically robust treatment of uncertain system velocities [Barua et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250211998B/abstract).

Finally, it remains an open question whether advanced hierarchical techniques—particularly full 3D field-level inference—can resolve standing cosmological tensions. While self-consistently modeling non-linear peculiar velocities via Bayesian frameworks yields tighter constraints on the Hubble constant $H_0$, recent findings suggest these dynamics only shift $H_0$ by $\sim 0.4 \pm 0.5$ km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$. This indicates that unaccounted-for local velocity fields are unlikely to explain the broader $H_0$ tension [Tsaprazi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250208385T/abstract). Future progress will rely heavily on leveraging hierarchical inference for joint [[3x2pt-analysis|3x2pt analysis]] and multi-messenger cosmology, demanding meticulous coordination between physical theory, the control of [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]], and scalable computational architecture. Extending this framework to multi-messenger astronomy marks a critical evolution; for instance, the ICAROGW Python package exemplifies modern hierarchical pipelines explicitly designed to infer astrophysical population properties and cosmology using noisy, heterogeneous observations from both gravitational waves and large galaxy surveys [Mastrogiovanni et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...682A.167M/abstract). Pushing these interdisciplinary boundaries even further, hierarchical Bayesian inference has recently been applied to exoplanet populations to infer global Galactic dark matter halo parameters from the subtle heat flow induced by theoretical dark matter annihilation within overheated exoplanets [Benito et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...07..038B/abstract).
