In observational astrophysics and cosmology, the accuracy of [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] is fundamentally limited by a combination of [[noise-statistics|Noise statistics]] and non-random [[estimation-bias|Estimation bias]]. While random noise decreases with increasing sample size (scaling proportionally to $1/\sqrt{N}$), systematic errors and [[parameter-bias|Parameter bias]] remain independent of survey volume. These effects arise from instrumental limitations, algorithmic assumptions in data pipelines, and intrinsic [[selection-function|Selection function]] constraints. Foundational phenomena such as Malmquist bias—where flux limits preferentially select bright objects and skew the derivation of the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] or peculiar velocities [Landy et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...391..494L/abstract)—and Eddington bias, where asymmetric [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] artificially inflates the apparent abundance of massive, high-redshift objects, demand rigorous [[error-estimation-in-astrophysics|Error estimation in astrophysics]]. Without proper mitigation, these non-random uncertainties contaminate the census of astronomical populations and can severely distort [[cosmological-inference|Cosmological inference]].

The interpretation of [[low-redshift-large-scale-structure|Low-redshift large-scale structure]] relies heavily on our ability to separate true cosmological signals from observational artifacts. As the field transitioned to massive three-dimensional [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] datasets, mitigating kinematic and geometric systematic biases became paramount [Peacock et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001defi.conf..221P/abstract). For instance, [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]] induced by the peculiar velocities of galaxies break spatial isotropy, artificially altering the measured [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] [Strauss et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996astro.ph.10033S/abstract). Concurrently, complex survey visibility masks, stellar contamination, and the observer's own peculiar motion can mimic primordial clustering patterns in the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]]. Furthermore, the growing reliance on broadband photometry introduces severe [[source-redshift-uncertainty|Source redshift uncertainty]] and [[catastrophic-photo-z-outliers|Catastrophic photo-z outliers]]. Using single-point photometric redshift estimates systematically skews parameter constraints, necessitating the use of the full [[probability-density-function-cosmology|Probability density function (cosmology)]] and a [[hierarchical-bayesian-model|Hierarchical Bayesian Model]] to recover unbiased measurements. Similar systematic challenges plague galaxy cluster mass estimation and weak lensing, where departures from idealized physical models—such as triaxiality or non-isothermal density profiles—routinely bias inferences of the Hubble constant ($H_0$).

This article explores the historical evolution and modern theoretical framework for identifying, modeling, and marginalizing over these observational systematics. It covers the mathematical formalisms of classic selection biases, kinematic distortions, and contamination in [[higher-order-clustering-statistics|Higher-Order Clustering Statistics]] and gravitational lensing observations. Furthermore, it highlights modern mitigation strategies, including machine learning-driven dimensionality reduction and unified covariance frameworks designed to simultaneously project [[super-sample-covariance|Super-sample covariance]] and non-Gaussian errors. Finally, the article outlines critical open questions in the era of next-generation [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]]. Chief among these are whether observed [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|Galaxy Bias and Clustering Statistics]] anomalies reflect true physical breakdowns of the Cosmological Principle or unmodeled survey geometries, how to break degeneracies between galaxy bias evolution and [[redshift-distribution|Redshift distribution]] at highly non-linear scales, and whether more complex intracluster medium models can resolve the enduring $10-15\%$ discrepancies between independent $H_0$ calibrations.

## Introduction to Systematic Uncertainties in Astronomy

In [[cosmological-inference|Cosmological inference]] and [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] is divided into two distinct categories: random statistical noise, which scales down with the inverse square root of the sample size, and non-random [[estimation-bias|Estimation bias]], which remains regardless of survey volume. Systematic errors arise from instrumental artifacts, data pipeline assumptions, and observational selection effects. If uncorrected, these biases fundamentally skew the interpretation of cosmic populations and the inference of cosmological parameters. Accurate [[error-estimation-in-astrophysics|Error estimation in astrophysics]] now requires either forward-modeling these effects or explicitly marginalizing over them during parameter extraction.

A primary source of systematic bias is the survey [[selection-function|Selection function]]. Because flux-limited surveys preferentially detect intrinsically bright objects at large distances, they inherently suffer from Malmquist bias. Traditional corrections often assume large-scale homogeneity, but failing to account for the true complexity of the selection function can generate artificial structural artifacts, such as spurious large-scale overdensities [Landy et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...391..494L/abstract). Furthermore, the [[completeness-limit|Completeness limit]] of a survey is rarely a sharp boundary. For example, redshift-dependent light loss due to isophotal dimming and seeing systematically biases the faint end of the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]], artificially mimicking [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]] [Dalcanton et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...495..251D/abstract). At high redshifts, steeply falling [[peak-mass-function|Peak mass function]] distributions combined with measurement scatter lead to Eddington bias—sometimes termed "Eddington's demon." In this regime, asymmetric scatter causes numerous lower-mass galaxies to be erroneously placed into high-mass bins, artificially inflating the inferred abundance of massive galaxies [Obreschkow et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5500O/abstract), [Krishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251113708K/abstract).

Data reduction pipelines, instrumental artifacts, and calibration models also introduce systematic shifts. Instrumental artifacts, such as magnitude-dependent offsets due to detector saturation in [[panchromatic-surveys|Panchromatic surveys]], require empirical calibration to avoid biasing [[summary-statistics|Summary statistics]] of bright objects like AGB stars [Lian et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...564A..84L/abstract). Similarly, significant systematic errors in space-based photometry, such as those found in Spitzer microlensing parallax measurements, can shift values higher than Galactic model predictions, particularly for faint events [Koshimoto et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....160..177K/abstract). In large photometric surveys, using single-point estimates (such as the most-likely redshift) for photometric redshifts introduces significant systematic biases in the estimation of [[nuisance-parameters-in-cosmology|Nuisance parameters in cosmology]] [Wittman et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...700L.174W/abstract). Consequently, the consensus has shifted toward utilizing the full [[probability-density-function-cosmology|Probability density function (cosmology)]], $p(z)$, to propagate uncertainties consistently [Leistedt et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881...80L/abstract). Similarly, relying on standard starburst templates to correct for dust attenuation in quiescent [[galaxy-populations|Galaxy populations]] fails to capture deviations in the UV spectral slope, which can lead to large systematic errors (factors of a few) in [[galaxy-property-distributions|Galaxy property distributions]] such as star formation rate estimates [Bell et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...577..150B/abstract). Furthermore, recent JWST deep observations of $5-25\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ [[differential-galaxy-counts|Differential galaxy counts]] reveal systematic discrepancies with semi-analytical models, which likely stem from how mid-IR aromatic features are treated in these models [Stone et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...972...62S/abstract).

Finally, systematic errors frequently stem from overly simplistic physical models. In [[amico-galaxy-cluster-catalogue|AMICO galaxy cluster catalogue]] studies, assuming spherical, isothermal profiles for the intracluster medium ignores density and temperature inhomogeneities—which often follow [[log-normal-distribution|Log-normal distribution]] models [Kawahara et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...687..936K/abstract). This model mismatch leads to a systematic $10-15\%$ underestimate of the [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] for the Hubble constant ($H_0$) when derived from the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect [Kawahara et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674...11K/abstract). Similarly, in strong gravitational lensing, the widespread use of [[cosmic-shear|Cosmic shear]] mass models rather than complex, pixel-based source reconstructions introduces biases in time-delay measurements, introducing a ${\sim}9\%$ systematic bias in $H_0$ [Cao et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RAA....22b5014C/abstract), [Eid et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250908227E/abstract). In [[low-redshift-large-scale-structure|Low-redshift large-scale structure]] analyses, the failure to account for observational systematics—such as survey visibility masks, stellar contamination, or the observer's peculiar motion—can induce false clustering signals that severely compromise constraints on the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] and linear growth rate [Agarwal et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...03..021A/abstract), [Bahr-Kalus et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...11..027B/abstract).

## Historical Evolution of Survey Statistics

The historical progression of [[low-redshift-large-scale-structure|Low-redshift large-scale structure]] analysis is characterized by a transition from two-dimensional angular projections to fully three-dimensional [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] datasets, a shift that necessitated increasingly sophisticated [[statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Statistical methods in astrophysics]] to mitigate systematic errors. Early statistical reviews established the fundamental framework for distinguishing non-random hierarchical clustering from random Poisson distributions on scales ranging from 0.1 to 100 [[comoving-correlation-length|Comoving correlation length]] [de Vaucouleurs et al. (1971)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1971PASP...83..113D/abstract), building upon early foundational work interpreting [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|Galaxy Bias and Clustering Statistics]] and the radio properties of galaxies [Sandage et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975gaun.book.....S/abstract). Observational constraints on dark matter from nucleosynthesis and rich cluster dynamics also laid early groundwork for understanding the [[galaxy-matter-cross-correlation|Galaxy-matter cross-correlation]] [Trimble et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ARA&A..25..425T/abstract).

Early observational constraints relied heavily on 2D imaging datasets such as the [[shane-wirtanen-catalog|Shane-Wirtanen catalog]] and the [[apm-galaxy-survey|APM Galaxy Survey]]. Because these early surveys lacked radial depth information, cosmologists utilized the [[angular-correlation-and-clustering|Angular Correlation and Clustering]] function $w(\theta)$ to infer three-dimensional spatial clustering. However, there were fundamental limitations of using low-order [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] estimators to provide a complete statistical description of observed clustering patterns [Coles et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992scma.conf...57C/abstract). For instance, the inversion of $w(\theta)$ in the APM survey successfully recovered the high-wavenumber 3D [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]], yielding a slope of $P(k) \propto k^{-1.25}$ [Baugh et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993MNRAS.265..145B/abstract). Early attempts to constrain structure formation models using higher-order statistics, such as applying the [[reduced-bispectrum|Reduced bispectrum]] to the Shane-Wirtanen catalog, demonstrated the mathematical feasibility of these estimators but were ultimately limited by systematic uncertainties in identifying the relationship between the galaxy distribution and the underlying mass distribution [Fry et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985ApJ...292..395F/abstract). Systematic comparisons of correlation function estimators demonstrated that the accurate determination of power-law slopes and large-scale homogeneity trends depends significantly on the choice of the estimator [Pons-Bordería et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...523..480P/abstract). To probe fluctuations at higher redshifts where standard optical galaxy surveys were limited, researchers successfully utilized radio sources and the X-ray background as alternative [[tracers|Tracers]] [Lahav et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996AcHPh..69..388L/abstract).

The field advanced dramatically with the advent of [[spectroscopy-surveys|Spectroscopy surveys]], which provided the radial distances required to construct genuine 3D maps of [[void-probability-function|Void probability function]] areas, filaments, and clusters. The [[cfa-redshift-survey|CfA Redshift Survey]] pioneered this effort, explicitly shifting the focus toward determining the statistical properties of 3D galaxy clustering [Huchra et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ASPC....5...41H/abstract). Subsequently, the [[las-campanas-redshift-survey|Las Campanas Redshift Survey]] provided 26,418 redshifts over 700 square degrees, allowing for the mapping of voids and walls on scales of 5000 km/s and detailed studies of the power spectrum in both 2D and 3D [Shectman et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...470..172S/abstract). Early efforts developed advanced techniques to treat arbitrary survey geometry and sampling in power spectrum estimation [Vogeley et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995wfsd.conf..142V/abstract). Furthermore, redshift surveys proved essential for characterizing the low surface brightness [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] and drawing links to star formation [Geller et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001PASP..113..405G/abstract). However, securing reliable radial distances brought new challenges. Early analytical studies of [[cross-correlation-analysis|Cross-correlation analysis]] techniques highlighted the risk of overestimating confidence levels when determining galaxy redshifts from noisy spectra [Heavens et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993MNRAS.263..735H/abstract). Furthermore, statistical analysis of early [[h-i-selection|H I selection]] standards revealed that redshift accuracy intrinsically increases with morphological type, from early- to late-type galaxies [Baiesi-Pillastrini et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986A&A...163....1B/abstract). In more modern local applications, efforts to create homogenized catalogs, such as the Gravitational Wave Galaxy Catalogue (GWGC) for sources within 100 Mpc, highlight the enduring necessity of correcting for [[completeness-limit|Completeness limit]] boundaries and [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] in local follow-up searches [White et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011CQGra..28h5016W/abstract). Additionally, using redshifts as distance proxies introduced [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]] caused by the peculiar velocities of galaxies. Estimators for the correlation function in redshift space, $\xi(s)$, and the power spectrum, $P(k)$, had to be meticulously remodeled to separate true cosmological clustering from dynamically induced anisotropies [Strauss et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996astro.ph.10033S/abstract). To obtain unbiased galaxy power spectra on linear scales, novel approaches incorporated Linear Maximum Likelihood and Karhunen-Loève compression [Hamilton et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..3604H/abstract).

As surveys scaled up to unprecedented volumes—culminating in massive datasets like the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]], which measured well over 100,000 redshifts across roughly 2,000 square degrees—simple [[shot-noise|Shot noise]] assumptions became entirely inadequate [Peacock et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001defi.conf..221P/abstract). During this era, researchers reviewed the progression toward Gpc-scale power spectrum measurements and their connection to structure at high redshifts [Guzzo et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002NuPhS.110..115G/abstract). The analysis of these massive datasets required a rigorous mathematical framework to derive cosmological constraints, specifically focusing on the precise treatment of redshift-space distortions and galaxy bias [Percival et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007LNP...720..157P/abstract). Statistical estimators had to account for complex survey geometries, visibility masks, and the [[selection-function|Selection function]]. To accurately quantify measurement uncertainties, analysts developed robust internal [[error-estimation-in-astrophysics|Error estimation in astrophysics]] techniques, transitioning to spatial jackknife [[sample-variance|Sample variance]] methods calibrated against mock catalogs to capture the true impact of [[supercluster-catalogue|SuperCluster Catalogue]] structures [Norberg et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.2435N/abstract).

Furthermore, the massive data volumes of the 2dFGRS era allowed for the high signal-to-noise measurement of the [[three-point-correlation-function|Three-point correlation function]]. Analyses of the [[reduced-three-point-correlation-function|Reduced three-point correlation function]], $Q_3 \sim \zeta/\xi^2$, unequivocally demonstrated that galaxies are biased tracers of the dark matter field, detecting a non-zero quadratic bias parameter at high significance [Gaztañaga et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.364..620G/abstract). Consequently, modern statistical estimators must now jointly model the power spectrum and bispectrum, explicitly marginalizing over selection effects to prevent severe parameter degradation and systematic shifts in [[cosmological-inference|Cosmological inference]] [Xavier et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...04..037X/abstract), [Agarwal et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...03..021A/abstract). Comprehensive textbooks, such as updated texts on dark energy and galaxy clusters [Sparke et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006gaun.book.....S/abstract), alongside foundational references for the statistical properties of the galaxy population [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract), document these theoretical and observational milestones.

## Malmquist and Eddington Biases

Malmquist and Eddington biases are two foundational [[estimation-bias|Estimation bias]] types that distort the inferred physical properties of [[galaxy-populations|Galaxy populations]]. They occur when observational selection limits and measurement uncertainties interact with steeply falling intrinsic population distributions, such as the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] or [[halo-multiplicity-function|Halo multiplicity function]].

**Malmquist Bias** 
Malmquist bias primarily affects [[sample-selection-biases|Sample selection biases]] in flux-limited samples. Because the observed flux $f$ of an object depends on its intrinsic luminosity $L$ and luminosity distance $d_L$ as $f = L / (4\pi d_L^2)$, surveys complete only down to a minimum flux threshold will preferentially detect intrinsically brighter objects at greater distances. If uncorrected, this effect systematically skews the derived [[luminosity-functions-and-scaling-relations|Luminosity Functions and Scaling Relations]]. 

For distance indicators like the Tully-Fisher relation, Malmquist bias introduces severe systematic errors in peculiar velocity estimates. Observational errors and line-of-sight effects can artificially inflate the scatter in [[scaling-relation-models|Scaling relation models]], potentially leading to 0.6 mag discrepancies in distance modulus estimates [Burstein et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ApJ...343...18B/abstract). Inadequate analytical corrections for Malmquist bias using lognormal distance errors have been shown to generate spurious large-scale peculiar velocity flows, potentially producing artifacts as large as the purported "Great Attractor" signal [Landy et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...391..494L/abstract). Standard empirical methods to mitigate Malmquist bias include constructing [[volume-limited-samples|Volume-limited samples]]—by enforcing a strict absolute magnitude limit $M_{\rm lim}$ over the analyzed volume—or applying completeness corrections such as the [[v-vmax-test|V/Vmax test]]. Alternatively, Bayesian analytical models that incorporate the survey's exact [[selection-function|Selection function]] can correct for distance bias without relying on assumptions of large-scale spatial homogeneity [Landy et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...391..494L/abstract).

**Eddington Bias**
Eddington bias, conversely, is driven by [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] rather than strict detection thresholds. It arises when stochastic scatter alters the observed shape of an underlying, non-uniform distribution. Because astronomical distributions like the galaxy mass function decline steeply at the high-mass end, there are vastly more low-mass objects than high-mass ones. Consequently, even symmetric observational errors result in an asymmetric net scatter: many more low-mass objects scatter upward into high-mass bins than high-mass objects scatter downward.

This "demon" effect [Obreschkow et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5500O/abstract) has profound implications for cosmological inference. A notable modern debate involves the apparent overabundance of massive, high-redshift galaxies observed by JWST. Initial interpretations suggested these galaxies required unphysically high baryon-to-stellar-mass conversion efficiencies (approaching or exceeding $100\%$), which would challenge standard $\Lambda$CDM structure formation models [Krishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251113708K/abstract). However, recent modeling demonstrates that this tension is largely explained by Eddington bias. Systematic uncertainties in stellar mass estimates interact with the extremely steep high-mass tail of the high-redshift halo mass function, causing lower-mass systems to scatter upward and artificially inflate the observed massive galaxy counts [Krishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251113708K/abstract). Moreover, for high-redshift galaxy surveys, [[cosmic-variance|Cosmic variance]] is the dominant source of uncertainty—except at the very brightest end—driven primarily by the variance in the underlying dark matter halo population [Trapp et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.2401T/abstract). Furthermore, early studies into the physical processes influencing high-redshift structure successfully employed [[galaxy-hlqso-cross-correlation-function|Galaxy-HLQSO cross-correlation function]] absorbers to map mass distributions independent of luminous galaxy selection limits [White et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997euvl.conf..219W/abstract).

Mitigating Eddington bias requires statistical techniques that avoid simple binning. Modern approaches utilize unbinned [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] frameworks, which allow for the simultaneous inference of the underlying generative distribution function while natively accounting for observational scatter, small number statistics, and the survey's selection function [Obreschkow et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5500O/abstract).

## Redshift-Space Distortions and Peculiar Velocity Biases

In cosmological surveys, the observed position of a galaxy in redshift space is shifted from its true real-space position by its peculiar velocity along the line of sight. This mapping from real space to redshift space breaks the assumption of isotropy, introducing apparent anisotropies in the measured [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] and [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]]. These phenomena, collectively known as [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]] (RSD), must be accurately modeled to recover the true underlying matter distribution and to extract robust cosmological parameter constraints [Strauss et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996astro.ph.10033S/abstract). The transformation to the redshift-space coordinate $\mathbf{s}$ from the real-space position $\mathbf{r}$ is given by:
$$ \mathbf{s} = \mathbf{r} + \frac{v_\parallel}{a H(a)} \hat{\mathbf{r}} $$
where $v_\parallel$ is the line-of-sight peculiar velocity, $a$ is the scale factor, and $H(a)$ is the Hubble parameter. 

On small, non-linear scales ($\lesssim 10 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$), the random peculiar velocities of galaxies within virialized dark matter halos cause structures to appear elongated along the line of sight, an artifact known as the "Fingers-of-God" effect. Modeling this suppression of clustering power relies heavily on accurately quantifying the [[pairwise-velocity-dispersion|Pairwise velocity dispersion]]. However, the assumption that galaxies perfectly trace the underlying dark matter velocity field is complicated by environmental and evolutionary stochasticity. For example, spectroscopic observations of cluster members demonstrate that star-forming galaxies exhibit a [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|Galaxy velocity dispersion function]] $17 \pm 4\%$ greater than that of passive galaxies [Bayliss et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837...88B/abstract). Such population-dependent velocity biases systematically alter dynamical mass estimates and require explicit calibration against simulations to avoid biasing mass-observable scaling relations [Bayliss et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837...88B/abstract), [Ferragamo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020EPJWC.22800011F/abstract). 

On larger, linear scales, the coherent infall of galaxies toward overdense regions flattens apparent structures along the line of sight (the Kaiser effect). While this regime provides a powerful probe of the [[f-8|fσ8]] linear growth rate of structure, the extraction of [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] values is highly sensitive to statistical biases. Errors in the reconstruction of the local [[galaxy-density-fields|Galaxy density fields]] can systematically skew measurements of both the growth rate and the Hubble constant [Turner et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526..337T/abstract). Statistical analyses of cosmic velocity fields using galaxy-number weighting are inherently biased and strongly dependent on the tracers chosen for the velocity statistics [Seto et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...520..409S/abstract). However, the evolution of the growth rate of structure can be measured efficiently by applying optimal redshift weighting that provides differential information across the survey volume without the need for discrete redshift shells [Ruggeri et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.2698R/abstract). Furthermore, the inclusion of wide-angle effects, light-cone evolution, and luminosity-dependent [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|Galaxy Bias and Clustering Statistics]] must be properly marginalized—often using advanced statistical frameworks like joint power spectrum-bispectrum analyses—to prevent severe degradation in parameter constraints [Xavier et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...04..037X/abstract), [Agarwal et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...03..021A/abstract).

In addition to the motion of the source galaxies, the peculiar motion of the observer introduces its own anisotropic distortions. If uncorrected, this observer motion induces a spurious clustering signal known as the Kaiser-Rocket effect. In idealized, cosmic-variance-dominated [[all-sky-surveys|All-sky surveys]] peaking at $z \sim 1.5$, this manifests as a $>5\sigma$ systematic effect [Bahr-Kalus et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...11..027B/abstract). While less dominant in surveys with realistic visibility masks, the Kaiser-Rocket effect remains a critical systematic that can significantly bias the inference of [[skewness-cosmology|Skewness (cosmology)]] ($f_{\mathrm{NL}}$). It is therefore essential that modern [[galaxy-clustering-and-redshift-estimation|Galaxy Clustering and Redshift Estimation]] pipelines explicitly model or project out these kinematic observer biases prior to cosmological inference [Bahr-Kalus et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...11..027B/abstract). Similar kinematic biases occur in our own Milky Way; in Galactic studies, assuming circular gas orbits when calculating kinematic distances introduces systematic distortions at the position of spiral arms, potentially creating spurious density structures in the Milky Way map [Gómez et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....132.2376G/abstract).

## Systematics in Galaxy Clustering and Higher-Order Statistics

Because galaxies inhabit dark matter halos, they serve as biased tracers of the underlying cosmic density field. Extracting the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] from these surveys requires rigorous treatment of [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|Galaxy Bias and Clustering Statistics]] as well as careful modeling of survey geometry. Additionally, joint analyses of large-scale structure with CMB and [[background-galaxy-distribution|Background galaxy distribution]] signals have served as vital tools for testing the Cosmological Principle and modeling stochastic biasing [Lahav et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999elss.conf..190L/abstract). The statistical physics perspective emphasizes comparing galaxy clustering patterns to the theoretical Harrison-Zeldovich spectrum and CMB fluctuations to test theoretical predictions against observation [Pietronero et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhyA..306..395P/abstract). Analysis of early [[large-galaxy-catalogs|Large galaxy catalogs]] data demonstrated that robust [[angular-correlation-and-clustering|Angular Correlation and Clustering]] estimates require rigorous masks for seeing, reddening, and bright stars to ensure large-scale measurements remain free from internal systematics [Scranton et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...579...48S/abstract). Unmodeled [[selection-function|Selection function]] components, luminosity-dependent bias evolution, and complex angular visibility masks can severely contaminate the clustering signal, leading to $>2\sigma$ artificial shifts in fundamental parameters such as $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$ [Hoellinger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204443H/abstract). Similarly, large-scale systematic errors can induce spurious bulk flows in peculiar velocity studies; for example, an apparent dipolar residual compared to standard SFD reddening maps was initially investigated as a map error, though the study ultimately concluded it was actually driven by a small systematic error in the measured $B-V$ colors of early-type galaxies rather than the maps themselves [Hudson et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999PASP..111...57H/abstract). Additionally, as noted previously, kinematic observer biases masquerading as true clustering can mimic signals of [[skewness-cosmology|Skewness (cosmology)]]. While techniques like mode projection can mitigate the large-scale clustering excess driven by stellar contamination [Leistedt et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1857L/abstract), breaking the inherent degeneracies between galaxy bias evolution and cosmic growth requires higher-order statistics. To formally test the statistical isotropy of the galaxy distribution and its sensitivity to survey sky coverage, the multipole vector approach—initially applied to the CMB—provides a robust formalism [Zunckel et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvD..84d3005Z/abstract).

At the two-point level, the amplitude of density fluctuations is perfectly degenerate with the linear bias. Introducing the [[reduced-bispectrum|Reduced bispectrum]] or the [[three-point-correlation-function|Three-point correlation function]] ($\zeta$) breaks this degeneracy. To constrain primordial non-Gaussianity specifically, three complementary approaches—bispectrum analysis, rare object abundance, and the large-scale clustering of halos—are heavily relied upon to break degeneracies and identify systematic errors [Verde et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AdAst2010E..64V/abstract). Joint analyses of the power spectrum and bispectrum allow for the linear growth rate to be measured to $10\%$ precision even after completely marginalizing over selection biases [Agarwal et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...03..021A/abstract). As an alternative, skew-spectra offer a simpler method than the full bispectrum for capturing tree-level redshift-space information and its dependence on galaxy bias and the logarithmic growth rate [Schmittfull et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...03..020S/abstract). In configuration space, the [[reduced-three-point-correlation-function|Reduced three-point correlation function]], $Q_3 \sim \zeta/\xi^2$, is frequently utilized to isolate non-gravitational contributions to clustering. Using the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]], measurements of $Q_3$ ruled out the simple linear, unbiased tracer hypothesis at the $9\sigma$ level by detecting a non-zero quadratic bias parameter of $c_2 = b_2/b_1 = -0.34^{+0.11}_{-0.08}$ [Gaztañaga et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.364..620G/abstract). 

Pushing clustering analyses to smaller, highly non-linear scales introduces a severe modeling challenge. Expanding the model to capture non-linear structure formation requires the introduction of numerous [[nuisance-parameters-in-cosmology|Nuisance parameters in cosmology]]; marginalizing over these can paradoxically degrade constraints on inflationary runnings back to the level of CMB-only measurements [Li et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862..137L/abstract). To circumvent rigid scale cuts ($k_{\rm max}$), modern pipelines increasingly adopt a "theoretical error" likelihood that treats modeling uncertainties as a smoothly varying [[analytical-covariance-matrix|Analytical covariance matrix]] term [Chudaykin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220406591C/abstract). Recent advances in field-level inference also allow systematic errors to be diagnosed directly from the inferred initial matter power spectrum before parameters are evaluated [Hoellinger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204443H/abstract). 

Finally, deriving clustering statistics implicitly assumes that the survey volume represents a "fair sample" of a self-averaging universe. However, the literature is currently in tension regarding the [[homogeneity-scale|Homogeneity scale]] of statistical distributions. While standard $\Lambda$CDM models predict a rapid transition to homogeneity, several analyses report [[multifractal-analysis|Multifractal analysis]] patterns ($D = 2.1 \pm 0.2$) and large-amplitude density fluctuations persisting up to $100-200\ h^{-1}{\rm Mpc}$ [Amendola et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997Fract...5..635A/abstract), [Sylos Labini et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...508...17S/abstract). For instance, analysis revealed that the conditional density in the 2dFGRS exhibits power-law behavior up to $40\ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$, raising significant questions about finite-size effects on correlation estimators [Vasilyev et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...447..431V/abstract). These observations contribute to an ongoing debate regarding the crossover scale toward true large-scale homogeneity and the persistence of power-law correlations on vast scales [Sylos Labini et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AIPC..822..294S/abstract). To objectively quantify whether observed clustering is artificially dominated by isolated superstructures, spatial jackknife resampling is widely employed to evaluate [[sample-variance|Sample variance]] within the survey geometry [Norberg et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.2435N/abstract).

## Photometric Redshift Errors and Catastrophic Outliers

Large [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]] increasingly rely on broadband photometry rather than time-intensive spectroscopy to estimate galaxy distances. However, the resulting [[source-redshift-uncertainty|Source redshift uncertainty]] introduces significant non-random errors into [[cosmological-inference|Cosmological inference]]. Historically, the development of empirical photometric-redshift relations up to $z \sim 1.2$ established the ensemble distribution as a vital tool for quantifying cosmological parameters and their measured errors [Brunner et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...516..563B/abstract). Using single-point photometric redshift estimates—such as the mode or mean of a distribution—leads to systematically biased parameter estimation, particularly for dark energy constraints derived from weak [[cosmic-shear|Cosmic shear]] and large-scale clustering [Wittman et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...700L.174W/abstract). 

A persistent challenge in photometric redshift estimation is the presence of [[catastrophic-photo-z-outliers|Catastrophic photo-z outliers]]—galaxies whose estimated redshifts deviate from their true values by large, non-Gaussian margins. These catastrophic errors frequently arise because the [[spectroscopy-surveys|Spectroscopy surveys]] used to train empirical models or calibrate priors are often small and non-representative of the fainter parent photometric samples [Quadri et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...725..794Q/abstract). Furthermore, degeneracies in broadband colors can cause algorithms to assign high probabilities to incorrect redshift solutions. Even a small percentage of catastrophic outliers can severely contaminate tomographic bins. Uncorrected systematic errors in tomographic redshift bin widths and distributions, $N(z)$, have been shown to systematically shift cosmological parameters; for instance, the clustering amplitude parameter $S_8 \equiv \sigma_8 (\Omega_m/0.3)^{1/2}$ can be biased by up to $7\%$ if redshift errors are improperly modeled [Hasan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.1029H/abstract).

To mitigate these biases, sophisticated hybrid modeling approaches have been developed that abandon point estimates entirely in favor of utilizing the full [[probability-density-function-cosmology|Probability density function (cosmology)]], $p(z)$. Standard [[template-fitting|Template fitting]] provides interpretable physical models but lacks the flexibility to adapt to instrumental photometry errors or incomplete spectral templates. As a solution, inference pipelines now frequently combine template fitting with flexible machine-learning frameworks to properly handle outliers and reconstruct unbiased target distributions (further discussed in *Modern Mitigation*) [Leistedt et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881...80L/abstract). 

In the absence of complete and representative spectroscopic calibration sets, spatial [[cross-correlation-analysis|Cross-correlation analysis]] offers a robust empirical method to constrain redshift distributions. Because galaxies cluster strongly with their physical neighbors, the spatial cross-correlation between a well-characterized spectroscopic sample and an unknown photometric sample can trace the true redshift distribution of the latter, even deep into the non-linear regime [Quadri et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...725..794Q/abstract), [Schmidt et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431.3307S/abstract). Modern clustering estimators can explicitly incorporate the contamination probabilities between redshift bins, recovering the true two-point correlation functions and their [[analytical-covariance-matrix|Analytical covariance matrix]] without bias, thereby eliminating the need to assume a perfectly clean tomographic sample [Awan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...890...78A/abstract).

## Biases in Galaxy Cluster and Lensing Observations

The accurate derivation of cosmological parameters from [[amico-galaxy-cluster-catalogue|AMICO galaxy cluster catalogue]] samples and [[cosmic-shear|Cosmic shear]] observations is frequently limited by systematic errors stemming from oversimplified physical or geometric models.

**Intracluster Medium and Sunyaev-Zeldovich Biases**
The combined analysis of the Sunyaev-Zeldovich (SZ) effect and X-ray emission is a powerful probe for the cosmic distance scale, yet it is highly susceptible to systematic deviations from the standard spherical isothermal $\beta$-model. Density and temperature inhomogeneities in the intracluster medium (ICM), which observations and hydrodynamic simulations indicate follow a lognormal probability density function, couple with departures from strict isothermality to systematically underestimate the spectroscopic temperature relative to the true emission-weighted temperature [Kawahara et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...687..936K/abstract). Consequently, the Hubble constant ($H_0$) derived from joint SZ/X-ray analyses in the [[planck-sz-cluster-catalog|Planck SZ cluster catalog]] is systematically underestimated by $10\%$ to $15\%$ [Kawahara et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674...11K/abstract). Furthermore, projecting the inverse-Compton scattering and X-ray emission along the line of sight for triaxial clusters introduces a systematic uncertainty, though statistical upper bounds show the error on the mean [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] of $H_0$ is limited to within $14\%$ at $99.7\%$ confidence for large cluster samples, rather than this value being a typical expected penalty [Sulkanen et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...522...59S/abstract). Earlier research similarly highlighted how projection effects and random orientations of triaxial clusters introduce substantial systematic shifts in the derivation of $H_0$ [Sulkanen et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997AAS...191.8507S/abstract). To combat selection biases, there has been a longstanding historical effort to create unbiased galaxy cluster samples using combined optical and X-ray selection [Gioia et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000cucg.confE...8G/abstract). Additionally, systematic interpretations of mass profiles rely on comprehensive summaries of dark matter distributions in clusters, and considerations of more exotic phenomena such as the impact of cosmic strings on structure formation [Roos et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph..5625R/abstract). Beyond galaxy clusters, systematics in the Cepheid distance scale, including sky subtraction and amplitude biases, significantly impact the local calibration of the Hubble constant [Owens et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021hst..prop16627O/abstract).

**Dynamical Mass Estimation**
Dynamical [[mass-bias|Mass bias]] in clusters is also sensitive to the specific galaxy populations used as kinematic tracers. Spectroscopic surveys demonstrate that the [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|Galaxy velocity dispersion function]] of star-forming cluster galaxies is $17 \pm 4\%$ greater than that of passive member galaxies [Bayliss et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837...88B/abstract). This population-dependent velocity bias necessitates explicit calibration to prevent systematic shifts in mass-observable [[scaling-relation-models|Scaling relation models]]. Furthermore, spatial correlations among offsets in the Fundamental Plane can lead to over $10\%$ contamination in peculiar velocity power spectra, necessitating explicit modeling of environment-dependent galaxy properties [Joachimi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454..478J/abstract). Moreover, when defining cluster masses, the standard reliance on [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] mapping to convert between different mass definitions can induce $>2\sigma$ systematic biases; mass conversions based on halo sparsity statistics have been shown to successfully reduce this systematic bias below statistical error thresholds [Gayoux et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251027505G/abstract). Additionally, low-frequency radio halo observations with LOFAR demonstrate that the distribution of clusters with and without radio halos is not compatible with a single mass-power scaling relation, suggesting complex dynamical biases in how massive clusters are identified and modeled [Cuciti et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...680A..30C/abstract).

**Strong and Weak Gravitational Lensing**
In galaxy-galaxy strong lensing and cluster lens modeling, the widespread use of simple elliptical power-law mass distributions introduces measurable systematic errors. Such model mismatches generate a spurious fitted external shear (typically $\gamma_{\rm ext} \approx 0.015$) and underestimate time delays by roughly $5\%$, which translates to a $\sim 9\%$ systematic bias in $H_0$ [Cao et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RAA....22b5014C/abstract). Utilizing spatially extended giant caustic-crossing arcs provides stringent constraints that can mitigate these issues, though they require complex pixel-based source reconstructions. These advanced reconstructions successfully correct resolution-limited assumptions in local mass models and significantly reduce arc model residuals compared to traditional point-source constraints [Eid et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250908227E/abstract), [Eid et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990..196E/abstract).

In the weak lensing regime, measurements of cosmic shear and the [[shear-correlation-function|Shear correlation function]] are fundamentally limited by [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] in source galaxy shapes, photometric redshift errors, and shear calibration. For higher-order topological statistics, such as weak-lensing Minkowski functionals, unmitigated observational systematics can degrade cosmological parameter constraints by a factor of a few [Shirasaki et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...786...43S/abstract). Finally, robust [[cosmological-inference|Cosmological inference]] requires high-fidelity analytical or simulated covariance matrices; ignoring complex survey geometries when computing these matrices has been shown to misestimate [[cosmic-variance|Cosmic variance]] terms by approximately $10\%$ [Reischke et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241006962R/abstract), [Reischke et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A.124R/abstract).

## Modern Mitigation: Hierarchical Bayesian Models and Machine Learning

Modern [[cosmological-inference|Cosmological inference]] demands robust mitigation of [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] and systematic biases. Early theoretical reviews anticipated this necessity, noting the profound impact of Bayesian approaches on precision cosmology and the analysis of multivariate datasets [Feigelson et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003sca..book.....F/abstract). Modern astrostatistics continues to address the growing challenge of treating heteroscedastic measurement errors, leading to the rapid adoption of [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]] techniques. Traditional methods often rely on point estimates or ad-hoc post-processing, but consensus has shifted toward utilizing full probability distributions, self-consistent generative models, and machine learning to explicitly forward-model data contamination.

As noted previously, relying on single-point photometric redshift estimates introduces severe [[parameter-bias|Parameter bias]] in derived cosmological constraints. A state-of-the-art solution involves a [[hierarchical-bayesian-model|Hierarchical Bayesian Model]] that combines physical [[template-fitting|Template fitting]] with flexible machine-learning components. By optimizing the full posterior distribution, these hierarchical models can simultaneously correct spectral energy distribution templates, recalibrate broadband photometry, and infer the underlying [[redshift-distribution|Redshift distribution]] without requiring perfectly representative spectroscopic training data [Leistedt et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881...80L/abstract). Frameworks such as ARES utilize joint Bayesian block-sampling to self-consistently infer 3D density fields while marginalizing over unknown coefficients of foreground templates and stellar contamination [Jasche et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...606A..37J/abstract). Furthermore, unsupervised dimensionality reduction techniques are increasingly applied alongside inference codes to identify and repair [[catastrophic-photo-z-outliers|Catastrophic photo-z outliers]] that would otherwise invisibly contaminate large [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Galaxy Surveys and Catalogs]] [Hovis-Afflerbach et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908..148H/abstract). Additionally, advanced population modeling tools now employ flexible score-based diffusion models to forward-model galaxy characteristics, accounting for parameter degeneracies and selection effects simultaneously [Alsing et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..274...12A/abstract).

For population statistics, such as the high-redshift galaxy mass function, traditional data binning subject to asymmetric scatter leads to severe Eddington bias. Modified [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] frameworks bypass binning entirely, accurately recovering the underlying distribution functions while natively accounting for [[selection-function|Selection function]] components, non-detections, and small-number statistics [Obreschkow et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5500O/abstract). 

Identifying systematic effects—such as visibility masks, redshift errors, and spatial selection functions—prior to final parameter inference is now possible using field-based implicit likelihoods. Techniques akin to approximate Bayesian computation allow researchers to diagnose misspecified nuisance parameters by examining the inferred initial [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]]. This ensures that subtly misspecified models, which can lead to parameter shifts exceeding $2\sigma$, are identified before committing to a final cosmological inference [Hoellinger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204443H/abstract).

Precision cosmology also hinges on the accurate modeling of covariance matrices, which must incorporate Gaussian, non-Gaussian, and [[super-sample-covariance|Super-sample covariance]] terms. Unified frameworks (e.g., OneCovariance) are now utilized to project these terms across multiple observables simultaneously, ensuring consistency across weak lensing and galaxy clustering probes [Reischke et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241006962R/abstract). However, the literature reveals ongoing tension regarding the estimation of covariance matrices from finite simulation suites. While non-linear shrinkage estimators can significantly reduce sampling noise, they may systematically overestimate the resulting parameter error bars; therefore, highly populated ensembles of approximate mock catalogs are often still required to guarantee unbiased inference [Gouyou Beauchamps et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.226G/abstract). Ultimately, the impact of these mitigation strategies on survey forecasting is routinely evaluated via [[fisher-matrix-analysis|Fisher matrix analysis]], which has demonstrated that selection effects and galaxy bias must be explicitly modeled and marginalized to preserve constraints on structure growth and the primordial power spectrum [Agarwal et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...03..021A/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions in Precision Cosmology

As observational astrophysics enters the era of next-generation large-volume galaxy surveys (e.g., Euclid and the [[desi-bright-galaxy-survey|DESI Bright Galaxy Survey]]), the unprecedented statistical power of these datasets shifts the primary bottleneck of precision cosmology entirely to systematic error mitigation. Performing robust [[cosmological-inference|Cosmological inference]] requires navigating several open challenges, from the estimation of complex covariance matrices to the forward modeling of field-based systematics. 

A foundational challenge lies in constructing precision covariance matrices that accurately capture Gaussian, non-Gaussian, and [[super-sample-covariance|Super-sample covariance]] terms. Estimating these matrices from finite suites of $N$-body simulations inherently introduces sampling noise. While non-linear shrinkage estimators can successfully reduce stochastic parameter shifts caused by this noise, they have been shown to systematically overestimate the resulting cosmological parameter error bars [Gouyou Beauchamps et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.226G/abstract). To address these inconsistencies, the field is moving toward unified software frameworks, which explicitly standardize the computation of cross-covariances for joint [[3x2pt-analysis|3x2pt analysis]].

Another active debate is how to optimally extract information from the non-linear, high-$k$ regime of the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] without inducing severe biases. Including small-scale clustering data theoretically tightens constraints on the inflationary spectral running. However, accurately modeling this regime requires marginalizing over hundreds of [[nuisance-parameters-in-cosmology|Nuisance parameters in cosmology]] to account for baryonic feedback, non-linear [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]], and complex galaxy bias. The inclusion of these parameters can degrade the resulting constraints back to the level of CMB-only measurements [Li et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862..137L/abstract). To circumvent the need for arbitrary scale cuts ($k_{\max}$), modern frameworks are exploring "theoretical errors" that smoothly penalize the [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] as theoretical uncertainties grow at smaller scales [Chudaykin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220406591C/abstract). Breaking the exact degeneracies between galaxy bias evolution and redshift distribution recovery at these scales remains a critical open problem [Schmidt et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431.3307S/abstract).

To control angular systematics—such as those induced by stellar contamination and survey visibility masks—there is a paradigm shift away from post-hoc correction toward field-based implicit likelihood inference. Recent simulator-based algorithms allow researchers to infer the initial matter power spectrum directly from the data, diagnosing misspecified [[selection-function|Selection function]] models *before* cosmological parameters are estimated. Uncorrected, these subtle misspecifications can induce $>2\sigma$ biases in the $(\Omega_m, \sigma_8)$ plane [Hoellinger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204443H/abstract). Future mitigation strategies will rely on forward-modeling angular systematics through highly detailed random catalogs that reproduce target detection probabilities; simulations show that if the mask is perfectly characterized, the underlying power spectrum can be recovered to sub-percent accuracy on large scales [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251120856E/abstract).

Finally, next-generation analyses must rigorously model wide-angle and relativistic observational effects that manifest on the largest scales. Disentangling these vast, scale-dependent observational systematics—such as the kinematic biases induced by the observer's motion—from primordial physics will increasingly demand [[multi-tracer-analysis|Multi-tracer analysis]] techniques to simultaneously suppress [[cosmic-variance|Cosmic variance]] and isolate the true cosmological signal.
