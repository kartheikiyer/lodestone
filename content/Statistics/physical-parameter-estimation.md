## Article

In observational astronomy, physical parameter estimation is the rigorous mathematical process of deriving intrinsic, often unobservable physical properties—such as stellar masses, cosmological density parameters, or gravitational wave source characteristics—from raw observational data. This discipline bridges the gap between theoretical physical models and observed phenomena by employing advanced [[statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Statistical methods in astrophysics]]. Historically rooted in frequentist approaches like [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] and simple grid-based $\chi^2$ minimization, the field has increasingly adopted probabilistic Bayesian frameworks to navigate complex, high-dimensional parameter spaces [[bibcode-2014bmc-book-h|bibcode:2014bmc..book.....H]]. By combining the likelihood of the observed data with physically motivated [[priors-in-astrophysics|Priors in astrophysics]], researchers can robustly map [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] and rigorously quantify [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]]. This approach is essential for breaking severe parameter degeneracies, such as the confusion between dust attenuation and star formation rates in multi-wavelength data [[bibcode-2024mnras-531-3242m|bibcode:2024MNRAS.531.3242M]].

The applications of parameter estimation span virtually all scales of the Universe. On galactic scales, [[sed-fitting|SED fitting]] techniques compare broad-band photometry and spectroscopy against theoretical templates to extract stellar masses, metallicities, and star formation histories [[bibcode-2011ascl-soft06010d|bibcode:2011ascl.soft06010D]]. On cosmic scales, estimating fundamental constants from the large-scale structure involves fitting galaxy power spectra and correlation functions while meticulously modeling redshift-space distortions and complex covariance matrices [[bibcode-2005astro-ph-3604h|bibcode:2005astro.ph..3604H]], [[bibcode-2006phrvd-74l3507t|bibcode:2006PhRvD..74l3507T]]. Across these domains, standard practice relies heavily on [[monte-carlo-methods-in-astrophysics|Monte Carlo Methods in Astrophysics]]. Algorithms such as standard Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) and [[ensemble-mcmc-sampling|Ensemble MCMC sampling]] are widely considered the gold standard to computationally evaluate highly correlated models and derive accurate credible intervals [[bibcode-2002phrvd-66j3511l|bibcode:2002PhRvD..66j3511L]]. 

As the field enters the era of Stage-IV surveys (such as those conducted by DESI, Euclid, and the Rubin Observatory), the sheer volume of data—comprising billions of individual objects—renders traditional MCMC sampling computationally prohibitive. Consequently, this article explores the modern paradigm shift toward data-driven and likelihood-free techniques, including [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]] and amortized neural posterior estimation, which can evaluate complex constraints in fractions of a second. Alongside outlining these computational advancements, this page surveys the ongoing challenges in the field. Key open questions include mitigating [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]] driven by incomplete theoretical models, determining the true intrinsic dimensionality of galaxy populations, and leveraging [[non-parametric-methods-in-astrophysics|Non-parametric methods in astrophysics]] to close the gap between simulated training sets and reality.

## Foundations of Statistical Inference in Astrophysics

The mathematical foundation of extracting physical properties from observational data has evolved from [[statistical-estimators-in-astronomy|Statistical estimators in astronomy]] to rigorous, probabilistic frameworks. Addressing the 21st-century statistical and computational challenges of linking vast astronomical data sets to highly nonlinear astrophysical models remains an overarching priority [[bibcode-2021anrsa-8-493f|bibcode:2021AnRSA...8..493F]]. While frequentist approaches like [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] and the [[least-squares-method|Least squares method]] remain foundational, they often struggle to capture the complex degeneracies inherent in high-dimensional astrophysical parameter spaces. However, these formulations remain incredibly powerful; for example, in weak lensing, galaxy ellipticity estimation can be formulated robustly as a statistical parameter estimation problem using unweighted image moments derived from jointly normal random variables [[bibcode-2019newa-69-58t|bibcode:2019NewA...69...58T]]. To further exploit these frameworks, the extension of maximum-likelihood analysis to second order can incorporate non-linear Fisher information, effectively lifting parameter degeneracies inherent in galaxy surveys [[bibcode-2001mnras-328-1027t|bibcode:2001MNRAS.328.1027T]]. 

The cornerstone of modern inference is the likelihood function, $\mathcal{L}(\theta) \equiv P(D|\theta)$, which quantifies the probability of observing the data $D$ given a set of physical parameters $\theta$. Historically, [[statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Statistical methods in astrophysics]] have relied heavily on the assumption of a [[gaussian-statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Gaussian Statistical Methods in Astrophysics]], which corresponds to minimizing a [[chi-squared-test|Chi-squared test]] statistic. However, for highly non-linear observables, such as those encountered in large-scale structure analyses, the exact likelihood can deviate significantly from a Gaussian form, necessitating more flexible approaches [[bibcode-2017mnras-469-2791h|bibcode:2017MNRAS.469.2791H]]. Indeed, it has been shown that composite Gaussian plus log-normal likelihoods outperform standard Gaussian approximations for the power spectrum, particularly when estimating primordial non-Gaussianity on large scales [[bibcode-2013apj-777-75s|bibcode:2013ApJ...777...75S]]. Furthermore, global parameter estimation frameworks naturally extend to fundamental kinematics, such as using geodetic and astrometric VLBI observations to directly estimate the Solar System's acceleration without relying on underlying Galactic models [[bibcode-2012scpma-55-329x|bibcode:2012SCPMA..55..329X]].

To address parameter degeneracies and systematically incorporate existing physical knowledge, Bayesian frameworks have become the standard paradigm. By applying Bayes' theorem, researchers map the full [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]], $P(\theta|D)$, by updating the likelihood with a prior probability $P(\theta)$:

$$P(\theta|D) = \frac{P(D|\theta)P(\theta)}{P(D)}$$

The use of physically motivated [[priors-in-astrophysics|Priors in astrophysics]] is highly effective in breaking degeneracies, such as those routinely encountered in multi-color photometric redshift estimation [[bibcode-2000apj-536-571b|bibcode:2000ApJ...536..571B]]. Furthermore, this paradigm naturally extends to the [[hierarchical-bayesian-model|Hierarchical Bayesian Model]], which allows for the simultaneous inference of individual object properties and survey-wide population distributions, thereby rigorously accounting for selection biases and measurement noise without resorting to arbitrary binning [[bibcode-2016mnras-460-4258l|bibcode:2016MNRAS.460.4258L]]. In addition, hybrid Bayesian methods for disk galaxy decomposition integrate photometric and kinematic data to statistically assess the need for multiple structural components like bulges and bars [[bibcode-2022mnras-513-6111r|bibcode:2022MNRAS.513.6111R]].

Because astrophysical posteriors rarely admit analytical solutions, numerical exploration is required. Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithms are widely considered the gold standard for recovering accurate parameter constraints [[bibcode-2002phrvd-66j3511l|bibcode:2002PhRvD..66j3511L]]. When [[model-comparison|Model comparison]] is necessary, [[nested-sampling|Nested sampling]] is commonly employed to compute the Bayesian evidence. However, the choice of sampling algorithm is non-trivial and actively debated in the literature; for instance, standard ellipsoidal nested samplers (e.g., MultiNest) have been shown to produce inconsistent Bayesian evidence estimates and artificially narrower [[credible-interval|Credible interval]] bounds compared to sliced sampling techniques like PolyChord [[bibcode-2023mnras-521-1184l|bibcode:2023MNRAS.521.1184L]]. To improve on this, techniques like the Geometric Nested Sampler have been developed as an adaptation that utilizes the geometrical interpretation of parameters to sample complex domains more efficiently [[bibcode-2019arxiv190900029j|bibcode:2019arXiv190900029J]]. Furthermore, algorithms such as Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) offer an efficient alternative to traditional MCMC for localizing regions of maximum likelihood in highly complex spaces, such as multi-dimensional semi-analytic models of galaxy formation [[bibcode-2015apj-801-139r|bibcode:2015ApJ...801..139R]].

The computational cost of comprehensive posterior exploration—often requiring tens to hundreds of CPU hours per galaxy—has driven the development of faster alternatives. Variational inference reformulates parameter estimation as an [[optimization-methods|Optimization methods]] problem, approximating the posterior from a known family of distributions and providing competitive results at a fraction of the computational cost of traditional sampling [[bibcode-2022pasa-39-1g|bibcode:2022PASA...39....1G]]. Alternatively, when the likelihood function is computationally intractable or the assumption of Gaussianity introduces systematic bias, [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]] enables likelihood-free inference. This is achieved by using forward generative models and distance metrics to constrain parameters, entirely bypassing traditional likelihood evaluations [[bibcode-2017mnras-469-2791h|bibcode:2017MNRAS.469.2791H]].

## Historical Evolution of Estimation Techniques

The historical development of [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] in astrophysics mirrors the exponential growth of observational data and computational power. Until the mid-20th century, observational astronomy was largely confined to optical wavebands. Today, the flood of multi-wavelength data from space and ground-based telescopes has necessitated a profound evolution in statistical techniques.

Early parameter extraction heavily relied on [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] and traditional frequentist analysis. For example, [[traditional-sed-fitting-techniques|Traditional SED fitting techniques]] evaluated galaxy properties by comparing photometric data against a discrete, pre-computed grid of simple stellar population templates. While computationally straightforward, this approach suffered severely from the "curse of dimensionality." As physical models evolved to incorporate high-dimensional spectral data using [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] codes like STARLIGHT, coarsely sampled parameter grids struggled with severe computational bottlenecks and failed to accurately capture complex degeneracies, such as those governing flexible star formation histories and stellar metallicity [[bibcode-2009mnras-399-1044r|bibcode:2009MNRAS.399.1044R]]. Furthermore, frequentist point estimators and the assumption of simple Gaussian (i.e., $\chi^2$) likelihoods were shown to introduce significant [[estimation-bias|Estimation bias]] when dealing with nonlinear combinations of observables or asymmetric uncertainty distributions [[bibcode-1995va-39-297h|bibcode:1995VA.....39..297H]], [[bibcode-1999apj-519-622h|bibcode:1999ApJ...519..622H]].

To overcome these limitations, the field underwent a paradigm shift toward Bayesian inference. By the early 2000s, Bayesian frameworks became the standard, allowing researchers to incorporate fundamental [[priors-in-astrophysics|Priors in astrophysics]] to effectively break degeneracies in multi-color space [[bibcode-2000apj-536-571b|bibcode:2000ApJ...536..571B]]. [[monte-carlo-methods-in-astrophysics|Monte Carlo Methods in Astrophysics]], specifically Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), replaced [[model-fitting|Model fitting]] as the gold standard for navigating high-dimensional spaces [[bibcode-2002phrvd-66j3511l|bibcode:2002PhRvD..66j3511L]]. Software codes like CosmoMC [[bibcode-2011ascl-soft06025l|bibcode:2011ascl.soft06025L]] and Prospector [[bibcode-2021apjs-254-22j|bibcode:2021ApJS..254...22J]] utilized MCMC to forward-model complex data and map full [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]], providing robust [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] without relying on singular point estimates.

However, the computational cost of MCMC algorithms scales poorly with the massive datasets produced by modern surveys. Evaluating a single galaxy's properties using MCMC can require $10$ to $100$ CPU hours [[bibcode-2022apj-938-11h|bibcode:2022ApJ...938...11H]]. To process the billions of objects expected from upcoming observatories, the field is currently transitioning toward simulation-based and likelihood-free inference. [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]] (ABC) bypasses rigid Gaussian likelihood assumptions by utilizing forward generative models and distance metrics, making it robust for complex large-scale structure models [[bibcode-2017mnras-469-2791h|bibcode:2017MNRAS.469.2791H]].

Alternatively, variational inference has emerged as a rapid method that recasts Bayesian inference as an optimization problem, taking orders of magnitude less time than MCMC, albeit at the cost of relying on distributional approximations [[bibcode-2022pasa-39-1g|bibcode:2022PASA...39....1G]]. Most recently, amortized neural posterior estimation (ANPE) and other deep learning frameworks have demonstrated the ability to infer high-dimensional [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] in $\sim 1$ second per galaxy while maintaining MCMC-level accuracy [[bibcode-2022apj-938-11h|bibcode:2022ApJ...938...11H]]. While the literature largely agrees on the computational necessity of these machine learning approaches, there remains an active tension: simulation-based inference models rely heavily on the accuracy of their training data, and out-of-domain applications can exhibit worsened calibration metrics and systematic biases [[bibcode-2024arxiv240920507r|bibcode:2024arXiv240920507R]].

## Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) and Stellar Population Inference

The interpretation of multi-wavelength galaxy observations relies heavily on [[sed-fitting|SED fitting]], a process wherein observed photometric and spectroscopic data are compared against theoretical templates to extract intrinsic galaxy properties. Key targets of this analysis include stellar mass, metallicity, and the star formation history (SFH). These theoretical templates are constructed using [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] (SPS) models, which combine simple stellar populations of varying ages and metallicities, alongside prescriptions for dust attenuation and nebular emission. To support these models, comprehensive empirical atlases of galaxy spectral energy distributions spanning from the ultraviolet to the mid-infrared are essential, as they significantly improve K-corrections and empirical star-formation rate calibrations [[bibcode-2014apjs-212-18b|bibcode:2014ApJS..212...18B]].

Modern SED modeling predominantly employs a Bayesian framework. This approach naturally incorporates physical [[priors-in-astrophysics|Priors in astrophysics]] and explores high-dimensional, highly correlated parameter spaces to derive robust [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] [[bibcode-2011ascl-soft06010d|bibcode:2011ascl.soft06010D]], [[bibcode-2021apjs-254-22j|bibcode:2021ApJS..254...22J]]. Pioneering tools like BPZ established the foundation for Bayesian photometric redshift estimation by naturally incorporating prior redshift distributions and empirical galaxy type mixes [[bibcode-2011ascl-soft08011b|bibcode:2011ascl.soft08011B]]. Today, modern popular tools such as [[magphys|MAGPHYS]], [[bagpipes-code|BAGPIPES (code)]], and Prospector forward-model the data using either [[ensemble-mcmc-sampling|Ensemble MCMC sampling]] or [[nested-sampling|Nested sampling]] to accurately characterize parameter uncertainties. The detailed use of time-evolving physical priors—such as constraints on stellar mass and expected star formation rate limits—within [[template-fitting|Template fitting]] codes additionally serves to break severe degeneracies in multi-color photometric redshift estimation [[bibcode-2015apj-801-20t|bibcode:2015ApJ...801...20T]]. 

The parameter space governing galaxy SEDs is notoriously degenerate. The classic age-metallicity degeneracy and the confusion between dust attenuation ($A_V$) and the star formation rate (SFR) pose significant observational challenges. Research demonstrates that incorporating infrared photometry (e.g., from the WISE satellite) is crucial for breaking the degeneracy between dust column density and intrinsic star formation [[bibcode-2024mnras-531-3242m|bibcode:2024MNRAS.531.3242M]]. 

The choice of the underlying SPS model introduces dominant systematic uncertainties into the parameter estimation. The literature is explicit about this tension: utilizing different SPS templates (such as the [[bruzual-charlot-stellar-population-synthesis-models|Bruzual-Charlot stellar population synthesis models]] versus those of Maraston) can yield relative stellar mass estimates that disagree by factors of up to $\sim 2$ overall, and by greater than a factor of 3 for distinct classes of galaxies, particularly those with recent star formation [[bibcode-2007apj-657l-5k|bibcode:2007ApJ...657L...5K]]. Furthermore, systematic tests of parameter recovery show that the median stellar age of a galaxy is generally constrained much more accurately than the absolute time elapsed since the onset of star formation [[bibcode-2015apj-804-8a|bibcode:2015ApJ...804....8A]].

To capture realistic galaxy evolution, contemporary models increasingly utilize non-parametric SFHs rather than rigid [[parametric-modeling|Parametric modeling]] forms. Highly flexible models like [[dense-basis-sed-fitting|Dense Basis (SED Fitting)]] have demonstrated the remarkable ability to predict detailed spectroscopic features—such as H$\alpha$ luminosity, $D_n4000$, and Balmer decrements—from broadband photometry alone, achieving a scatter of $\sim 0.18$ dex for H$\alpha$ predictions [[bibcode-2017apj-837-170l|bibcode:2017ApJ...837..170L]]. However, Bayesian [[model-comparison|Model comparison]] indicates that increasing model complexity is only justified when the discriminative power of the data is sufficient; otherwise, simpler models may be statistically favored [[bibcode-2023apjs-269-39h|bibcode:2023ApJS..269...39H]].

Despite the complexity of physical models, the intrinsic dimensionality of wide-band photometric data is relatively low, estimated at approximately $4.3 \pm 0.5$ independent parameters for star-forming galaxies and $2.9 \pm 0.2$ for passive galaxies [[bibcode-2024arxiv240402962c|bibcode:2024arXiv240402962C]]. Given this lower intrinsic dimensionality and the massive data volumes expected from upcoming large-scale surveys, the field is rapidly adopting machine learning approaches like Simulation-Based Inference (SBI) and Amortized Neural Posterior Estimation (ANPE). These methods effectively serve as advanced forms of [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]]. ANPE pipelines can estimate complex SED posteriors in roughly $1$ second per galaxy—reducing the computational cost by orders of magnitude compared to traditional MCMC methods—while maintaining excellent agreement in physical parameter recovery [[bibcode-2022apj-938-11h|bibcode:2022ApJ...938...11H]], [[bibcode-2022mls-t-3dlt04k|bibcode:2022MLS&T...3dLT04K]].

## Cosmological Parameter Estimation from Large-Scale Structure

The spatial distribution of matter and galaxies encodes fundamental information about the Universe’s expansion history and the growth of cosmic structure. [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] in this regime relies primarily on two-point statistics derived from [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Galaxy Surveys and Catalogs]]: the spatial two-point correlation function $\xi(r)$ and its Fourier equivalent, the power spectrum $P(k)$. By modeling these observables, researchers can extract key cosmological parameters, including the matter density $\Omega_m$, the amplitude of matter fluctuations $\sigma_8$, the dark energy equation of state $w$, and the sum of neutrino masses $\sum m_\nu$ [[bibcode-2002phrvd-66j3511l|bibcode:2002PhRvD..66j3511L]], [[bibcode-2006phrvd-74l3507t|bibcode:2006PhRvD..74l3507T]]. In Fourier space, researchers can also incorporate mode projection into pseudo-$C_\ell$ power spectrum estimation to remove contaminants like astrophysical foregrounds while recasting the evaluation into numerically efficient closed-form expressions [[bibcode-2017mnras-465-1847e|bibcode:2017MNRAS.465.1847E]]. Baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO) imprinted on the power spectrum serve as a robust standard ruler, providing comoving distance measurements that are largely independent of background curvature or dark energy assumptions [[bibcode-2006phrvd-74l3507t|bibcode:2006PhRvD..74l3507T]]. Modern surveys, such as the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), apply these principles to extract recent measurements of the growth rate of structure ($f\sigma_8$) from the joint analysis of galaxy density and momentum fields in their DR1 peculiar velocity surveys [[bibcode-2025arxiv251203231q|bibcode:2025arXiv251203231Q]].

To yield unbiased parameter constraints, theoretical models must meticulously account for the mapping between real space and observational coordinates. Redshift-space distortions (RSD), caused by the peculiar velocities of galaxies falling into gravitational potential wells, significantly alter the observed clustering signal. Correct modeling of RSD is critical; failing to include it, or incorrectly relying on the Limber approximation at inappropriate scales, introduces significant systematic biases into the derived cosmological parameters [[bibcode-2020jcap-10-017b|bibcode:2020JCAP...10..017B]]. Conversely, rigorous analyses can successfully employ the Limber approximation to vastly accelerate computations by safely restricting the analysis exclusively to the angular scales where the approximation remains valid [[bibcode-2019mnras-489-3385t|bibcode:2019MNRAS.489.3385T]], [[bibcode-2020mnras-491-4869t|bibcode:2020MNRAS.491.4869T]]. Additionally, weak lensing magnification bias must be incorporated, particularly for deep surveys. Neglecting magnification bias or applying inaccurate scale cuts leads to significant systematic errors in the [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] of $\sigma_8$ and the primordial non-Gaussianity parameter $f_{\rm NL}$ [[bibcode-2020jcap-10-017b|bibcode:2020JCAP...10..017B]], [[bibcode-2024arxiv241016358c|bibcode:2024arXiv241016358C]]. Furthermore, constraining higher-order primordial non-Gaussianity relies heavily on multi-survey approaches; joint analyses of deep surveys like the SKA and Euclid are essential to break degeneracies between parameters such as $f_{\rm NL}$ and $\tau_{\rm NL}$ [[bibcode-2016phrvd-93l3506y|bibcode:2016PhRvD..93l3506Y]]. Analyses must also carefully address "super-sample" density fluctuations; treating the mean density fluctuation as an additional model parameter can actively mitigate resulting degeneracies in the measured power spectrum [[bibcode-2014phrvd-90j3530l|bibcode:2014PhRvD..90j3530L]]. To tackle these challenges comprehensively, tools like the ARES framework have been developed for joint Bayesian inference of the underlying density field and the power spectrum. This framework explicitly accounts for non-trivial survey geometries and crucial anti-correlations between galaxy bias and power [[bibcode-2013apj-779-15j|bibcode:2013ApJ...779...15J]].

A dominant challenge in large-scale structure (LSS) analysis is determining the precision via the [[analytical-covariance-matrix|Analytical covariance matrix]] of the power spectrum or correlation function. Traditionally, the covariance is estimated using thousands of mock catalogs to minimize sampling noise, which is computationally expensive [[bibcode-2023arxiv230605988g|bibcode:2023arXiv230605988G]]. When the number of $N$-body simulations is limited, the sample covariance matrix becomes noisy and ill-conditioned. To mitigate this, non-linear shrinkage estimators (such as NERCOME) can be applied to regularize the precision matrix, though they may systematically overestimate error bars [[bibcode-2024arxiv240213783l|bibcode:2024arXiv240213783L]], [[bibcode-2023arxiv230605988g|bibcode:2023arXiv230605988G]]. Alternatively, an analytical covariance combined with data compression techniques (such as MOPED) and Taylor expansion interpolations can reduce the computational time required for LSS analysis from months to mere hours [[bibcode-2024mnras-530-4519l|bibcode:2024MNRAS.530.4519L]]. 

The structural assumptions underlying the covariance matrix also affect parameter recovery. For instance, non-Gaussian contributions to the covariance arising from non-linear clustering must be included for scale cuts $k_{\max} > 0.2 \, h/{\rm Mpc}$; ignoring these terms artificially tightens error bars and biases central parameter values [[bibcode-2023arxiv230605988g|bibcode:2023arXiv230605988G]]. Moreover, recent approaches such as the "theoretical error" likelihood allow for clustering analysis without fixed scale cuts ($k_{\max}$), organically accounting for the gradual increase in theoretical uncertainty at smaller scales [[bibcode-2022arxiv220406591c|bibcode:2022arXiv220406591C]]. While many studies assume a fixed covariance, the literature remains divided on the necessity of a parameter-dependent covariance matrix. Some analyses suggest that parameter-dependence has a negligible effect as the number of survey modes increases [[bibcode-2019ojap-2e-3k|bibcode:2019OJAp....2E...3K]], whereas others indicate it can reveal multi-modal posteriors and impact constraints [[bibcode-2024arxiv241021722j|bibcode:2024arXiv241021722J]].

Finally, standard approaches assume a Gaussian functional form for the likelihood. However, this assumption is often imperfect at large scales or in the highly non-linear regime. Adding higher-order statistics, such as the bispectrum, helps break parameter degeneracies to accurately measure the linear growth rate [[bibcode-2021jcap-03-021a|bibcode:2021JCAP...03..021A]]. Furthermore, the $N$-probability density function ($N$-pdf) of galaxy density fluctuations can be used to jointly constrain galaxy bias and structural amplitude [[bibcode-2016jcap-03-005a|bibcode:2016JCAP...03..005A]]. For three-point statistics specifically, computational efficiency can be massively improved by calculating triplet counts through one-dimensional integrals, bypassing the need for generating large random catalogues [[bibcode-2019mnras-486l-105p|bibcode:2019MNRAS.486L.105P]]. To bypass rigid likelihood constraints entirely, there is a growing shift toward [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]] (ABC) and simulation-based inference (SBI). These "likelihood-free" methods use forward generative models to infer parameters, directly circumventing the need for an explicit analytic likelihood and demonstrating high fidelity in recovering properties like the halo occupation distribution and cosmological parameters [[bibcode-2017mnras-469-2791h|bibcode:2017MNRAS.469.2791H]], [[bibcode-2024arxiv240920507r|bibcode:2024arXiv240920507R]].

## Covariance Modeling and Precision Matrix Estimation

Accurate evaluation of the covariance matrix and its inverse, the precision matrix, is fundamental to evaluating likelihoods and deriving robust posteriors in [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]]. Errors in covariance modeling constitute a dominant source of [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] and systematic bias, particularly affecting the recovery of key cosmological parameters like $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$ in large-scale structure (LSS) surveys [[bibcode-2021mnras-508-3125f|bibcode:2021MNRAS.508.3125F]], [[bibcode-2023arxiv230605988g|bibcode:2023arXiv230605988G]]. 

Traditionally, covariance matrices are estimated using a large ensemble of mock catalogs. Because generating high-fidelity simulations is computationally expensive, the sample size $n$ is often comparable to the dimensionality of the data vector. This limited sampling causes the empirical covariance matrix to be noisy, and consequently, the inverted precision matrix becomes highly ill-conditioned [[bibcode-2024arxiv240213783l|bibcode:2024arXiv240213783L]], [[bibcode-2025mnras-537-21l|bibcode:2025MNRAS.537...21L]]. This [[sample-variance|Sample variance]] propagates directly into the parameter posteriors as stochastic noise.

To address the instability of the empirical precision matrix at low $n$, researchers have developed advanced shrinkage estimators. These methods construct a weighted combination of the empirical sample covariance and a structured target matrix. Non-linear shrinkage estimators, such as NERCOME (Non-parametric Eigenvalue-Regularised COvariance Matrix Estimator), effectively suppress sampling noise. However, the literature reveals a clear tension: while NERCOME stabilizes the inversion and reduces posterior scatter, it can systematically overestimate the resulting parameter error bars [[bibcode-2023arxiv230605988g|bibcode:2023arXiv230605988G]], [[bibcode-2025a-a-693a-226g|bibcode:2025A&A...693A.226G]]. Alternatively, direct linear shrinkage applied to the precision matrix has been shown to improve posterior distributions without requiring a massive ensemble of mocks [[bibcode-2024arxiv240213783l|bibcode:2024arXiv240213783L]]. Additionally, "residual smoothing" is a technique that allows researchers to identify missing features in model covariance matrices using mock catalogs and smoothly incorporate them without significantly degrading overall precision [[bibcode-2019arxiv191104670o|bibcode:2019arXiv191104670O]].

Data-driven resampling methods offer computationally lighter alternatives for internal covariance estimation. Jackknife resampling applied to mock catalogs can match the variance accuracy of standard sample estimators while requiring approximately seven times fewer simulations [[bibcode-2016arxiv160600233e|bibcode:2016arXiv160600233E]]. Recent hybrid approaches, such as Debiased Internal Covariance Estimation with Shrinkage (DICES), combine spatial jackknife resampling with linear shrinkage to guarantee a non-singular matrix. This approach has been shown to improve the relative error of the covariance by over 30% compared to standard jackknife techniques [[bibcode-2025arxiv250609118e|bibcode:2025arXiv250609118E]]. Another alternative technique is the Linear Construction (LC) method for fast two-point correlation function covariance, which efficiently estimates covariance for small random catalogs and linearly combines them to achieve unbiased results for large surveys [[bibcode-2022a-a-666a-129k|bibcode:2022A&A...666A.129K]].

A completely different approach bypasses simulations by using an [[analytical-covariance-matrix|Analytical covariance matrix]]. When combined with data compression techniques like MOPED and Taylor expansion interpolations of the power spectrum, analytical covariances can reduce computation times from months to mere hours while recovering highly consistent parameter constraints [[bibcode-2024mnras-530-4519l|bibcode:2024MNRAS.530.4519L]]. Regardless of the methodology chosen, it is well-established that non-Gaussian contributions arising from non-linear clustering must be explicitly included for scale cuts $k > 0.2 \ h/\text{Mpc}$; neglecting these terms biases both the central parameter estimates and their inferred uncertainties [[bibcode-2023arxiv230605988g|bibcode:2023arXiv230605988G]], [[bibcode-2025a-a-693a-226g|bibcode:2025A&A...693A.226G]].

Finally, the field remains divided on whether the covariance matrix should be updated dynamically as a function of the physical parameters during [[statistical-inference-and-data-methods|Statistical Inference and Data Methods]]. Some studies argue that a fixed, parameter-independent covariance matrix is an exceptionally good approximation that becomes increasingly valid as the number of survey modes grows, contributing negligible bias [[bibcode-2019ojap-2e-3k|bibcode:2019OJAp....2E...3K]]. Conversely, recent analyses demonstrate that parameter-dependent covariance matrices can significantly influence inference, occasionally leading to artificially tighter constraints or revealing complex, multi-modal posteriors that a fixed covariance would mask [[bibcode-2024arxiv241021722j|bibcode:2024arXiv241021722J]].

## Machine Learning and Simulation-Based Inference (SBI)

The era of next-generation large-scale surveys requires processing billions of galaxies, rendering traditional explicit-likelihood approaches computationally prohibitive. Standard [[monte-carlo-methods-in-astrophysics|Monte Carlo Methods in Astrophysics]] (MCMC) algorithms can require 10 to 100 CPU hours per galaxy to thoroughly sample complex parameter spaces [[bibcode-2022apj-938-11h|bibcode:2022ApJ...938...11H]]. Consequently, [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] is undergoing a major paradigm shift toward data-driven, simulation-based techniques that can drastically accelerate inference. The historical use of neural network committees for cosmological datasets dates back over two decades, with early findings identifying that computational costs for parameter estimation via neural networks scale as favorably as $N^{3/2}$ [[bibcode-2001astro-ph-12359p|bibcode:2001astro.ph.12359P]]. 

An early step toward relaxing explicit likelihood assumptions was the adoption of [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]] (ABC). Traditional cosmological analyses often assume a Gaussian (or $\chi^2$) functional form for the likelihood, which can be mathematically incorrect in highly non-linear regimes. ABC bypasses this assumption by relying on a forward generative model of the data and a distance metric to compare simulated and observed summary statistics. This likelihood-free approach has successfully recovered complex constraints, such as the halo occupation distribution, without biasing the target parameters [[bibcode-2017mnras-469-2791h|bibcode:2017MNRAS.469.2791H]].

More recently, the field has rapidly integrated deep learning to perform Amortized Neural Posterior Estimation (ANPE). In this framework, neural networks are trained on massive suites of simulated observations to directly map data to [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]]. Inference is "amortized" because the heavy computational cost is paid upfront during the training phase; once trained, evaluating the posterior for a new observation requires only a single forward pass of the network. Pipelines such as SEDFLOW and DIGS demonstrate that ANPE can reproduce the accuracy of traditional [[sed-fitting|SED fitting]] and Markov Chain Monte Carlo in roughly one second per galaxy [[bibcode-2022apj-938-11h-bibcode-2022mls-t-3dlt04k|bibcode:2022ApJ...938...11H], [bibcode:2022MLS&T...3dLT04K]]. Beyond SED fitting and full posterior exploration, supervised machine learning methods combined with parameter space optimization have been deployed to rapidly derive accurate star formation rates directly from photometry for tens of millions of galaxies [[bibcode-2019mnras-486-1377d|bibcode:2019MNRAS.486.1377D]]. A key technology enabling this is normalizing flows, which learn complex, high-dimensional probability densities by transforming a simple base distribution through invertible neural networks [[bibcode-2024aj-168-80c-bibcode-2022phrvd-105f3529d|bibcode:2024AJ....168...80C], [bibcode:2022PhRvD.105f3529D]]. Recent comparative studies have demonstrated that machine learning frameworks, such as Pt-SNE, significantly outperform traditional SED-fitting codes like LePhare, offering major improvements in accuracy and a reduction in systematic bias for stellar mass recovery [[bibcode-2025arxiv251220544a|bibcode:2025arXiv251220544A]]. Beyond photometry, the application of deep learning extends to spectroscopic data. Advanced models like YOLO have been implemented for end-to-end detection, classification, and discrete parameter estimation directly from raw 2D slitless spectroscopic traces, demonstrating the growing capacity of AI to interpret raw pixel data [[bibcode-2025aj-170-256z|bibcode:2025AJ....170..256Z]].

As black-box architectures become more prevalent, there is an emerging focus on interpretability; recent studies have applied causal analysis and mutual information decomposition to deep learning models to explicitly understand how morphological information contributes to stellar mass estimation [[bibcode-2025arxiv250923901z|bibcode:2025arXiv250923901Z]]. Machine learning has also provided insights into the fundamental structure of astronomical data. Non-parametric dimensionality reduction reveals that the intrinsic dimensionality of wide-band galaxy photometry is remarkably low: the variance can be described by approximately $4.3 \pm 0.5$ independent parameters for star-forming galaxies and $2.9 \pm 0.2$ for passive galaxies [[bibcode-2024arxiv240402962c|bibcode:2024arXiv240402962C]]. 

Despite the computational speedup, accurately characterizing [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] remains critical. Sparse Gaussian processes (e.g., GPz) have been successfully applied to capture heteroscedastic (input-dependent) noise in [[photometry-and-redshift-estimation|Photometry and Redshift Estimation]], outperforming standard artificial neural networks in recovering well-calibrated variances [[bibcode-2016mnras-462-726a|bibcode:2016MNRAS.462..726A]]. Furthermore, conformalized quantile regression has been incorporated as a powerful tool to convert machine learning point predictions into rigorous error bars for galaxy property recovery [[bibcode-2024astro-3-14g|bibcode:2024Astro...3...14G]]. 

The primary tension in this data-driven shift is the strict dependence of machine learning models on the fidelity of their training simulations. The literature emphasizes that when neural estimators are tested on out-of-domain data—observations whose underlying physical properties or noise characteristics differ systematically from the training mock catalogs—their calibration metrics worsen significantly [[bibcode-2024arxiv240920507r|bibcode:2024arXiv240920507R]]. Closing this simulation-to-reality gap is a major open challenge to ensure that [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]] do not dominate over statistical precision in future cosmological inference.

## Multi-Messenger and Multi-Scale Parameter Extraction

Parameter estimation frequently spans multiple scales of the Universe. On galactic scales, [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] has historically been utilized to constrain the total mass distribution of the Milky Way by meticulously modeling the phase-space coordinates of outer satellite galaxies [[bibcode-1992mnras-255-105k|bibcode:1992MNRAS.255..105K]]. On larger scales, combining multiple probes remains essential. The robust extraction of physical parameters often requires breaking degeneracies that plague single-probe observations. By integrating diverse data streams—ranging from multi-wavelength cluster observables to multi-messenger gravitational wave signals—researchers can achieve significantly tighter constraints on both astrophysical and cosmological scales.

**Galaxy Cluster Mass Estimation**
In the study of galaxy clusters, [[mass-estimators|Mass estimators]] rely heavily on combining multi-wavelength data, such as optical weak lensing and the microwave Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect. Joint Bayesian analysis of these phenomena enables the simultaneous modeling of the dark matter potential, gas density, and temperature, routinely recovering cluster gas fractions to better than $30\%$ precision [[bibcode-2003mnras-346-489m|bibcode:2003MNRAS.346..489M]]. Establishing a definitive cluster mass scale relies comprehensively on these multi-wavelength methods to track and mitigate pervasive systematic biases and selection effects [[bibcode-2019ssrv-215-25p|bibcode:2019SSRv..215...25P]]. However, extracting unbiased masses requires accounting for significant geometric and observational systematics. Assuming spherical Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profiles for highly triaxial halos elongated along the line of sight can cause mass overestimation by up to $50\%$ and inflate concentration parameters by a factor of 2 [[bibcode-2007mnras-380-149c|bibcode:2007MNRAS.380..149C]]. Additionally, contamination by line-of-sight interlopers strongly impacts [[membership-probability|Membership probability]] calculations and remains the primary driver of [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] in dynamical mass scaling relations [[bibcode-2018mnras-481-324w|bibcode:2018MNRAS.481..324W]].

**Gravitational Wave Host Localization**
In multi-messenger astronomy, identifying the host galaxy of a compact binary coalescence requires synthesizing gravitational wave (GW) [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] with [[large-galaxy-catalogs|Large galaxy catalogs]]. The primary challenge is the strong parameter degeneracy between the binary's inclination angle and its luminosity distance. Incorporating higher-order GW harmonics, specifically the $(3,3)$ mode, into the parameter estimation pipeline strongly breaks this degeneracy, yielding up to a $10^3$ improvement in angular resolution and a $10^4$ improvement in luminosity distance resolution [[bibcode-2023phrvd-108f4046g|bibcode:2023PhRvD.108f4046G]]. Another emerging frontier involves "dark sirens"—gravitational wave events without electromagnetic counterparts. Recent methods demonstrate the extraction of the Hubble parameter directly from unedited compact object merger catalogs using detection-level information, completely bypassing the computationally expensive per-candidate [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] [[bibcode-2025arxiv251208164h|bibcode:2025arXiv251208164H]].

To process these events on timescales relevant for electromagnetic follow-up, rapid localization codes like FIGARO and SKYFAST deploy non-parametric methods, such as a [[dirichlet-process-mixture|Dirichlet process mixture]] model. These codes can reconstruct analytic posteriors and generate ranked host-galaxy lists in minutes, utilizing only $\sim 10\%$ of the samples generated by standard sampling algorithms [[bibcode-2022mnras-517l-5r|bibcode:2022MNRAS.517L...5R]], [[bibcode-2024arxiv240713695d|bibcode:2024arXiv240713695D]], [[bibcode-2025a-a-694a-245d|bibcode:2025A&A...694A.245D]]. Despite these algorithmic advances, incorporating external catalogs directly into parameter estimation can sometimes fail to improve upon a simple post-hoc catalog cross-match, though mass reweighting of galaxy priors has shown success for specific events like GW170817 [[bibcode-2024arxiv241014663m|bibcode:2024arXiv241014663M]].

**Multi-Tracer Cosmological Constraints**
On cosmological scales, [[multi-tracer-analysis|Multi-tracer analysis]] leverages the distinct clustering biases of different [[tracers|Tracers]] (e.g., luminous red galaxies versus emission-line galaxies) to suppress cosmic variance. Applying [[multitracer-estimators|Multitracer estimators]] to angular power spectra has been shown to drastically tighten cosmological constraints, yielding a $44\%$ improvement in the $2\sigma$ upper bound on the sum of neutrino masses $\sum m_\nu$ [[bibcode-2021mnras-502-2952t|bibcode:2021MNRAS.502.2952T]]. In the realm of multi-tracer redshift analysis, cross-correlating unknown photometric samples with spectroscopic reference catalogs enables clustering-based redshift inference. This technique robustly extracts the true redshift distributions for arbitrary data sets without requiring prior [[sed-fitting|SED fitting]] knowledge or detailed template matching [[bibcode-2015mnras-447-3500r|bibcode:2015MNRAS.447.3500R]]. However, joint analyses such as cross-correlating galaxy density fields with cosmic microwave background (CMB) lensing potentials are highly sensitive to [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]]. For instance, mismatch in redshift binning caused by broad photometric error tails can lead to $2\sigma$--$5\sigma$ underestimations of the cross-correlation amplitude, requiring scattering matrix formalisms to recover unbiased values for the clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$ [[bibcode-2024a-a-690a-338s|bibcode:2024A&A...690A.338S]].

## Systematic Errors, Biases, and Uncertainty Quantification

The reliability of parameter extraction in astrophysics is fundamentally limited by [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]]. While statistical uncertainties steadily decrease with larger data volumes, systematic errors arising from incomplete physical models, uncharacterized [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]], and survey selection effects can lead to severe [[parameter-bias|Parameter bias]] if not properly mitigated.

**Observational Systematics in Cosmology**
In large-scale structure analyses, the estimation of the covariance matrix is a dominant source of systematic uncertainty. Because covariance matrices are often estimated from a finite number of $N$-body simulations, they suffer from sampling noise that propagates into the precision matrix. Techniques such as non-linear shrinkage estimators (e.g., NERCOME) can significantly reduce this noise, although they may systematically overestimate error bars [[bibcode-2023arxiv230605988g-bibcode-2024arxiv240213783l|bibcode:2023arXiv230605988G], [bibcode:2024arXiv240213783L]]. Furthermore, neglecting non-Gaussian contributions to the covariance matrix from non-linear clustering biases parameter constraints at scale cuts $k > 0.2 \, h/\mathrm{Mpc}$ [[bibcode-2023arxiv230605988g|bibcode:2023arXiv230605988G]]. Theoretical modeling choices also introduce systematics; for example, inappropriately adopting the Limber approximation or neglecting lensing magnification bias in galaxy clustering can introduce significant systematic biases into the derived cosmological parameters in modern deep surveys [[bibcode-2020jcap-10-017b-bibcode-2020mnras-491-4869t|bibcode:2020JCAP...10..017B], [bibcode:2020MNRAS.491.4869T]]. The relative impact of different systematics strongly relies on the survey; end-to-end analysis results for the LSST survey identify photometric redshift spectroscopic calibration biases as more critical to cosmological parameter constraints than dust map resolution or incomplete covariance matrices [[bibcode-2024arxiv240914265a|bibcode:2024arXiv240914265A]]. To control angular systematics in spectroscopic surveys, a highly effective strategy is constructing random catalogues based explicitly on survey visibility masks and detection models linked to signal-to-noise ratios [[bibcode-2025arxiv251120856e|bibcode:2025arXiv251120856E]].

**Selection Effects and Eddington Bias**
Astronomical catalogs are subject to flux limits and incomplete sampling, leading to [[sample-selection-biases|Sample selection biases]]. When relying on template-fitting photometric redshift methods, models often struggle with large photometric errors and parameter degeneracies. [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]] models can mitigate this by generalizing the template-fitting framework to simultaneously infer the underlying population distribution and individual galaxy properties, self-consistently accounting for selection effects and flux errors without arbitrarily calibrating on biased spectroscopic training samples [[bibcode-2016mnras-460-4258l|bibcode:2016MNRAS.460.4258L]]. To ensure robust uncertainty quantification, evaluating the precise performance of photometric redshift probability density functions (PDFs) relies on sophisticated metrics; for instance, conditional density estimate (CDE) loss has been developed as a principled metric to assess survey-specific photo-$z$ accuracy for upcoming observatories like LSST [[bibcode-2020mnras-499-1587s|bibcode:2020MNRAS.499.1587S]]. Furthermore, independently validating these photometric redshift uncertainties is crucial; angular cross-correlations between photometric datasets and overlapping spectroscopic surveys provide a powerful, independent consistency check on the validity of the summed $p(z)$ distributions [[bibcode-2016mnras-463-3737c|bibcode:2016MNRAS.463.3737C]].

In galaxy cluster studies, observational scatter coupled with the steeply declining mass function leads to Eddington bias, where the number of low-mass clusters scattering into a high-mass bin exceeds the number scattering out. Modified [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] approaches can account for this bias without binning the data [[bibcode-2018mnras-474-5500o|bibcode:2018MNRAS.474.5500O]]. Additionally, membership contamination by line-of-sight interlopers is the primary source of [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] in dynamical mass scaling relations [[bibcode-2018mnras-481-324w|bibcode:2018MNRAS.481..324W]]. Even when membership is perfectly known, halo triaxiality can cause cluster masses to be overestimated by up to $50\%$ if the system is elongated along the line of sight [[bibcode-2007mnras-380-149c|bibcode:2007MNRAS.380..149C]].

**Parameter Degeneracies**
Physical parameter spaces are frequently beset by severe degeneracies, which can artificially distort [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]]. In [[sed-fitting|SED fitting]], extracting star formation histories and metallicities is notoriously hampered by the age-metallicity degeneracy [[bibcode-2009mnras-399-1044r|bibcode:2009MNRAS.399.1044R]]. Similarly, degeneracies between dust attenuation ($A_V$) and the star formation rate can only be reliably broken by including infrared photometry [[bibcode-2024mnras-531-3242m|bibcode:2024MNRAS.531.3242M]]. The choice of [[stellar-population-synthesis|Stellar Population Synthesis]] models can also systematically shift estimated stellar masses due to differing assumptions within the underlying evolutionary tracks [[bibcode-2007apj-657l-5k|bibcode:2007ApJ...657L...5K]].

Degeneracies also plague other domains of astrophysics. In gravitational wave parameter estimation, the strong correlation between binary inclination angle and luminosity distance can be broken by including higher-order waveform harmonics (such as the 3,3 mode), which has been shown to improve distance resolution by orders of magnitude [[bibcode-2023phrvd-108f4046g|bibcode:2023PhRvD.108f4046G]]. Finally, Bayesian inference is highly sensitive to the choice of priors in degenerate spaces. For instance, attempts to fit galactic rotation curves can spuriously rule out a constant Newtonian gravitational constant $G$ if uniform priors are used; adopting empirically motivated log-normal priors demonstrates that such variations are artifacts of parameter degeneracy rather than new physics [[bibcode-2021a-a-646l-13l|bibcode:2021A&A...646L..13L]].

## Non-Parametric and Flexible Modeling Frameworks

In astrophysical [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]], traditional [[parametric-modeling|Parametric modeling]] often forces complex phenomena into rigid analytic forms. To overcome the systematic biases induced by these constraints, the field has increasingly adopted [[non-parametric-methods-in-astrophysics|Non-parametric methods in astrophysics]] and flexible machine learning frameworks. These approaches bypass exact functional specifications, allowing the data to organically shape the underlying mappings and [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]]. Early applications demonstrated that non-parametric methods can map the primordial matter power spectrum directly in $k$-space, circumventing assumptions about speculative early-Universe physics [[bibcode-2002phrvd-66j3508t|bibcode:2002PhRvD..66j3508T]]. 

**Gaussian Process Regression**
[[non-parametric-gaussian-process-regression|Non-parametric Gaussian process regression]] has become a cornerstone for continuous, flexible modeling. In photometric redshift estimation, sparse Gaussian processes (e.g., the GPz framework) are utilized to jointly optimize the predictive mean and variance, effectively capturing heteroscedastic (input-dependent) noise resulting from non-uniform data density and photometric uncertainties [[bibcode-2016mnras-462-726a|bibcode:2016MNRAS.462..726A]]. To mitigate bias induced by standard squared-error loss, some GP frameworks explicitly minimize the target metric directly in their objective function:
$$ \Delta z = \frac{z_s - z_p}{1 + z_s} $$
incorporating distribution-based weighting schemes to optimize performance [[bibcode-2016mnras-455-2387a|bibcode:2016MNRAS.455.2387A]]. Beyond redshifts, Gaussian processes facilitate the reconstruction of source images and lens mass distributions in strong gravitational lensing [[bibcode-2020arxiv201007032c|bibcode:2020arXiv201007032C]]. However, caution is required; while Gaussian processes can yield smaller formal uncertainties—such as in attempts to measure the Hubble parameter from relative galaxy ages—they can also induce strongly correlated results that artificially restrict the parameter space [[bibcode-2023mnras-518-585a|bibcode:2023MNRAS.518..585A]].

**Score-Based Models for Structural Inference**
Beyond traditional Gaussian processes, modern deep generative non-parametrics are making major strides in structural analysis. The implementation of score-based likelihoods combined with diffusion model priors for Bayesian image deconvolution enables researchers to effectively recover subtle structural features in faint galaxies, providing high-fidelity estimates of physical surface brightness profiles suitable for next-generation imaging surveys [[bibcode-2025aj-169-254a|bibcode:2025AJ....169..254A]].

**Conditional Density Estimation**
For deriving redshift posteriors from broad-band photometry, the community is shifting toward [[conditional-density-estimation|Conditional density estimation]] (CDE). Rather than outputting a point estimate or standard Gaussian error, CDE reconstructs the full conditional probability density function $f(z|\mathbf{x})$ of the redshift $z$ given photometric covariates $\mathbf{x}$. This non-parametric approach is crucial for capturing asymmetric and multimodal distributions, particularly under the influence of [[sample-selection-biases|Sample selection biases]] where spectroscopic training samples do not perfectly represent the photometric target data [[bibcode-2016arxiv160401339i|bibcode:2016arXiv160401339I]]. Recent work has explored incorporating soft physical constraints into CDE loss functions to regularize the neural networks, though current evidence suggests that simple, unconstrained data-driven baselines often perform comparably well [[bibcode-2023arxiv231013624e|bibcode:2023arXiv231013624E]].

**Dirichlet Process Mixtures**
In multi-messenger astronomy, Bayesian non-parametrics like the [[dirichlet-process-mixture|Dirichlet process mixture]] are critical for rapid parameter space exploration. Algorithms such as SKYFAST and FIGARO employ a Dirichlet Process Gaussian Mixture Model to analytically reconstruct the posterior distributions of gravitational wave events [[bibcode-2024arxiv240713695d|bibcode:2024arXiv240713695D]], [[bibcode-2025a-a-694a-245d|bibcode:2025A&A...694A.245D]]. By modeling the joint distribution of sky position, luminosity distance, and orbital inclination angle, these frameworks can generate highly accurate host galaxy localizations using only a small fraction ($\sim 10\%$) of the samples from full parameter estimation runs, vastly accelerating electromagnetic follow-up.

**Self-Organizing Maps and Flexible SEDs**
For highly complex [[sed-fitting|SED fitting]], unsupervised learning techniques like Self-Organizing Maps (SOMs) are increasingly employed to project high-dimensional wide-band photometry onto a 2D topological grid. This technique maps how physical properties—such as stellar mass, star formation rate, and age—vary across galaxies with similar spectral energy distributions [[bibcode-2025arxiv250604138a|bibcode:2025arXiv250604138A]]. SOMs have proven highly effective in bridging different photometric systems (e.g., JWST and CSST/Euclid) to robustly extract parameters without relying entirely on traditional templates [[bibcode-2025arxiv251000187w|bibcode:2025arXiv251000187W]]. Furthermore, replacing standard parametric star formation histories with flexible, non-parametric models (e.g., [[dense-basis-sfh|Dense Basis SFH]]) removes rigid priors, significantly improving the recovery of both broad-band fluxes and detailed spectroscopic features [[bibcode-2017apj-837-170l|bibcode:2017ApJ...837..170L]].

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Upcoming Stage-IV surveys, such as the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), the Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST), and the Euclid mission, will produce datasets containing billions of galaxies. Extracting physical parameters from these massive catalogs using traditional Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques is computationally prohibitive, often requiring tens to hundreds of CPU hours per galaxy [[bibcode-2022apj-938-11h|bibcode:2022ApJ...938...11H]]. To address these big data challenges, [[statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Statistical methods in astrophysics]] are rapidly shifting toward Simulation-Based Inference (SBI) and Amortized Neural Posterior Estimation (ANPE). These machine learning frameworks can evaluate complex, high-dimensional [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] in approximately one second per galaxy while maintaining accuracies comparable to traditional MCMC [[bibcode-2022apj-938-11h|bibcode:2022ApJ...938...11H]]. Tools such as PZFlow [[bibcode-2024aj-168-80c|bibcode:2024AJ....168...80C]] and GalSBI [[bibcode-2024arxiv241208722f|bibcode:2024arXiv241208722F]] provide probabilistic forward modeling to generate highly realistic mock catalogs, which are crucial for training and validating these neural density estimators. The massive influx of data also necessitates automated, reproducible workflows; the development of modular pipelines like TXPipe ensures end-to-end catalog-to-cosmology frameworks for joint weak lensing and galaxy clustering (3x2pt) analyses in next-generation surveys such as LSST [[bibcode-2023ojap-6e-13p|bibcode:2023OJAp....6E..13P]].

Another major frontier is the development of fully differentiable forward modeling pipelines. By utilizing auto-differentiation frameworks like JAX, researchers can construct end-to-end differentiable models of galaxy images and spectral data cubes. For example, the `pysersic` package leverages JAX for rapid Bayesian inference of structural parameters [[bibcode-2023joss-8-5703p|bibcode:2023JOSS....8.5703P]], while the RUBIX pipeline enables differentiable forward modeling of integral-field unit (IFU) data, calculating exact gradients with respect to particle-based simulation inputs [[bibcode-2025arxiv251117110s|bibcode:2025arXiv251117110S]]. These gradient-based approaches allow for highly efficient [[optimization-methods|Optimization methods]] and enable seamless integration with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo or neural network-based inference, moving parameter estimation beyond rigid analytical templates.

As statistical uncertainties shrink to sub-percent levels, [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] will be dominated by [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]]. A key open question is how to confidently reconcile parameters derived from differing photometric systems. A promising direction is cross-survey calibration, which leverages ultra-deep, high-resolution data from observatories like the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) to calibrate empirical models for wider, shallower surveys. For instance, hybrid machine learning techniques—such as combining Self-Organizing Maps with [[sed-fitting|SED fitting]]—have been used to train models on JWST/NIRCam and MIRI data. These calibrated models can then be applied to estimate stellar masses and star formation rates for forthcoming wide-field surveys like Euclid and the China Space Station Telescope (CSST), bypassing discrepancies between distinct photometric systems [[bibcode-2025arxiv251000187w|bibcode:2025arXiv251000187W]]. Another critical frontier is preparing for next-generation galaxy-scale strong lensing surveys. Forecasts indicate that upcoming missions, such as CSST, will wield immense cosmological constraining power, providing highly precise measurements of the matter density and the dark energy equation of state via strong lensing sample analyses [[bibcode-2025arxiv251108030w|bibcode:2025arXiv251108030W]].

Finally, future models must better capture the [[inherent-stochasticity|Inherent stochasticity]] of galaxy formation and evolution. Simplistic parametrizations of star formation histories and dust laws often fail to represent the true structural complexity of galaxies, leading to systematic parameter biases [[bibcode-2015apj-804-8a|bibcode:2015ApJ...804....8A]]. Determining the intrinsic dimensionality of the galaxy parameter space remains an active debate; while physical models grow increasingly complex, recent [[non-parametric-methods-in-astrophysics|Non-parametric methods in astrophysics]] studies suggest that wide-band photometry may only support the extraction of $4.3 \pm 0.5$ independent parameters for star-forming galaxies, fundamentally limiting the complexity of models that can be constrained without spectroscopy [[bibcode-2024arxiv240402962c|bibcode:2024arXiv240402962C]]. Furthermore, the assumption of ergodicity in galaxy populations is being challenged. Studies suggest that only "partial ergodicity" exists, meaning that ensemble averages of galaxy populations do not perfectly substitute for the evolutionary time-averages of individual galaxies [[bibcode-2024mnras-532-4774s|bibcode:2024MNRAS.532.4774S]]. Overcoming these conceptual limitations will require novel statistical approaches and a deeper understanding of the [[priors-in-astrophysics|Priors in astrophysics]] governing the cosmos.
