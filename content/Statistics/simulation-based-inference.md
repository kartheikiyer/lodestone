## Article

**Simulation-based inference** (SBI), frequently referred to as [[likelihood-free-inference|Likelihood-free inference]], is a highly flexible statistical framework used to infer physical parameters from complex observational data when the underlying likelihood function is analytically intractable. Standard Bayesian parameter estimation and [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] rely on evaluating an explicit likelihood function, $P(D|\theta)$, which maps physical parameters $\theta$ to the observed data $D$. In modern astrophysics and cosmology, however, this true likelihood is often unknown or computationally prohibitive to evaluate due to the highly non-linear nature of structure formation, complex baryonic physics, and intricate observational selection effects. Traditionally, analyses have resorted to assuming Gaussian likelihoods—an approximation that fails to capture non-linear and non-Gaussian regimes [Hahn et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract), [Bellomo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...10..016B/abstract). SBI circumvents this bottleneck by implicitly defining the likelihood through stochastic forward modeling. By generating high-fidelity mock data from fundamental physical principles—such as large-scale $N$-body simulations or hydrodynamical galaxy models—SBI connects theoretical parameters directly to observables, enabling rigorous parameter estimation in previously inaccessible regimes.

## Historical Evolution: From ABC to Neural Amortization

The historical development of SBI in astrophysics began with distance-based [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]] (ABC). In traditional ABC, a rejection-sampling technique is used to draw posterior samples: parameters $\theta$ are sampled from a prior, a forward generative model simulates mock data $x_{\rm mock}$, and the sample is accepted if the distance between the simulated and observed summary statistics falls below a specified tolerance threshold [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract). Early efforts successfully deployed ABC algorithms like COSMOABC to infer cosmological parameters from galaxy cluster counts [Ishida et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&C....13....1I/abstract), and utilized Population Monte Carlo importance sampling to constrain the halo occupation distribution (HOD) of galaxies [Hahn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2791H/abstract).

While mathematically rigorous, ABC is constrained by the "curse of dimensionality." As the dimensionality of the parameter space or the summary statistics increases, the acceptance rate of simulated samples plummets, requiring a computationally prohibitive number of expensive forward simulations. During this evolution, intermediate [[likelihood-free-inference|likelihood-free approaches]] such as the SELFI algorithm were developed to linearize black-box forward models, enabling the inference of the primordial power spectrum extending into non-linear scales [Leclercq et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.4237L/abstract). To fully overcome the computational bottleneck, the field underwent a paradigm shift driven by deep learning. Modern approaches employ Amortized [[neural-posterior-estimation|Neural posterior estimation]] (ANPE) and neural ratio estimation, utilizing highly expressive architectures like normalizing flows to directly approximate the posterior distribution $p(\theta | x)$. The primary advantage of ANPE is *amortization*: the computationally expensive simulation step is performed upfront to generate a comprehensive training set. Once trained, evaluating the posterior for new observational data requires only a single forward pass, sidestepping the need for additional simulations during inference and accelerating parameter estimation by factors of $10^3$ to $10^6$ compared to traditional Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods [Alsing et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..249....5A/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract).

## Foundational Generative Models: Simulations and Galaxy Bias

To bypass explicit analytical likelihoods, SBI relies on the ability to forward-model realistic mock observations. At the cosmological scale, this necessitates generative models that map primordial initial conditions to observable galaxy distributions. 

The backbone of these forward models consists of $N$-body simulations, such as the QUIJOTE or MillenniumTNG suites, which capture the non-linear gravitational evolution of dark matter [Hernández-Aguayo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2556H/abstract). Because running tens of thousands of large-volume simulations is computationally expensive, techniques such as emulator-based scaling and control variates have been developed. Control variates can effectively increase the simulation volume by a factor of up to 100 in the non-linear regime [Doytcheva et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..184D/abstract). Other significant efforts include the CosmoGridV1 suite, which provides extensive lightcone simulations specifically designed for map-level inference and the training of machine learning models across the $w$CDM parameter space [Kacprzak et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...02..050K/abstract). Additionally, the GLASS code facilitates the creation of deep, high-resolution lightcone simulations by iteratively building nested shells of matter, weak lensing signals, and galaxy populations [Tessore et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..11T/abstract).

While hydrodynamical simulations capture baryonic physics directly, their high computational cost generally precludes their direct use in massive parameter sweeps. Instead, the galaxy-halo connection is typically bridged using parameterized prescriptive models like the Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD), semi-analytic models (SAM), or subhalo abundance matching (SHAM). Modern frameworks, such as SIMBIG, combine high-resolution $N$-body simulations with flexible HOD models to extract cosmological constraints from non-linear scales using [[neural-posterior-estimation|neural density estimators]] [Hahn et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...04..010H/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109h3534H/abstract). Additionally, hybrid forward models—such as those based on the Aemulus suite—combine dark matter dynamics with analytic Lagrangian bias expansions to simultaneously model galaxy clustering and weak lensing [Kokron et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.1422K/abstract). 

Furthermore, image-to-image machine learning architectures, such as U-Nets, are increasingly used to "paint" baryonic properties (e.g., gas density and X-ray flux) directly onto dark matter-only simulations by training on full-physics suites like IllustrisTNG [Chadayammuri et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.2812C/abstract). Recent architectures like CHARM utilize multi-stage neural spline flows to map computationally inexpensive low-resolution dark matter fields into high-fidelity mock halo catalogs [Pandey et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240909124P/abstract). Phenomenological frameworks like [[galsbi|GalSBI]] also utilize SBI to construct galaxy population models, constraining galaxy population parameters and redshift distributions by matching forward-modeled deep field images directly to observations [Fischbacher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...06..007F/abstract).

## Machine Learning Architectures for Density Estimation

The mapping between physical parameters $\theta$ and observables $x$ is typically learned using specific deep learning architectures. 

* **Normalizing Flows:** These are the most ubiquitous architectures for density estimation in SBI. They operate by applying a series of invertible, differentiable transformations to a simple base distribution to model complex, highly non-Gaussian target posteriors. Within cosmological clustering and spectral energy distribution (SED) [[model-fitting|Model fitting]], flow-based emulators regularly achieve percent-level accuracy at a fraction of the traditional computational cost [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221100723H/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract).
* **Neural Compression for Weak Lensing:** Deep learning is actively used to compress complex datasets into informative low-dimensional summaries prior to density estimation. The analysis of Dark Energy Survey (DES) Year 3 weak-lensing maps using SBI and neural compression of peak counts and power spectra achieved a factor of two improvement in dark energy parameter precision compared to standard power spectrum methods [Jeffrey et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240302314J/abstract).
* **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs):** While normalizing flows typically operate on compressed summary statistics or structured grids, GNNs are explicitly designed for irregular, sparse data. By treating galaxies as nodes and their spatial relationships as edges, GNNs preserve the full 3D phase-space information of the cosmic web. GNNs applied to $(25\,h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc})^3$ volumes containing $\sim 1000$ galaxies can infer the matter density parameter $\Omega_m$ with approximately 12% precision using only 3D positions and radial velocities, improving to 4-8% when internal galaxy properties are included [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract), [de Santi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952...69D/abstract). Bayesian GNNs have also been successfully applied to hierarchical weak lensing inference, estimating convergence from photometric sightlines with an $H_0$ error budget under 1% [Park et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953..178P/abstract).
* **Diffusion Models for Point Clouds:** Recent developments include diffusion-based generative models that treat galaxies directly as a collection of 3D points. This point-cloud approach enables field-level emulation and conditional likelihood computation without the need for discrete voxelization, preserving spatial continuity [Cuesta-Lazaro et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109l3531C/abstract).
* **Neural Ratio Estimation (NRE):** For problems requiring marginalization over thousands of nuisance parameters—such as inferring host-galaxy dust extinction and absolute magnitudes from Supernovae Ia light curves while marginalizing over $\sim 4000$ latent variables—methods like Truncated Marginal Neural Ratio Estimation (TMNRE) reframe density estimation as a classification task, implicitly learning the likelihood-to-evidence ratio [Karchev et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.3881K/abstract). In strong gravitational lensing, this methodology enables the direct measurement of dark matter substructure from pixel-level data without requiring explicit likelihood evaluations [Anau Montel et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.2746A/abstract).

## Computational Acceleration and Differentiable Pipelines

Recent advances in SBI leverage surrogate emulators and differentiable programming to drastically accelerate calculations. Neural network emulators are increasingly used to replace numerically expensive components of forward models. For instance, the COSMOPOWER suite of neural emulators rapidly evaluates matter and Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) power spectra, delivering evaluation speedups of $\mathcal{O}(10^4)$ for Bayesian parameter estimation [Spurio Mancini et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.1771S/abstract). Similarly, the `matryoshka` suite introduces EFTEMU, a neural-network emulator explicitly designed to accelerate Effective Field Theory of Large-Scale Structure (EFTofLSS) analyses of galaxy power spectrum multipoles by three orders of magnitude [Donald-McCann et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.3106D/abstract). 

In parallel, the transition to differentiable programming frameworks—most notably JAX—has transformed the construction of inference pipelines. JAX allows researchers to utilize automatic differentiation to compute exact, analytic gradients of numerical models with respect to their input parameters, bypassing the computational expense of finite-difference approximations.

Access to exact gradients unlocks highly efficient, gradient-based sampling algorithms such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) and variational inference. For instance, the `jax-cosmo` library provides an end-to-end differentiable pipeline for computing background evolution and angular power spectra, allowing for exact, tuning-free Fisher matrix evaluations [Campagne et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..15C/abstract). Similarly, the Differentiable Stellar Population Synthesis (DSPS) package implements empirical stellar metallicity and dust attenuation models natively in JAX, achieving evaluation speedups of $300$ to $400$ on GPUs [Hearin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1741H/abstract). Tools like `PyBird-JAX` embed neural network emulators within a differentiable JAX framework to compute one-loop effective field theory predictions in $0.2$ ms on a GPU, representing a $10^3$ to $10^4$ speedup over its non-differentiable predecessor [Reeves et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250720990R/abstract). Overcoming the discrete nature of galaxy population schemes, researchers have also implemented differentiable stochastic sampling and reinforcement learning techniques to enable gradient-based inference (such as HMC) over stochastic Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD) models [Horowitz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.2473H/abstract). Tools like 21CMEMU provide emulators for reionization-era galaxy and intergalactic medium (IGM) properties, achieving sub-percent accuracy and evaluation speedups of $10^4$ [Breitman et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.9833B/abstract).

To maximize information extraction without relying solely on expensive large-volume simulations, frameworks such as Hybrid SBI (HySBI) combine simulation-based inference on non-linear small scales with standard perturbation theory on large scales [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250513591Z/abstract). Furthermore, to handle computational constraints when the number of available simulations is limited, shrinkage estimators like NERCOME have been shown to significantly improve the stability of the cosmological precision matrix, effectively reducing stochastic shifts in parameter posteriors [Looijmans et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240213783L/abstract).

## Extracting Non-Gaussian Information from the Cosmic Web

Late-time gravitational evolution drives structure formation into the non-linear regime, transforming the initially Gaussian primordial density field into a highly non-Gaussian cosmic web. Standard analytical techniques, such as perturbation theory, are restricted to quasi-linear scales. By utilizing high-fidelity forward models to map the relationship between cosmological parameters and mock data, SBI allows for the robust extraction of information from deeply non-linear scales (e.g., $k > 0.25 \, h/\mathrm{Mpc}$).

Analyses utilizing the SIMBIG forward-modeling framework demonstrate that SBI yields constraints on the clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$ that are 27% more precise than standard two-point power spectrum analyses, effectively providing the statistical equivalent of analyzing a 60% larger survey volume [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221100723H/abstract). Specific non-Gaussian statistics evaluated within this framework include:

* **Bispectrum and Skew Spectra:** Including the bispectrum monopole yields constraints on $\sigma_8$ that are $2.4\times$ tighter, and on the matter density $\Omega_m$ that are $1.2\times$ tighter, than the power spectrum alone [Hahn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109h3534H/abstract). Redshift-space skew spectra effectively compress this information, improving bounds on $\Omega_m$, $\Omega_b$, and the spectral index $n_s$ by 10% to 35% [Hou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109j3528H/abstract).
* **Line Correlation Function (LCF):** As a phase-sensitive statistic, the LCF accesses spatial information inaccessible to two-point statistics, successfully breaking the degeneracy between linear galaxy bias and the clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$ [Ali et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.2743A/abstract).
* **Marked Power Spectra:** Weighting the galaxy field by its local environmental density before computing the power spectrum improves $\sigma_8$ constraints by a factor of $1.2$ relative to standard perturbation theory [Massara et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240404228M/abstract).
* **$k$-th Nearest Neighbor (kNN) Statistics:** The use of kNN statistics provides a robust way to model non-linear galaxy clustering down to transverse scales of $3 \, h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$, yielding percent-level constraints on $\sigma_8$ [Yuan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.1993Y/abstract).
* **Wavelet Scattering Transform (WST):** Multiscale topological descriptors like the WST systematically extract highly non-linear phase information, tightening $\sigma_8$ constraints by up to a factor of $3.1$ over standard methods [Pierre et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250703086P/abstract).
* **Betti Curves and Persistent Homology:** Multiscale topological descriptors like Betti curves complement traditional clustering statistics by capturing the connectivity of voids and filaments, effectively breaking cosmological parameter degeneracies [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251207236L/abstract).
* **Neural Compression:** 3D Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) can automatically learn optimal data compressions directly from the galaxy field, yielding $\sigma_8$ constraints $2.65\times$ tighter than baseline approaches [Lemos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231015256L/abstract).

## Field-Level Inference and Information Field Theory

In contrast to summarizing the 3D galaxy distribution, **field-level inference (FLI)** directly evaluates the likelihood of the entire observed 3D density field, natively capturing the complete non-Gaussian information encoded in the cosmic web [Schmidt et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...01..042S/abstract), [Nguyen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240303220N/abstract). These forward-modeling methods are theoretically grounded in Information Field Theory (IFT), which provides a rigorous mathematical framework for reconstructing continuous signals from discrete, noisy observations by minimizing the Gibbs free energy of the posterior distribution [Enßlin et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvE..82e1112E/abstract). 

The fundamental power of FLI lies in a proven theoretical link: utilizing an $m$-th order forward model in field-level inference automatically incorporates the physical information equivalent to analyzing all $n$-point correlation functions up to order $m+1$ [Schmidt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250415351S/abstract). FLI treats primordial density fluctuations as a high-dimensional set of latent variables that must be jointly inferred alongside global cosmological parameters, resulting in parameter spaces with millions of dimensions. This requires advanced samplers like HMC [Jasche et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407...29J/abstract) or neural optimization frameworks like LULO, which enable gradient-free FLI even when using non-differentiable structure formation codes [Doeser et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.542.1403D/abstract). 

A prominent implementation of this philosophy is the Bayesian Origin Reconstruction from Galaxies (BORG) algorithm. BORG assimilates galaxy survey data to simultaneously infer cosmological parameters, reconstruct 3D primordial matter fluctuations, and derive late-time velocity fields [Jasche et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...625A..64J/abstract), [Ramanah et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...621A..69R/abstract). Similarly, the COSMIC BIRTH algorithm utilizes Gibbs sampling and augmented Lagrangian perturbation theory to map galaxies in a light-cone back to their primordial Lagrangian positions, enabling efficient dark matter field reconstructions [Kitaura et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502.3456K/abstract). The Manticore-Local project successfully utilized BORG to generate a detailed Bayesian reconstruction of the local universe ($z \lesssim 0.05$), reproducing prominent structures like the Coma and Virgo clusters within $1^\circ$ of their observed sky positions [McAlpine et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540..716M/abstract). Furthermore, extending FLI to perform joint density and velocity reconstruction—utilizing tracers such as the kinematic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (kSZ) effect or supernovae—significantly enhances the recovery of primordial initial conditions compared to analyzing density fields alone [Bayer et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...06..046B/abstract). 

FLI has also been augmented by Effective Field Theory (EFT) frameworks, such as the `LEFTfield` code. Analyses using `LEFTfield` have demonstrated a factor of $3.5$ to $5.2$ improvement in the precision of $\sigma_8$ compared to standard analyses combining the power spectrum and bispectrum on the exact same data modes [Nguyen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240303220N/abstract). Furthermore, field-level EFT frameworks have been extended to redshift space in the `LEFTfield` code, demonstrating that a systematic expansion of velocity bias is required for unbiased inference of the growth rate [Stadler et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...10..069S/abstract). Joint field-level inference of the Baryon Acoustic Oscillation (BAO) scale reduces measurement errors by up to a factor of $1.6$ [Babić et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...11..066B/abstract). Further expanding FLI capabilities, the noise-field formulation within EFT has been developed to effectively capture non-Gaussian and density-dependent galaxy stochasticity directly at the field level [Rubira et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251105484R/abstract).

## Diverse Applications: From Stellar Populations to Cluster Masses

Beyond cosmological parameter estimation, SBI is increasingly used to infer the physical properties of individual objects and complex multi-wavelength populations.

* **Galaxy SED Fitting & Populations:** Amortized [[neural-posterior-estimation|Neural posterior estimation]] (NPE) and emulator-based frameworks have revolutionized Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) [[model-fitting|Model fitting]]. By training neural estimators on high-fidelity stellar population synthesis models, NPE achieves speedups of $10^3$ to $10^6$, reducing the inference time for parameters such as stellar mass, metallicity, and star formation history from hours down to milliseconds per object [Alsing et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..249....5A/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...11H/abstract). These efficiency gains enable full Bayesian population-level inference on catalogs containing $10^5$ galaxies [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167...16L/abstract). Additionally, tools like **pop-cosmos** leverage score-based diffusion models alongside emulators (such as SPECULATOR) to achieve fast posterior sampling for hundreds of thousands of individual galaxies, creating data-driven population models [Thorp et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240619437T/abstract). However, the literature cautions that the choice of underlying models and prior distributions can still introduce systematic offsets, sometimes altering inferred stellar masses by $\sim 0.2$ dex [Iglesias-Navarro et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250604336I/abstract). Furthermore, SBI workflows can reliably infer non-parametric star formation histories and metallicities directly from optical absorption spectra, performing fits five orders of magnitude faster than traditional inversion codes [Iglesias-Navarro et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A..58I/abstract).
* **Combined Probes and Weak Lensing:** In multi-probe cosmology, deep-learning frameworks such as DeepLSS jointly analyze cosmic shear and galaxy clustering maps. By extracting non-Gaussian features, DeepLSS significantly decorrelates the structure growth parameter $S_8$ from the intrinsic alignment amplitude, breaking degeneracies present in standard analyses [Kacprzak et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvX..12c1029K/abstract).
* **Galaxy Cluster Mass Estimation:** SBI provides a robust alternative for determining cluster masses, bypassing the high scatter of explicit mass-observable scaling relations. 3D convolutional neural networks and normalizing flows have been deployed to infer dynamical cluster masses directly from the projected phase-space distributions of member galaxies. These networks natively marginalize over interloper galaxies, achieving an overall logarithmic residual scatter as low as $0.028$ dex—a nearly four-fold improvement over classical scaling relations [Kodi Ramanah et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.4080K/abstract), [Kodi Ramanah et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.1985K/abstract). Furthermore, SBI can bypass mass estimation entirely; for example, CNNs have been utilized to infer cosmological parameters directly from X-ray observable diagrams (such as count rates and hardness ratios) [Kosiba et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240906001K/abstract). Moving to direct cosmological applications, the first application of SBI to real galaxy cluster data was performed on the Planck MMF3 sample, demonstrating that cluster count cosmology can be conducted entirely without resorting to [[likelihood-free-inference|explicit likelihoods]] [Zubeldia et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.112h3536Z/abstract).
* **Strong Gravitational Lensing:** Formulating an explicit likelihood for pixel-level lensing data is computationally intractable when attempting to marginalize over thousands of "subthreshold" dark matter subhaloes. Hierarchical SBI and TMNRE directly extract the subhalo mass function from image data, placing tight constraints on the warm dark matter cutoff scale and the overall abundance of substructure to a precision of $\mathcal{O}(10)\%$ [Brehmer et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...886...49B/abstract), [Anau Montel et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.2746A/abstract). Furthermore, NPE provides automated modeling of strongly lensed quasars, yielding constraints such as a population mean mass density slope of $\gamma_{\rm lens} = 2.13 \pm 0.06$ [Erickson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241010123E/abstract). In a related domain, astrometric datasets have been analyzed with SBI to detect global lensing signatures of dark matter substructure, showing enhanced sensitivity compared to traditional two-point correlation statistics [Mishra-Sharma et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MLS&T...3aLT03M/abstract).
* **21-cm Signal and Reionization:** SBI is increasingly applied to the cosmic dawn and epoch of reionization. By mapping fundamental parameters to the 21-cm power spectrum, SBI can provide accurate posteriors with an order of magnitude fewer simulations than classical explicit likelihood methods [Prelogović et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.4239P/abstract).
* **Galactic Archaeology:** In the study of the Milky Way's formation, SBI bridges chemical evolution models with observations. Tools like CASBI and the combination of galactic chemical evolution (GCE) models like CHEMPY with NPE enable rigorous constraints on the Milky Way's merger history and initial mass function (IMF) slope from stellar chemical abundances [Viterbo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241117269V/abstract), [Buck et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250302456B/abstract). At the Local Group scale, SBI has been used to infer the combined mass of the Milky Way and M31 from *Gaia* and HST data, yielding a total mass estimate of approximately 4.6 trillion solar masses while accounting for non-Gaussian observational errors [Lemos et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.103b3009L/abstract).
* **Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs):** SBI has recently been applied to FRBs to constrain the dispersion measure-redshift relation and host galaxy contributions by modeling electron density fields [Konar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241007084K/abstract).
* **Gravitational Wave Populations:** SBI accommodates the complex selection effects inherent in gravitational wave (GW) astronomy. Packages such as ICAROGW infer the mass and redshift distributions of compact binary coalescences (CBCs) alongside cosmological parameters from noisy, incomplete, and heterogeneous GW event catalogs [Mastrogiovanni et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230517973M/abstract).

## Handling Survey Realism and Model Misspecification

Translating SBI from idealized mock catalogs to real observational data requires a rigorous treatment of survey geometry, selection functions, and measurement noise. Unlike traditional analytical approaches—which may rely on simplified error models that bias estimates in Stage-IV surveys [Bellomo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...10..016B/abstract), [Bernal et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...10..017B/abstract)—SBI natively incorporates complex systematics directly into the forward model. 

To address internal modeling uncertainties, methodologies have been developed to propagate neural network approximation errors using validation-phase statistics, ensuring that amortized inference remains unbiased even with imperfect theory emulators [Grandón et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022OJAp....5E..12G/abstract). However, a major bottleneck in applying SBI to real astronomical surveys is that observations are frequently heterogeneous, featuring varying noise properties, transient observing conditions, or missing data. Standard [[neural-posterior-estimation|NPE]] requires the training data to perfectly match the target's noise characteristics. To bypass this, methodologies like SBI++ accommodate out-of-distribution (OOD) measurement errors and missing data by marginalizing over nearby realizations within the training set, recovering accurate posteriors without requiring completely new custom-noise simulations [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221103747W/abstract), [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952L..10W/abstract). Another approach to handling diverse data sets involves embedding explicit external likelihoods—such as those from the Planck CMB legacy analysis—directly into SBI frameworks via effective simulators, enabling coherent global cosmological scans alongside Stage-IV galaxy surveys [Abellán et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722990A/abstract).

Despite these advances, the most critical open challenge for modern SBI is **model misspecification**. Because parameter posteriors are implicitly derived from forward-modeled training data, the inference is fundamentally bottlenecked by the physical fidelity of the underlying simulations. Several studies have demonstrated that SBI networks can be highly sensitive to the specific subgrid baryonic prescriptions employed during training. For instance, networks trained on one hydrodynamical suite (like IllustrisTNG) may yield significantly biased constraints when tested on another (like SIMBA) [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929..132V/abstract). This vulnerability is exacerbated when observational data exhibits model misspecification, under which the inferred posterior can become highly sensitive to the specific neural network architecture (e.g., the normalizing flow) used for the inference [Régaldo-Saint Blancard et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109h3535R/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions in the Stage-IV Era

To ensure reliable parameter recovery as SBI scales to Stage-IV precision levels (e.g., Euclid, DESI, LSST), ongoing methodological development is focused on validating robustness and ensuring domain generalization. As Stage-IV surveys begin delivering data, comprehensive pipelines such as the Cosmology Likelihood for Observables in Euclid (CLOE) are being developed as primary inference frameworks to seamlessly execute joint 3x2pt analyses and manage the associated complex systematics [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251009118E/abstract).

* **Domain Generalization:** To mitigate OOD vulnerabilities, research has shown that machine learning models can generalize across different hydrodynamic simulations without performance loss [de Santi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952...69D/abstract). Separately, techniques such as semantic alignment losses are being employed to map features between fast approximate gravity solvers (e.g., L-PICOLA) and high-fidelity $N$-body mocks, bridging the gap between inexpensive simulations and accurate physical models [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240902256L/abstract). Other strategies dynamically identify OOD observations at inference time by discarding components of the summary statistic that exhibit inconsistency across simulators [Pierre et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250703086P/abstract). 
* **Validation Protocols:** Rigorous validation pipelines, such as **simulation-based calibration**, are now routinely employed to verify coverage probabilities across the prior volume [Régaldo-Saint Blancard et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109h3535R/abstract). Cross-validation on independent mock catalogs—and community-wide blind challenges like the SIMBIG mock challenge [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...04..010H/abstract) and the "Beyond-2pt" project [ThBeyond-2pt Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990...99B/abstract)—serve as critical stress tests.
* **Scaling and Data Compression:** Fully exploiting massive cosmic volumes remains computationally demanding. Solutions involve compressing raw field-level data into low-dimensional representations via 3D CNNs or GNNs [Lemos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231015256L/abstract), or applying Marginal Neural Ratio Estimation to summary statistics that have been pre-compressed using techniques like Principal Component Analysis (PCA) [Franco-Abellán et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...11..057F/abstract). Other areas of exploration include advanced microcanonical samplers (e.g., MCLMC) to navigate $\geq 10^6$-dimensional parameter spaces [Simon-Onfroy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250420130S/abstract), and utilizing hybrid approaches that balance explicit field-level analysis with neural amortization. Resolving the tension between extracting maximal non-linear information and guaranteeing robustness against theoretical uncertainty defines the frontier of modern simulation-based cosmology.
