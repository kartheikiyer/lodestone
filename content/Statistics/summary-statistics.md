#
In cosmology and the study of the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]], **summary statistics** are essential mathematical tools used to compress the massive, high-dimensional data of modern [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Galaxy Surveys and Catalogs]] into tractable, lower-dimensional representations. Because the exact three-dimensional positions and properties of millions of galaxies cannot be directly evaluated in a traditional likelihood function, researchers rely on these metrics to quantify spatial clustering and extract fundamental [[cosmological-parameters-and-frameworks|Cosmological Parameters and Frameworks]], such as the matter density $\Omega_m$ and the amplitude of density fluctuations [[f-8|fσ8]]. Historically, the foundational "workhorses" of this data compression have been standard two-point statistics: the [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] in configuration space and its Fourier transform, the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] [Peebles et al. (1974)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1974A&A....32..197P/abstract), [Bernardeau et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhR...367....1B/abstract). Because primordial density perturbations are presumed to be [[nearly-scale-invariant-adiabatic-primordial-fluctuations|nearly Gaussian]], these two-point metrics perfectly describe the initial density field and remain relatively easy to model analytically on large, linear scales where independent Fourier modes do not strongly couple.

However, as the Universe evolves, late-time [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]] induces significant mode-coupling, driving the cosmic density field away from Gaussianity. Consequently, standard two-point statistics fail to capture the critical phase information locked in the non-linear regime. To extract this remaining information and break persistent degeneracies—such as the confounding relationship between linear [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy bias]] and $\sigma_8$—cosmologists employ "beyond-two-point" summary statistics [Martínez et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002sgd..book.....M/abstract). These include [[higher-order-statistics-in-cosmology|Higher-Order Statistics in Cosmology]] like the [[matter-bispectrum|Matter bispectrum]] and the [[three-point-correlation-function|Three-point correlation function]], which are sensitive to both gravitationally induced non-Gaussianity and primordial non-Gaussian signatures (e.g., $f_{\rm NL}$). Additionally, topological and geometric descriptors, most notably [[minkowski-functionals|Minkowski functionals]] and the [[void-probability-function|Void probability function]], provide complementary sensitivity to the morphological features of the cosmic web, rigorously characterizing the complex connectivity of filaments, nodes, and voids.

Translating these abstract summary statistics into precise cosmological constraints requires sophisticated modeling frameworks, ranging from [[standard-perturbation-theory|Standard Perturbation Theory]] on large scales to the phenomenological halo model on highly non-linear scales. Observers must also carefully account for [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]], finite survey geometries, and observational noise by constructing an accurate [[analytical-covariance-matrix|Analytical covariance matrix]]. Looking to the future, the unprecedented data volumes of Stage IV surveys are driving a paradigm shift toward novel metrics, such as $k$-th nearest neighbor statistics and marked power spectra, alongside simulation-based [[cosmological-inference|Cosmological inference]] powered by machine learning. This article explores the theoretical foundations, historical evolution, and observational systematics of cosmological summary statistics, highlighting pressing open questions in achieving lossless data compression [Tegmark et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...480...22T/abstract) and mitigating effects like [[super-sample-covariance|Super-sample covariance]].

## Foundations of Summary Statistics in Large-Scale Structure

Modern [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|galaxy surveys]] observe the three-dimensional spatial distribution of millions of luminous objects, mapping the underlying [[three-dimensional-cosmic-density-fields|Three-dimensional cosmic density fields]]. The raw observational data—a high-dimensional catalog of coordinates, redshifts, and galaxy properties—is computationally intractable for direct likelihood evaluation. Consequently, the field of [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|large-scale structure cosmology]] relies on rigorous data compression techniques to distill this vast parameter space into lower-dimensional metrics known as summary statistics. These statistics must capture the maximum possible cosmological information to constrain fundamental parameters.

The standard theoretical and observational "workhorses" of large-scale structure analysis are two-point statistics: the [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] (2PCF), denoted $\xi(r)$, and its Fourier conjugate, the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]], $P(k)$ [Bernardeau et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhR...367....1B/abstract). The 2PCF quantifies the spatial clustering of the Universe by measuring the excess probability, relative to a uniform random distribution, of finding two galaxies separated by a given comoving distance $r$. While $\xi(r)$ provides an intuitive measure of spatial clustering, it is often mathematically advantageous to analyze the power spectrum on large scales. Because initial density perturbations are presumed to be Gaussian, and because independent Fourier modes evolve independently in the [[linear-growth-rate|linear regime]], $P(k)$ amplitudes on large scales are uncoupled from small-scale nonlinearities, making them easier to model analytically. To ensure these foundational assumptions hold in practice, cosmologists employ tools such as multipole vectors to rigorously test the fundamental assumption of statistical isotropy in the observed large-scale structure [Zunckel et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvD..84d3005Z/abstract).

Translating raw survey catalogs into these summary statistics requires the careful application of [[statistical-estimators-in-astronomy|Statistical estimators in astronomy]]. Robust estimators for the 2PCF, such as the widely adopted Landy-Szalay estimator, are designed to mitigate the effects of complex survey geometries and selection functions by comparing the data pair counts to simulated random catalogs. However, these estimators can still introduce biases depending on catalog normalization and the finite depth of the sample [Kerscher et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999A&A...343..333K/abstract). 

While two-point statistics provide remarkably tight constraints on standard parameters, they are fundamentally incomplete descriptions of the late-time Universe. Non-linear gravitational collapse induces non-Gaussianity, transferring critical cosmological information out of the power spectrum and into higher-order moments [Wolk et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454..560W/abstract). Relying solely on two-point statistics limits the ability to break degeneracies between the [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy bias]] and the amplitude of matter fluctuations. Extracting the remaining information requires moving beyond standard estimators to explore [[higher-order-statistics-in-cosmology|Higher-Order Statistics in Cosmology]], thereby driving the ongoing evolution of data compression strategies in astrophysics.

## Historical Evolution: From Scaling Laws to Precision Cosmology

The historical development of summary statistics in cosmology mirrors the evolution of observational data, transitioning from early two-dimensional [[angular-correlation-and-clustering|angular catalogs]] to the massive three-dimensional maps of modern precision cosmology.

Before the advent of extensive redshift measurements, the quantification of the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] relied primarily on 2D angular counts. Early analyses utilizing the angular covariance function revealed a simple [[power-law-distribution|power law]] over a wide range of scales, showing no natural spatial division between groups, clusters, and superclusters [Peebles et al. (1974)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1974A&A....32..197P/abstract). This unbroken power-law behavior led to the hypothesis of [[universal-clustering-law|scaling laws]] and [[self-similarity-astrophysics|self-similar clustering]] in the galaxy distribution. By rendering the cluster-cluster correlation function dimensionless, researchers found evidence for scale invariance in the clustering process, alongside stronger gravitational enhancements on smaller scales for individual galaxies [Szalay et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985Natur.314..718S/abstract). 

These scaling laws fueled significant interest in [[fractal-analysis-in-cosmology|Fractal Analysis in Cosmology]]. A persistent debate emerged regarding the exact scale at which the [[universe|Universe]] transitions from a highly structured, fractal-like state to the uniformity demanded by the cosmological principle. While the cosmological principle formally precludes a purely self-similar fractal structure on the largest scales, early data established that the galaxy distribution exhibits [[multifractal-approach|fractal-like behavior]] (with a fractal dimension $D \approx 2$) up to at least $20 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Joyce et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...443...11J/abstract). Extensive reviews have chronicled this historical debate, detailing the scaling laws, the fractal-like behavior of cosmic structure, and the physical transitions they represent in modern astrophysics [Jones et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004RvMP...76.1211J/abstract). Subsequent analyses of the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) clarified the ambiguity, demonstrating a convergence to a well-defined [[homogeneity-scale|Homogeneity scale]] in volumes roughly equivalent to a sphere of radius $70 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Joyce et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...443...11J/abstract).

The field was revolutionized by the CfA Redshift Survey, which provided the first comprehensive 3D maps of the [[low-redshift-universe|local universe]] [Huchra et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ASPC....5...41H/abstract). The shift to 3D allowed researchers to quantify previously obscured morphological features. Early 3D summary statistics were deployed to measure voids of $30-50 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ and coherent dense structures spanning $\sim 100 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ using tools like the [[void-probability-function|Void probability function]] and topological genus [Vogeley et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PhDT........23V/abstract). During this era, geometric clustering metrics, including [[minkowski-functionals|Minkowski functionals]] and the [[minimal-spanning-tree|Minimal spanning tree]], were introduced to objectively quantify the filamentary and sheet-like geometries of the cosmic web [Krzewina et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.278..869K/abstract).

As [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Galaxy Surveys and Catalogs]] expanded to gigaparsec volumes (e.g., SDSS, BOSS), the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] and the [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] cemented their roles as the primary workhorses of precision cosmology. These 2-point statistics placed tight constraints on fundamental parameters, such as bounding the [[nearly-scale-invariant-adiabatic-primordial-fluctuations|spectral index]] of primordial fluctuations to roughly $0.6 < n < 1.1$ [Lyth et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995pcc..conf..255L/abstract). 

However, because the late-time density field is highly non-Gaussian due to [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]], it became evident that 2-point statistics alone miss a significant fraction of available cosmological information [Sefusatti et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvD..74b3522S/abstract). This realization has driven the modern frontier of summary statistics toward "beyond-2pt" observables—such as the [[three-point-correlation-function|Three-point correlation function]] and nearest neighbor distances—which break parameter degeneracies and extract vital non-linear phase information from contemporary 3D surveys [Yuan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.6283Y/abstract).

## Standard Two-Point Statistics and Redshift-Space Distortions

The foundation of modern large-scale structure analysis rests on standard two-point statistics: the [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] $\xi(r)$ in configuration space and its Fourier transform, the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] $P(k)$. While both statistics encode the fundamental clustering properties of a [[gaussian-statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Gaussian random field]], $P(k)$ is often preferred for extracting cosmological parameters on large scales. Because the density field remains in the linear regime on these scales, different Fourier modes evolve independently. Conversely, the amplitude of the correlation function at any given separation $r$ is affected by the coupling of many different modes, making $P(k)$ less susceptible to small-scale non-linearities and easier to interpret theoretically.

Observations of the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] are inherently conducted in redshift space. Peculiar velocities of galaxies along the line of sight perturb observed redshifts, inducing [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]] (RSD). This mapping introduces a distinct anisotropy into the observed clustering signal. On large scales, coherent bulk flows toward overdensities artificially enhance the clustering amplitude (the Kaiser effect), while on small, non-linear scales, rapid virialized motions within [[mass-distribution-and-functions|dark matter halos]] heavily suppress power along the line of sight, creating the so-called "Fingers-of-God" (FoG) effect [Okumura et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JCAP...11..014O/abstract). To leverage large-scale velocity information in real space, advanced reconstruction techniques have been deployed to recover the underlying [[three-dimensional-cosmic-density-fields|three-dimensional velocity and mass-density fields]] directly from peculiar velocity catalogs [Dekel et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997gsr..proc..245D/abstract).

The standard theoretical framework for modeling RSD in two-point statistics is the streaming model, which maps real-space clustering to redshift space using the [[probability-density-function-cosmology|Probability density function (cosmology)]] (PDF) of line-of-sight pairwise peculiar velocities. High-resolution N-body simulations have demonstrated that the traditional Gaussian ansatz for this velocity PDF is inaccurate on small scales. Instead, a mixture of Gaussians provides a significantly better fit and vastly outperforms simple approximations when modeling the [[redshift-space-correlation-function|Redshift-space correlation function]] [Kuruvilla et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.2256K/abstract). Because RSD models are highly sensitive to small-scale dynamics, improperly modeled FoG effects can introduce severe systematic biases in cosmological parameter estimates [Leicht et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.2137L/abstract).

To isolate and analyze the anisotropic signal, the [[two-dimensional-power-spectrum|Two-dimensional power spectrum]] $P(k,\mu)$, where $\mu$ is the cosine of the angle to the line of sight, is typically decomposed via a [[multipole-expansion|Multipole expansion]] into monopole, quadrupole, and hexadecapole moments. Crucially, the choice of multipole expansion basis—such as utilizing associated [[legendre-multipoles|Legendre multipoles]] instead of standard ones—significantly impacts the convergence and information recovery when modeling anisotropic signals. The anisotropies captured by these multipoles are driven not only by RSD but also by the [[alcock-paczynski-test|Alcock-Paczynski (AP) effect]], which arises when an incorrect background cosmology is assumed to convert observed redshifts and angles into physical distances. Extending the AP test to the intrinsic alignment of galaxy images provides a unique pathway to break degeneracies between the cosmic expansion history and the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] of structure [Okumura et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494..694O/abstract). Measuring these coupled anisotropic effects permits robust constraints on the amplitude of perturbations, often parameterized by the combination [[f-8|fσ8]].

For photometric surveys, observers often rely on projected clustering metrics such as the [[angular-power-spectrum|Angular power spectrum]]. Furthermore, on the largest observable scales, proper modeling must incorporate general relativistic corrections to the observed galaxy number density—effects that are essential for testing general relativity against alternative gravity models [Yoo et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014CQGra..31w4001Y/abstract). Additionally, combining these two-point clustering metrics with weak lensing yields the observable $E_g$ statistic, an excellent multiprobe test of modified gravity; however, its robust application requires careful accounting for scale- and bias-dependent lensing contributions in photometric surveys [Ghosh et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...06..010G/abstract).

## Higher-Order Statistics and Non-Gaussianity

While the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] $P(k)$ and the [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] completely describe a Gaussian random field, the late-time density field of the Universe is highly non-Gaussian. As structures evolve into the quasi-linear and non-linear regimes, independent Fourier modes begin to couple, encoding critical cosmological information in their relative phases [Bernardeau et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhR...367....1B/abstract). [[higher-order-statistics-in-cosmology|Higher-order statistics]] are therefore required to extract the full information content of the density field on small scales and to properly model the complex topology of the cosmic web.

The primary tools for characterizing this non-Gaussianity are the bispectrum and trispectrum, which are the Fourier-space equivalents of the [[three-point-correlation-function|Three-point correlation function]] and the [[four-point-correlation-function|Four-point correlation function]], respectively. A major advantage of these higher-order statistics is their ability to break the degeneracy between linear [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy bias]] ($b_1$) and the amplitude of matter fluctuations, typically parameterized by $\sigma_8$. Because the [[matter-bispectrum|Matter bispectrum]] depends differently on bias and the growth factor than the power spectrum, joint analyses can disentangle these effects, often improving bounds on $\sigma_8$ by nearly a factor of two compared to two-point statistics alone [Sefusatti et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvD..74b3522S/abstract). 

Beyond gravitationally induced non-Gaussianity, higher-order statistics are sensitive probes of the initial conditions of the Universe. While standard [[nearly-scale-invariant-adiabatic-primordial-fluctuations|inflationary models]] predict nearly Gaussian primordial perturbations, alternative models generate primordial non-Gaussianity parameterized by amplitudes such as $f_{\rm NL}$. Bispectrum measurements from large-scale imaging and redshift surveys have the potential to constrain these parameters with a precision rivaling or exceeding cosmic microwave background constraints [Hashimoto et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..93j3537H/abstract). Furthermore, the equivalence principle imposes a consistency relation connecting the scale-dependent bias in the power spectrum to the dipolar part of the bispectrum, providing a robust consistency check for the primordial origin of any detected non-Gaussianity [Assassi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...12..043A/abstract).

In the weak gravitational lensing regime, higher-order multi-point statistics offer profound insights. For example, geometric methods like shear triplet statistics provide a powerful probe of dark energy properties [Sereno et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.377..229S/abstract). Extending to the [[matter-trispectrum|trispectrum]], researchers construct 3D [[weak-lensing-power-spectrum|weak lensing]] observables such as kurt-spectra and cumulant correlators to probe the structural evolution of the Universe [Munshi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.411.2161M/abstract). Given the high dimensionality of these higher-order measurements, recent advancements have demonstrated that the complex three-point correlation function signal in weak lensing can be efficiently compressed into just the first few harmonic multipoles without significant information loss [Samario-Nava et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250619811S/abstract).

A significant tension in the practical application of higher-order statistics is their computational cost. Estimating the full bispectrum or trispectrum is computationally demanding, heavily impacted by the scaling of triplet-counting algorithms and the numerical integration of highly oscillatory Bessel functions in projected fields [Assassi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JCAP...11..054A/abstract), [Fang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...05..010F/abstract). Although novel algorithms and random-catalog-free estimators have accelerated these calculations [Pearson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486L.105P/abstract), [Philcox et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PNAS..11911366P/abstract), the full covariance matrix of the bispectrum remains difficult to evaluate.

To bypass these computational bottlenecks, several proxy statistics have been developed. The skew-spectrum captures much of the amplitude-like information of the bispectrum (such as $f_{\rm NL}$ and bias parameters) at a fraction of the computational cost [Moradinezhad Dizgah et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...04..011M/abstract). [[skewness-patterns|Skewness patterns]] in joint fits alongside the traditional power spectrum have been shown to reduce marginalized errors on parameters such as $f_{\rm NL}$ and $n_s$ by up to 44% [Dai et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...08..007D/abstract). Another alternative is the line correlation function (LCF), a three-point phase statistic that isolates the Fourier phases. Because the LCF is immune to linear bias, it specifically targets non-linear clustering and can improve parameter constraints by approximately 20% over standard [[n-point-spatial-correlations|two-point statistics]] [Eggemeier et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.2496E/abstract), [Ali et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.2743A/abstract).

## Geometric and Topological Descriptors of the Cosmic Web

While traditional [[n-point-spatial-correlations|n-point spatial correlations]] are foundational, they often fail to capture the complex connectivity and phase information of the cosmic web. To characterize the topology and morphology of filaments, nodes, and voids, astronomers employ geometric and topological descriptors. 

Among the most robust of these measures are [[minkowski-functionals|Minkowski functionals]] [Buchert et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995lssu.conf..156B/abstract). In three dimensions, these functionals completely describe the morphological properties of a continuous field. They are additive, translationally and rotationally invariant, and computationally efficient to evaluate, requiring only $\mathcal{O}(N)$ operations for a given threshold level [Novikov et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999IJMPD...8..291N/abstract). To lowest order, Minkowski functionals depend on three generalized skewness parameters that effectively probe the [[matter-bispectrum|Matter bispectrum]], making them powerful tools for detecting primordial and gravitationally induced non-Gaussianity in both the cosmic microwave background and galaxy clustering maps [Pratten et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.3209P/abstract). Another valuable tool is the cumulant generating function, which serves as a novel observable capable of capturing non-Gaussian one-point statistics on mildly non-linear scales.

A related topological measure is the genus of isodensity contours, which quantifies the connectivity (the number of "holes" minus isolated regions) of a smoothed density field. The shape and amplitude of the genus-threshold density relation serve as strict tests of phase coherence; for instance, the large-scale topology of the universe resembles a multiply-connected filamentary net rather than isolated clumps [Vogeley et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PhDT........23V/abstract). However, the genus and other [[isodensity-analysis|Isodensity Analysis]] metrics (such as contour surface area and intersection lengths) are highly sensitive to non-linear evolution and [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]], which introduce systematic anisotropies that can alter the amplitude of two-dimensional genus measurements by $10\%-20\%$ [Taruya et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...550..528T/abstract).

The [[void-probability-function|Void probability function]] (VPF) offers another complement to standard clustering metrics. Defined as the probability that a randomly placed volume of a given size and shape is entirely empty of galaxies, the VPF mathematically encapsulates the entire hierarchy of higher-order correlation functions [Vogeley et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PhDT........23V/abstract). It has the advantage of being free from normalization effects that can complicate two-point functions, offering a direct window into [[high-order-galaxy-statistics|High-order galaxy statistics]] and demonstrating scaling invariance in the galaxy distribution [Maurogordato et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...369...30M/abstract), [Fry et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...306..358F/abstract). To evaluate these massive features alongside their dense counterparts, researchers utilize extreme value statistics, providing a rigorous mathematical framework for assessing the statistical significance of exceptionally large structures, such as the Shapley supercluster or immense voids, compared to expectations from Gaussian initial conditions [Sheth et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.417.2938S/abstract).

Beyond continuous fields, discrete descriptors like percolation analysis and the [[minimal-spanning-tree|Minimal spanning tree]] (MST) quantify the connectivity of the cosmic web. Percolation statistics analyze how structural linkages form as a function of linking length. The percolation threshold evolves strongly with redshift, tracing the growth of clustering; however, it remains notably sensitive to survey sampling density and shot noise, which must be carefully controlled [Regos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240702574R/abstract). Separately from MSTs, filament statistics based on sequences of spatial links—evaluating discrete curvature, planarity, and torsion—have been shown to discriminate between competing dark matter models at greater than $4\sigma$ significance [Davé et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995astro.ph..5142D/abstract). Furthermore, applying information theory to these networks, graph entropy effectively distinguishes the clustered cosmic web from random point distributions, providing a simple yet powerful measure of connectivity [Valentina García-Alvarado et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200808164V/abstract).

## Advanced Spatial Metrics: kNN and Marked Statistics

While the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] and the [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] serve as the standard workhorses for extracting cosmological parameters on large, linear scales, they fail to capture the full information encoded in the [[higher-order-statistics-in-cosmology|non-Gaussian]] late-time density field. To probe highly non-linear scales and elucidate the underlying relationship between galaxies and their host dark matter halos, modern clustering analyses increasingly rely on advanced spatial metrics. 

**k-th Nearest Neighbour (kNN) Statistics**
Rather than counting pairs of galaxies at fixed separations, k-th nearest neighbour statistics measure the distribution of distances from random query points (or data points) to their $k$-th nearest data point. Because this distance distribution is mathematically tied to the [[void-probability-function|Void probability function]] and counts-in-cell statistics, it provides a computationally efficient means of capturing [[n-point-spatial-correlations|n-point spatial correlations]] of all orders [Yuan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.3935Y/abstract).

In practice, measuring kNN statistics offers massive improvements in parameter constraints. The introduction of 2D kNN statistics allows for the disentanglement of the projected real-space galaxy distribution from line-of-sight [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]] [Yuan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.3935Y/abstract). By successfully modeling kNN statistics down to transverse scales, studies using simulated mock catalogs have demonstrated the ability to place unbiased $\sim 2\%$ constraints on the clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$ [Yuan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.1993Y/abstract). Furthermore, kNN statistics significantly tighten constraints on extended halo occupation distribution (HOD) parameters, such as velocity bias and galaxy assembly bias.

**Marked Statistics and Field Transformations**
Another powerful approach to extracting non-linear information is the use of marked statistics, such as the [[mark-correlation-function|Mark correlation function]] and the marked power spectrum. In standard spatial statistics, all galaxies are weighted equally. Marked statistics, by contrast, assign a weight (or "mark") to each galaxy based on an intrinsic property (e.g., luminosity, color, or star formation rate) or its local environmental density [Sheth et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.11773S/abstract).

This targeted weighting selectively enhances the signal from specific environments, thereby breaking degeneracies inherent in standard HOD modeling [White et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.395.2381W/abstract). For instance, a marked power spectrum designed to up-weight low-density regions can improve constraints on $\sigma_8$ by a factor of 2, and tighten bounds on $\Omega_m$, $\Omega_b$, $h$, $n_s$, and the neutrino mass $M_\nu$ relative to the standard unweighted [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] [Massara et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951...70M/abstract). Similarly, luminosity-weighted power spectra yield figures of merit for cluster scaling relations that are orders of magnitude higher than unweighted clustering probes [Balaguera-Antolínez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...563A.141B/abstract). 

Other transformative approaches seek to alter the field itself. Gaussianization transforms are designed to mitigate the effects of scale-dependent galaxy bias and non-linear evolution, thereby aiding in the robust recovery of the underlying dark-matter power spectrum shape [McCullagh et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457.3652M/abstract). However, this agreement breaks down in redshift space due to Fingers-of-God effects. For highly non-linear and non-Gaussian fields—such as large-scale reionization maps—Wavelet Phase Harmonics have emerged as optimal summary statistics to fully capture these complex morphological features [Lin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221014273L/abstract).

**Implementation and Challenges**
Deploying these advanced metrics in observational data presents unique theoretical challenges. Because they are highly sensitive to the non-linear regime, they cannot be easily modeled using analytic perturbation theory. Instead, they typically require full forward modeling utilizing high-fidelity $N$-body simulations to predict the statistics and their covariance matrices accurately [Yuan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.6283Y/abstract). Consequently, analyzing these complex statistics often relies on simulation-based [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]] or automated data compression to bypass explicit analytic likelihoods [Lehman et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108957L/abstract).

## Modeling Frameworks: Perturbation Theory and the Halo Model

To accurately extract cosmological parameters from summary statistics such as the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] and [[matter-bispectrum|Matter bispectrum]], theoretical frameworks must bridge the gap between primordial initial conditions and the observed late-time distribution of galaxies. 

On large scales, where density fluctuations are small ($\delta \ll 1$), [[standard-perturbation-theory|Standard Perturbation Theory]] (SPT) and Lagrangian perturbation theory are the primary analytic tools [Bernardeau et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhR...367....1B/abstract). These frameworks expand the matter density and velocity fields in a perturbative series, providing robust predictions for the weakly non-linear regime of gravitational clustering. This approach naturally predicts the emergence of gravitationally induced non-Gaussianity, allowing for the analytic calculation of higher-order cumulants and correlation functions without ad hoc assumptions [Juszkiewicz et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996astro.ph..2134J/abstract). To extend the validity of these perturbative expansions, Renormalization Group techniques have been developed. Furthermore, angular-averaged consistency relations for matter density correlations provide powerful tests within this framework, offering tools to detect primordial non-Gaussianities and gravity modifications entirely independently of [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy biasing]] [Valageas et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvD..89l3522V/abstract).

As observations probe smaller scales, traditional perturbation theory eventually breaks down due to shell-crossing and deeply non-linear dynamics. To circumvent this, the [[effective-field-theory-of-large-scale-structure|Effective Field Theory of Large Scale Structure]] (EFTofLSS) introduces effective counterterms and stochastic contributions that mathematically encapsulate unresolved short-distance physics. The EFT framework provides a rigorous, closed expansion for clustering, incorporating local density, tidal fields, and higher-derivative operators [Desjacques et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhR...733....1D/abstract). These techniques are now essential for field-level inference and for computing precision beyond-two-point observables [Vlah et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...05..061V/abstract) [Chen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...01..027C/abstract).

In the deeply [[non-linear-structure-formation|non-linear regime]] ($\delta \gg 1$), the phenomenological halo model becomes the standard paradigm. It posits that all matter is partitioned into bound [[mass-distribution-and-functions|dark matter halos]]. Under this framework, any $n$-point summary statistic is naturally decomposed into contributions from clustering within a single halo (the 1-halo term) and correlations between distinct halos (the 2-halo term). The halo model seamlessly integrates with the Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD), which describes how galaxies populate these dark matter structures. Incorporating non-linear effects via the halo approach mitigates systematic biases in parameter constraints that arise when assuming a simple scale-independent linear bias [Cooray et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.348..250C/abstract). Furthermore, higher-order statistics like the [[matter-trispectrum|Matter trispectrum]] provide critical leverage to break degeneracies between HOD parameters and the underlying cosmology. Hybrid approaches combining perturbation theory for the 2-halo regime with virialized models for the 1-halo regime allow for the rapid, accurate generation of mock catalogs and [[analytical-covariance-matrix|analytical covariance matrices]] [Scoccimarro et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.329..629S/abstract).

Finally, mapping these real-space theoretical predictions to observables requires careful modeling of [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]] (RSD) utilizing the streaming model. As noted in prior sections, correctly modeling the highly non-linear "Finger-of-God" velocity dispersions necessitates non-Gaussian velocity PDFs, ensuring unbiased parameters are extracted from anisotropic clustering statistics [Kuruvilla et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.2256K/abstract).

## Systematics, Bias, and Covariance Modeling

Interpreting cosmological summary statistics requires meticulously disentangling the underlying cosmological signal from various observational systematics, theoretical biases, and statistical noise. Among the most significant challenges are the modeling of galaxy bias, the treatment of finite survey geometries, and the accurate estimation of covariance matrices.

Galaxies are inherently biased tracers of the underlying [[dark-matter-power-spectrum|Dark matter power spectrum]]. While a simple linear bias parameter $b_1$ is often sufficient on very large scales, the relationship between the galaxy and matter density fields becomes complex, non-linear, and scale-dependent on smaller scales. In standard [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] and power spectrum analyses, this introduces a strict degeneracy between the linear bias and the amplitude of matter fluctuations, $\sigma_8$. Breaking this degeneracy is a primary motivation for employing [[higher-order-statistics-in-cosmology|Higher-Order Statistics in Cosmology]]. Observables such as the [[matter-bispectrum|Matter bispectrum]], the [[three-point-correlation-function|Three-point correlation function]], and proxy skew-spectra provide complementary sensitivity to the non-linear growth of structure, improving parameter constraints [Sefusatti et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvD..74b3522S/abstract) [Ali et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.2743A/abstract).

Furthermore, the finite volume of any observational survey introduces [[super-sample-covariance|Super-sample covariance]] (SSC). Density fluctuations with wavelengths larger than the survey scale cannot be directly observed but act as a background density perturbation that modulates the local expansion and growth rate of structures within the surveyed volume [Li et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvD..90j3530L/abstract) [Taruya et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...11..061T/abstract). Failing to account for SSC results in a severe underestimation of the true [[sample-variance|Sample variance]], leading to degraded constraints and artificially tight credible intervals for [[cosmological-parameters-and-frameworks|Cosmological Parameters and Frameworks]].

To properly evaluate theoretical likelihoods, these systematic effects must be synthesized into an accurate [[analytical-covariance-matrix|Analytical covariance matrix]] or derived from massive ensembles of high-fidelity simulations. Beyond the simple Gaussian approximation, non-linear gravitational evolution and SSC couple distinct Fourier modes, producing a complex covariance structure with significant off-diagonal components [Klypin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.4602K/abstract). Developing computationally efficient frameworks to model these full covariance matrices is essential for interpreting the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] in modern datasets. To complement theoretical covariances, observational analyses frequently rely on spatial jackknife resampling. This serves as an objective way to estimate errors and quantify the impact of massive superstructures on galaxy clustering statistics, ensuring that derived covariances remain robust to sample variance [Norberg et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.2435N/abstract).

Mathematical approximations that have historically simplified theoretical modeling are also breaking down under the statistical weight of contemporary data. A notable example is the [[limber-approximation|Limber approximation]], widely used to evaluate the [[angular-power-spectrum|Angular power spectrum]] by simplifying the integration over highly oscillatory spherical Bessel functions. For upcoming Stage IV surveys like LSST or Euclid, relying on the Limber approximation introduces unacceptable systematic biases in cosmological parameter estimation [Fang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...05..010F/abstract). Consequently, analysts must now employ exact "beyond-Limber" integration methods, leveraging generalized FFTLog algorithms [Assassi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JCAP...11..054A/abstract) [Fang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...05..010F/abstract), to ensure that theoretical predictions match the sub-percent statistical precision of the observations.

## Information Theory and Optimal Data Compression

As modern [[large-galaxy-catalogs|Large galaxy catalogs]] grow to encompass millions of objects, the dimensionality of the raw observables renders brute-force [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] computationally intractable. Extracting parameter constraints efficiently requires compressing this data into low-dimensional summary statistics while strictly preserving the underlying Fisher information. 

The mathematical foundation for optimal data compression in cosmology frequently relies on the Karhunen-Loève transform, which allows for the quadratic "precompression" of massive datasets into signal-to-noise eigenmodes prior to parameter extraction [Tegmark et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...480...22T/abstract). For higher-order clustering, explicit non-linear compression algorithms group [[three-point-correlation-function|Three-point correlation function]] (bispectrum) measurements by their triangle geometry and subsequently maximize the Fisher information with respect to the targeted cosmological parameters. This two-step process is functionally lossless and has been shown to shrink marginalized credible intervals compared to standard analyses of the full data vector [Gualdi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497..776G/abstract). Similarly, data compression schemes have been utilized to efficiently compress the joint data vectors of multi-probe analyses that combine [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] clustering, cosmic shear, and magnification [Eifler et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440.1379E/abstract).

Beyond dimensionality reduction, concepts from information theory explicitly quantify the physical information budget of the cosmic web. Information entropy can be related analytically to the mass variance of the density distribution in the regime of small fluctuations. This relationship provides a novel mechanism to measure large-scale linear bias and detect signatures of non-Gaussianity on small scales independently of the specific tracer [Pandey et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.4239P/abstract). In topological studies, graph entropy provides a discrete analogue to continuous connectivity scalars. Yielding values for the galaxy distribution, graph entropy provides a computationally simple statistic that is highly sensitive to sampling density and redshift-space distortions [Valentina García-Alvarado et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200808164V/abstract). In exploring the global information budget, recent studies have quantified the 'entropic drop' caused by cosmic structure formation, which also reveals the lack of a universal scale of uniformity at scales below $130 \, h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Sarkar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240703042S/abstract).

In the highly non-linear regime, traditional power spectra suffer significant information loss due to mode coupling. Analytically formulated "sufficient statistics" attempt to recapture this lost information by modulating the amplitude with small-scale clustering properties, potentially doubling the effective volume of dense [[low-redshift-large-scale-structure|low-redshift surveys]] [Wolk et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454..560W/abstract). Additionally, to explicitly recapture structural information from noisy tracers, optimal local observables—such as logarithmic and power transformations—are frequently deployed to "Gaussianize" the field and enhance signal-to-noise [Carron et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..306..235C/abstract).

A major modern paradigm shift in this domain is the transition from explicit analytic likelihoods to simulation-based inference (SBI), often referred to as likelihood-free inference. SBI frameworks pair high-fidelity forward models with neural networks to perform automatic data compression, computationally learning the optimal, interpretable summary statistics directly from mock catalogs [Lehman et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108957L/abstract). This circumvents the need for an explicit [[analytical-covariance-matrix|Analytical covariance matrix]], which is notoriously difficult to model accurately for complex survey geometries and non-Gaussian fields. 

There remains an active debate in the literature regarding whether combined summary statistics can ever perfectly compress the information encoded in the Fourier phases of the large-scale structure without redundancy. Alternative approaches advocate for pure field-level [[cosmological-inference|Cosmological inference]], reconstructing the initial conditions and density maps directly without reducing the data to intermediate [[n-point-correlation-function|N-point correlation function]] metrics. While field-level inference bypasses the structural information loss inherent to summary statistic compression, it requires sophisticated joint Bayesian frameworks to avoid the biases present in traditional maximum a posteriori estimators [Zhou et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.108h3506Z/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The advent of Stage IV surveys demands high-precision tools to extract non-linear information from small scales [Yuan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.6283Y/abstract). While the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] and [[two-point-correlation-function|Two-point correlation function]] have historically been the workhorses of [[cosmological-inference|Cosmological inference]], they fail to capture the full non-Gaussian nature of the late-time density field. Consequently, a major frontier in astrophysics is the development of "beyond-2pt" statistics capable of breaking parameter degeneracies, such as the inherent degeneracy between linear galaxy bias and the amplitude of fluctuations, $\sigma_8$ [Ali et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.2743A/abstract).

This pursuit has driven a paradigm shift from traditional, likelihood-based inference to simulation-based inference (SBI) and field-level forward modeling. Historically, inference relied on deriving explicit analytic likelihoods and an analytical covariance matrix using formalisms like [[standard-perturbation-theory|Standard Perturbation Theory]] or the [[effective-field-theory-of-large-scale-structure|Effective Field Theory of Large Scale Structure]]. However, these perturbative approaches break down in the highly non-linear regime. By leveraging high-fidelity $N$-body simulations, SBI bypasses the need for explicit analytic likelihoods [Yuan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.6283Y/abstract). Within this framework, machine learning is increasingly utilized for automatic data compression to learn optimal, low-dimensional "sufficient statistics" directly from [[galaxy-density-fields|Galaxy density fields]]. These learned statistics can capture non-linear features while remaining interpretable with respect to underlying physical parameters and baryonic physics [Lehman et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108957L/abstract).

Despite these advancements, the field is currently characterized by several active debates and open questions. Chief among them is determining which specific beyond-2pt summary statistic is observationally optimal. Recent community data challenges have benchmarked various approaches, including density-split clustering, nearest neighbor statistics, void statistics, and joint power spectrum and bispectrum analyses [Beyond-2pt Collaboration et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240502252B/abstract). For instance, 2D kNN statistics can efficiently disentangle projected spatial distributions from redshift-space distortions. However, there is a recognized tension in the literature: these novel methods require extensive, computationally expensive simulations for calibration and validation, which can severely limit their flexibility compared to analytic approaches [Yuan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.6283Y/abstract).

Looking forward, several theoretical challenges must be addressed to maximize the utility of [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Galaxy Surveys and Catalogs]]. First, researchers must determine how to optimally combine multiple summary statistics to achieve lossless data compression without double-counting redundant information. Second, it remains an open question whether full field-level inference can eventually replace summary statistics altogether, entirely avoiding the information loss inherent in data compression [Beyond-2pt Collaboration et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240502252B/abstract). Third, as statistical precision increases, systematic errors such as [[super-sample-covariance|Super-sample covariance]]—arising from density fluctuations on scales larger than the survey volume—must be rigorously quantified. These super-sample modes modulate the local growth of structure and can act as an unconstrained cosmological parameter, substantially degrading the precision of extracted parameters if not accurately modeled [Li et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvD..90j3530L/abstract), [Taruya et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...11..061T/abstract).
