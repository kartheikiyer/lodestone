**Statistical Inference and Data Methods** encompass the mathematical frameworks and computational algorithms used to extract physical insights and quantify uncertainties from complex astronomical observations. Historically, [[statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Statistical methods in astrophysics]] relied heavily on frequentist tools, utilizing Poisson statistics and basic correlation estimators to evaluate the spatial clustering of galaxies [de Vaucouleurs et al. (1971)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1971PASP...83..113D/abstract), [Ling et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987PhDT........12L/abstract), [Hamilton et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..3603H/abstract). However, as astronomical surveys have expanded into petabyte-scale datasets with highly correlated, multi-dimensional parameter spaces, the field has recognized the limitations of rigid analytic likelihoods and simple point estimates [Feigelson et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003sca..book.....F/abstract). Consequently, a major paradigm shift has established Bayesian probability theory as the foundational standard for [[scientific-data-analysis|Scientific data analysis]], providing a mathematically rigorous mechanism to model heterogeneous noise, propagate non-Gaussian uncertainties, and solve underdetermined inverse problems [Hobson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009bmc..book.....H/abstract).

At the core of modern [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] is the recovery and analysis of full [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]]. To bridge the gap between individual, noisy measurements and broad underlying physics, researchers frequently employ the [[hierarchical-bayesian-model|Hierarchical Bayesian Model]] (HBM), which simultaneously infers object-level properties and population-wide hyperparameters. Exploring these complex posteriors relies on advanced computational engines; while traditional Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) remains a staple, [[fitting-algorithms|Fitting algorithms]] like Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) and [[gibbs-sampling|Gibbs sampling]] are now strictly necessary to sample the million-dimensional parameter spaces encountered in 3D density field reconstructions [Jasche et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407...29J/abstract). Moreover, when the physical processes driving an observable are so non-linear that the likelihood function becomes analytically intractable, the field increasingly turns to Simulation-Based Inference (SBI). By training [[density-estimation-techniques|Density estimation techniques]] on high-fidelity forward simulations, SBI enables ultra-fast [[model-fitting|Model fitting]] and robust [[conditional-density-estimation|Conditional density estimation]] without an explicit likelihood. 

This article outlines the application of these advanced statistical pipelines across diverse astrophysical domains, detailing how they are deployed for tasks ranging from non-parametric Gaussian Process reconstructions of cosmic velocity fields to the probabilistic extraction of stellar masses and photometric redshifts. It examines the critical methodologies used to mitigate observational systematics, handle missing data, and correct for covariate shift when training data is unrepresentative of the target survey. To support the pedagogy and application of these sophisticated pipelines, modern software toolkits like AstronomyCalc have emerged, facilitating practical astronomical data analysis, methodology teaching, and synthetic data generation [Giri et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250105491G/abstract). Finally, the article highlights the pressing open questions facing the advent of Stage IV surveys. These include the ongoing challenge of maintaining computational scalability in SBI while ensuring robustness against misspecified physics models, the integration of multi-modal machine learning into traditional [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] workflows, and the resolution of underlying theoretical tensions, such as whether large-scale galaxy fluctuations truly adhere to the statistical "self-averaging" assumed by standard cosmology.

## Evolution of Statistical Methods in Astronomy

The historical development of [[statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Statistical methods in astrophysics]] reflects a steady transition from basic frequentist comparisons to sophisticated generative and probabilistic models. Historical developments in mapping high-redshift structure initially relied on the statistical evaluations of faint galaxies, quasar absorption lines, and weak gravitational distortion [White et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997euvl.conf..219W/abstract). In early spatial statistics, researchers typically tested the nonrandom clustering of galaxies by comparing observed distributions against a statistically uniform Poisson distribution [de Vaucouleurs et al. (1971)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1971PASP...83..113D/abstract). The CfA redshift survey marked a critical turning point; using bootstrap resampling, researchers demonstrated that classical Poisson error estimates significantly underestimated the true uncertainties in two-point correlation functions [Ling et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987PhDT........12L/abstract). Early power spectrum analysis also relied heavily on frequentist estimators, most notably the [[least-squares-method|Least squares method]] and the Feldman-Kaiser-Peacock method [Hamilton et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..3603H/abstract). Foundational, pedagogical reviews of these power spectrum estimation basics further established the enduring importance of maximum likelihood frameworks and Fisher matrices for later cosmology [Hamilton et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009LNP...665..415H/abstract).

As astronomy formally transitioned into the Big Data era, characterized by massive all-sky catalogs spanning from gamma-rays to radio waves [Mickaelian et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016BaltA..25...75M/abstract), observational data grew unprecedentedly in volume and complexity. The limitations of point estimates and rigid analytic likelihoods became glaringly apparent [Feigelson et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003sca..book.....F/abstract). Data releases like the Fifth Data Release of the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS DR5) provided a key expansion of photometric and spectroscopic data that enabled early wide-area statistical investigations [Adelman-McCarthy et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJS..172..634A/abstract). This catalog scale catalyzed a paradigm shift toward Bayesian inference for [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] [Hobson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009bmc..book.....H/abstract). Modern large-scale structure inference requires sampling from multivariate, highly non-Gaussian lognormal Poissonian [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] [Jasche et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407...29J/abstract). To navigate parameter spaces with millions of dimensions, researchers adopted advanced [[fitting-algorithms|Fitting algorithms]] such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC), enabling the joint inference of 3D density fields, luminosity-dependent biases, and the cosmological power spectrum [Jasche et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...779...15J/abstract). The community has reached a strong consensus that robust cosmological analyses require the rigorous propagation of uncertainties; relying on the traditional method of stacking full photometric redshift probability density functions itself yields systematically biased population distributions [Malz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..127M/abstract).

The modern era of [[scientific-data-analysis|Scientific data analysis]] is defined by petabyte-scale datasets from surveys like Gaia, SDSS, Euclid, and the Atacama Cosmology Telescope (ACT). The summary of the Gaia Data Release 3 (DR3) exemplifies this massive expansion, highlighting the release of mean spectra, expanded radial velocity catalogs, and the vast increase in the astrophysical characterization of over a billion sources [Gaia Collaboration et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A...1G/abstract). ACT's DR4 and DR5 data products have also been instrumental, providing arcminute-resolution maps and precise cosmological likelihoods for large-scale structure studies [Mallaby-Kay et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..255...11M/abstract). Handling these scale challenges has historically driven algorithmic innovation. The global iterative solution used by the European Space Agency's Gaia mission mathematically linked astrometric and astrophysical data, allowing for the continuous, iterative updating of instrument parameters and object properties across millions of stars [Jordi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1105.6166J/abstract). Similarly, massive spectroscopic datasets required automated pipelines; SDSS DR10 introduced the first APOGEE near-infrared spectroscopic data for statistically studying Milky Way chemical abundances [Ahn et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..211...17A/abstract), while the SDSS-III Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey (BOSS) successfully introduced an automated spectral classification pipeline with a 98.7% success rate using refined templates in Data Release 9 [Bolton et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....144..144B/abstract).

Today, the field is increasingly turning to Simulation-Based Inference (SBI) and implicit likelihood frameworks. By training neural [[density-estimation-techniques|Density estimation techniques]] on forward-simulated data, SBI bypasses the need for an explicit likelihood function and can accelerate parameter inference by factors of $1,000$ to $10,000$ over traditional [[ensemble-mcmc-sampling|Ensemble MCMC sampling]] methods [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221103747W/abstract), [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952L..10W/abstract). Concurrently, machine learning approaches—such as multi-modal foundation models and normalizing flows—are now utilized to probabilistically infer properties like stellar mass and redshift directly from pixel-level morphological data and photometry [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315312E/abstract), [Amira Siddiqa et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251112737A/abstract).

Despite these sophisticated statistical frameworks, fundamental tensions remain in the underlying assumptions used to model large-scale structure. For instance, the assumption of "self-averaging" galaxy fluctuations is challenged by statistical analyses suggesting that the galaxy distribution is inhomogeneous up to scales of $100 \text{ Mpc}/h$, conflicting with standard $\Lambda$CDM predictions on scales above $30 \text{ Mpc}/h$ [Sylos Labini et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...508...17S/abstract). Such discrepancies, along with the broader 21st-century challenges of detecting extremely faint signals in complex noise, highlight the necessity of fostering the joint development of new methodologies between astronomers and statisticians [Feigelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AnRSA...8..493F/abstract).

## Bayesian Frameworks and Hierarchical Modeling

Bayesian probability theory has become the standard framework for [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] and uncertainty propagation in modern astrophysics, superseding frequentist point-estimators for complex, high-dimensional datasets [Hobson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009bmc..book.....H/abstract). By treating probability as a mathematically rigorous measure of uncertainty, Bayesian methods provide a robust mechanism to solve underdetermined inverse problems, such as reconstructing undistorted source profiles in gravitational lensing or extracting non-linear 3D density fields from discrete galaxy tracers [Brewer et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0809.0939B/abstract), [Jasche et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407...29J/abstract). Early applications heavily relied on such probabilistic foundations; for example, performing joint [[fitting-algorithms|Fitting algorithms]] of X-ray imaging and Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect data provided a rigorous Bayesian framework to accurately determine galaxy cluster properties [Bonamente et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...614...56B/abstract).

Central to this approach is the estimation and analysis of [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]]. The astronomical community has reached a strong consensus that point estimates are insufficient and often systematically biased for precision cosmology. For example, in photometric redshift estimation, the traditional method of stacking full [[density-estimation-techniques|Density estimation techniques]] yields a systematically biased estimate of the redshift distribution $n(z)$ [Malz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..127M/abstract). Therefore, robust cosmological inference instead requires sophisticated hierarchical frameworks that properly propagate non-Gaussian uncertainties and account for degeneracies without resorting to simple PDF stacking [Leistedt et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881...80L/abstract), [Malz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..127M/abstract). Crucially, if the prior probability density used to generate a photometric redshift PDF is implicit or unknown, the resulting posteriors cannot be self-consistently used for downstream scientific inference [Malz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..127M/abstract).

To bridge the gap between individual object measurements and population-wide physics, the field increasingly relies on the [[hierarchical-bayesian-model|Hierarchical Bayesian Model]] (HBM). A hierarchical model introduces population-level hyperparameters, $\alpha$, which govern the prior functions of individual object parameters, $\theta_i$. The joint posterior for the population is expressed as:
$$ P(\{\theta_i\}, \alpha | \{D_i\}) \propto P(\alpha) \prod_{i} P(D_i | \theta_i) P(\theta_i | \alpha) $$
where $D_i$ represents the observational data for the $i$-th object. 

HBMs represent the most rigorous method for [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] in stellar population synthesis (SPS). They allow researchers to self-consistently separate competing sources of bias—such as spectral energy distribution (SED) template inaccuracies and heteroscedastic observational noise—while simultaneously recalibrating photometric observations across thousands of correlated parameters [Leistedt et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881...80L/abstract), [Johnson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..254...22J/abstract). Historically, the computational intractability of evaluating full SPS likelihoods inside an HBM posed a severe bottleneck, but this barrier is increasingly being overcome by replacing expensive physical models with accelerated neural emulators [Leistedt et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..264...23L/abstract).

Hierarchical frameworks are equally vital in large-scale structure (LSS) inference. For instance, Bayesian field-level inference allows for the joint reconstruction of the underlying cosmic shear field and its tomographic power spectra using iterative techniques like [[gibbs-sampling|Gibbs sampling]] [Alsing et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.4452A/abstract). Similar joint-inference approaches are used to simultaneously recover the 3D matter density field, the cosmological power spectrum, and luminosity-dependent galaxy bias from survey data [Jasche et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...779...15J/abstract).

Beyond structural modeling, physically motivated prior functions are critical for robust classification. By adjusting posteriors with priors that reflect the realistic, magnitude-dependent distributions of extragalactic sources in the Universe, modern classifiers can achieve purities exceeding 99% in star-galaxy-quasar separation, demonstrating the necessity of integrating physical domain knowledge into statistical pipelines [Hughes et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...668A..99H/abstract).

## Markov Chain Monte Carlo and Advanced Sampling Techniques

The transition from frequentist approaches to Bayesian frameworks has established Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) as the standard computational engine for mapping [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] in astrophysics [Hobson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009bmc..book.....H/abstract), [Brewer et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0809.0939B/abstract). However, as astronomical datasets expand and physical models grow to encompass highly correlated parameter spaces, traditional random-walk MCMC becomes inefficient. Advanced [[fitting-algorithms|Fitting algorithms]] are now strictly necessary to perform robust [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] in high-dimensional regimes.

**Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) and the No-U-Turn Sampler**
Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) suppresses the random-walk behavior of standard MCMC by utilizing the gradient of the log-posterior to simulate Hamiltonian dynamics across the parameter space. This method scales exceptionally well to high dimensions. For example, in non-linear large-scale structure (LSS) inference, HMC is uniquely capable of sampling multivariate, highly non-Gaussian lognormal-Poissonian posteriors across millions of dimensions to successfully recover the filamentary cosmic density field from galaxy surveys [Jasche et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407...29J/abstract), [Jasche et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...779...15J/abstract). 

An increasingly popular extension is the No-U-Turn sampler (NUTS), which automatically tunes the integration path lengths of HMC to optimize exploration. NUTS has been leveraged to infer the Milky Way's dark matter halo mass profile from 6D phase-space data, yielding a tightly constrained mass of $M(<100\,\mathrm{kpc}) = 0.69_{-0.04}^{+0.05} \times 10^{12}\, M_\odot$ [Shen et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...925....1S/abstract). It also serves as the sampling backend for probabilistic morphological [[model-fitting|Model fitting]] codes (like `pysersic`), where it extracts robust posterior distributions for galaxy structural parameters from images in a few minutes, or produces optimized point estimates in mere seconds [Pasha et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JOSS....8.5703P/abstract).

**Advanced MCMC in Complex Modalities**
MCMC has also been critical for cross-wavelength studies. For instance, performing joint [[ensemble-mcmc-sampling|Ensemble MCMC sampling]] analyses of Chandra X-ray imaging spectroscopy and Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect data provides a rigorous framework to determine precise galaxy cluster distances and internal cluster properties [Bonamente et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...614...56B/abstract). Beyond continuous imaging maps, Bayesian MCMC is highly effective for automated Gaussian component decomposition and robust parameter classification in large extragalactic spectral-line surveys [Oh et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.5021O/abstract). Furthermore, parallel MCMC is leveraged by Bayesian image decomposition tools like GALPHAT to efficiently explore the full parameter space of two-dimensional surface brightness distributions, providing comprehensive posteriors for Sérsic model parameters and capturing complex structural degeneracies that simple optimization algorithms miss [Yoon et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.1625Y/abstract).

**Gibbs and Nested Sampling**
When joint distributions are intractable but conditional distributions are analytically accessible, [[gibbs-sampling|Gibbs sampling]] provides a powerful alternative. In hierarchical cosmic shear inference, Gibbs sampling is used to iteratively draw samples from an underlying 3D shear map and its corresponding tomographic power spectra. By conditioning the map on the power spectrum and vice versa, this approach neatly bypasses the artificial correlations and information loss introduced by complicated survey masks in frequentist estimators [Alsing et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.4452A/abstract). 

While HMC and Gibbs focus primarily on parameter constraints, [[nested-sampling|Nested sampling]] simultaneously estimates the posterior and calculates the Bayesian evidence required for model selection. Tools such as BAGPIPES utilize nested sampling algorithms (like MULTINEST) to explore the parameter space of stellar population synthesis models, deriving complex star formation histories from combined photometric and spectroscopic data [Carnall et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.4379C/abstract).

**Tensions with Variational Inference**
Despite these algorithmic improvements, the computational intractability of explicit likelihood evaluations remains a major bottleneck for modern survey volumes. Consequently, there is an active tension in the literature between exact sampling methods and approximate optimization algorithms. Variational Inference (VI) can process massive datasets orders of magnitude faster than MCMC by reframing inference as an optimization problem—minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence between a parameterized distribution and the true posterior [Gunapati et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASA...39....1G/abstract). However, if the chosen variational family is not sufficiently expressive, VI may fail to accurately capture complex, non-Gaussian posterior shapes, leading to biased cosmological or physical inferences [Seljak et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190104454S/abstract). As a result, exact MCMC methods and their advanced variants remain the gold standard for unbiased uncertainty propagation.

## Simulation-Based and Likelihood-Free Inference

Traditional [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] relying on Markov Chain Monte Carlo or [[nested-sampling|Nested sampling]] requires an explicit, analytically tractable likelihood function. However, the complex, non-linear astrophysical processes that map cosmological initial conditions to modern observables often yield highly non-Gaussian distributions where the likelihood is fundamentally intractable. To address this, the field is increasingly adopting Simulation-Based Inference (SBI), also known as Implicit Likelihood Inference (ILI). This paradigm bypasses the need for analytic likelihoods by utilizing high-fidelity forward-modeling simulations to directly learn the relationship between physical parameters and observables [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221103747W/abstract). To supply the massive training sets required for these models, the community heavily relies on foundational resources like the CAMELS project, which provides thousands of state-of-the-art hydrodynamic simulations specifically designed to train machine learning models for cosmology and astrophysics [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...54V/abstract).

A foundational technique within this framework is Neural Posterior Estimation (NPE). NPE frequently leverages normalizing flows—a class of invertible neural networks—to construct flexible, high-dimensional [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] from sets of simulated mock data [Massara et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240404228M/abstract). Once trained, this "amortized inference" yields massive computational advantages for [[scientific-data-analysis|Scientific data analysis]]. For instance, in the context of galaxy photometric fitting, SBI pipelines can estimate physical parameters and photometric redshifts in seconds or minutes per object. This represents a computational speedup of $10^3$ to $10^4$ compared to traditional sampling methods, while maintaining comparable accuracy [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221103747W/abstract), [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952L..10W/abstract), [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110640H/abstract).

SBI has proven exceptionally powerful at extracting non-linear information that is often lost when using standard summary statistics. In large-scale structure clustering, deploying NPE on marked power spectra—where galaxies are weighted by their local density—tightens constraints on the amplitude of structure growth ($\sigma_8$) by up to $1.2\times$ over traditional power spectrum analyses [Massara et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240404228M/abstract). Comprehensive ILI frameworks are now being applied across diverse domains, achieving state-of-the-art results in modeling galaxy-scale strong lensing [Poh et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221105836P/abstract) and inferring galaxy cluster masses from X-ray photometry [Ho et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..54H/abstract). It is also increasingly used to extract constraints from galaxy cluster abundances, achieving posterior bounds comparable to traditional [[fitting-algorithms|Fitting algorithms]] [Reza et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022mla..confE..20R/abstract), and to learn high-redshift galaxy formation physics from line intensity mapping signals via pipelines like LIMFAST [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250907060S/abstract). Furthermore, SBI excels at recovering the Milky Way's merger history from multi-dimensional stellar chemical abundances [Viterbo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241117269V/abstract).

Despite its computational efficiency, the literature highlights fundamental tensions regarding SBI's sensitivity to the fidelity of the forward model. Because the neural [[density-estimation-techniques|Density estimation techniques]] perfectly trusts the simulations it trains on, subtle model misspecifications can heavily skew results. However, the nature of this sensitivity varies significantly across physical models. For instance, Modi et al. demonstrated that SBI is remarkably robust to changes between exact $N$-body simulations and approximate particle-mesh gravity models, but conversely, remains highly sensitive to misspecified Halo Occupation Distributions (HOD), which can drive biased recoveries of cosmological parameters [Modi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230915071M/abstract). In other contexts, reliance on approximate gravity models or unmodeled large-scale systematics can indeed yield significant bias ($>2\sigma$) [Hoellinger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204443H/abstract). Furthermore, properly capturing realistic covariance matrices (e.g., noise correlations driven by photometric aperture effects or the stellar initial mass function) is required to prevent the generation of artificially narrow or incorrectly multimodal posteriors [Jo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241021722J/abstract).

Finally, traditional SBI frameworks historically struggled with heterogeneous survey data containing "out-of-distribution" (OOD) measurement errors and missing observations. Recent algorithmic extensions, such as SBI++, solve this by marginalizing over nearby data realizations in the training set. While this test-time adaptation slightly slows inference compared to baseline NPE, it ensures robust [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] in the presence of fluctuating telescope conditions and non-representative training data [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221103747W/abstract), [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952L..10W/abstract).

## Supervised and Unsupervised Machine Learning in Surveys

Modern large-scale photometric and astrometric surveys, such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) and Gaia, produce catalogs containing billions of sources. Processing these massive datasets requires automated [[scientific-data-analysis|Scientific data analysis]] pipelines, wherein both supervised and unsupervised machine learning algorithms have become indispensable for source classification, anomaly detection, and [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]].

**Supervised Classification and Covariate Shift**
The foundational task of star-galaxy classification—as well as the identification of quasars—is now routinely handled by supervised tree-based algorithms. Decision trees, Random Forests, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) are heavily utilized due to their scalability and interpretability [Ball et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...650..497B/abstract). Early general toolkits, like ASTROMLSKIT, laid the groundwork by packaging [[fitting-algorithms|Fitting algorithms]] such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Naive Bayes for supernovae and exoplanet classification [Saha et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150407865S/abstract). For instance, the Apsis framework within Gaia Data Release 3 provided automated source classification and detailed astrophysical parameter inference for over 1.6 billion objects [Creevey et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..26C/abstract). Specific modules within this framework (such as DSC, QSOC, and UGC) were critically designed for non-stellar classification and redshift determination . Among these sub-systems, Random Forest and XGBoost classifiers successfully categorized over 12.4 million variable sources into 24 distinct variability classes, ranging from pulsating stars to active galactic nuclei [Rimoldini et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..14R/abstract). 

A significant challenge in these supervised frameworks is "covariate shift"—a regime where the spectroscopic training data is not representative of the fainter, broader photometric target data. Traditional machine learning models can suffer from severe biases when extrapolating into these out-of-distribution regions. To mitigate this, advanced statistical techniques such as stratified learning and propensity score conditioning are employed to artificially balance the covariates, drastically improving [[conditional-density-estimation|Conditional density estimation]] accuracy on unrepresentative target data [Autenrieth et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210611211A/abstract). Additionally, foundational data calibration is essential before machine learning deployment; efforts like the New York University Value-Added Galaxy Catalog (NYU-VAGC) improved photometric calibration consistency, successfully reducing systematic errors from 2% to 1% to secure more robust large-scale structure statistics [Blanton et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AJ....129.2562B/abstract). On the algorithmic side, explicitly adjusting model outputs with prior functions reflecting realistic spatial and magnitude distributions can push classification purities to $>99\%$ using XGBoost on combined optical and infrared data [Hughes et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...668A..99H/abstract).

**Unsupervised Learning and Anomaly Detection**
For exploratory data analysis, where predefined labels are unavailable, unsupervised learning techniques are highly effective. Algorithms such as Kohonen self-organizing maps (SOMs) can project high-dimensional spectral data into two-dimensional topological maps, clustering similar objects and isolating rare or anomalous spectra without prior assumptions [in der Au et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...547A.115I/abstract). In stellar contexts, clustering techniques utilizing mixture models and expectation maximization have been powerfully deployed to locate embedded stellar clusters, such as those found in the UKIDSS Galactic Plane Survey catalogue [Solin et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...542A...3S/abstract). Similarly, combining rank correlation with Principal Component Analysis (PCA) enables researchers to efficiently extract unobservable stellar properties, such as age and mass, from vast photometric datasets like GAIA and PLATO [Angelou et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839..116A/abstract). Recently, the paradigm has shifted toward self-supervised foundation models. Models like AstroPT, pre-trained on hundreds of thousands of multi-modal Euclid images and spectral energy distributions (SEDs), generate highly informative latent embeddings. These embeddings naturally isolate anomalous sources (such as merging or ringed galaxies) and significantly outperform fully supervised baselines in zero- or few-shot inference tasks using only a fraction of the available labels [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315312E/abstract).

**Deep Generative Models for Parameter Estimation**
Beyond discrete classification, machine learning is increasingly used to infer continuous physical properties from survey imaging. Traditional SED [[model-fitting|Model fitting]] can be computationally prohibitive and often suffers from parameter degeneracies, such as the confusion between dust attenuation and stellar age. To overcome this, deep generative architectures—specifically the Variational Autoencoder (VAE) coupled with Normalizing Flows—are now used to map broad-band photometry and pixel-level images directly to [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] of physical parameters [Amira Siddiqa et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251112737A/abstract). By leveraging [[conditional-density-estimation|Conditional density estimation]] to integrate morphological pixel data alongside photometric fluxes, these models successfully break dust-age degeneracies, enabling the rapid, probabilistic estimation of stellar mass, star formation rate, and metallicity in a fraction of the time required by traditional sampling methods [Yunus et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251205078Y/abstract).

## Gaussian Processes and Non-parametric Methods

In modern astrophysical data analysis, non-parametric inference allows researchers to reconstruct complex, continuous fields without committing to rigid analytical functional forms. This flexibility is vital when performing [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] and [[model-fitting|Model fitting]] for spatially or temporally continuous phenomena, where parametric models might suppress subtle physical features. Gaussian Process (GP) regression is the foundational tool in this domain, treating the target field as a realization of a multi-dimensional Gaussian distribution over functions.

A primary limitation of standard Gaussian Process regression is its $\mathcal{O}(N^3)$ computational scaling for $N$ data points, which is intractable for large modern surveys. To apply non-parametric inference to massive data sets, researchers frequently employ Stochastic Variational Gaussian Process Regression (SVGPR). SVGPR approximates the true GP by introducing a subset of $M$ "inducing points" ($M \ll N$) that summarize the data, thereby reducing the computational complexity to $\mathcal{O}(M^3)$. In Galactic dynamics, SVGPR has been deployed on a sample of $\sim 8 \times 10^5$ stars from the Gaia DR3 Radial Velocity Survey to model the Milky Way's mean velocity field and velocity dispersion tensor [Hapitas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250710317H/abstract). By factoring the objective function to model both the mean field and the dispersion tensor simultaneously, SVGPR avoids arbitrary spatial binning. This non-parametric reconstruction reveals asymmetric, correlated structures in the diagonal components of the dispersion tensor—direct kinematic evidence of the Gaia phase spiral and Galactic disequilibrium [Hapitas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250710317H/abstract).

On extragalactic and cosmological scales, non-parametric methods are essential for 3D matter density and velocity field reconstruction. Advanced tomographic frameworks reconstruct the underlying density and velocity fields at Cosmic Noon ($z \sim 2.5$) by jointly analyzing synergistic data sets, such as the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest (which probes low-density regions) and discrete spectroscopic galaxy surveys (which trace density peaks). When analyzing these large-scale kinematics, non-parametric approaches must carefully account for observational limitations; historical studies highlight the bias introduced by galaxy number-weighted sampling in cosmic velocity field analyses and emphasize the need to carefully model stochastic bias mechanisms [Seto et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...520..409S/abstract). Addressing these challenges, joint continuous reconstruction facilitates a rigorous dynamical definition of protoclusters by estimating their local velocity fields and tracing their evolution forward to $z=0$ [Horowitz et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...906..110H/abstract). For direct 3D reconstructions, specialized software packages like VIRBIUS apply Bayesian inference to deduce complex 3D velocity fields and extract true unperturbed distances from highly non-linear spectroscopic catalogues . Similarly, Multi-Task Gaussian Processes (MTGP) are used to continuously model the redshift evolution of interdependent cosmological observables—such as the baryon acoustic oscillation scale $D_M(z)/r_d$ and the structure growth rate $f\sigma_8(z)$—from survey data, allowing tests of the background $\Lambda$CDM expansion without imposing a rigid parameterized cosmology [Mukherjee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241213973M/abstract).

Beyond continuous spatial maps, GPs are heavily utilized to infer the underlying average profiles of structural observables. For instance, the Population Profile Estimator (PoPE) framework employs Gaussian processes to recover the mean radial profiles and population variances of dark matter, gas, and stellar distributions from highly noisy simulation data, extracting ensemble-level structure without pre-defining the profile's shape [Farahi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....161...30F/abstract). Furthermore, in observational settings dealing with "high-dimensional low sample size" (HDLSS) data—where non-parametric and dimensional-reduction techniques overlap—methods such as noise-reduction Principal Component Analysis (PCA) have been successfully applied to map high-dimensional ALMA spectroscopic observations to trace molecular gas structures [Takeuchi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271...44T/abstract). Algorithms like GPZ utilize Gaussian processes as non-parametric regression networks for photometric redshift estimation. By incorporating varied photometric features and angular sizes, GPZ constructs highly accurate mappings from multi-band photometry to redshift, while naturally outputting reliable predictive variances for the resulting [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] [Gomes et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475..331G/abstract).

## Inference of Stellar Populations and Galaxy Properties

The extraction of physical parameters—such as stellar mass ($M_*$), age, metallicity, and star formation history (SFH)—from broadband photometry and spectroscopy is a cornerstone of galaxy evolution studies. This process relies heavily on Stellar Population Synthesis (SPS) models. While early approaches to Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) fitting relied on simple grid-based template matching, modern methodologies treat SED fitting as a rigorous [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] problem rooted in Bayesian forward modeling. 

Because the mapping between physical parameters and observed SEDs involves highly correlated, moderate-to-high dimensional parameter spaces, comprehensive exploration is required to avoid biased results. Software packages like Prospector address this by coupling flexible forward modeling with [[adaptive-markov-chain-monte-carlo|Adaptive Markov Chain Monte Carlo]] sampling to derive robust [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] for individual galaxies [Johnson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..254...22J/abstract). Similarly, the BAGPIPES (Bayesian Analysis of Galaxies for Physical Inference and Parameter EStimation) framework utilizes [[nested-sampling|Nested sampling]] to fit arbitrary combinations of spectroscopic and photometric data. This approach enables the evaluation of complex, non-parametric SFHs, which are crucial for distinguishing between different galaxy quenching mechanisms [Carnall et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.4379C/abstract). These multi-dimensional inference techniques are also vital when analyzing complex integral-field spectroscopic data, such as that from the SAMI Galaxy Survey, which extensively samples 2D kinematics and gas ionization across thousands of targets [Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..991C/abstract). Understanding the dependence of specific physical traits, such as the environmental dependence of u-band luminosity for late-type and low-mass galaxies, highlights the statistical complexity of observing these populations [Deng et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PASJ...66...22D/abstract). Consequently, detailed analyses increasingly rely on advanced software pipelines; for example, the CAVITY project employs the `pyPipe3D` pipeline for extracting spatially resolved galactic properties [Sánchez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RMxAA..60..323S/abstract).

When extending these models to large survey populations, standard Bayesian approaches can struggle with unrepresentative spectroscopic calibration samples and template inaccuracies. Implementing a [[hierarchical-bayesian-model|Hierarchical Bayesian Model]] offers a rigorous solution by jointly inferring the physical parameters of individual galaxies alongside population-level hyperparameters [Leistedt et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881...80L/abstract). This self-consistent separation of observational noise and SED template bias is particularly critical for estimating photometric redshifts ($z_{\rm phot}$). The community has established a strong consensus that the established method of stacking full photometric redshift [[density-estimation-techniques|Density estimation techniques]] itself yields a systematically biased population distribution; accurately recovering the true distribution requires hierarchical frameworks rather than simple PDF stacking [Malz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..127M/abstract).

A persistent tension in traditional Bayesian SED fitting is its computational cost. Evaluating complex SPS models often requires hours of computation per object, rendering classical sampling approaches intractable for next-generation surveys containing billions of galaxies. To circumvent this bottleneck, the field is rapidly transitioning toward neural emulation and Simulation-Based Inference (SBI). By training neural density estimators on forward-simulated spectra, SBI pipelines can bypass explicit likelihood evaluations entirely, reducing processing time to seconds or less per galaxy while maintaining excellent posterior calibration [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110640H/abstract). Alternatively, tools like the 'Speculator' neural network emulator are used to accelerate explicit likelihood calculations within a traditional MCMC framework, achieving massive speedups without abandoning the explicit likelihood model [Thorp et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..145T/abstract). 

Furthermore, data-driven approaches are extending SED fitting beyond one-dimensional spectra and photometry. Recent work utilizing generative machine learning, such as [[conditional-density-estimation|Conditional density estimation]], allows for the direct integration of pixel-level morphological imaging into the inference pipeline. Incorporating spatial information has been shown to successfully mitigate notoriously difficult parameter degeneracies, such as the dust-age degeneracy, yielding noticeably tighter constraints on physical galaxy properties than traditional photometry alone [Yunus et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251205078Y/abstract).

## Statistical Challenges in Cosmological Redshift Estimation

Accurate cosmological inference from weak gravitational lensing and large-scale structure relies critically on the determination of the redshift distribution, $n(z)$, of vast galaxy samples. Because spectroscopic confirmation is observationally expensive for billions of sources, modern surveys depend heavily on photometric redshifts (photo-$z$). A strong consensus in the literature dictates that individual photo-$z$ point estimates are insufficient for precision cosmology, as they hide non-trivial systematic uncertainties, and that even the traditional method of stacking full [[density-estimation-techniques|Density estimation techniques]] yields systematically biased estimates of $n(z)$ [Malz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..127M/abstract). Instead, robust analysis requires advanced hierarchical inference to accurately recover the population distribution while propagating these non-Gaussian uncertainties [Leistedt et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881...80L/abstract), [Malz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..127M/abstract).

To correctly recover $n(z)$, the underlying prior probability densities must be explicitly known or self-consistently modeled; if a photo-$z$ pipeline utilizes an implicit or unknown prior, the resulting [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] cannot be rigorously combined for population-level inference [Malz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..127M/abstract). To address this, two primary methodologies have emerged: hierarchical Bayesian modeling and machine learning-based density estimation.

**Hierarchical Bayesian Modeling and Template Fitting**
Traditional template fitting provides interpretable [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] but often lacks the flexibility to account for inaccuracies in stellar population synthesis (SPS) templates or heteroscedastic observational noise. Historically, wide-field photometric redshift calibration relied strictly on empirical template-fitting anchored by spectroscopic training samples, a method notably pioneered in the CFHTLS T0004 release [Coupon et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...500..981C/abstract). Modern approaches build upon this by utilizing a [[hierarchical-bayesian-model|Hierarchical Bayesian Model]] to jointly infer the physical parameters of individual galaxies alongside population-level hyperparameters [Leistedt et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881...80L/abstract), [Leistedt et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..264...23L/abstract). By simultaneously performing a global statistical calibration of the photometric observations and the spectral energy distribution (SED) models, these frameworks produce highly compact and accurate redshift posteriors. While physical SPS models have historically been computationally intractable for massive datasets, the recent integration of neural emulators has accelerated these inferences, reducing evaluation times from hours to seconds per object [Leistedt et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..264...23L/abstract), [Thorp et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..145T/abstract). Furthermore, forward modeling of galaxy populations explicitly accounts for survey selection cuts, reducing strict reliance on spectroscopic calibration data [Alsing et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..264...29A/abstract).

**Machine Learning and Covariate Shift**
Alternatively, supervised machine learning models efficiently estimate redshifts by learning direct, non-linear mappings from photometry to spectroscopic redshifts. Applications like PhotoRApToR, a Java-based tool utilizing Multi Layer Perceptron with Quasi Newton Algorithm (MLPQNA), underscore the utility of deploying robust machine learning for photo-$z$ estimation [Cavuoti et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..306..307C/abstract). Historically, robust machine learning architectures have tackled these problems effectively. For example, utilizing nearest-neighbor instance-based algorithms to generate full probabilistic photometric redshifts for galaxies and quasars in SDSS and GALEX data significantly reduced catastrophic errors in optical quasar samples [Ball et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...683...12B/abstract). 

However, these data-driven methods frequently struggle with the aforementioned issue of covariate shift. Currently, there is an active debate over the optimal mitigation strategy. While some researchers advocate for ML-based propensity conditioning to artificially balance the covariates across parameter space [Autenrieth et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210611211A/abstract), others argue that physics-based hierarchical Bayesian frameworks inherently generalize better to regimes lacking complete spectroscopic coverage [Leistedt et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881...80L/abstract), [Alsing et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..264...29A/abstract). A comparative analysis of machine learning frameworks (such as METAPHOR and ANNz2) and template-fitting methods (like BPZ) on KiDS-ESO-DR3 data demonstrates that relying on a single approach can be insufficient; instead, a combined set of statistical estimators is necessary to properly evaluate PDF reliability and accuracy [Amaro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.3116A/abstract).

**Multi-modal and Image-Based Inference**
A recent paradigm shift in photo-$z$ estimation involves moving beyond pure broadband photometry. The integration of morphological, pixel-level imaging data alongside SEDs helps to break classical parameter degeneracies. Techniques like image-based [[conditional-density-estimation|Conditional density estimation]] [Yunus et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251205078Y/abstract) and self-supervised multi-modal foundation models pre-trained on large-scale datasets (e.g., Euclid imaging) [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315312E/abstract) have demonstrated that leveraging spatial information significantly tightens redshift posteriors. In some cases, foundation models fine-tuned on multi-modal data outperform fully supervised photometric pipelines using only 1% of the available spectroscopic labels [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315312E/abstract).

## Mitigating Observational Systematics and Missing Data

Modern astronomical surveys are inherently heterogeneous, suffering from varying seeing conditions, missing observations, and complex selection functions. Robust [[scientific-data-analysis|Scientific data analysis]] requires treating these observational systematics natively within the inference pipeline to prevent biased cosmological and astrophysical constraints.

**Survey Geometry and Selection Effects**
In large-scale structure (LSS) analyses, estimators must account for angular masking, fiber collisions, and survey boundaries. The MKSAMPLE algorithm, for instance, was essential for robustly constructing the BOSS DR12 large-scale structure catalogues and meticulously quantifying these survey masks [Reid et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.1553R/abstract). While traditional approaches modify correlation estimators directly—for example, by incorporating the integral constraint and masking algorithms [Scranton et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...579...48S/abstract)—modern forward-modeling frameworks embed these effects directly into the simulated data. For instance, the SIMBIG framework incorporates full survey realism, including fiber collisions and angular masks, to extract robust constraints on $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$ from galaxy clustering [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...04..010H/abstract). At the field level, [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] of the non-linear density and velocity fields can naturally handle highly structured survey geometries and selection effects by deploying Hamiltonian Monte Carlo within a Bayesian framework [Jasche et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407...29J/abstract). Furthermore, estimating accurate covariance matrices in the presence of massive superstructures requires robust internal resampling. Techniques such as spatial jackknife resampling provide an objective way to quantify clustering errors and assess whether a specific galaxy catalogue truly represents a "fair sample" of the universe [Norberg et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.2435N/abstract). However, unmodeled systematics, such as misspecified survey masks or approximate gravity models, can introduce significant biases exceeding $2\sigma$ in the inferred cosmological parameters, necessitating rigorous prior diagnostic checks [Hoellinger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204443H/abstract).

**Out-of-Distribution Errors and Missing Data in SBI**
As explored previously, simulation-based inference (SBI) provides massive computational speedups but traditionally requires the simulated training data to possess noise characteristics identical to the observed data. This assumption frequently breaks down in real surveys characterized by variable sky conditions and heteroscedastic noise. Methodological extensions like the SBI++ framework have solved this limitation by processing out-of-distribution (OOD) measurement errors using standard SBI evaluations and addressing missing data by marginalizing over nearby data realizations in the training set [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221103747W/abstract), [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952L..10W/abstract). While this missing data marginalization increases the computational cost per object from $\sim 1$ second to $\sim 1.5$ minutes, it securely expands the applicability of likelihood-free [[model-fitting|Model fitting]] to datasets with severe observational gaps without requiring the expensive retraining of neural density estimators [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221103747W/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The advent of Stage IV cosmological surveys, such as Euclid and Rubin LSST, presents unprecedented challenges for [[scientific-data-analysis|Scientific data analysis]] and [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]]. These surveys will catalog billions of galaxies, rendering traditional explicit likelihood methods—which can require $\gtrsim 10$ hours per object fit—computationally intractable [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221103747W/abstract). Achieving the necessary computational scalability requires a paradigm shift toward simulation-based inference (SBI) and amortized neural posteriors. These techniques bypass the need for an explicit likelihood function, delivering speedups of $10^3$ to $10^4$ and reducing inference times to mere seconds per object [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952L..10W/abstract), [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110640H/abstract).

A major future direction to support this scaling involves constructing end-to-end differentiable forward modeling pipelines. Differentiable "fuzzy" cosmic-web models, which utilize sigmoid-based gradient operations, now allow for efficient field-level inference directly compatible with machine learning frameworks [Rosselló et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603969R/abstract). However, an open question remains regarding the robustness of these pipelines to model misspecification. The literature highlights a critical tension: while SBI can extract powerful non-Gaussian information from small-scale clustering, it is highly sensitive to certain choices in the forward model. While SBI has proven robust to changes between exact and particle-mesh gravity models, subtle misspecifications in halo-occupation distributions (HOD) can propagate into biased cosmological parameter recovery . In other contexts, reliance on approximate gravity models can also yield biases exceeding $2\sigma$ in the $(\Omega_m, \sigma_8)$ plane [Hoellinger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241204443H/abstract). Maintaining computational speed while scaling implementations that handle "out-of-distribution" (OOD) measurement errors and missing data remains an active area of methodology development [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221103747W/abstract), [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952L..10W/abstract).

Another transformative development is the rise of multi-modal foundation models for [[model-fitting|Model fitting]] and classification. Historically, photometric redshift estimation relied on predefined spectral templates or pure photometry. Recent frameworks introduce foundation models (e.g., AstroPT) pre-trained on hundreds of thousands of Euclid images and spectral energy distributions (SEDs). These models outperform fully supervised methods in redshift and stellar mass estimation using only $1\%$ of the training labels [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315312E/abstract). Furthermore, physics-informed architectures utilizing Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) and [[conditional-density-estimation|Conditional density estimation]] are beginning to integrate morphological pixel-level data directly into SED fitting. This multi-modal approach effectively mitigates dust-age degeneracies and allows for the probabilistic inference of emission line fluxes and central black hole masses solely from imaging data [Amira Siddiqa et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251112737A/abstract), [Yunus et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251205078Y/abstract).

Despite these algorithmic advances, several fundamental statistical questions remain unresolved. Methodologically, there is an ongoing challenge in recovering mathematically rigorous [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] for photometric redshifts when the underlying machine learning prior is implicit or unknown. Because the traditional stacking of full probability density functions yields systematically biased population distributions, developing hierarchical frameworks capable of deriving $n(z)$ without fully known priors remains a crucial hurdle for precision cosmology [Malz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..127M/abstract). Physically, a debate persists regarding the "self-averaging" property of spatial galaxy fluctuations. While the standard $\Lambda$CDM model assumes statistical homogeneity on large scales, some analyses of SDSS data suggest the Universe remains inhomogeneous and non-self-averaging on scales up to $100 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$, conflicting with standard fluctuation frameworks [Sylos Labini et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...508...17S/abstract). Resolving these tensions will require deeper synergies between high-dimensional [[statistical-methods-in-astrophysics|Statistical methods in astrophysics]] and highly scalable forward simulations.
