## Article

**Statistical methods in astrophysics** encompass the mathematical, computational, and probabilistic frameworks required to extract physical insights from observational data. As astronomy has transitioned from the manual analysis of photographic plates to the petascale data streams of modern automated surveys, rigorous statistical treatments have become an indispensable component of the field [Feigelson et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003sca..book.....F/abstract). Broadly categorized under the interdisciplinary umbrella of "Astrostatistics," these methods integrate [[frequentist-inference|Frequentist inference]], Bayesian probability theory, and machine learning to solve underdetermined inverse problems. By rigorously modeling instrumental noise and background signals, statistical techniques are essential for robust [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] and [[uncertainty-quantification|Uncertainty quantification]] across highly complex, multi-dimensional [[parameter-estimation|parameter spaces]]. These frameworks address fundamental astrophysical challenges, ranging from constraining the $\Lambda$CDM cosmological model to the automated filtering of transient light curves.

At the core of modern astrophysical data analysis is a widespread paradigm shift toward probabilistic reasoning. Bayesian frameworks, frequently driven by algorithms like Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) and [[nested-sampling|Nested sampling]], have established themselves as the gold standard for exploring high-dimensional [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] in cosmology and stellar modeling [Hobson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009bmc..book.....H/abstract), [Lewis et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ascl.soft06025L/abstract). Concurrently, specialized frequentist approaches—such as the application of the Cash $C$-statistic for Poissonian X-ray data—remain necessary to mitigate [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]] that traditional $\chi^2$ approximations inadvertently introduce. To quantify the spatial distribution of galaxies, astronomers rely on spatial point processes and "beyond-two-point" clustering statistics to map the cosmic web [Shectman et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...470..172S/abstract), [Martínez et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002sgd..book.....M/abstract). In the realm of large-scale, time-domain observatories, deep learning architectures and [[probabilistic-classification|Probabilistic classification]] algorithms now perform complex tasks like galaxy morphology classification and photometric redshift estimation, handling high-dimensional image and [[functional-data-analysis|Functional data analysis]] natively.

As the volume and complexity of astronomical data continue to outpace traditional computational capabilities, the field is rapidly advancing toward Simulation-Based Inference (SBI) and implicit likelihood methods. By leveraging neural [[density-estimation-techniques|Density estimation techniques]], SBI can bypass analytically intractable likelihood functions, offering massive computational speedups over exact MCMC while retaining accurate [[error-estimation-in-astrophysics|Error estimation in astrophysics]]. Despite these methodological successes, significant open questions remain. Researchers are actively working to mitigate the vulnerability of machine learning models to "covariate shift"—the statistical mismatch between simulated training sets and heterogeneous observed data. Furthermore, advanced statistical techniques are actively being deployed to resolve enduring physical debates, such as utilizing causal discovery algorithms to probe galaxy-black hole coevolution, or evaluating deep spatial statistics to test the foundational assumption of cosmological homogeneity against competing models of a fractal universe.

## Historical Evolution of Astrostatistics

Historically, the disciplines of astronomy and statistics were intimately intertwined, with early astronomical problems driving foundational developments in statistical theory. Foundational works in historical frameworks derived basic mathematical equations for galaxy evolution and universe expansion in Big Bang cosmology [Silk et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980bbce.book.....S/abstract). Comprehensive textbooks like *Advanced Astrophysics* [Duric et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003adas.book.....D/abstract) and *The Tapestry of Modern Astrophysics* [Shore et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002tma..book.....S/abstract) summarized the necessary background on orbital mechanics, statistical treatments of stellar structure, and image formation and reconstruction. During the 20th century, however, the fields largely drifted apart as astronomy focused increasingly on physical astrophysics and statistics pivoted toward human and biological sciences [Feigelson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0903.0416F/abstract). Observational astronomy during this era relied heavily on manual [[astronomical-data-science-and-reduction|data reduction]] and human visual inspection. Mid-century initiatives, such as the photographic plates of the Palomar Observatory Sky Survey, provided foundational datasets that revolutionized stellar evolution and cosmology, yet their scientific exploitation was inherently bottlenecked by manual processing capacities [Sandage et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ARA&A..37..445S/abstract).

The observational landscape shifted dramatically in the early 1990s with the advent of digital detectors and the first automated digital sky surveys, ushering astronomy into the terascale "Big Data" era [Djorgovski et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221201493D/abstract). As digital surveys emerged, defining clean datasets with well-characterized [[sample-selection-biases|selection functions]] became critical. For example, the cross-identification of GALEX and SDSS catalogs was achieved using specialized geometric approaches for primaries [Budavári et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...694.1281B/abstract). Surveys began generating datasets with billions of feature vectors spanning hundreds of dimensions, necessitating the automation of fundamental tasks such as star-galaxy separation. This computational challenge spurred the development of specialized [[astronomical-data-analysis-software|Astronomical data analysis software]] and the formal emergence of "Astroinformatics"—a collaborative discipline bridging astrophysics, computer science, and massive data analytics [Saha et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150407865S/abstract). During this transitional period, astronomers also began increasingly employing rigorous Bayesian and [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|likelihood methods]] to test controversial hypotheses, such as claims of redshift quantization and periodicities in binary galaxy data [Cocke et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...393...59C/abstract). To fully exploit these complex, multi-wavelength datasets, the astronomical community spearheaded the conceptualization and development of the federated Virtual Observatory, designed to unify disparate global databases [Feigelson et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003sca..book.....F/abstract).

This deluge of digital data catalyzed a modern resurgence in the intersection of astronomy and statistics, formally recognized as "Astrostatistics" [Feigelson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0903.0416F/abstract). Broad 21st-century statistical and computational challenges in the field heavily feature the rigorous treatment of heteroscedastic [[measurement-uncertainty|measurement errors]] and the detection of faint periodic signals amidst complex instrumental noise [Feigelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AnRSA...8..493F/abstract). As surveys transitioned from terascale static catalogs to petascale time-domain and synoptic data streams, traditional semi-analytic models and [[fitting-algorithms|manual template-fitting techniques]] became computationally intractable [Djorgovski et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221201493D/abstract). Furthermore, astronomers recognized that conventional summary statistics and standard $\chi^2$ approximations often introduced severe biases, even in high-count Poisson regimes [Humphrey et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...693..822H/abstract). 

Consequently, the turn of the 21st century saw a fundamental paradigm shift in cosmological data analysis toward Bayesian probability theory [Hobson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009bmc..book.....H/abstract). Bayesian inference quickly became the standard framework for rigorous [[uncertainty-quantification|Uncertainty quantification]], [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]], and complex [[model-comparison|Model comparison]] in high-dimensional parameter spaces. The push for computational reproducibility also defines the modern era, famously illustrated by the use of containerized [[astronomical-software|Astronomical software]] packages to reconstruct complex imaging results, such as the first image of a black hole in the galaxy M87 from the Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration [Patel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022CSE....24e..42P/abstract). Concurrently, new educational toolkits like AstronomyCalc have emerged to integrate theoretical cosmological physics with [[statistical-inference-and-data-methods|practical data analysis methods]] like MCMC, enabling a new generation of scientists to explore cosmic dynamics natively in Python [Giri et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250105491G/abstract). Today, processing petascale data streams relies on an integration of modern Astrostatistics and artificial intelligence, showcasing an evolution from the manual inspection of photographic plates to the absolute necessity of automated, robust statistical methodologies in observational astrophysics.

## Bayesian Inference and Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)

The shift towards Bayesian approaches in the 21st century marks a fundamental evolution in cosmological and astrophysical data analysis [Hobson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009bmc..book.....H/abstract). Foundational theoretical work in the statistical mechanics of stellar systems early on explored the entropy of cosmic bodies and the Hamiltonian-Jacobi dynamics of galactic orbits [Lynden-Bell et al. (1973)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1973dses.conf...91L/abstract). Today, Bayesian inference provides a rigorous computational framework for solving underdetermined inverse problems and performing [[physical-parameter-estimation|Physical parameter estimation]] by evaluating the [[posterior-distributions|posterior distribution]]. This is given by Bayes' theorem:
$$P(\theta|D) = \frac{P(D|\theta)P(\theta)}{P(D)}$$
where $D$ represents the observational data, $\theta$ the model parameters, $P(D|\theta)$ the likelihood, and $P(\theta)$ the prior. This approach is now the standard for robust [[uncertainty-quantification|Uncertainty quantification]] across the field [Brewer et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0809.0939B/abstract).

To explore these posterior distributions, astrophysicists traditionally rely on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. Standard MCMC algorithms, such as those implemented in widespread [[astronomical-data-analysis-software|Astronomical data analysis software]] like CosmoMC [Lewis et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ascl.soft06025L/abstract), are effective for models with moderate dimensionality. However, standard MCMC is often prohibitively slow when applied to high-dimensional problems, such as large-scale imaging or non-linear 3D density field reconstructions [Jasche et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407...29J/abstract), [Marignier et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..45M/abstract).

To overcome these computational bottlenecks, advanced sampling techniques have been widely adopted:
* **Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC):** By utilizing the gradient of the log-posterior, HMC efficiently explores high-dimensional, highly non-Gaussian parameter spaces. HMC has proven critical for precision non-linear large-scale structure inference, successfully sampling joint posterior distributions with millions of dimensions to recover the 3D cosmic web while properly propagating non-Gaussian errors [Jasche et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407...29J/abstract), [Jasche et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...779...15J/abstract).
* **[[nested-sampling|Nested sampling]]:** Frequently used for Bayesian [[model-selection|Model selection]] and [[model-comparison|Model comparison]], nested sampling efficiently calculates the Bayesian evidence (the marginal likelihood, $P(D)$). Alternatively, in fields like gravitational wave astronomy, Bayesian evidence for competing models is often computed using robust alternatives like reversible jump MCMC, Savage-Dickie ratios, and Laplace approximations [Cornish et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007PhRvD..76h3006C/abstract).
* **Trans-dimensional MCMC:** In this variant, the dimensionality of the parameter space is allowed to vary during the sampling process. This has been used to enforce wavelet sparsity in weak lensing mass-mapping, producing naturally parsimonious models of dark matter distributions using less than $1\%$ of the potential maximum number of wavelet coefficients [Marignier et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..45M/abstract).

In scenarios involving heterogeneous datasets, the [[hierarchical-bayesian-model|Hierarchical Bayesian Model]] allows for the simultaneous calibration of instrumental systematics and physical models. For example, hierarchical frameworks have been deployed to simultaneously model photometric redshift distributions, correct spectral templates, and recalibrate broad-band photometry without requiring perfectly representative training data [Leistedt et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881...80L/abstract). In multi-epoch surveys, multi-epoch modeling utilizing Bayesian generative approaches—such as the parameterization of images using shapelet functions—is similarly employed to systematically control observational biases in data reduction pipelines [Bosch et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhDT.......226B/abstract).

However, Bayesian methods demand careful attention to [[priors-in-astrophysics|Priors in astrophysics]]. The literature demonstrates that inaccurate or overly broad priors can drive inference toward unphysical results. For instance, applying flat priors to Bayesian fits of galaxy rotation curves can incorrectly suggest that the Newtonian gravitational constant $G$ varies from galaxy to galaxy—a false signal that vanishes when an empirically motivated log-normal prior is applied [Li et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646L..13L/abstract).

Given the massive computational cost of exact MCMC for next-generation surveys, Variational Inference (VI) has emerged as a much faster alternative for [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] [Gunapati et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASA...39....1G/abstract). VI casts inference as an [[optimization-methods|optimization problem]], minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence between an approximate distribution and the true posterior. While VI can provide competitive parameter estimates orders of magnitude faster than MCMC, an active tension remains in the literature: both standard VI and MCMC suffer from sampling noise in stochastic KL divergence minimization, prompting the development of new optimization techniques [Seljak et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190104454S/abstract). This maintains a standing debate over the optimal balance between computational efficiency and absolute accuracy in cosmological inference.

## Frequentist Methods and Systematic Error Mitigation

While Bayesian frameworks dominate high-dimensional [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]], [[frequentist-inference|Frequentist inference]] remains crucial for hypothesis testing, goodness-of-fit assessments, and the mitigation of [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]]. 

A standard frequentist workhorse for [[model-fitting|Model fitting]] in astrophysics is the $\chi^2$ statistic. However, applying standard $\chi^2$ approximations to Poisson-distributed astronomical data yields intrinsically biased parameter estimates, even in the high-count regime. This bias persists unless the number of data bins is significantly smaller than $\sqrt{N_c}$, where $N_c$ is the total number of counts [Humphrey et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...693..822H/abstract). For example, in X-ray astronomy, the [[least-squares-method|Least squares method]] or $\chi^2$ minimization can systematically skew recovered physical parameters, inducing a $5\%$--$10\%$ shift in galaxy cluster scaling relations [Humphrey et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...693..822H/abstract). To avoid these statistical biases, the Cash $C$-statistic—a [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|maximum likelihood estimator]] strictly derived for Poisson distributions—must be used in place of $\chi^2$ [Humphrey et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...693..822H/abstract). In parallel, exact probabilistic methods for background subtraction in high-energy event lists have been developed to fully account for Poisson fluctuations without resulting in unphysical negative counts [Ehlert et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515.5185E/abstract). Moreover, frequentist tools like the modified maximum likelihood (MML) method offer high-speed alternatives to computationally expensive [[hierarchical-bayesian-model|hierarchical modeling]]. MML has proven highly effective for recovering underlying physical distributions, such as galaxy mass functions, while successfully accounting for Eddington bias and the effects of cosmic structure [Obreschkow et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5500O/abstract).

In precision cosmology, characterizing large-scale structure requires the meticulous removal of observational contaminants (e.g., stellar densities, seeing variations, and galactic extinction) from survey data. Traditionally, these spatially varying systematics are mitigated using template subtraction. However, detailed mathematical analyses demonstrate that standard template subtraction induces a bias in angular clustering estimates, such as the two-point correlation function and power spectra [Elsner et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.2095E/abstract).

To correct this, mode projection algorithms have been developed to deproject the contaminated modes from the covariance matrix. Basic mode projection mathematically guarantees an unbiased estimator of the true underlying clustering signal, whereas some "extended" mode projection frameworks inadvertently reintroduce bias [Elsner et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.2095E/abstract). These mapping techniques can be generalized within a common [[distribution-regression-framework|regression framework]], allowing for extensions that treat both additive and multiplicative contamination in pseudo-$C_\ell$ power spectrum analyses [Weaverdyck et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.5061W/abstract). Furthermore, adopting specialized posterior distributions for the matter power spectrum can significantly reduce measurement biases in primordial non-Gaussianity constraints while lowering the computational burden of systematic model testing [Kalus et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2573K/abstract). While these deprojection techniques successfully yield unbiased high-precision clustering measurements, the cleaning process generally comes at the cost of increasing the overall estimator variance [Elsner et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.2095E/abstract).

Frequentist goodness-of-fit testing is also evolving to meet the demands of modern, low-noise datasets. While the classical $\chi^2$ test evaluates model fits, alternative metrics like the Leave One Out-Probability Integral Transform (LOO-PIT) are increasingly adopted to precisely characterize the match between model and data. Combining LOO-PIT distributions with standard Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests can outperform standalone $\chi^2$ tests in identifying spurious signals, such as emission line interlopers in baryon acoustic oscillation surveys [Nguyen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...01..008N/abstract). Additionally, classical frequentist tests, such as the Neyman-Pearson lemma, remain highly effective in [[probabilistic-classification|Probabilistic classification]] tasks, successfully separating distinct source populations like millisecond and normal pulsars in period-period derivative space [Lee et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.2832L/abstract).

## Simulation-Based and Implicit Likelihood Inference

As astronomical datasets grow in complexity and scale, traditional [[maximum-likelihood-estimation|Maximum likelihood estimation]] and exact Bayesian sampling often encounter computationally intractable analytic likelihoods. Simulation-Based Inference (SBI)—also known as implicit or "likelihood-free" inference—bypasses the need for an explicit analytic likelihood function $p(d|\theta)$. Instead, it relies on complex forward-modeling and simulated mock data to directly estimate [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] or likelihood ratios. Closely related to [[approximate-bayesian-computation|Approximate Bayesian computation]], modern SBI leverages machine learning to overcome the curse of dimensionality.

To support these robust methods, projects like MillenniumTNG utilize advanced semi-analytic galaxy formation models, such as L-GALAXIES, to generate high-precision mock galaxy catalogs on continuous past lightcones specifically for direct survey comparisons [Barrera et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.6312B/abstract). The core mechanism of SBI typically involves neural [[density-estimation-techniques|Density estimation techniques]] and normalizing flows. These architectures learn the highly non-linear mapping between physical parameters and observable summary statistics. Normalizing flows are also utilized for covariant posterior decomposition, a technique that identifies the specific parameter combinations most effectively constrained by complex cosmological experiments like DES and the Planck satellite [Dacunha et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.105f3529D/abstract). General-purpose frameworks, such as the Learning the Universe Implicit Likelihood Inference (LtU-ILI) pipeline, now allow researchers to deploy these neural architectures for a variety of tasks, ranging from inferring galaxy cluster masses from X-ray photometry to capturing physical dust parameters from galaxy colors [Ho et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..54H/abstract). 

The primary advantage of SBI is its massive computational acceleration without a significant loss in accuracy. Once the neural density estimator is trained, evaluating the posterior is highly efficient, often achieving speedups of $\sim 10^4$ over traditional sampling methods. For example, inferring physical parameters from the light profiles of galaxies can be reduced from $\gtrsim 10$ hours per fit to approximately $1$ second per object [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952L..10W/abstract). Furthermore, Bayesian emulator optimization—which interpolates between a limited number of expensive simulations using Gaussian processes—can drastically reduce [[uncertainty-quantification|Uncertainty quantification]] metrics. When applied to the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest, optimized emulation shrinks the $95\%$ [[credible-interval|credible volume]] by $90\%$ and reduces the $1\sigma$ error on the amplitude of small-scale primordial fluctuations by $38\%$ [Rogers et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...02..031R/abstract).

SBI is increasingly prevalent in cosmological [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] and large-scale structure analyses. It is an essential tool for "beyond-two-point" statistics, enabling field-level inference and joint power spectrum and bispectrum analyses where the analytic likelihood is highly non-Gaussian and difficult to formulate [Beyond-2pt Collaboration et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240502252B/abstract). In this context, the CAMELS-SAM suite has effectively demonstrated that neural networks can strictly marginalize over complex astrophysical uncertainties to constrain cosmology using clustering summary statistics like counts-in-cells [Perez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954...11P/abstract). In high-energy astrophysics, implicit likelihood and advanced statistical techniques leverage the inherent "clumpiness" of photons from point sources to separate faint extragalactic dark matter annihilation signals from dominant astrophysical backgrounds [Mishra-Sharma et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180904665M/abstract). Beyond cosmology, normalizing flows are used to upsample coarse $N$-body simulations; the GALAXYFLOW algorithm accurately estimates stellar phase-space densities to generate realistic synthetic star catalogs that mirror observational data [Lim et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533..143L/abstract), complementing advanced mock catalog generators like `py-ananke` [Thob et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JOSS....9.6234T/abstract).

Despite its power, the literature highlights a significant tension: standard SBI is highly susceptible to covariate shift. It requires the simulated training data to perfectly match the statistical characteristics of the observed data. In practice, real astronomical surveys are heterogeneous, suffering from missing observations, out-of-distribution measurement errors, and variable noise due to fluctuating telescope conditions. If unaddressed, this discrepancy leads to heavily biased inference. To resolve this, recent methodological extensions like SBI++ have been developed to marginalize over missing data and approximate out-of-distribution errors using evaluations over nearby data realizations in the training set. While this robust treatment slows the inference process from $\sim 1$ second to $\sim 1.5$ minutes per object, it remains significantly faster than traditional methods and critically expands the reliability of SBI for heterogeneous datasets, such as deep extragalactic imaging from the James Webb Space Telescope [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221103747W/abstract), [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952L..10W/abstract).

## Spatial Point Processes and Large-Scale Structure

The spatial distribution of galaxies is canonically modeled as a stochastic spatial point process [Martínez et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002sgd..book.....M/abstract). The primary summary statistics for quantifying this process are the two-point correlation function, $\xi(r)$, and its Fourier conjugate, the power spectrum $P(k)$ [Hamilton et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009LNP...665..415H/abstract). To construct accurate power spectra models, theoreticians deploy growth models for density fluctuations that utilize Fourier harmonics of hydrodynamical parameters to derive foundational power-law relations [Tugay et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017OAP....30...51T/abstract). Accurate extraction of observational statistics requires rigorous treatments of [[sample-selection-biases|Sample selection biases]] and observational systematics, such as varying seeing conditions, Galactic reddening, and bright star masks [Scranton et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...579...48S/abstract). Notably, traditional template subtraction methods used to remove spatially varying systematics have been mathematically shown to introduce [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]] into angular clustering estimates, necessitating the use of specifically derived unbiased estimators or mode projection techniques [Elsner et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.2095E/abstract), [Weaverdyck et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.5061W/abstract). When dealing with inherent non-uniformities or gaps in sampling, such as those present in the VIPERS survey, Bayesian filters and nearest-neighbor algorithms are frequently used to rigorously restore homogeneous sky coverage [Cucciati et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...565A..67C/abstract). Furthermore, robust [[error-estimation-in-astrophysics|Error estimation in astrophysics]] for spatial data frequently employs spatial jackknife resampling to objectively quantify the influence of individual superstructures on measured clustering statistics [Norberg et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.2435N/abstract).

While the power spectrum effectively captures the linear regime of density perturbations, testing the fundamental assumption of statistical isotropy in galaxy survey catalogs provides a necessary mathematical check on standard models, often achieved using rigorous multipole vector statistics [Zunckel et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvD..84d3005Z/abstract). Furthermore, the highly non-linear topology of the cosmic web requires "beyond-two-point" statistics [Beyond-2pt Collaboration et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240502252B/abstract). Morphological structures such as galaxy clusters, filaments, sheets, and voids are quantified using a variety of specialized point process algorithms. For example, the Minimal Spanning Tree (MST) provides an objective, threshold-independent method for identifying filamentary structures [Ling et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987PhDT........12L/abstract), while spatial tessellations [Ling et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987PhDT........12L/abstract) and velocity shear analyses [Libeskind et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453L.108L/abstract) are often employed to delimit voids and sheets. Broadening the statistical domain, rotation measure grids obtained from radio interferometers now utilize new statistical approaches to actively study the magnetic properties of these cosmic filaments and galaxy clusters [Vacca et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015aska.confE.114V/abstract). Recent advancements also incorporate nearest-neighbor distributions, density-split clustering, and bispectrum analyses to maximize the extraction of cosmological information from the non-linear regime [Beyond-2pt Collaboration et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240502252B/abstract).

Field-level inference has emerged as a paradigm shift, utilizing the full three-dimensional density field rather than reduced summary statistics. Formulating large-scale structure analysis as an underdetermined inverse problem allows for rigorous Bayesian [[uncertainty-quantification|Uncertainty quantification]] [Jasche et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.407...29J/abstract). Advanced high-dimensional sampling algorithms, such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC), enable the joint [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] of the 3D density field, the cosmological power spectrum, and luminosity-dependent galaxy biases directly from the discrete point process [Jasche et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...779...15J/abstract). These methods seamlessly propagate non-Gaussian uncertainties into the [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] without requiring the prior assumption of a background power spectrum.

Despite the standardization of these methods, a persistent debate in the literature concerns the fractal nature of the galaxy distribution and the transition scale to large-scale homogeneity. In standard $\Lambda$CDM models, the universe is expected to behave as a self-similar structure at small scales but transition to homogeneity at scales $\sim 10 \, h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Borgani et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995PhR...251....1B/abstract). However, multiple statistical analyses of spatial fluctuations argue that the galaxy distribution exhibits scale-invariant, fractal properties on scales up to 40-70 $h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Sylos Labini et al. ## Automated Classification and Photometric Redshifts

The deluge of data from wide-field synoptic sky surveys necessitates the use of automated pipelines and [[astronomical-data-analysis-software|Astronomical data analysis software]] for the characterization of billions of observed sources. Over the past decade, [[probabilistic-classification|Probabilistic classification]] algorithms have largely superseded traditional color-cut methods for tasks such as star-galaxy separation and object identification. In the time domain, boosting and [[density-estimation-techniques|kernel density estimation]] (KDE) techniques have been robustly developed for effective photometric supernova typing using light curve data [Newling et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.1987N/abstract). 

In addition to discriminative classifiers, finite mixture models are extensively used for semi-parametric density estimation. These probabilistic models excel at identifying overlapping source populations in survey data, which is particularly useful for analyzing distinct stellar clusters and effectively rejecting background contamination [Kuhn et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv171111101K/abstract). Morphological automation has additionally advanced; the symmetry-peakiness-alignment (SPA) criterion enables the confident automated identification of relaxed galaxy clusters across varying levels of data quality [Mantz et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..199M/abstract). For multi-class prediction, Random Forest classifiers trained on multi-band photometry can achieve classification accuracies exceeding $99\%$ for stars and galaxies, and $>94\%$ for quasars [Bai et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AJ....157....9B/abstract). Deep learning architectures, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), have similarly proven highly effective, reaching success rates of nearly $95\%$ in object classification directly from one-dimensional spectra [Hála et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.8341H/abstract). Moving to radio astronomy, frameworks integrating unsupervised clustering and CNNs are heavily used to efficiently classify integrated HI spectral profiles [Jaimes-Illanes et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250111657J/abstract). 

However, machine learning (ML) models are highly sensitive to covariate shift—the statistical mismatch between the distributions of the spectroscopic training sets and the target photometric survey data. To mitigate the resulting biases, researchers employ techniques like stratified learning using propensity scores to re-weight non-representative training samples [Autenrieth et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210611211A/abstract). To further augment modeling and correct disparate data, semi-supervised Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) combined with noise-emulating modules are utilized for deep image-to-image translation and realistic noise reconstruction across astronomical imaging surveys [Lin et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210107389L/abstract).

A critical application of these algorithms is the estimation of photometric redshifts (photo-$z$) and their associated [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] (often referred to as PDZs). Accurate photo-$z$ estimates are a strict prerequisite for precision cosmology measurements, such as weak lensing and large-scale structure analyses. The literature highlights an ongoing tension between two primary photo-$z$ paradigms: spectral energy distribution (SED) template fitting and empirical ML methods. While ML approaches generally provide superior point-estimate precision when trained on dense, representative data, they frequently fail to produce reliable probability density functions in sparsely populated regions of color-space, such as at redshifts $z > 1$ [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...644A..31E/abstract). Conversely, template-fitting methods extrapolate more robustly into out-of-distribution regimes and yield physically interpretable models, but they often fail to capture all the non-linear information present in the photometry [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...644A..31E/abstract). 

To resolve this conflict, the field is increasingly adopting hybrid methodologies. Extensive statistical analyses of probability density functions—such as those run on KiDS-ESO-DR3 galaxies—reveal that a combined set of statistical estimators is required to accurately assess the reliability of any photometric redshift PDF [Amaro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.3116A/abstract). Other "collaborative" approaches use SED fitting to identify coarse spectral types, which then specialize the training of distinct machine learning regression models, significantly improving overall photo-$z$ precision [Cavuoti et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.2039C/abstract). Furthermore, [[hierarchical-bayesian-model|Hierarchical Bayesian models]] have been developed to combine the strengths of both approaches, using flexible ML-like parameterizations to simultaneously correct spectral templates and recalibrate photometric observations [Leistedt et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881...80L/abstract). Recent breakthroughs also involve bypassing simple broad-band photometry catalogs entirely in favor of processing raw pixel-level images using CNNs. Incorporating morphological information from images alongside photometry has been shown to reduce redshift estimation bias by a factor of $10$ in the range $0.1 < z < 1.25$ [Do et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241000271D/abstract). To perform rigorous [[error-estimation-in-astrophysics|Error estimation in astrophysics]] with these complex models, architectures like Bayesian CNNs have been coupled with conformal prediction techniques, achieving an impressive root-mean-square error of $0.098$ and a standard outlier rate of just $3.9\%$ on modern datasets [Jones et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..159J/abstract). 

Beyond classification and redshift estimation, generative deep learning frameworks such as Conditional Flow Matching are now utilizing image data to break long-standing physical degeneracies in parameter inference, such as the confusion between dust attenuation and stellar age in galaxy populations [Yunus et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251205078Y/abstract). Moreover, machine learning is increasingly deployed to augment manual data analysis by discovering non-linear, high-dimensional scaling relations. This approach has successfully been used to reduce intrinsic scatter in cluster mass estimates, such as those derived from the Sunyaev-Zeldovich flux-mass relation [Wadekar et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PNAS..12002074W/abstract).

## Functional and High-Dimensional Data Analysis

Cosmic demographics have historically relied on multivariate statistics applied to fixed-length vectors of object properties. However, the proliferation of automated digital sky surveys has increasingly shifted the analytical focus toward [[functional-data-analysis|Functional data analysis]] (FDA). Instead of discrete scalar values, FDA deals with data that are inherently continuous functions, such as light curves (brightness time series) and spectra (brightness versus wavelength) [Loredo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240814466L/abstract). In these contexts, rigorous analytical statistics are pervasive: statistical lensing methods are essential for reconstructing cluster matter distributions and determining the Hubble constant from multiply-imaged sources [Wu et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996FCPh...17....1W/abstract), while analytic statistical methods are routinely employed to map average surface brightness and time variations induced by gravitational microlensing from stellar masses [Deguchi et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ApJ...335...67D/abstract). In the realm of gas and fluid dynamics, early comparative studies demonstrated that second-order flux-splitting methods significantly outperform first-order Godunov schemes for modeling astrophysical gas flows [van Albada et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982A&A...108...76V/abstract). These robust computational approaches naturally extend to relativistic fluid dynamics models, providing the deep mathematical basis required for analyzing heavy ion reactions and galaxy formation in cosmological contexts [Anile et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989LNM..1385.....A/abstract). Statistical methods are also applied in high-energy astrophysics to model the flow of mass and energy in stellar-mass black hole systems, providing a framework to balance accretion energy with environmental feedback via radiation, jets, and winds [Fender et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016LNP...905...65F/abstract). Modern numerical codes, such as AREPO, now routinely implement ideal magnetohydrodynamics on unstructured moving grids to provide automatic mesh adaptivity for complex astrophysical fluid problems [Pakmor et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.1392P/abstract). However, analyses of [[error-estimation-in-astrophysics|numerical errors]] in these high-dimensional simulations show that while time-reversible integration can handle necessary adaptive stepping, it critically does not guarantee energy conservation compared to specialized symplectic integrators [Hernandez et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.5570H/abstract).

Simultaneously, the immense spectral and spatial resolution of modern instruments, such as integral field units, frequently places datasets in the High-Dimensional Low Sample Size (HDLSS) regime. In the HDLSS limit, the number of data dimensions $d$ vastly exceeds the number of available observed samples $n$, such that $n \ll d$ [Takeuchi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271...44T/abstract). The scientific interpretation of these vast, high-dimensional stellar spectroscopic samples relies heavily on accurately compiled high-quality atomic transition data to extract precise chemical abundances [Heiter et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUGA..30..458H/abstract).

Astronomical functional data present unique statistical challenges: they typically suffer from sparse, irregular, and asynchronous sampling, which is further compounded by heteroscedastic [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] [Loredo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240814466L/abstract). In time-domain studies, dedicated statistical methods for variability selection in sparsely sampled optical data enable the crucial identification of low-luminosity active galactic nuclei in deep surveys like the GOODS fields [Villforth et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...723..737V/abstract). To rigorously analyze complex functional populations while accounting for these systemic effects, researchers frequently utilize a [[hierarchical-bayesian-model|Hierarchical Bayesian Model]] framework. Such frameworks allow for the construction of functional mixed-effects models and stochastic process models that accumulate information both along and across the measured functions. For example, population-based approaches utilizing [[non-parametric-gaussian-process-regression|Non-parametric Gaussian process regression]] can successfully infer the average profiles and population variances of spatial distributions from noisy, low signal-to-noise measurements, entirely avoiding the need to bin data or rigidly parameterize the mean profile shape [Farahi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....161...30F/abstract).

In the HDLSS regime, standard statistical estimators often fail or become ill-posed, traditionally forcing astronomers to discard information to satisfy $d < n$. To extract physical signals without sacrificing dimensionality, specialized variants of Principal Component Analysis (PCA) have been developed. High-dimensional techniques such as noise-reduction PCA (NRPCA) and automatic sparse PCA (A-SPCA) allow for robust [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]] and feature extraction even when $n \ll d$. When applied to complex, high-dimensional data such as Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) spectroscopic maps, high-dimensional PCA can successfully decouple the spatial structure of systemic rotation (Doppler shifts) from subtle spectral features, enabling the quantitative isolation of global outflows in starburst galaxies like NGC 253 [Takeuchi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271...44T/abstract). A significant data compression method is the MOPED algorithm, which massively reduces data dimensionality to make high-dimensional spectral likelihood surfaces computationally tractable, enabling rapid and robust stellar population modeling and the extraction of galaxy star formation histories [Panter et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.343.1145P/abstract).

To further handle the massive influx of functional data expected from next-generation observatories, Bayesian FDA is increasingly being integrated with machine learning algorithms. By combining functional mixed-effects modeling with neural networks and representation learning, it is possible to compress highly complex functional data into tractable, low-dimensional parametric models [Loredo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240814466L/abstract). This hybrid approach allows for the retention of physical interpretability and rigorous uncertainty quantification while overcoming the computational bottlenecks historically associated with high-dimensional functional inference.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The advent of next-generation observatories like Euclid and LSST will generate petascale datasets, necessitating rapid and highly scalable statistical frameworks. A major frontier in managing this data volume is Simulation-Based Inference (SBI). By replacing analytically tractable likelihoods with forward-modeled simulations, SBI enables complex inference while natively handling missing data and out-of-distribution [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952L..10W/abstract). While SBI can provide [[posterior-distributions|Posterior distributions]] with accuracies comparable to Markov Chain Monte Carlo at $10^4$ times the speed, a critical open question is how to ensure simulated training sets perfectly match the heterogeneous characteristics of observed data without introducing severe [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]].

In cosmological [[parameter-estimation|Parameter estimation]], a critical paradigm shift is underway from [[statistical-estimators-in-astronomy|summary statistics]]—such as the power spectrum—to field-level and Beyond-2pt Statistics. While the two-point correlation function adequately captures galaxy clustering in the linear regime, it discards vital non-linear information. Upcoming surveys require exploiting the full 3D density field through higher-order measures like bispectra, void statistics, and density-split clustering. Recent community data challenges confirm that extracting masked parameters via Beyond-2pt Statistics yields robust cosmological constraints, though optimizing the underlying effective field theories and ensuring unbiased inference remain active areas of research [Beyond-2pt Collaboration et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240502252B/abstract).

The pervasive use of machine learning in astrophysical pipelines has surfaced a new set of open questions regarding the integration of physical laws into ML architectures. In photometric redshift estimation, for example, it is recognized that hybrid approaches combining spectral energy distribution template fitting with machine learning are highly accurate. However, the literature remains conflicted on how to optimally select between ML and template-fitting for individual galaxies, particularly in sparsely populated regions of color-space where ML struggles [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...644A..31E/abstract). To mitigate the vulnerability of ML to covariate shifts, future directions involve embedding physics directly into neural architectures, such as utilizing graph neural networks to emulate baryonic property mappings onto dark matter halos four orders of magnitude faster than traditional semi-analytic models [Jespersen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941....7J/abstract).

A burgeoning frontier is Causal Discovery, which aims to move beyond simple correlations to identify directed causal structures in observational data where controlled experiments are impossible. Early applications using algorithms like Peter-Clark have suggested that central galaxy density and velocity dispersion causally drive supermassive black hole mass [Pasquato et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231115160P/abstract). However, whether causal discovery methods can reliably separate correlation from causation in the presence of unobserved confounding variables remains a fundamental open question. 

Finally, significant statistical tensions persist regarding fundamental cosmological properties. Recent systematic evaluations have found unresolved internal and external inconsistencies in 54 of the 66 tested redshift-independent distance indicators, complicating the construction of a robust distance ladder [Nájera et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.541..671N/abstract). Furthermore, a standing debate continues over the large-scale structure of the universe: while standard $\Lambda$CDM models assume a transition to homogeneity at scales $\sim 10$ Mpc/$h$ [Borgani et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995PhR...251....1B/abstract), alternative statistical analyses suggest that the galaxy distribution may exhibit fractal scaling properties on scales from 70 Mpc/$h$ [Lovyagin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AstBu..64..217L/abstract) up to $\sim 1000$ Mpc/$h$ [Pietronero et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ptc..conf..223P/abstract), with local structure potentially challenging large-scale spatial self-averaging limits [Sylos Labini et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...508...17S/abstract), maintaining fundamental questions over the core assumptions of large-scale homogeneity.
