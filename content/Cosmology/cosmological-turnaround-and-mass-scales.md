In the standard paradigm of [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]], the **cosmological turnaround radius** ($r_t$) represents the critical spatial boundary where the inward gravitational pull of a collapsed matter [[galaxy-overdensities|overdensity]] exactly balances the repulsive, outward [[expansion-rate|expansion]] of the background Hubble flow. Mathematically formalized as the Zero Radial Acceleration Surface (ZRAS), this scale defines the absolute maximum physical extent of [[gravitationally-bound-structures|Gravitationally bound structures]]. Inside the turnaround radius, matter has decoupled from the cosmic expansion and is actively infalling or undergoing virialization; outside of it, particle kinematics are overwhelmingly dominated by the expanding Universe [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Distinct from internal equilibrium metrics such as the traditional virial radius or the [[splashback-radius|Splashback radius]]—which marks the apocenter of recently accreted material—the turnaround scale identifies the definitive outer envelope of a dark matter halo before its density profile asymptotically merges with the unperturbed cosmic background.

The characterization of turnaround mass and spatial scales is fundamentally linked to the historical measurement of the cosmic [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] and the establishment of the $\Lambda$CDM standard cosmological model [Allen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ARA&A..49..409A/abstract). According to the idealized Spherical Collapse Model (SCM), the mean enclosed matter density at the turnaround boundary—the turnaround density ($\rho_{ta}$)—acts as a universal, mass-independent cosmological probe. Its redshift evolution uniquely encodes both the matter density ($\Omega_m$) and the dark energy density ($\Omega_\Lambda$) without relying on traditional distance ladders. Furthermore, the repulsive force exerted by the cosmological constant ($\Lambda$) fundamentally alters boundary dynamics; to successfully arrest the local cosmic expansion, structures such as the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]] and massive [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] require enclosed total masses that are 5-10% higher than purely Newtonian estimates would predict [Benisty et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhLB..85839033B/abstract). 

This article explores the theoretical foundations and observational realities of the cosmological turnaround scale. It traces the trajectory of density perturbations from the linear regime through non-linear collapse, detailing the analytical [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]] used to extrapolate well-measured cluster interiors out to the vast turnaround boundary, where enclosed masses typically reach $M_t \approx 1.7 M_v$. Because these extended outer envelopes correspond to a relatively low present-day density contrast ($\delta \sim 11$), identifying them requires sophisticated multi-wavelength methodologies. The article reviews how techniques such as weak lensing (via [[cosmic-shear|Cosmic shear]]), the kinematic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect, and the mapping of the [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]] are utilized to trace non-equilibrium infall kinematics and measure total gravitating masses at cluster outskirts.

Finally, the article addresses the pressing open questions and systematic uncertainties complicating the use of turnaround scales in precision cosmology. A persistent challenge is the observed offset between analytical SCM predictions and three-dimensional $N$-body simulations—a discrepancy driven primarily by halo asphericity and complex environmental geometries. Addressing these geometric deviations is crucial as researchers increasingly deploy local kinematic measurements at turnaround scales to test General Relativity against modified gravity models. Furthermore, while utilizing local turnaround dynamics offers a promising, independent pathway to alleviate the [[hubble-expansion-and-tension|Hubble Expansion and Tension]], realizing this potential requires meticulously disentangling true cosmic expansion from the extensive bulk flows that challenge our understanding of the universe's [[homogeneity-scale|Homogeneity scale]].

## Theoretical Foundations: The Turnaround Radius and Surface

The theoretical foundation of the turnaround scale rests on the competition between two opposing forces: the inward pull of gravitational instability and the outward push of the cosmic expansion. Initial density perturbations expand alongside the background universe until they reach a critical density; theoretical length and mass scales derived from these initial density and velocity fields act as fossil imprints that dictate the characteristic sizes of [[dark-matter-halo-scales|Dark Matter Halo Scales]] and larger structures seen today [Mouri et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PThPh.123..187M/abstract). At this point, the local gravitational attraction overcomes the Hubble flow, causing the region to halt its expansion, decouple from the background universe, and begin collapsing [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

The precise boundary where this transition occurs is known as the turnaround radius, $r_t$, which defines the zero radial acceleration surface (ZRAS) of a halo. At this surface, a test particle experiences no net radial acceleration. Inside $r_t$, matter is actively infalling or undergoing [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|virialization]]; outside $r_t$, matter kinematics are primarily governed by the Hubble flow [Cupani et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.390..645C/abstract), [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240411183K/abstract).

Quantitatively, the spherical collapse model (SCM) provides the classical framework for predicting these spatial and mass scales. However, note that the standard spherical collapse model faces conceptual challenges regarding energy conservation and the non-Doppler nature of the cosmological redshift in a geometric expansion framework [Baryshev et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AIPC..822...23B/abstract). Despite these considerations, in a $\Lambda$CDM universe, $r_t$ is typically found to be roughly $3$ to $3.5$ times the traditional [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|virial radius]] ($r_v$) [Cupani et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.390..645C/abstract), [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240218634K/abstract). Accordingly, the turnaround mass, $M_t$, which integrates all matter enclosed within $r_t$, is approximately $1.7$ times the virial mass ($M_v$) [Cupani et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.390..645C/abstract). At redshift $z=0$, the turnaround density contrast relative to the background universe is theoretically and numerically established to be $\delta \sim 11$ [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240218634K/abstract), [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A..71K/abstract).

The inclusion of dark energy, modeled as a cosmological constant ($\Lambda$), fundamentally alters the dynamics at the turnaround surface. Because $\Lambda$ exerts a repulsive outward force, a larger central mass is required to arrest the local cosmic expansion. Extending ZRAS definitions to include the cosmological constant increases the required total mass of local galaxy groups by $5\%-10\%$ compared to purely Newtonian estimates [Benisty et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240514944B/abstract), [Benisty et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhLB..85839033B/abstract).

The absolute density at turnaround, $\rho_{ta}$, has recently been identified as a uniquely powerful cosmological probe. The SCM predicts that the evolution of $\rho_{ta}(z)$ encodes both $\Omega_m$ and $\Omega_\Lambda$ independently of other standard distance measures [Pavlidou et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...638L...8P/abstract). However, translating this theoretical concept to observational reality presents challenges. N-body simulations reveal a slight offset between SCM predictions for $\rho_{ta}$ and the actual measured densities of simulated halos at $z=0$. This tension is primarily driven by halo asphericity and complex environmental effects; however, when researchers construct a strictly sphericity-selected sample of halos, the theoretical SCM predictions are recovered perfectly, independent of redshift and background cosmology [Korkidis et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..87K/abstract).

Finally, the physical nature of the turnaround surface must be distinguished from inner boundary metrics like the [[splashback-radius|Splashback radius]]. While the splashback radius denotes the apocenter of the first orbital crossing—characterized by a steep drop in the density profile due to phase-space caustics—the turnaround radius lies further out. Beyond $r_t$, the logarithmic slope of the density profile diverges from SCM predictions of a collapsed halo and instead asymptotically merges with the unperturbed Gaussian statistics of the surrounding Hubble flow [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240411183K/abstract). Establishing accurate mass scaling relations across these nested [[dark-matter-halo-scales|Dark Matter Halo Scales]] remains a critical focus for mapping the total [[mass-distribution-and-functions|mass distribution]] of the universe [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240218634K/abstract).

## Historical Development of Cosmic Mass Scales

The determination of cosmic mass scales is inextricably linked to the historical measurement of the cosmic [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] and the discovery of [[dark-matter-mass|dark matter]]. Early theoretical work explored the growth of structure from initial fluctuations through the post-recombination epoch, establishing the foundations for dissipationless collapse models [Gott et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980phco.proc..561G/abstract). Following the realization that galaxies reside in an expanding universe, the expansion rate was parameterized by the [[hubble-expansion-and-tension|Hubble constant]], $H_0 \equiv 100 h \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$ [Primack et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996dmu..conf....1P/abstract). The history of the Hubble Constant is characterized by decades of effort to calibrate the **distance ladder**—a sequence of overlapping distance indicators reaching from local stellar parallaxes to cosmologically distant Type Ia supernovae [Tully et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230511950T/abstract). To improve precision along this ladder, researchers have successfully incorporated the use of surface brightness fluctuations (SBF) as a distance indicator for early-type galaxies to help calibrate the Cepheid zero point [Blakeslee et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..283..280B/abstract). Early estimates of $H_0$ varied by nearly a factor of two, fundamentally limiting the precision of inferred mass, size, and [[expansion-age|Expansion age]] scales of the Universe [Huchra et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992Sci...256..321H/abstract).

Concurrently, dynamical measurements on galaxy and cluster scales revealed the **missing mass problem**. Observations of extended [[universal-rotation-curves|rotation curves]] in spiral galaxies left little doubt that visible matter could not account for the total enclosed mass [Gunn et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980RSPTA.296..313G/abstract). The inferred mass-to-light ratio systematically increases as a function of scale: from $M/L \sim 30 h (M/L)_\odot$ at radii of $\sim 30 h^{-1} \text{ kpc}$ in galaxies, to $M/L \sim 100 h (M/L)_\odot$ in [[galaxy-supergroups|galaxy groups]], and reaching $M/L \sim 350 h (M/L)_\odot$ at $\sim 1 \text{ Mpc}$ in the largest virialized [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). This scale-dependent discrepancy solidified the paradigm that galaxies and clusters are embedded in massive, extended [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|dark matter halos]]. Indeed, observational studies have established a distinct linear log-log correlation between the dark and visible mass distributions across a vast range of scales, extending from individual spiral galaxies up to massive clusters [Mishchenko et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IJMPA..20.3124M/abstract).

Throughout the 1980s and 1990s, cosmologists debated whether the Universe was matter-dominated ($\Omega_m = 1$) or possessed a lower matter density ($\Omega_m \approx 0.3$) [Primack et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996dmu..conf....1P/abstract). The turning point occurred in the late 1990s when distance-redshift relations of Type Ia supernovae revealed [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]], and measurements of cosmic microwave background (CMB) anisotropies confirmed a flat spatial geometry. The late 1990s saw a major synthesis of themes regarding dark matter nature and the physics of galaxy formation, highlighting the interlocking roles of observation and simulation [Weinberg et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..117..578W/abstract). These discoveries necessitated the inclusion of dark energy, firmly establishing the $\Lambda$CDM framework as the standard cosmological model [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract), [Allen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ARA&A..49..409A/abstract). Within this concordance model, the universal energy budget is dominated by dark energy ($\sim 70-75\%$) and dark matter ($\sim 25-30\%$), providing a robust foundation for modeling structure formation.

Despite the broad successes of the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm on large scales, significant historical and modern tensions persist. The ongoing calibration of the local distance ladder consistently yields $H_0 \approx 73-75 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$, conflicting at the $\sim 5\sigma$ level with early-universe CMB predictions of $H_0 \approx 67.4 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$ [Tully et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230511950T/abstract). This suggests the standard model may still be incomplete, a problem heavily discussed in the context of the [[hubble-expansion-and-tension|Hubble Expansion and Tension]]. 

Furthermore, the fundamental nature of the missing mass remains contested at sub-galactic scales. While cold dark matter successfully reproduces large-scale clustering [Kravtsov et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..7463K/abstract), persistent small-scale anomalies—such as the cusp-core transition in dwarf galaxies [Hayashi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722155H/abstract) and unexpectedly flat [[universal-rotation-curves|rotation curves]] extending to $\sim 1 \text{ Mpc}$ around isolated galaxies [Hoeneisen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241217869H/abstract)—highlight ambiguities in [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|Dark Matter Halo Properties and Statistics]]. Additionally, rotation curves of Low Surface Brightness (LSB) galaxies indicate they inhabit considerably less dense and more extended dark matter halos than their high surface brightness counterparts [de Blok et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..117...39D/abstract). These tensions have led some researchers to argue that baryonic feedback is insufficient, and that modifications to general relativity (e.g., MOND) might still be required to fully explain observed kinematics [Sellwood et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..240..311S/abstract), [Ferreira et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Sci...326..812F/abstract).

## Non-linear Growth and the Path to Virialization

The transition of cosmic matter from the linear regime to [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]] occurs when initial density perturbations amplify to the point that their local density contrast $\delta \gg 1$. Analytically, structure growth can be modeled using pair-conservation equations, which provide a robust method for computing the non-linear mass correlation function seamlessly from the linear to the strongly non-linear regime [Caldwell et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...547L..93C/abstract). In the standard spherical collapse model, as a bounded overdense region expands along with the background Universe, its self-gravity continuously decelerates its local [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]]. Eventually, this expansion halts entirely at a critical boundary known as the turnaround point.

The spatial boundary at which gravitational attraction exactly offsets the background Hubble flow is the turnaround radius ($r_{ta}$), or more generally for asymmetric systems, the Zero Radial Acceleration Surface (ZRAS) [Benisty et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240514944B/abstract), [Benisty et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhLB..85839033B/abstract). Analytically and in cosmological N-body simulations, $r_{ta}$ provides a physically motivated boundary for bound structures, distinct from inner equilibrium scales. Following turnaround, the perturbation separates from the cosmic expansion and undergoes gravitational collapse. Gigaparsec-scale hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate that properly modeling this non-linear growth is critical, as small-scale baryonic effects can significantly bias galaxy clustering measurements all the way up to the baryonic acoustic oscillation scale [Lee et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908...11L/abstract). Within the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], structure formation proceeds bottom-up: smaller dark matter halos collapse early and subsequently merge to build larger, more massive systems. 

As matter falls inward past the turnaround radius, it crosses a physical caustic known as the [[splashback-radius|Splashback radius]]—the apocenter of the first orbital crossing—where the density profile sharply diverges from the idealized spherical collapse model [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240411183K/abstract). Inside this boundary, violent relaxation and phase mixing drive the system toward final virialization. The resulting structural profiles of these innermost regions can be complex; spectroscopic studies targeting [[central-galaxies|brightest cluster galaxies]] alongside gravitational arcs are highly effective at constraining inner dark matter slopes, yielding profiles that often contrast with pure N-body simulation predictions [Sand et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.10703S/abstract). However, in the extended outer regions between the virial boundary and $r_{ta}$, orbital crossing times remain comparable to the Hubble time. Consequently, these outer envelopes consist of infalling, non-equilibrium material rather than a fully relaxed fluid.

The turnaround scale is of particular interest as a cosmological probe because the turnaround density ($\rho_{ta}$) serves as a robust, mass-independent indicator of the background cosmology. Theoretical models indicate that the redshift evolution of $\rho_{ta}$ uniquely encodes the matter density $\Omega_m$ and dark energy density $\Omega_\Lambda$, offering a local method to constrain $\Lambda$ independently of other standard probes [Pavlidou et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...638L...8P/abstract), [Korkidis et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..87K/abstract). However, a measured offset exists between the $\rho_{ta}$ predicted by the idealized spherical collapse model and values extracted from simulations. This discrepancy is primarily attributed to halo asphericity and complex environmental effects. Recent work has shown that statistically selecting highly spherical halos can effectively eliminate this offset, allowing simulated clusters to perfectly align with analytical predictions and reliably recover the underlying [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]] [Korkidis et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..87K/abstract).

## Turnaround Density as a Cosmological Probe

The turnaround radius ($r_t$) represents the critical boundary where the attractive gravitational force of [[gravitationally-bound-structures|Gravitationally bound structures]] exactly offsets the outward cosmic expansion. Within this radius, the mean enclosed matter density, known as the turnaround density ($\rho_{ta}$), serves as a robust, mass-independent cosmological probe [Pavlidou et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...638L...8P/abstract). According to the spherical collapse model (SCM), turnaround density evolution over [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]] uniquely encodes the background cosmology, offering a method to constrain cosmological parameters like the matter density $\Omega_m$ and the dark energy density $\Omega_\Lambda$ independently of other standard probes [Pavlidou et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...638L...8P/abstract), [Korkidis et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..87K/abstract). Complementing this local density probe, the zero-crossing of the [[redshift-space-correlation-function|two-point galaxy correlation function]] serves as a robust "standard ruler" closely linked to the horizon size at matter-radiation equality [Prada et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1111.2889P/abstract). Similarly, measuring the baryonic oscillation 'wavelength' directly in Fourier space offers another complementary standard ruler capable of probing and tightening constraints on the dark energy equation of state [Blake et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...594..665B/abstract). Together, these metrics bridge local non-linear boundaries with primordial cosmic scales.

Quantitative measurements from $N$-body cosmological simulations demonstrate that the turnaround scale encompasses a significantly larger volume than the virialized core, capturing immense quantities of infalling material prior to virialization. To visualize the interplay between attractive gravity and repulsive dark energy across these boundaries, the $\Lambda$ significance graph is frequently employed. This diagnostic plots the ratio of the mean enclosed matter density to the dark energy density, $\langle \rho_M \rangle / \rho_\Lambda$, against the radius $R$. The Zero Radial Acceleration Surface (ZRAS) corresponds to the critical balance point where $\langle \rho_M \rangle / \rho_\Lambda = 2$, demarcating gravity-dominated cluster interiors from dark energy-dominated exteriors [Teerikorpi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...577A.144T/abstract). Accounting for the cosmological constant's repulsive effect at these extended scales requires an upward revision of total mass estimates when dynamical analysis of local [[galaxy-supergroups|Galaxy supergroups]] and [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] is performed [Benisty et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhLB..85839033B/abstract), [Benisty et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240514944B/abstract).

Despite the theoretical elegance of $\rho_{ta}$, translating analytical SCM predictions to complex observational datasets and simulations presents challenges. The literature notes a systematic offset between the theoretically predicted $\rho_{ta}$ and the values measured in three-dimensional $N$-body simulations of [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]]. This discrepancy is largely driven by halo asphericity and complex environmental effects beyond the turnaround radius. However, recent work analyzing [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|Dark Matter Halo Properties and Statistics]] demonstrates that this offset is not an intractable failure of the model. By constructing a sphericity-selected sample of dark matter halos, the analytical SCM predictions are cleanly recovered, yielding a zero-offset $\rho_{ta}$ that accurately reflects the underlying cosmology across all redshifts [Korkidis et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..87K/abstract).

## Mass Scaling Relations at the Cluster Outskirts

The turnaround radius ($r_t$) defines the critical boundary separating gravitationally bound, non-expanding structures from the expanding Hubble flow. Measuring the [[enclosed-mass|Enclosed mass]] at these vast cluster outskirts is observationally challenging. Consequently, establishing mass scaling relations that connect observable masses in the dense interiors of [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] to the total gravitating mass enclosed at the turnaround boundary is essential for precision cosmology.

To bridge this radial gap, researchers have developed an analytic universal outer density profile utilizing excursion set theory. This approach provides a statistical framework for determining the density and local environment of dark matter halos. When coupled with the spherical collapse model (SCM), excursion set theory allows for the analytical tracking of non-linear structure growth extending into the [[quasi-linear-regime|Quasi-linear regime]] and out to the unperturbed cosmic background [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240218634K/abstract), [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A..71K/abstract). These analytical models predict that beyond the turnaround radius, where the Hubble flow dominates, the logarithmic slope of the median cluster density profile becomes universal. However, within the turnaround scale, the actual [[density-profiles|Density profiles]] diverge from standard SCM predictions due to shell-crossing and the formation of orbit caustics, a dynamical signature intimately connected to the [[splashback-radius|Splashback radius]] [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240411183K/abstract).

Applying this universal outer density profile yields theoretical mass scaling relations that can extrapolate interior mass estimates to large radii. Dynamical studies utilizing cosmological simulations reveal that the velocity anisotropy profile ($\beta(r)$) of infalling material reaches a prominent peak near $1.7 R_{200}$. By rigorously analyzing these kinematic boundaries, researchers have developed robust power-law scaling relations that tightly connect hydrostatic, infall, and turnaround radii across scales [Abdullah et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250404575A/abstract). The resulting excursion set-derived analytical mass profiles match the outer mass distribution of simulated clusters to better than $30\%$, with exact precision depending on the specific halo mass. From these profiles, the extracted mass scaling relations exhibit an offset of less than $15\%$ compared to cosmological N-body simulations, demonstrating their efficacy in probing structure formation dynamics at cluster outskirts without relying on expensive computational models [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240218634K/abstract), [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A..71K/abstract).

Finally, evaluating [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]] at the extreme outskirts of galaxy groups and clusters requires explicitly accounting for the repulsive force of dark energy. The cosmological constant ($\Lambda$) alters the zero radial acceleration surface, increasing the local expansion rate against Newtonian attraction. To maintain a bound state at the observed turnaround surface, the estimated masses of local galaxy groups and clusters must be systematically higher than predictions that neglect $\Lambda$ [Benisty et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhLB..85839033B/abstract), [Benisty et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240514944B/abstract).

## Dynamics of the Local Universe and Cosmic Flows

The dynamics of the local universe are governed by the continuous interplay between the isotropic expansion of the cosmic background and the gravitational pull of local overdensities. The realization of departures from an isotropic Hubble flow dates back to early gravitational perturbation models of the redshift field in the Local Supercluster [Davis et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ARA&A..21..109D/abstract). While the global expansion follows the Hubble law, the gravitational attraction of massive structures induces deviations known as the [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]]. Mapping these kinematics allows astrophysicists to reconstruct the underlying matter distribution and identify the boundaries of gravitationally bound environments.

Within the [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|Local Volume and Nearby Galaxy Studies]] (typically defined as $D < 10 \text{ Mpc}$), the local kinematics are heavily influenced by the balance between the local Hubble flow and the mutual gravitational attraction of general [[galaxy-supergroups|local galaxy groups]]. Interestingly, observational data confirms that the linear Hubble flow preserves its kinematical identity even at distances as close as a few megaparsecs, which poses a unique physical puzzle for dynamical models including dark energy [Karachentsev et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Afz....46..399K/abstract). The boundary separating this locally bound region from the expanding background is highly sensitive to the cosmological constant ($\Lambda$). By incorporating the repulsive force of dark energy into the zero radial acceleration surface (ZRAS), the estimated total masses of local galaxy groups must be revised upward. Specifically, accounting for $\Lambda$ increases local group masses by 5-10%, yielding a precise kinematic turnaround mass for the Local Group of $M \approx (2.47 \pm 0.08) \times 10^{12} M_\odot$ [Benisty et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhLB..85839033B/abstract).

On larger scales, peculiar velocities trace extensive "basins of attraction" (BoA)—domains of gravitational potential minima toward which matter flows. The cosmography of these regions is mapped using comprehensive peculiar velocity catalogs, such as the Cosmicflows datasets. The literature is currently ambiguous regarding the exact hierarchical classification of our local BoA. Earlier reconstructions suggested that the Milky Way and its neighbors reside within a distinct structure named the [[laniakea-supercluster|Laniakea Supercluster]]. However, recent probabilistic analyses of the Cosmicflows-4 (CF4) compilation suggest that Laniakea may not be an independent BoA, but rather a sub-region of the much larger Shapley basin. For context, the largest securely identified BoA in the local universe is associated with the Sloan Great Wall, which encompasses a volume of over $15.5 \times 10^6 \, (h^{-1} \text{ Mpc})^3$ [Valade et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1610V/abstract).

The magnitude of these local cosmic flows presents a persistent tension regarding the [[homogeneity-scale|Homogeneity scale]] of the Universe. While surveys like SDSS confirm statistical homogeneity is generally reached at scales of $\sim 100 \, h^{-1} \text{ Mpc}$, and there is specific observational evidence indicating a preferred scale of $\sim 100 \text{ Mpc}$ in the spatial distribution of high-density structural regions [Einasto et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.11334E/abstract), long-range correlations result in a persistent lack of self-averaging even at the largest probed scales [Sylos Labini et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010JCAP...06..021S/abstract). ## Testing Gravity and Dark Energy with the Turnaround Scale

The turnaround radius ($r_t$) defines the outermost boundary of a gravitationally bound structure, where the local gravitational attraction exactly offsets the background cosmic expansion. As a Zero Radial Acceleration Surface (ZRAS), this boundary is profoundly sensitive to the global expansion history and provides a pristine laboratory for investigating [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]]. In the standard $\Lambda$CDM model, the repulsive force exerted by the Cosmological Constant ($\Lambda$) shifts the turnaround surface inward compared to a purely matter-dominated universe. Accounting for this background repulsion is essential when dynamically determining the total masses of [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] and [[galaxy-supergroups|Galaxy supergroups]] [Benisty et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhLB..85839033B/abstract). Furthermore, at galactic scales, analyzing the [[universal-rotation-curves|rotation curves]] of isolated galaxies fitted with standard NFW profiles can directly place an upper bound on the repulsive density of the cosmological constant, estimating it to be $\approx 10^{-25} \text{ kg/m}^3$ [Benisty et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240516650B/abstract).

According to the spherical collapse model, the average matter density enclosed within the turnaround radius, $\rho_{ta}$, is a universal quantity for all halos at a given redshift. Tracking the redshift evolution of $\rho_{ta}$ enables a direct, local measurement of the dark energy density $\Omega_{\Lambda, 0}$ that is entirely independent of the matter density $\Omega_{m, 0}$ and other standard cosmological probes [Pavlidou et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...638L...8P/abstract). Complementing this, direct determinations of the Hubble parameter $H(z)$ over varying redshifts independently constrain models of the dark energy equation of state without relying on integral measures like luminosity distance [Samushia et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...650L...5S/abstract). Additionally, the gravitational potential decay rate—isolated via cross-correlations between gravitational lensing and large-scale structure (LSS)—provides a novel, independent dark energy constraint by directly probing the integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect at cosmological scales [Dong et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938...72D/abstract). Recent studies have successfully extended the measurement of this gravitational potential decay rate to redshifts $z < 1.4$ by incorporating data from the DESI imaging surveys and Planck cosmic microwave background maps [Dong et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982...99D/abstract). Further constraints can be drawn from the high-redshift universe; specifically, utilizing high-redshift X-ray observations of cluster gas fractions allows researchers to measure the perturbation growth factor across $z=0-2$, substantially improving dark energy parameter constraints [Vikhlinin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S.304V/abstract). 

While cosmological $N$-body simulations reveal a slight offset between theoretical predictions of $\rho_{ta}$ and numerical results due to halo asphericity, restricting analyses to highly spherical systems successfully recovers the background cosmology [Korkidis et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..87K/abstract). The analysis of curvature invariants, such as the Kretschmann scalar in the McVittie spacetime, indicates that dark energy definitively dominates the kinematics at these extended structural boundaries [Benisty et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250403009B/abstract).

Beyond the standard model, the turnaround scale and the surrounding [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]] provide stringent tests for modified gravity theories. For instance, the presence of a consistent fundamental acceleration scale across diverse objects like galaxies and clusters suggests new physics that must be accounted for in structure formation models [Edmonds et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IJMPD..2943030E/abstract). Alternatives to General Relativity typically introduce scale-dependent fifth forces that alter the growth of structure and the kinematics of the [[quasi-linear-regime|Quasi-linear regime]]. Modified gravity models are often classified by their screening mechanisms (e.g., chameleon or $f(R)$ gravity) which theoretically hide these extra forces in high-density regions while distinctly modifying perturbations in metric potentials across spatial scales ranging from kiloparsecs to gigaparsecs [Jain et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AnPhy.325.1479J/abstract). A comprehensive framework also exists for testing Horndeski class theories and evaluating scalar degrees of freedom by utilizing the anisotropy of galaxy clustering at large scales [Bose et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180200989B/abstract). Combining weak lensing and galaxy clustering measurements allows for robust constraints on modified gravity, though accurate modeling of structure formation in the non-linear regime remains a prerequisite [Heymans et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IJMPD..2748005H/abstract). The mean pairwise streaming velocity of clusters—measurable via the kinematic Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect—can discriminate between General Relativity and these modified gravity models by directly comparing the observed expansion history against structural growth rates [Kosowsky et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvD..80f2003K/abstract). Furthermore, testing these modified gravity theories is increasingly supported by modern computational techniques. The COLA method offers a computationally efficient way to produce mock galaxy catalogs in modified gravity, facilitating the validation of probes like the galaxy bispectrum and void-galaxy correlations [Fiorini et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230307121F/abstract). Additionally, machine learning emulators like Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) efficiently simulate and analyze both dark matter and hydrodynamical structural data [Tamosiunas et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201108786T/abstract).

Furthermore, the turnaround and interior scales of clusters offer unique arenas for testing the Weak Equivalence Principle in the dark sector. By measuring the subtle gravitational redshift of light escaping the deep potential wells of [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] and comparing this signal to the internal velocity dispersion, researchers can test whether dark matter falls universally in gravitational fields. Any additional interaction that alters the trajectory of dark matter would signal a violation of the Equivalence Principle. Current measurements of cluster kinematics constrain the presence of any equivalence-violating fifth force in the dark sector to a precision of $7-14\%$, demonstrating the critical role of cluster and turnaround mass scales in probing fundamental physics [Di Dio et al. ## Observational Methodologies and Systematic Uncertainties

Identifying the cosmological turnaround scale—the definitive boundary where local gravitational attraction exactly balances the cosmic expansion—presents a formidable observational challenge. Because the present-day turnaround density contrast is quite low and occurs at extended distances from cluster cores, current mass measurement techniques require sophisticated multi-wavelength methodologies to accurately characterize this regime [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240218634K/abstract). Because systematic uncertainties in cluster mass estimation directly propagate into biases in cosmological parameter analysis, large-scale numerical simulations are essential for calibrating these scales and minimizing systematic errors [Pratt et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019SSRv..215...25P/abstract). However, the accuracy of these calibrations can be fundamentally limited by computational constraints, as the finite mass resolution and physical interparticle separation in cosmological $N$-body simulations introduce collision errors and suppress accuracy at sub-galactic scales [Splinter et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997astro.ph.11105S/abstract).

**Weak Lensing and the Splashback Feature**
Weak gravitational lensing provides a direct probe of the total mass distribution, circumventing the need to assume dynamical equilibrium at cluster outskirts. Lensing shear and convergence fields allow researchers to trace the density profile out to the [[splashback-radius|Splashback radius]], a physical boundary closely associated with the turnaround scale that demarcates the apocenter of the most recently accreted matter. Advanced probabilistic algorithms for mass mapping from weak lensing shear can now infer the distribution of merging galaxy clusters without assuming Gaussian-distributed shape noise [Schneider et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839...25S/abstract). Furthermore, magnification bias—the lensing-induced change in background source counts—provides an alternative probe of mass density profiles that avoids systematics associated with galaxy shapes [González-Nuevo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251213895G/abstract). Measurements of the splashback radius via [[cosmic-shear|Cosmic shear]] are particularly valuable because they can break the traditional degeneracy between the matter density $\Omega_M$ and the clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$, which are otherwise conflated in the $S_8$ parameter [Haggar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...972...28H/abstract). 

However, systematic uncertainties complicate these measurements. The literature reflects a persistent observational tension: studies frequently find smaller splashback radii when derived from galaxy number density profiles rather than underlying dark matter distributions. While dynamical friction is known to decay subhalo orbits and effectively reduce the apparent splashback radius, recent idealized simulations show that this effect cannot fully account for the observed discrepancies in massive [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] ($M_{200} > 10^{14} \mathrm{M_\odot}$) [O'Shea et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240518468O/abstract).

**Sunyaev-Zel'dovich Effect**
The Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect, particularly the kinetic SZ (kSZ) distortion of the cosmic microwave background, offers an independent method for tracing the velocity fields of hot intracluster gas [Kitayama et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PTEP.2014fB111K/abstract). By measuring the mean pairwise streaming velocities of galaxy clusters, the kSZ effect can probe the bulk infall motions near the turnaround radius. This velocity data provides a powerful tool for discriminating between General Relativity and modified gravity models by comparing the observed expansion history against structure growth rates [Kosowsky et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvD..80f2003K/abstract). Separately, precise spectroscopic measurements of gravitational redshift are required to test the Weak Equivalence Principle in the dark sector [Di Dio et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260119861D/abstract).

**Redshift Surveys and Kinematics**
Large-scale galaxy redshift surveys identify the turnaround radius $r_t$ by tracking the kinematics of the infall region, defined as the scale where the local mean velocity of infalling galaxies is perfectly balanced by the Hubble flow [Cupani et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.390..645C/abstract). 

These kinematic measurements must account for several systematic errors. Non-linear peculiar velocities, such as the [[fingers-of-god|Fingers of God]] effect, and geometric distortions related to the [[alcock-paczynski-test|Alcock-Paczynski test]] can obscure the true infall signature [Raccanelli et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015aska.confE..31R/abstract), [Bueno Belloso et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvD..86b3530B/abstract). Despite the complexities introduced by these non-linear effects, utilizing higher wavenumbers in the analysis of the matter power spectrum can help to cleanly break parameter degeneracies between dark energy and total neutrino mass [Bielefeld et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...05..023B/abstract). Furthermore, the theoretical framework relies heavily on the Spherical Collapse Model (SCM). There is a documented offset in the turnaround density $\rho_{ta}$ between SCM predictions and $N$-body simulations, which has been shown to strongly correlate with halo asphericity and broader environmental deviations from spherical symmetry [Korkidis et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..87K/abstract). Beyond simple kinematics, it has recently been shown that actively modeling intrinsic galaxy alignments (IA) in full-shape galaxy clustering analyses acts to squeeze and enhance resulting dynamical dark energy constraints, further refining our understanding of these boundary scales [Shim et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241208150S/abstract).

**Mass Scaling Relations**
Because the direct observational assessment of mass at the turnaround radius remains highly uncertain, modern methodologies rely heavily on [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]]. Analytical frameworks utilizing excursion set theory have been developed to extrapolate well-measured interior cluster masses out to the extended turnaround regime. These models achieve agreements with cosmological simulations to within $15\%$ [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240218634K/abstract). Such robust scaling relations are essential for utilizing the turnaround scale as a mass-independent cosmological probe to constrain dark energy.

## Open Questions: Non-Spherical Geometry and the Hubble Tension

The derivation of cosmological parameters from local turnaround structures offers a promising, independent diagnostic for the standard $\Lambda$CDM model. However, realizing this potential requires overcoming significant theoretical and observational challenges, primarily concerning the breakdown of spherical symmetry and the interpretation of local kinematics.

**Halo Asphericity and Sphericity-Selected Samples**
The standard Spherical Collapse Model (SCM) predicts a universal density contrast at the turnaround radius, $\rho_{ta}$, that strongly depends on the cosmological parameters $\Omega_m$ and $\Omega_\Lambda$. However, $N$-body simulations reveal a measurable, redshift-dependent offset between the SCM predictions and the actual turnaround densities of massive halos [Korkidis et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..87K/abstract). This discrepancy originates from the assumption of perfect spherical symmetry, which fails to capture the complex, triaxial environments characteristic of [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]]. The density offset strongly correlates with the degree of environmental asphericity around the halo. By employing metrics to quantify deviations from symmetry, researchers can construct sphericity-selected samples of halos where the SCM offset vanishes. Within these highly spherical sub-samples, the simulated background cosmology can be robustly recovered independently of redshift [Korkidis et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..87K/abstract).

**Local Kinematics and the Hubble Tension**
The $\sim 5\sigma$ discrepancy between local distance ladder measurements ($H_0 \approx 73-75 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$) and early-universe cosmic microwave background predictions ($H_0 \approx 67.4 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$)—known as the [[hubble-expansion-and-tension|Hubble Expansion and Tension]]—has motivated the use of local structure dynamics as an independent probe [Tully et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230511950T/abstract). On [[sub-megaparsec-scales|Sub-megaparsec scales]], the boundary of a bound structure is defined by the zero radial acceleration surface, where local Newtonian attraction is exactly offset by the repulsive force of the cosmological constant. Incorporating $\Lambda$ into local group dynamics is necessary for accurate boundary definitions [Benisty et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhLB..85839033B/abstract).

Despite this theoretical framework, the literature remains ambiguous regarding the precision and viability of local kinematic methods to resolve the Hubble tension. While the local velocity-radius relation around groups and clusters contains measurable imprints of dark energy, recovering $H_0$ from these local flows via extended Lemaître-Tolman models yields a substantial scatter, with an average uncertainty of $\sim 25\%$ [Benisty et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251011382B/abstract). Furthermore, these kinematic fits systematically underestimate true halo masses by approximately $5\%$ [Benisty et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251011382B/abstract). Accurate cosmological constraints require careful isolation of dynamical tracers; for instance, filtering for low-mass, [[relatively-isolated-galaxies|isolated galaxy pairs]] is required to trace the average background expansion with minimal contamination from the local [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]] [Bueno Belloso et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvD..86b3530B/abstract).

These difficulties are compounded by ongoing debates over large-scale dynamical homogeneity. Recent compilations of peculiar velocities demonstrate that bulk flows remain unexpectedly high ($315 \pm 40 \text{ km s}^{-1}$) at distances up to $150 \, h^{-1} \text{ Mpc}$, suggesting that the universe's dynamical scale of homogeneity is not reached until at least $200-300 \, h^{-1} \text{ Mpc}$ [Courtois et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.187C/abstract). These bulk motions can significantly distort the local expansion field, complicating efforts to use local turnaround scales to resolve broader cosmological tensions or accurately map the pure Hubble flow [Courtois et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.187C/abstract), all within our understanding of the broader [[homogeneity-scale|Homogeneity scale]].
