The **Friedmann equations** are a set of fundamental differential equations in [[general-relativity|General Relativity]] that govern the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] of a homogeneous and isotropic universe. Derived independently by Alexander Friedmann in 1922 and Georges Lemaître in 1927, these equations arise from applying Einstein's field equations to the highly symmetric Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric. They represent the mathematical backbone of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]], dictating how the spatial extent of the universe—parameterized by the cosmic scale factor, $a(t)$—evolves over time in response to its constituent [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]], thermodynamic pressure, and intrinsic [[spatial-curvature|Spatial curvature]] [Peacock et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999coph.book.....P/abstract), [Dodelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020moco.book.....D/abstract). By reducing the complex, non-linear dynamics of general relativistic [[spacetime-geometry|Spacetime geometry]] to a set of manageable ordinary differential equations, the Friedmann framework established the theoretical foundation for the expanding universe.

The core theoretical framework comprises two main equations. The first Friedmann equation relates the square of the Hubble expansion rate to the total energy density of the cosmic fluid and the geometric curvature of the universe. The second, often called the acceleration equation, determines the rate of change of the cosmic expansion and demonstrates that in relativity, unlike in [[newtonian-dynamics|Newtonian dynamics]], both energy density and pressure act as active sources of [[gravity|Gravity]]. Together, they trace the universe's evolution through distinct epochs dominated by different energetic constituents—ranging from a primordial [[radiation-dominated-universe|Radiation-dominated universe]] to eras governed by non-relativistic matter and, ultimately, vacuum energy. Within the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm, the relative contributions of these components are quantified by dimensionless [[omega-parameters|Omega parameters]] that accurately reproduce observations of the cosmic microwave background and local distance-redshift relations [Wijenayake et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..94h3501W/abstract).

While the Friedmann equations perfectly describe an idealized, smooth cosmos, this article explores how they are extended and challenged by the universe's true structural complexity. On sub-horizon scales, [[cosmological-perturbation-theory|Cosmological Perturbation Theory]] and [[linear-theory|Linear theory]] are required to model how small density fluctuations grow via gravitational instability to form the cosmic web. The presence of these localized structures raises the unresolved question of [[cosmological-backreaction|Cosmological backreaction]], which investigates whether [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]] significantly alters the global expansion dynamics. Furthermore, to address phenomenological mysteries like dark matter and late-time cosmic acceleration, the article examines alternative [[general-relativity-frameworks|General Relativity Frameworks]]—such as $f(R)$ models, [[massive-gravity|Massive gravity]], and relativistic modifications to gravity like TeVeS [Skordis et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvL..96a1301S/abstract). Finally, the text reviews current observational constraints and outstanding open questions, including the $H_0$ Hubble tension, the coincidence problem of dark energy, and the search for the underlying [[initial-conditions-in-astrophysics|Initial Conditions in Astrophysics]] that must ultimately be described by a theory of quantum gravity.

## Historical Development and Theoretical Origins

The theoretical foundations of the Friedmann equations are deeply rooted in the initial attempts to apply Albert Einstein's newly formulated theory of [[general-relativity|General Relativity]] to the universe as a whole. Following his completion of the theory in 1916, Einstein sought to construct a self-consistent mathematical model of the cosmos. Guided by the prevailing philosophical bias of the era, he expected the universe to be eternal and unchanging. However, he found that the standard Einstein field equations inherently predicted a dynamic [[spacetime-geometry|Spacetime geometry]]—one that must either be expanding or contracting under the influence of [[gravity|Gravity]]. 

To enforce a static universe, Einstein introduced a modification to his field equations, adding a cosmological constant, $\Lambda$. The modified field equations take the form:
$$R_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2}R g_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4} T_{\mu\nu}$$
where $R_{\mu\nu}$ is the Ricci tensor, $R$ is the [[ricci-scalar|Ricci scalar]], $g_{\mu\nu}$ is the metric tensor, and $T_{\mu\nu}$ is the energy-momentum tensor. The $\Lambda$ term acts as a repulsive force capable of counterbalancing gravitational attraction, thereby permitting a static—albeit highly unstable—solution.

The cosmological paradigm shifted significantly in 1922 when the Russian mathematician Alexander Friedmann published a broader class of solutions. By dispensing with the requirement of a static universe, Friedmann demonstrated that Einstein's equations naturally admitted expanding solutions. These dynamic models were independently rediscovered in 1927 by the Belgian physicist Georges Lemaître. Both physicists grounded their derivations in the Cosmological principle, which postulates that the distribution of matter and energy in the universe is both homogeneous and isotropic on large scales.

Applying the Cosmological principle to the general relativistic framework severely restricts the allowable geometry of the universe, reducing it to the highly symmetric Robertson-Walker metric. The dynamics of this metric are entirely determined by a time-dependent scale factor, $a(t)$, and a constant [[spatial-curvature|Spatial curvature]] signature, $K$. The Friedmann equations govern the temporal evolution of $a(t)$ by relating it to the total [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]] $\rho$ and pressure $P$ of the universe's constituents. Together, this framework forms the backbone of standard [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]].

Following Edwin Hubble's observational discovery of cosmic expansion, Einstein famously discarded the cosmological constant. Nevertheless, the theoretical framework established by Friedmann and Lemaître remained intact and highly adaptable. It was later realized that the cosmological constant could be formally moved to the right-hand side of the field equations and interpreted as an energy component with an energy-momentum tensor $T_{\mu\nu} = \frac{c^4 \Lambda}{8\pi G} g_{\mu\nu}$. This corresponds to a vacuum energy with mass density $\rho_\Lambda = \frac{\Lambda c^2}{8\pi G}$ and a strictly negative pressure $P_\Lambda = -\rho_\Lambda c^2$. Today, the standard model utilizing the expanding solutions first derived by Friedmann and Lemaître partitions the cosmic energy budget into non-relativistic matter, a radiation component, and dark energy to describe the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] of the universe. However, this $\Lambda$CDM partitioning contains what some researchers describe as unpalatable coincidences and potential inconsistencies, leading to arguments that the standard theoretical framework remains incomplete [Melia et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.419.2579M/abstract).

## The FLRW Metric and Geometric Foundations

The Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric forms the foundational geometric backbone of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]]. Derived from the application of [[general-relativity|General Relativity]] to a universe governed by the cosmological principle, it provides a self-consistent framework for describing a spacetime characterized by large-scale homogeneity and isotropy [Dodelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020moco.book.....D/abstract), [Peacock et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999coph.book.....P/abstract). Originally explored by Albert Einstein and subsequently formalized by Alexander Friedmann in 1922 and Georges Lemaître in 1927, the FLRW exact solutions demonstrate that a uniform universe must be either expanding or contracting.

The geometric properties of this highly symmetric spacetime are captured by the Robertson-Walker metric. Using spherical comoving coordinates, the invariant line element perfectly partitions the [[spacetime-geometry|Spacetime geometry]] into two distinct components: a time-dependent cosmic scale factor, $a(t)$, and a time-independent spatial geometry governed by the curvature signature, $K$ [Peacock et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999coph.book.....P/abstract). 

The scale factor $a(t)$ parameterizes the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] of the universe, tracking how proper physical distances between fundamental observers—such as galaxies sitting at rest within the [[comoving-frame|Comoving frame]]—scale dynamically with cosmic time. By inserting the FLRW metric into the Einstein field equations alongside an appropriate energy-momentum tensor for a perfect fluid, one isolates the Friedmann equations, which explicitly link the temporal evolution of $a(t)$ to the background [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]] (matter, radiation, and dark energy) [Peacock et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999coph.book.....P/abstract).

The curvature signature $K$ dictates the global [[spatial-curvature|Spatial curvature]] of the universe on constant-time hypersurfaces. By properly scaling the radial coordinate, $K$ is typically normalized to take one of three discrete values: closed, positively curved (spherical) geometry; flat, Euclidean geometry; and open, negatively curved (hyperbolic) geometry [Peacock et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999coph.book.....P/abstract). 

While the standard FLRW background is an enormously successful baseline for $\Lambda$CDM, it remains an idealized approximation that smoothes over the universe's ubiquitous small-scale structures. Because the field equations of relativity are highly non-linear, the average of the metric over an inhomogeneous matter distribution is not strictly guaranteed to match the metric of the average density [Ostrowski et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AcPPS..10..407O/abstract). This introduces the problem of [[cosmological-backreaction|Cosmological backreaction]]—explored in detail in later sections—which investigates whether local nonlinear inhomogeneities significantly alter large-scale expansion dynamics. Furthermore, fundamental assumptions regarding the strict Doppler interpretation of redshifts in idealized FLRW models can lead to world-line contradictions if the universe possesses certain multiply-connected, non-trivial topologies . To probe these potentially multi-connected topologies, researchers employ techniques such as 'cosmic crystallography,' which analyzes pair separation histograms of cosmic sources to identify distinct topological signatures left in the large-scale distribution of matter [Lehoucq et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996A&A...313..339L/abstract). Despite these theoretical nuances, the FLRW metric remains the indispensable starting point for modeling both background evolution and structural perturbations.

## Fundamental Friedmann Equations

The fundamental Friedmann equations form the backbone of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]], governing the expansion of a homogeneous and isotropic universe. Derived by applying the field equations of [[general-relativity|General Relativity]] to the Robertson-Walker metric, these equations determine the time evolution of the cosmic scale factor $a(t)$ as a function of the total [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]] $\rho$ and cosmic pressure $P$ [Peacock et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999coph.book.....P/abstract), [Dodelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020moco.book.....D/abstract).

The first Friedmann equation relates the expansion rate—an integral part of the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]]—to the energy content and [[spatial-curvature|Spatial curvature]] of the universe. Specifically, the square of the expansion rate is determined by the total mass-energy density $\rho$, adjusted by a correction term for the spatial curvature signature $K$, which is conventionally normalized to take values of $+1, 0,$ or $-1$ for closed, flat, and open geometries, respectively. In standard cosmological models, the total density is partitioned into distinct fluid components: non-relativistic matter, radiation, and vacuum energy or a cosmological constant. As the universe expands, these components scale differently with the scale factor $a(t)$, driving distinct epochs of cosmic evolution.

The second Friedmann equation, often called the acceleration equation, describes the rate of change of the cosmic expansion rate. This equation highlights a profound distinction between [[newtonian-dynamics|Newtonian dynamics]] and Einstein's relativity. While the first Friedmann equation (without a cosmological constant) can be derived purely from Newtonian physics for a matter-dominated universe, the Newtonian derivation fails to produce the relativistic pressure contributions. In the relativistic framework, however, cosmic pressure acts as a source of [[gravity|Gravity]] and explicitly contributes to gravitational deceleration as a relativistic correction [Dodelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020moco.book.....D/abstract). 

The acceleration equation dictates the conditions necessary to produce an accelerating universe. To overcome the attractive effects of standard gravity, the universe must be dominated by a component with a strongly negative pressure. The cosmological constant $\Lambda$ serves precisely this role, contributing an effective mass density $\rho_\Lambda = \Lambda c^2 / 8\pi G$ and an associated negative pressure $P_\Lambda = -\rho_\Lambda c^2$. Current distance-redshift observations show that this repulsive vacuum energy overwhelms standard gravitational attraction at late times, driving an ever more rapid expansion. In the formal language of general relativity, this late-time cosmic acceleration requires a violation of the Strong Energy Condition (SEC), a violation that observations suggest began sometime between the epoch of galaxy formation and the present day [Visser et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PhRvD..56.7578V/abstract).

## Cosmological Constituents and Scaling Laws

The [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] of a homogeneous and isotropic universe is fundamentally governed by the [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]] $\rho$ and pressure $P$ of its constituents. By combining the Friedmann equations with the conservation of energy-momentum, one obtains a fluid continuity equation that dictates how a component's density evolves as the universe expands. For a fluid characterized by a constant ratio of pressure to density, this continuity equation yields a straightforward scaling law with respect to the scale factor.

In the standard cosmological paradigm, the universe's energy budget is partitioned into three primary constituents [Dodelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020moco.book.....D/abstract):
*   **Radiation and relativistic particles:** Characterized by significant pressure, this component includes photons and neutrinos. Its energy density dilutes faster than ordinary matter due to the combined effects of volume expansion and cosmological redshift. The universe was primarily governed by this fluid at early times, an epoch known as the [[radiation-dominated-universe|Radiation-dominated universe]].
*   **Non-relativistic matter:** Comprising both baryonic matter and cold dark matter, this "dust" exerts negligible pressure. Its density scales purely with the expanding volume. While the dark matter behaves mostly as a collisionless fluid, the baryonic component undergoes complex cosmic evolution; observations reveal that galaxy formation involved substantial gas inflow at high redshifts, followed by intense epochs of star formation, heavy element synthesis, and widespread chemical enrichment [Pei et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...522..604P/abstract).
*   **Dark energy:** In the baseline $\Lambda$CDM model, dark energy is identified with the cosmological constant $\Lambda$, which acts as a vacuum energy with a strongly negative pressure. This yields a constant energy density that overcomes gravitational attraction to drive the late-time accelerated expansion of the universe [Peacock et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999coph.book.....P/abstract). Observations of this accelerated expansion imply a violation of the strong energy condition between the epoch of galaxy formation and the present day [Visser et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PhRvD..56.7578V/abstract).

To quantify the relative contributions of these constituents, cosmologists define dimensionless [[omega-parameters|Omega parameters]] as the ratio of a component's present-day density to the critical density required to yield a flat geometry. The Friedmann equations can then be recast entirely to express the expansion rate as a function of the relative fractional contributions from radiation, matter, spatial curvature, and dark energy.

While the $\Lambda$CDM model successfully matches broad cosmological observations, the underlying nature of the dark sector remains an active area of research. Alternative theories often seek to replace the cosmological constant with dynamical fields, such as in [[quintessence-models|Quintessence models]], or through modifications to [[general-relativity|General Relativity]] itself [Basilakos et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PhRvD..87l3529B/abstract). For example, formulations of [[massive-gravity|Massive gravity]] introduce modified field equations that generate effective fluid terms mimicking quintessence or a cosmological constant [Cardone et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvD..85l4005C/abstract). 

Furthermore, the standard scaling laws assume a perfectly homogeneous background. This assumption is challenged by [[cosmological-backreaction|Cosmological backreaction]], which proposes that the averaging of small-scale inhomogeneities from [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]] might significantly influence the global expansion rate. While some authors suggest that backreaction could potentially account for late-time acceleration without dark energy [Macpherson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191013380M/abstract), current macroscopic gravity constraints heavily restrict its ability to act as a complete replacement for the dark sector. The extent to which these non-linear inhomogeneities bias precision parameter estimation remains an active area of investigation.

## Linear Perturbations and Gravitational Instability

In the standard cosmological model, the universe is strictly homogeneous and isotropic only on the largest observable scales. To describe the formation of galaxies and large-scale structures, the background Friedmann framework must be extended using [[cosmological-perturbation-theory|Cosmological Perturbation Theory]]. In this paradigm, structures form from small initial fluctuations—often described by a primordial spectrum related to the [[initial-conditions-in-astrophysics|Initial Conditions in Astrophysics]]—which evolve through the mechanism of gravitational instability. The overarching growth and distribution of these structures is well-described by the classic cold dark matter (CDM) model, providing a unified phenomenological view from inflationary production to late-time structure formation [Liddle et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PhR...231....1L/abstract).

The stability of a density perturbation is dictated by the competition between self-gravity and internal pressure gradients. In an expanding universe, whose rate is defined by the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]], these perturbations will only collapse if they exceed the Jeans mass threshold. In a static background, this would lead to exponential collapse; however, the expansion introduces a damping effect. Consequently, the [[linear-theory|Linear theory]] growth of perturbations in a matter-dominated universe is damped to a power law in time [Peacock et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999coph.book.....P/abstract), [Dodelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020moco.book.....D/abstract).

On scales larger than the Hubble horizon, or when dealing with relativistic components, a full relativistic treatment is required. [[metric-perturbations|Metric perturbations]] $\delta g_{\mu\nu}$ are coupled to the perturbed energy-momentum tensor via the [[linearized-equations|Linearized equations]]. These scalar perturbations are typically quantified by [[gravitational-potentials|Gravitational potentials]] $\Phi$ [Dodelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020moco.book.....D/abstract). 

The amplitude of these perturbations as a function of time is quantified by the [[growth-function|Growth function]]. The evolution of density contrasts heavily depends on the dominant energy component of the universe. During the [[radiation-dominated-universe|Radiation-dominated universe]], the growth of sub-horizon dark matter perturbations is strongly suppressed due to the rapid expansion driven by the highly pressurized radiation fluid. Once the universe enters the matter-dominated era, the density contrast for pressureless matter grows as a power-law in time [Dodelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020moco.book.....D/abstract). The transition between these two regimes is encoded in the [[cosmological-transfer-function|Cosmological transfer function]], which acts as a filter on primordial fluctuations. To further constrain theoretical models, researchers exploit symmetries in the [[newtonian-dynamics|Newtonian dynamics]] of motion to derive powerful consistency relations for the n-point correlation functions of overdensities [Kehagias et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013NuPhB.873..514K/abstract). Furthermore, extending these analyses to second order in perturbation theory significantly improves analytical approximations for calculating observable quantities within large survey volumes [Fuentes et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210610181F/abstract). Similarly, examining second-order density fluctuations directly provides a rigorous physical basis for understanding galaxy bias within [[general-relativity|General Relativity]] [Yoo et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvD..90l3507Y/abstract). 

As perturbations grow into the non-linear regime, analytical approximations often break down. To address this, renormalization group techniques are applied to [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]]; these methods identify an intrinsic UV cut-off that significantly improves the convergence of power spectrum evolution at small scales [Matarrese et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007JCAP...06..026M/abstract). 

Because the standard Friedmann equations only constrain the background evolution, the study of perturbation growth provides a critical discriminant between a cosmological constant ($\Lambda$) and modified theories of gravity. While the $\Lambda$CDM model predicts well-defined growth rates, alternative frameworks often predict modified, scale-dependent growth rates and altered relations between metric potentials and matter overdensities [Barreira et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvD..86l4016B/abstract), [Basilakos et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PhRvD..87l3529B/abstract). Analyzing perturbations can also expose fatal flaws in alternative models; for example, $f(G)$ Gauss-Bonnet gravity induces negative instabilities on small scales that contradict the observed galaxy spectrum [de Felice et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MPLA...25..885D/abstract). Precise measurements of the growth of structure therefore remain one of the most powerful methods for testing relativity on cosmological scales [Durrer et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022GReGr..54...88D/abstract).

## Modifications in Alternative Gravity Theories

While the standard Friedmann equations derived from [[general-relativity|General Relativity]] successfully describe the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] of the $\Lambda$CDM model, alternative theories of [[gravity|Gravity]] modify these equations to explain late-time cosmic acceleration without invoking a cosmological constant. These modifications typically appear either as geometrical corrections to the left-hand side of the Friedmann equations or as effective energy-momentum tensors on the right-hand side.

**Geometrical Extensions and Anisotropic Models**
Some theories fundamentally alter the underlying spacetime geometry. For instance, the possibility of a $\Lambda$CDM model formulated in [[finsler-geometry|Finsler geometry]] (Randers-Finsler space) has been explored; in this framework, observations are described without dark energy by suppressing the density parameter $\Omega_\Lambda$ to zero [Chang et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ChPhC..36..710C/abstract). Alternatively, the derivative expansion framework proposes describing cosmological phenomena—including cosmic redshift and late-time acceleration—within a single static metric rather than an expanding FLRW background [Haddad et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021GReGr..53...80H/abstract). Furthermore, dark energy can be modeled with an anisotropic equation of state within the Bianchi I geometric framework, potentially explaining large-scale anomalies in the CMB quadrupole [Koivisto et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008JCAP...06..018K/abstract).

**$f(R)$ Gravity and Nonlocal Models**
In $f(R)$ gravity, the Einstein-Hilbert action is modified by replacing the [[ricci-scalar|Ricci scalar]] $R$ with an arbitrary function $f(R)$. The resulting modified Friedmann equation incorporates higher-order derivatives of the scale factor $a(t)$. Viable parametrizations mimic the $\Lambda$CDM background expansion while predicting distinct, scale-dependent modifications to the linear growth rate of cosmic structures [Basilakos et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PhRvD..87l3529B/abstract). Nonlocal gravity models also reproduce the background expansion but predict quantifiable differences in structure growth compared to standard general relativity [Park et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PhRvD..87b4003P/abstract). However, further generalizations, such as $f(R,T)$ gravity, are generally ruled out due to their inability to properly recover the necessary dynamics of a [[radiation-dominated-universe|Radiation-dominated universe]] [Jeakel et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PDU....4301401J/abstract).

**Scalar-Tensor Models**
Scalar-tensor models introduce a scalar field non-minimally coupled to the spacetime geometry. The modified Friedmann equations in these theories can naturally drive an epoch of accelerated expansion. While their background expansion can be tuned to match standard models, they are highly distinguishable at the level of [[metric-perturbations|Metric perturbations]]. For instance, on sub-Hubble scales, scalar-tensor theories can amplify dark energy density perturbations by a factor of $\sim 10^4$ compared to minimally coupled [[quintessence-models|Quintessence models]]. This leads to a scale-independent evolution and a vanishing effective speed of sound for the scalar field, which mildly amplifies matter perturbations through an anti-correlation mechanism [Bueno Sanchez et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvD..81j3505B/abstract).

**Massive Gravity and Minkowski Spacetime Formulations**
Covariant formulations of [[massive-gravity|Massive gravity]], which assign a non-zero mass to the graviton, also lead to a modified Friedmann equation. In a spatially flat universe, the massive graviton contributes terms that act as effective fluids mimicking dust, a cosmological constant, and quintessence [Cardone et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvD..85l4005C/abstract). These modifications affect the innermost and outermost stable circular orbits for structures ranging from individual stars to massive galaxy clusters [Rincón et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Univ....7..278R/abstract). At a more fundamental level, the conceptual framework of cosmology in [[minkowski-spacetime|Minkowski spacetime]] offers a radical departure. In this approach, cosmic expansion is reinterpreted as a variation of mass, length, and time scales through metric transformations [Lombriser et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40o5005L/abstract). Additionally, vector theories of gravity formulated in Minkowski space-time can pass observational tests while avoiding singularities offered by standard evolutionary paths [Svidzinsky et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0904.3155S/abstract).

**Constraints and Pathologies**
Not all modifications yield stable solutions. $f(G)$ gravity is effectively ruled out by fundamental instabilities during the matter and radiation epochs [de Felice et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MPLA...25..885D/abstract). Similarly, Galileon gravity models predict that [[gravitational-potentials|Gravitational potentials]] anomalously grow rather than decay at late times, leaving observables in strong tension with current data [Barreira et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvD..86l4016B/abstract). Beyond background expansion, alternative models can be tested using strong-field gravitational lensing to discriminate between different black hole metrics [Bozza et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhRvD..66j3001B/abstract). At the local scale, modified gravity theories can also be tightly constrained by the precession angle of planets in the Solar System, utilizing the [[gravitational-constant|Gravitational constant]] and analytic formulae to test deviations from standard dynamics [Chan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.6238C/abstract).

## Inhomogeneous Cosmology and Backreaction

The standard derivation of the Friedmann equations relies heavily on the assumption that the universe is homogeneous and isotropic on large scales. However, because the field equations of [[general-relativity|General Relativity]] are non-linear, the dynamic evolution of an averaged [[spacetime-geometry|Spacetime geometry]] is not necessarily equivalent to the evolution of a perfectly smooth FLRW background [Ostrowski et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AcPPS..10..407O/abstract). This mathematical discrepancy yields an effective kinematic contribution to the global expansion rate known as [[cosmological-backreaction|Cosmological backreaction]].

To rigorously investigate how localized structures influence the global [[expansion-history|Expansion history]], cosmologists employ exact inhomogeneous solutions to Einstein's equations. The [[szekeres-model|Szekeres model]], for instance, provides a quasi-spherical exact solution representing a time-dependent mass dipole superposed on a monopole, making it particularly well-suited for modeling the adjacent structures of voids and superclusters without relying on perturbative approximations [Bolejko et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006yosc.conf....9B/abstract). Similarly, spherically symmetric [[tolman-bondi-models|Tolman-Bondi models]] are utilized to study the dynamics of galaxy groups and larger localized flows [Peirani et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...488..845P/abstract).

These exact solutions highlight a crucial issue known as the "averaging problem." Tracing light propagation through non-linear inhomogeneities reveals that distance-redshift relations can deviate substantially from the predictions of the standard Friedmann model if a given line of sight preferentially samples underdense voids or overdense filaments [Meures et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.419.1937M/abstract). Consequently, performing proper averages on the observational null cone is critical to avoid systematic biases in parameter estimation [Coley et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0905.2442C/abstract). To observationally verify the homogeneity assumption underlying the Copernican Principle, researchers have proposed tests using the 'fossil record' of distant galaxies, providing a direct check against extreme models [Heavens et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011JCAP...09..035H/abstract).

A highly debated question in [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]] is whether backreaction could be solely responsible for the observed late-time acceleration of the universe, thereby eliminating the need for dark energy [Macpherson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191013380M/abstract). While certain formalisms suggest a significant effect, observational constraints derived from macroscopic gravity frameworks place tight bounds on the backreaction density parameter [Wijenayake et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..94h3501W/abstract). Therefore, the current consensus leans toward the conclusion that while small-scale inhomogeneities introduce measurable corrections, they are insufficient to explain cosmic acceleration without an independent dark energy component.

## Galaxy Dynamics and Alternatives to Dark Matter

The standard interpretation of flat galaxy rotation curves relies heavily on the presence of non-baryonic dark matter. However, a diverse class of theories seeks to explain these dynamical anomalies by modifying [[general-relativity|General Relativity]] and the associated [[gravitational-potentials|Gravitational potentials]] on galactic scales. Alternatively, within standard relativity, it has been demonstrated that post-Newtonian corrections to the dynamics of self-gravitating ideal gases can naturally reproduce the characteristic behavior of rotation curves without invoking non-baryonic dark matter [Kremer et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..93f4073K/abstract).

Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) postulates a critical acceleration scale below which gravity diverges from inverse-square laws. To embed this within a covariant framework, relativistic extensions replace the standard metric with a multiplet of interacting fields [Skordis et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvL..96a1301S/abstract). While such theories can reproduce flat galaxy rotation curves, critical analyses suggest they may exhibit field instabilities within dense matter distributions [Bruneton et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007PhRvD..76l4012B/abstract). To combat this, theorists integrate screening effects into MOND-like theories, ensuring negligible deviations from general relativity at solar system scales while reproducing the necessary galactic dynamics [Babichev et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvD..84f1502B/abstract).

Another major alternative is Scalar-Tensor-Vector Gravity (STVG), which uses a modified potential featuring a repulsive Yukawa contribution to accurately reproduce cluster dynamics without dark matter [Mishra et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PhRvD..88j4036M/abstract). Similarly, extensions of mimetic gravity naturally yield a MOND-law in the [[newtonian-dynamics|Newtonian dynamics]] limit [Vagnozzi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017CQGra..34r5006V/abstract).

Alternative frameworks attempt to derive modified dynamics from fundamental principles. The application of [[scale-invariant-solutions|Scale-invariant solutions]] to geodesic equations modifies the empty-space equations of relativity [Maeder et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240308759M/abstract). Thermodynamic gravity formulations suggest that MOND behavior arises from temperature-dependent corrections to equipartition laws, yielding an acceleration tied to the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] [Pikhitsa et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1010.0318P/abstract), [Rostami et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251105632R/abstract). Other modifications include Cotton gravity, which has fit the rotation profiles of galaxies by supplementing potentials with a linear term [Harada et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106f4044H/abstract). axion cosmology also provides a related approach, where the effective sound speed and Jeans scale of axion fields alter the [[cosmological-transfer-function|Cosmological transfer function]] and the resulting matter power spectrum [Marsh et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151007633M/abstract). Thus, the literature reflects an active debate over the optimal method to resolve galactic dynamics within standard cosmological backgrounds.

## Observational Constraints and Model Testing

The Friedmann equations form the quantitative basis of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]], directly linking the energy content of the universe to its [[expansion-history|Expansion history]]. To constrain the relative constituent parameters and test the validity of underlying models, independent observational probes must be combined to map the background expansion and the growth of density perturbations.

A primary method for testing the equations is the measurement of the distance-redshift relation using Type Ia supernovae (SNIa). As standardisable candles, SNIa provide direct measurements of the Hubble parameter. Observations initially revealed the late-time acceleration of the universe, requiring either a cosmological constant or dynamical dark energy to dominate the present-day [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]]. However, interpreting these measurements relies on the assumption of homogeneity; small-scale density contrasts can cause systematic deviations from standard distance-redshift relations [Meures et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.419.1937M/abstract).

To break degeneracies, SNIa data are typically combined with measurements of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) and baryon acoustic oscillations. Joint likelihood analyses place stringent constraints on Friedmann parameters [Wijenayake et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..94h3501W/abstract). These combined probes have established the spatially flat paradigm, while also tightly constraining extensions such as modified gravity.

Because of the unpalatable coincidences found in standard models, some researchers propose alternative frameworks, such as the $R_h=ct$ universe model, which offers an alternative partitioning of energy components [Melia et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.419.2579M/abstract). To test [[general-relativity|General Relativity]] against these diverse models, cosmologists employ parametrized approaches that allow for model-independent measurement of both expansion and growth without assuming the validity of field equations a priori [Tegmark et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhRvD..66j3507T/abstract).

Despite the success of the standard model, precision cosmology has revealed the Hubble tension—a discrepancy between local expansion measurements and values inferred from the CMB. Numerous modifications have been proposed, such as the inclusion of a negative radiation term [Godłowski et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhLB..642...13G/abstract) or emergent dark energy arising from stochastic structure formation [Lapi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205823L/abstract). Consequently, the Friedmann framework remains under rigorous empirical scrutiny.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm based on the Friedmann equations successfully describes a broad range of cosmological observations, several fundamental theoretical and observational challenges remain unresolved. These open questions drive ongoing research into the nature of the dark sector and the foundational assumptions of homogeneity.

**Dark Energy Evolution and the Coincidence Problem**
A primary conceptual challenge in [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]] is the *coincidence problem*: the observation that matter and dark energy densities are currently of the same order of magnitude. Alternative frameworks propose that cosmic acceleration—a defining feature of our [[expansion-history|Expansion history]]—is driven by time-dependent evolution. Models suggest dark energy might be an emergent phenomenon arising from the stochastic ensemble averaging of structures [Lapi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205823L/abstract). Testing these dynamical models is complicated by the "dark degeneracy," where a time-dependent equation of state can be mapped mathematically onto models of interacting dark matter [Barbosa et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PDU....4501526B/abstract).

**The Hubble Constant Tension**
As discussed previously, precise local measurements of the expansion rate currently disagree with inferred values. This tension has motivated numerous extensions to the Friedmann framework. While modifying the late-time expansion can alleviate both the $H_0$ tension and the related structure growth tension [Lapi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205823L/abstract), it remains unclear whether a late-universe resolution is viable without spoiling other background constraints.

**Averaging and Cosmological Backreaction**
The standard equations assume a perfectly homogeneous metric. As [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]] proceeds, the spatial average of the local metric is not strictly equal to the metric of the average density. While some researchers suggest [[cosmological-backreaction|Cosmological backreaction]] from small-scale inhomogeneities could mimic dark energy [Macpherson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191013380M/abstract), recent observational constraints have tightly bounded its kinematic contribution [Wijenayake et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..94h3501W/abstract).

**Quantum Gravity, Early Universe, and Topological Anomalies**
Finally, the classical equations inevitably break down at $t \to 0$, necessitating a theory of quantum gravity to describe the origin of the universe and establish the [[initial-conditions-in-astrophysics|Initial Conditions in Astrophysics]]. While inflation is the standard mechanism, alternative approaches using field theory attempt to derive [[scale-invariant-solutions|Scale-invariant solutions]] for the power spectrum directly from quantum gravitational fluctuations [Hamber et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Univ....5...31H/abstract). Early-universe modifications provide mechanisms for significant expansion without standard phase transitions [Mijić et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986PhRvD..34.2934M/abstract). Furthermore, density fluctuations may have generated primordial black holes, which remain a crucial dark matter candidate [Carr et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975ApJ...201....1C/abstract). On larger scales, a scale-dependent "running" of the [[gravitational-constant|Gravitational constant]] could potentially emerge at astrophysical distances, offering a mechanism to explain flat galaxy rotation curves without dark matter [Reuter et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004JCAP...12..001R/abstract). Recent discoveries of surprisingly massive galaxies at high redshifts have prompted further alternative solutions, such as hybrid models that theoretically stretch the age of the universe [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.3385G/abstract). Additionally, exploring exotic topological features remains an active pursuit, where stable wormholes embedded in open Friedmann models could theoretically act as accelerators or generators of radiation [Kirillov et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020EPJC...80...45K/abstract).
