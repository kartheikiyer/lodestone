In physical cosmology, **non-linear structure formation** describes the advanced stages of cosmic evolution where primordial [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]] grow to mass overdensities significantly greater than unity ($\delta \gg 1$). While the early universe and large-scale fluctuation modes are well-described by [[linear-theory|Linear theory]] and a simple [[linear-growth-factor|Linear growth factor]], local regions eventually decouple from the background Hubble expansion due to runaway [[gravitational-instability|Gravitational instability]]. Upon reaching a maximum turnaround radius, these regions undergo rapid gravitational collapse and violent relaxation to form gravitationally bound dark matter halos characterized by near-universal [[density-profiles|Density profiles]] [Peebles et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981lssu.book.....P/abstract), [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996IAUS..171..255N/abstract). This late-time [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]] transforms the initially smooth density field into the highly complex [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]], a vast interconnected topology of expansive voids, two-dimensional sheets (or [[zel-dovich-pancake|Zel’dovich pancake]]s), one-dimensional filaments, and dense cluster nodes [Gott et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJ...319....1G/abstract).

Because analytical frameworks such as [[cosmological-perturbation-theory|Cosmological Perturbation Theory]] strictly break down as the variance of the density field approaches and exceeds unity, characterizing this fully non-linear regime relies heavily on high-resolution [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]] [White et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986isos.book..228W/abstract). Historically, $N$-body simulations were instrumental in establishing the Cold Dark Matter (CDM) paradigm, demonstrating that structures assemble via [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]]—a "bottom-up" process where low-mass halos collapse first and subsequently merge into larger systems [White et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987IAUS..117..263W/abstract). To complement numerical efforts, analytical tools like spherical and [[ellipsoidal-collapse|Ellipsoidal collapse]] models, alongside the [[press-schechter-formalism|Press-Schechter formalism]], provide crucial mathematical descriptions for predicting the mass function and formation histories of collapsed objects. Furthermore, analyzing this regime reveals how non-linear evolution intrinsically generates phase correlations, transforming [[nearly-gaussian-initial-conditions|Nearly Gaussian initial conditions]] into highly skewed mass distributions that must be quantified by higher-order statistics like the [[matter-bispectrum|Matter bispectrum]] and [[matter-trispectrum|Matter trispectrum]].

Despite the tremendous success of the $\Lambda$CDM framework on macroscopic scales, mapping the non-linear dark matter distribution to the observable universe requires the integration of complex baryonic physics. [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] are heavily utilized to track gas dynamics, radiative cooling, and non-gravitational feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei, which regulate galaxy assembly within dark matter potential wells. However, profound open questions remain regarding [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]]. Tensions such as the cusp-core and missing satellites problems persistently challenge standard collisionless dark matter models, prompting investigations into whether these anomalies arise from incomplete sub-grid feedback models or require fundamental modifications to the dark matter particle itself. Simultaneously, modern theoretical research employs the [[effective-field-theory-of-large-scale-structure|Effective Field Theory of Large Scale Structure]] and specialized simulations to disentangle primordial signals from late-time gravitational effects, probing the subtle non-linear imprints of massive neutrinos and dynamical dark energy in preparation for next-generation precision galaxy surveys.

## Foundations of Non-linear Growth

The formation of large-scale structure is fundamentally driven by [[gravitational-instability|Gravitational instability]], which amplifies primordial [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]] over time. At early epochs, the mass density contrast $\delta(\mathbf{x}, t) = \delta\rho/\rho$ is significantly less than unity, and its evolution is well described by [[linear-theory|Linear theory]] following the foundational Einstein-Boltzmann equations [Dodelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020moco.book.....D/abstract). In an expanding universe, this linear growth scales as a power law of time—parameterized by the [[linear-growth-factor|Linear growth factor]], $D(t)$—rather than the exponential runaway collapse that would occur in a static background [Peebles et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981lssu.book.....P/abstract). Small perturbations in the background [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]] induce a [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]], representing systematic deviations from the uniform Hubble expansion that channel matter into developing gravitational potential wells.

As perturbations grow to order $\delta \sim 1$, they transition out of the linear regime. The local gravitational pull of an overdensity begins to significantly decelerate its expansion relative to the cosmological background. Eventually, the region halts its expansion entirely at a point known as turn-around (see [[cosmological-turnaround-and-mass-scales|Cosmological Turnaround and Mass Scales]]). At this stage, the physical size of the perturbation reaches its maximum before local gravity definitively overcomes the Hubble flow, initiating rapid, non-linear collapse [Peebles et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981lssu.book.....P/abstract). 

While spherical models offer a useful heuristic, realistic non-linear collapse in a random density field is generically aspherical. The [[zel-dovich-approximation|Zel'dovich approximation]] demonstrates that collapse typically proceeds sequentially: compression along the principal axis of the local tidal tensor forms a two-dimensional [[zel-dovich-pancake|Zel’dovich pancake]], followed by collapse along the second axis into a filament, and finally along the third axis into a fully collapsed, virialized dark matter halo. To extend the Zel'dovich approximation beyond the initial crossing of particle trajectories, the adhesion approximation introduces artificial viscosity to model non-linear fragmentation and the subsequent development of these structures into vast cellular networks [Weinberg et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990MNRAS.247..260W/abstract).

Statistically, the transition into the non-linear regime profoundly alters the geometry of the density field. Even if the universe begins with strictly [[gaussian-initial-conditions|Gaussian initial conditions]], non-linear gravitational evolution introduces complex phase correlations. This leads to a highly non-Gaussian mass distribution characterized by isolated, high-density peaks separated by vast, underdense voids [Sahni et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...476L...1S/abstract), [Coles et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000Natur.406..376C/abstract). On large scales, the two-point correlation function scales with the square of the linear growth factor, $\xi(r, t) \propto [D(t)]^2$. However, on small scales entering the strongly non-linear regime, the clustering amplitude steepens and grows roughly as the cube of the scale factor, $a^3$. This scaling reflects the nearly constant physical density of virialized clumps decoupling from the background matter density, which falls off as $a^{-3}$ [Peebles et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981lssu.book.....P/abstract). These dense structures, particularly massive galaxy clusters, serve as fundamental cosmological probes; their volume abundance and spatial clustering in X-ray observations provide powerful tools for testing gravity theories and mapping the cosmic [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] [Clerc et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023hxga.book..123C/abstract).

Analytically tracking this evolution becomes increasingly difficult as the mass variance of the density field exceeds unity. While higher-order perturbation theory can successfully model the transfer of power to smaller scales in the weakly non-linear regime ($\sigma \le 1$) [Baugh et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994MNRAS.270..183B/abstract), [Juszkiewicz et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996astro.ph..2134J/abstract), these formalisms break down rapidly for $\delta \gg 1$. Consequently, tracking the intricate, collisionless dynamics of dark matter beyond turn-around and into the fully formed [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] strictly requires high-resolution $N$-body [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]] [Bertschinger et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ARA&A..36..599B/abstract), [Frenk et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002RSPTA.360.1277F/abstract).

## Historical Development of Structure Formation Models

The theoretical study of [[gravitational-instability|Gravitational instability]] traces its origins to Jeans (1902), who first demonstrated that density perturbations grow when self-gravity overcomes internal pressure gradients. The application of this stability analysis to an expanding cosmological background was later developed by Gamow and Teller (1939) and Lifshitz (1946). A critical result of Lifshitz's linear perturbation analysis was that in an expanding universe, the growth rate of the density contrast $\delta = \delta \rho / \rho$ is heavily damped by the cosmic expansion. This results in a power-law growth in time ($\delta \propto t^\alpha$ with $\alpha > 0$) rather than the exponential run-away process seen in static backgrounds [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

Understanding the transition from linear growth to non-linear collapse required new analytical frameworks, which saw rapid development in the early 1970s. Gunn and Gott (1972) introduced a simple spherically symmetric model—often termed the Gunn-Gott model—describing the full evolutionary track of a perturbation: linear expansion, turn-around from the Hubble flow, and dissipationless collapse into a virialized state. Their model showed that the final quasi-equilibrium system has a characteristic radius approximately half of its radius at turn-around. Around the same time, the [[zel-dovich-approximation|Zel'dovich approximation]] (1970) provided a kinematic treatment of non-linear collapse without assuming spherical symmetry. It revealed that gravitational collapse is generically anisotropic, proceeding first along a single axis to form a sheet-like structure known as a [[zel-dovich-pancake|Zel’dovich pancake]]. Building upon the spherical collapse criteria of the Gunn-Gott model, Press and Schechter (1974) applied a threshold overdensity to a field of [[gaussian-initial-conditions|Gaussian initial conditions]], establishing the [[press-schechter-formalism|Press-Schechter formalism]]. This framework allowed for the first analytical estimations of the mass function and abundance of collapsed dark matter halos [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). To further map these structures, analytical approaches incorporated Burgers' equation to track non-linear evolution, allowing for the determination of galaxy mass and velocity distributions within hierarchical clustering models [Doroshkevich et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990MNRAS.246...10D/abstract).

As analytical methods reached their limits in the highly non-linear regime ($\delta \gg 1$), the advent of [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]] in the 1980s triggered a major theoretical paradigm shift. Initial models often assumed Hot Dark Matter (HDM)—composed of fast-moving particles like massive neutrinos. The HDM framework predicted a "top-down" scenario where large supercluster-sized pancakes formed first and later fragmented into galaxies. However, early N-body simulations demonstrated that neutrino-dominated universes rapidly developed monolithic structures far more massive than any observed objects, a prediction fatally at variance with the observed galaxy distribution [White et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986isos.book..228W/abstract), [White et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987IAUS..117..263W/abstract).

This failure led to the widespread adoption of Cold Dark Matter (CDM) as the standard paradigm. In CDM models, structure formation proceeds via a "bottom-up" or [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]] process. Slow-moving dark matter particles lack the free-streaming length to erase small-scale power, allowing sub-galactic perturbations to collapse first and merge over time to form progressively larger clusters. Early N-body simulations confirmed that CDM-dominated universes successfully reproduced the clustering properties and mass distributions of observed galaxies [White et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986isos.book..228W/abstract), cementing Cold Dark Matter as the foundational assumption for modeling large-scale structure in [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]].

## Analytical Frameworks: Spherical and Ellipsoidal Collapse

To bridge the gap between [[linear-theory|Linear theory]] and fully non-linear [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]], analytical frameworks relying on simplified geometries are widely employed. The most foundational of these is the spherical collapse model, which considers the evolution of a spherically symmetric, uniform overdensity in an expanding universe. The overdense region initially expands with the background cosmology, but its self-gravity eventually halts this expansion at a "turnaround" radius. The region subsequently collapses and undergoes violent relaxation to form a gravitationally bound structure. According to the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|Virial Theorem and Parameters]], the final radius of this virialized dark matter halo is approximately half of its turnaround radius. A central result of this model is the linearly extrapolated critical overdensity for collapse, typically denoted as $\delta_c \approx 1.68$ in an Einstein-de Sitter cosmology [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). 

This critical threshold forms the basis of the [[press-schechter-formalism|Press-Schechter formalism]], which links the initial Gaussian density field to the abundance of non-linear collapsed objects. A more rigorous mathematical underpinning for this mapping is provided by excursion set theory. In this framework, the smoothed density contrast at a given spatial point is modeled as a stochastic random walk as the variance $S$ (inversely related to the smoothing mass scale $M$) is varied. A halo is assumed to form on the largest mass scale where the random walk crosses the absorbing barrier at $\delta = \delta_c$. This formalism permits analytical calculations of halo abundances, merger histories, and spatial clustering [Scannapieco et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...571..585S/abstract).

However, the assumption of strict spherical symmetry is a severe oversimplification. As demonstrated by the [[zel-dovich-approximation|Zel'dovich approximation]], gravitational collapse in a generic Gaussian random field is intrinsically triaxial, proceeding sequentially along three principal axes to form a [[zel-dovich-pancake|Zel’dovich pancake]] (sheet), a filament, and finally a virialized halo. To account for this, the [[ellipsoidal-collapse|Ellipsoidal collapse]] model extends the analytical framework by incorporating the influence of surrounding tidal shear fields. Within the excursion set approach, ellipsoidal dynamics replaces the constant threshold $\delta_c$ with a mass-dependent "moving" ellipsoidal collapse barrier. This modification significantly improves agreement with N-body simulations at both the high- and low-mass tails compared to the traditional spherical model.

Despite the mathematical elegance and broad utility of these analytical models, the precise correspondence between initial linear conditions and final non-linear structures remains an active area of investigation. Detailed comparisons with high-resolution simulations reveal that identifying galaxies or halos strictly with local maxima in the smoothed linear density field—often termed the "peaks formalism"—does not perfectly predict the actual halos that assemble through chaotic, clumpy [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]] [Katz et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993MNRAS.265..689K/abstract). Consequently, while spherical and ellipsoidal collapse models offer indispensable physical intuition and baseline scaling relations, capturing the full dynamical complexity of non-linear mass assembly ultimately requires sophisticated computational techniques. Building on these foundations, the "halo approach" or halo model provides a comprehensive framework for modeling the non-linear galaxy power spectrum by describing exactly how galaxies populate and occupy these virialized dark matter halos [Cooray et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.348..250C/abstract).

## Morphology and Topology of the Cosmic Web

The large-scale distribution of matter in the late-time Universe is characterized by a complex, interconnected network known as the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]]. This intricate morphology is the direct outcome of [[gravitational-instability|Gravitational instability]], which amplifies small primordial density fluctuations into the highly non-linear regime ($\delta \gg 1$). The structural texture of the cosmic web—dominated by vast, underdense voids bounded by two-dimensional sheets, intersecting one-dimensional filaments, and massive dense nodes—is primarily established by density perturbations on scales up to $\sim 100 \ h^{-1}\text{ Mpc}$ [Suhhonenko et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...531A.149S/abstract). Perturbations on larger scales tend to modulate the richness of gravitationally bound structures and the emptiness of voids without altering the fundamental web-like topology.

The kinematic origin of this morphology can be understood analytically via the [[zel-dovich-approximation|Zel'dovich approximation]]. Because gravitational collapse in a generic, random-phase density field is locally asymmetric, an overdense region will typically compress along its principal axes at different rates. The collapse along the first axis produces a flattened, sheet-like structure commonly referred to as a [[zel-dovich-pancake|Zel’dovich pancake]]. Subsequent collapse along the second and third axes forms filamentary structures and, ultimately, fully virialized, roughly spherical dark matter halos. This sequence governs the flow of matter out of voids, onto pancakes, along filaments, and into cluster nodes. Observations and N-body simulations reveal that the resulting coherent structures can span enormous distances, with cluster orientations remaining aligned over scales up to $200-300 \text{ Mpc}$ [West et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250619826W/abstract). The non-linear phase coupling that drives this hierarchical formation also leads to a quantifiable decrease in the global information entropy of the density field, estimated at a $\sim 0.004\%$ drop as structures emerge from the linear background [Coles et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000Natur.406..376C/abstract), [Sarkar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240703042S/abstract). At the largest scales, this interconnected web can be categorized by "basins of attraction" (BoA)—regions defined by gravitational potential minima where matter flows converge. Observations mapping these flows indicate that the Milky Way is likely part of the massive [[laniakea-supercluster|Laniakea Supercluster]] or Shapley structures, illustrating the vast reach of these cosmic basins [Valade et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1610V/abstract).

To rigorously quantify the topology of the cosmic web, cosmologists employ statistical measures such as the genus curve and Minkowski functionals. These metrics characterize the connectivity and shape of isodensity surfaces, providing a robust test of [[gaussian-initial-conditions|Gaussian initial conditions]] [Gott et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJ...319....1G/abstract). In the weakly non-linear regime (where the mass variance is $\sigma_0 < 0.1$), analytical predictions for Minkowski functionals derived from [[cosmological-perturbation-theory|Cosmological Perturbation Theory]] match numerical simulations to within a few percent for primordial non-Gaussianity parameters $|f_{\text{NL}}| < 1000$ [Hikage et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.385.1613H/abstract). In the fully non-linear regime, these statistics deviate from perturbation theory, though they remain powerful tools for disentangling the non-Gaussianity induced by late-time gravitational evolution from intrinsic primordial signatures.

The topology of the cosmic web also exhibits a strong [[configuration-dependence|Configuration dependence]] relative to the specific luminous tracers observed. Hydrodynamical simulations indicate that early-forming elliptical galaxies cluster strongly in high-density nodes, exhibiting an isolated "meatball" topology. In contrast, late-forming spiral galaxies trace out a connected, "sponge-like" topology typical of filaments and sheet margins [Gott et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...465..499G/abstract). Notably, standard analytical biasing schemes often fail to accurately capture this morphology-density relation, underscoring a persistent theoretical ambiguity in mapping the underlying dark matter distribution to the observable galaxy population.

## Dark Matter Halos: Internal Structure and Virialization

The end state of non-linear [[gravitational-instability|Gravitational instability]] for collisionless matter is a quasi-equilibrium structure known as a dark matter halo. During the collapse of an overdense region, the rapidly fluctuating gravitational potential allows particles to exchange energy in a process called violent relaxation. This mechanism enables the system to reach a virialized state governed by [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]], bypassing the need for two-body encounters and effectively erasing many details of the initial conditions.

A hallmark of these virialized structures, as consistently revealed by [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]], is the remarkable universality of their internal [[density-profiles|Density profiles]]. Regardless of their total mass or specific formation history, halos formed via [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]] in a Cold Dark Matter (CDM) cosmology adopt a highly similar mass distribution. This is classically described by the Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996IAUS..171..255N/abstract), where the logarithmic density slope transitions gradually from $-1$ in the central regions to $-3$ at large radii:
$$ \rho(r) \propto \frac{1}{(r/r_s)(1 + r/r_s)^2} $$
where $r_s$ is a characteristic scale radius. Why chaotic, clumpy hierarchical merging consistently acts as an attractor to produce this universal profile remains an active area of theoretical investigation. Recent ultra-high-resolution simulations have shown that spherically averaged profiles actually exhibit small but systematic deviations from the exact NFW form. In the mean, they are often more accurately parameterized by an [[einasto-profile|Einasto profile]], which accommodates a continuously varying logarithmic slope without converging to a fixed central power law.

Beyond the smoothed density distribution, CDM halos are richly textured with substructure. Because they assemble from the bottom up via the merging of smaller progenitors, many of these smaller halos survive the violent relaxation of the main collapse. They persist as gravitationally bound subhalos orbiting within the host's primary potential well.

However, the internal structure and substructure of simulated dark matter halos exhibit significant tensions with astrophysical observations. First, $N$-body simulations of standard CDM predict steeply rising central density "cusps," whereas observations of dwarf galaxies frequently infer flattened density "cores" (the cusp-core problem). Second, simulations predict vastly more subhalos than the observed number of dwarf satellite galaxies surrounding the Milky Way (the dwarf galaxy or missing satellites problem) [Kamionkowski et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000PhRvL..84.4525K/abstract).

The literature remains ambiguous and highly active regarding the resolution of these discrepancies. One leading paradigm suggests that standard CDM is fundamentally correct, but that baryonic physics—such as the expulsion of gas via supernovae and stellar feedback—alters the dark matter distribution, flattening central cusps and dynamically suppressing star formation in low-mass subhalos [Burkert et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322..489B/abstract). Conversely, alternative theories argue that these small-scale failures indicate a need to modify the dark matter model itself. Invoking mechanisms such as Warm Dark Matter (WDM) or interacting dark matter could intrinsically erase small-scale power and prevent the formation of overly dense cores and abundant subhalos without relying entirely on complex baryonic feedback [Hoeneisen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JMPh...14.1741H/abstract).

## Numerical Simulations and N-body Techniques

While [[cosmological-perturbation-theory|Cosmological Perturbation Theory]] provides a rigorous framework for tracking density fluctuations in the linear and [[quasi-linear-regime|Quasi-linear regime]] (where the density variance is $\sigma^2 \le 1$), analytical approximations inevitably break down as structures undergo highly non-linear gravitational collapse ($\delta \gg 1$) [Baugh et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994MNRAS.270..183B/abstract), [Juszkiewicz et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996astro.ph..2134J/abstract). To bridge the gap between [[gaussian-initial-conditions|Gaussian initial conditions]] in the early universe and the fully evolved, non-linear cosmic density field, researchers rely heavily on [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]] [Bertschinger et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ARA&A..36..599B/abstract), [Frenk et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002RSPTA.360.1277F/abstract). To further refine the accuracy of perturbation theories prior to full $N$-body collapse, researchers have developed efficient spectral methods employing Chebyshev polynomials to calculate the full dynamical evolution of gravitational-coupling kernels to all perturbative orders .

The backbone of computational structure formation is the suite of N-body simulations, which integrate the equations of motion for discrete, collisionless particles representing dark matter. Early N-body models were instrumental in distinguishing between competing dark matter paradigms. They demonstrated that neutrino-dominated Hot Dark Matter (HDM) models produced a "top-down" sequence that formed unacceptably large super-lumps before fragmenting into galaxies, which severely conflicted with observational timing [White et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986isos.book..228W/abstract), [White et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987IAUS..117..263W/abstract). Conversely, Cold Dark Matter (CDM) models successfully predicted a "bottom-up" [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]] scenario. High-resolution N-body simulations of CDM reliably reproduce the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]]—a complex topological network of dense clusters, intersecting filaments, planar sheets, and expansive voids that closely mirrors observed galaxy distributions [Weinberg et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..148...21W/abstract), [Suto et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.11575S/abstract). To fully leverage these simulations and compare them against observations from large-scale galaxy surveys like the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS), researchers employ the Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD), an empirical framework used to relate galaxies statistically to their underlying dark matter halos [Weinberg et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..283....3W/abstract). Historically, to accurately match these structures to both observed cluster abundances and CMB anisotropies, researchers also performed extensive explorations of parameter space for critical-density dark matter models, varying the spectral index, Hubble parameter, and neutrino fraction [Liddle et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.281..531L/abstract).

As computing power has increased, so has the fidelity and resolution of non-linear simulations, revealing immense substructure within individual virialized objects. However, standard N-body particle discretization has mathematical limitations, particularly when tracking species with significant thermal velocities, such as relic massive neutrinos. To address this, specialized Vlasov-Poisson simulations have been developed. Rather than sampling the fluid with discrete macro-particles, these codes directly integrate the six-dimensional collisionless [[boltzmann-equation|Boltzmann equation]] (Vlasov) equation coupled with the Poisson equation:
$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{\mathbf{p}}{m a^2} \cdot \nabla f - m \nabla \phi \cdot \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{p}} = 0 $$
where $f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{p}, t)$ is the [[phase-space-density|Phase space density]] and $\phi$ is the gravitational potential. This provides an accurate, sampling-independent representation of neutrino free-streaming and its non-linear clustering alongside cold dark matter [Yoshikawa et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904..159Y/abstract).

Despite their profound success in capturing large-scale [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]], pure dark matter N-body simulations face persistent challenges on sub-galactic scales. High-resolution runs typically predict overly dense central cusps and an overabundance of dwarf subhalos compared to observations—tensions known as the cusp-core and missing satellites problems [Kamionkowski et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000PhRvL..84.4525K/abstract), [Bartelmann et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013SSRv..177....3B/abstract). Resolving these discrepancies demands moving beyond collisionless dynamics to include complex baryonic physics, such as radiative cooling, star formation, and feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei. Furthermore, accurately capturing multi-physics phenomena, such as the amplification of magnetic fields in the highly turbulent, non-linear intra-cluster medium, remains a significant numerical hurdle for modern structure formation codes [Donnert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018SSRv..214..122D/abstract).

## Higher-Order Statistics and Non-Gaussianity

In standard cosmological models, the initial density perturbations are presumed to follow [[nearly-gaussian-initial-conditions|Nearly Gaussian initial conditions]]. For a purely Gaussian field, the statistical properties are entirely captured by the two-point correlation function or its Fourier equivalent, the [[dark-matter-power-spectrum|Dark matter power spectrum]]. However, as the universe evolves into the non-linear regime, gravitational instability inherently couples previously independent Fourier modes, generating non-trivial phase correlations [Coles et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000Natur.406..376C/abstract). This non-linear evolution transforms the initially symmetric [[phase-space-density|Phase space density]] into a highly skewed field characterized by vast underdense voids and compact, high-density peaks, requiring statistical measures beyond the power spectrum to be fully described.

The lowest-order statistic that captures this gravity-induced non-Gaussianity is the three-point correlation function, or in Fourier space, the [[matter-bispectrum|Matter bispectrum]]. The bispectrum is defined by the ensemble average of three density modes:
$$ \langle \delta(\mathbf{k}_1) \delta(\mathbf{k}_2) \delta(\mathbf{k}_3) \rangle = (2\pi)^3 \delta_D(\mathbf{k}_1 + \mathbf{k}_2 + \mathbf{k}_3) B(k_1, k_2, k_3) $$
Because the bispectrum scales roughly as the square of the power spectrum in the [[quasi-linear-regime|Quasi-linear regime]], it is often useful to define the [[reduced-bispectrum|Reduced bispectrum]], $Q(k_1, k_2, k_3)$. This normalized measure divides out the primary dependence on the power spectrum amplitude, making it highly sensitive to the underlying shape of the non-linear mode coupling. The [[configuration-dependence|Configuration dependence]] of the bispectrum provides a powerful probe of gravitational dynamics; for example, the "squeezed limit" of the bispectrum yields exact multi-species consistency relations that can test for velocity biases between baryons and dark matter [Peloso et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014JCAP...04..011P/abstract). Additionally, analyzing the anisotropy in the redshift-space power spectrum, generated by peculiar velocities during collapse, provides a powerful method to estimate the growth rate parameter $\beta$ [Cole et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994MNRAS.267..785C/abstract). Furthermore, this cosmic growth rate exhibits marked environmental dependence; non-linear structure grows measurably faster in high-density regions compared to the slow evolution within low-density voids [Achitouv et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98j3502A/abstract).

Moving beyond the three-point level, the [[four-point-correlation-function|Four-point correlation function]] and its Fourier counterpart, the [[matter-trispectrum|Matter trispectrum]], track next-to-leading-order mode-coupling behaviors. These higher-order moments are particularly sensitive to the morphology of the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]], distinguishing the connected network of filaments and sheets (such as the [[zel-dovich-pancake|Zel’dovich pancake]]) from spherically symmetric halos. 

A major observational challenge in modern cosmology is disentangling primordial non-Gaussianity (typically parameterized by $f_{\rm NL}$) from the "late-time" non-Gaussianity generated naturally by non-linear gravitational evolution, biased galaxy formation, and redshift-space distortions [Verde et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001NYASA.927...54V/abstract). Theoretical predictions for the shape and amplitude of the bispectrum are crucial for this separation. Studies have shown that while primordial non-Gaussianity influences topological measures, non-linear gravity produces competing signals of comparable amplitude at weakly non-linear scales, creating ambiguities that require precision modeling to resolve [Hikage et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.385.1613H/abstract). 

In the mildly non-linear regime (where the density variance $\sigma^2 \lesssim 1$), [[standard-perturbation-theory|Standard Perturbation Theory]] provides accurate predictions for these higher-order statistics [Gaztañaga et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..215...21G/abstract). As perturbation theory breaks down at smaller scales, the [[effective-field-theory-of-large-scale-structure|Effective Field Theory of Large Scale Structure]] (EFTofLSS) has emerged as a state-of-the-art framework to systematically compute the power spectrum and bispectrum. By utilizing appropriate counterterms and IR-resummation schemes, EFTofLSS successfully matches high-resolution N-body simulations and allows for rigorous constraints on primordial features and cosmological parameters using current redshift surveys [Colas et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...06..001C/abstract), [Tomlinson et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...08..040T/abstract), [Calderon et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250406183C/abstract).

## Baryonic Physics, Feedback, and Galaxy Assembly

While the foundational framework of non-linear structure formation is governed by the [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]] of dark matter, the visible universe is shaped by the complex gas-dynamical and radiative processes of baryons. Comprehensive theoretical treatments of this transition highlight the intricate interplay between collisionless dark matter dynamics, halo structure, and the complex dissipative processes of star formation and galaxy interactions [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). During the non-linear collapse of density perturbations, collisionless dark matter undergoes violent relaxation to form quasi-equilibrium halos with near-universal density profiles. In contrast, infalling baryonic gas is shock-heated to the virial temperature of the forming potential well, reaching temperatures of $T \sim 10^5 - 10^6 \text{ K}$ in the filaments and nodes of the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]]. If radiative cooling is efficient, the gas loses entropy and pressure support, condensing into the centers of dark matter halos to form rotationally supported disks and fuel star formation [Katz et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...391..502K/abstract). This process is often conceptualized via a simplified "inside-out" galaxy formation scenario, representing a synthesis of dark matter halo physics and gaseous angular momentum redistribution that fundamentally governs disk assembly [Kepner et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...520...59K/abstract).

Under the paradigm of [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]], the first non-linear baryonic objects are hypothesized to be metal-free Population III stars. These are predicted to form within molecular hydrogen-cooled minihalos of mass $10^5 - 10^8 M_\odot$ at redshifts $z \sim 10 - 30$ [Planelles et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015SSRv..188...93P/abstract), [El Bourakadi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251019863E/abstract). As these initial structures accrete mass and merge, they assemble into progressively larger galaxies. Current numerical models indicate that massive galaxies had already established fundamental dynamical scaling relations by $z = 4 - 6$ [Oñorbe et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732L..32O/abstract). Galaxy mergers play a critical role in this hierarchical assembly, triggering intense starbursts, feeding central supermassive black holes, and dynamically transforming rotation-dominated disks into pressure-supported elliptical remnants [Cox et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..399..284C/abstract).

However, a purely dissipational model of gravity and cooling over-predicts the cosmic star formation rate and produces excessively massive central galaxies. To regulate star formation and match observed galaxy mass functions, non-gravitational heating mechanisms—collectively known as feedback—are strictly required. Stellar feedback, driven by supernovae and stellar winds, heats and expels gas from shallow potential wells. This mechanism has been heavily investigated as a potential astrophysical solution to the small-scale tensions of standard cold dark matter cosmology, such as the cusp-core and missing satellites problems. Rapid, bursty outflows can alter the central gravitational potential, systematically flattening the "cuspy" dark matter density profiles predicted by dark-matter-only models into the flatter cores observed in dwarf galaxies [Burkert et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322..489B/abstract), [Primack et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150502821P/abstract). At the high-mass end, active galactic nuclei (AGN) provide immense energy injection that prevents catastrophic cooling flows and regulates star formation in massive elliptical galaxies and clusters. Furthermore, non-thermal components play a substantial role; for instance, cosmic ray acceleration at large-scale shocks can contribute up to several tenths of the total pressure inside galaxy clusters, dynamically influencing cluster evolution [Miniati et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..215...27M/abstract).

Modeling these highly non-linear, multi-scale processes is the primary domain of modern [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]]. While these simulations successfully reproduce the broad statistical properties of luminous matter, the literature reflects ongoing tensions regarding the exact interplay of feedback mechanisms. Although cluster properties on intermediate scales conform well to expected gravity-driven self-similar scaling relations, complex feedback causes significant deviations from self-similarity in the dense core regions [Planelles et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015SSRv..188...93P/abstract). Current state-of-the-art simulations still struggle to accurately reproduce the observed internal structure of cluster cores, indicating that contemporary phenomenological models of AGN and stellar feedback remain incomplete [Bartelmann et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013SSRv..177....3B/abstract), [Planelles et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015SSRv..188...93P/abstract).

## The Influence of Neutrinos and Non-Standard Dark Energy

While standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology successfully reproduces large-scale structure clustering via [[linear-theory|Linear theory]], the non-linear regime serves as a crucial testing ground for non-standard extensions to the model. The inclusion of massive neutrinos, dynamical dark energy, or modifications to general relativity leaves distinct structural imprints on sub-megaparsec scales that can break degeneracies present in cosmic microwave background (CMB) and [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] data.

The free-streaming of massive neutrinos suppresses the growth of structure on scales smaller than their free-streaming length. In the highly non-linear regime, modeling this suppression requires treating neutrinos beyond a simple homogeneous background. Direct integration of the six-dimensional Vlasov-Poisson equations alongside standard $N$-body methods reveals that massive neutrinos exhibit coherent streaming motions and local phase space variations; for instance, the effective neutrino "temperature" around massive galaxy clusters can vary by several percent relative to the cosmic mean [Yoshikawa et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904..159Y/abstract). Historically, before the widespread adoption of cold dark matter, mechanisms such as double inflation were also proposed to reconcile hot dark matter models with the observed large-scale voids and peculiar velocities by generating the necessary extra power on large scales [Turner et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJ...323..423T/abstract). Today, dedicated simulation suites, such as [[massivenus|MassiveNuS]], are frequently employed to calibrate the precise impact of neutrino mass bounds on the non-linear [[dark-matter-power-spectrum|Dark matter power spectrum]] and halo mass function. 

Similarly, dynamical dark energy and modified gravity models alter the non-linear collapse dynamics of dark matter halos. Scalar-field-coupled dark matter ($\phi$CDM) and inhomogeneous quintessence models can yield an expansion history that is virtually indistinguishable from standard $\Lambda$CDM, yet they demonstrably modify the abundance of virialized halos at small scales [Zhao et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...712L.179Z/abstract). Inhomogeneous dark energy, where the scalar field collapses alongside dark matter, alters the redshift of collapse and shifts the linearly extrapolated critical overdensity threshold [Nunes et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.368..751N/abstract). 

To quantify the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] of perturbations in these models, cosmologists often utilize the [[growth-index|Growth index]] $\alpha$, defined via $f \simeq \Omega_m^\alpha$. In variable dark energy models, increasing the dark energy contribution at early times decreases the growth index and suppresses the overall density contrast $\delta$ [Bidgoli et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006IJMPD..15.1455B/abstract). Modified gravity models involving Yukawa-type fifth forces or energy-momentum-squared gravity (EMSG) also predictably alter the abundance of massive clusters. Weaker large-scale gravity delays collapse, reducing the number of high-mass halos while leaving the formation histories of low-mass objects largely unperturbed [Martino et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvD..79h4013M/abstract). In the case of EMSG, theoretical predictions show a decrease in galaxy cluster number counts compared to $\Lambda$CDM, though the literature notes that these models exhibit unphysical instabilities for certain negative parameter ranges [Farsi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.108b3524F/abstract).

Finally, alternatives to collisionless cold dark matter, such as interacting dark matter (IDM) models coupled to Standard Model particles, heavily suppress small-scale structure due to collisional damping in the early universe. Tracking the late-time, non-linear evolution of these modified primordial power spectra relies heavily on perturbative frameworks. The [[effective-field-theory-of-large-scale-structure|Effective Field Theory of Large Scale Structure]] (EFTofLSS) has been successfully adapted to replicate the redshift-space halo power spectrum for IDM, providing a rigorous method to forecast and constrain non-standard dark matter interactions using next-generation spectroscopic surveys [Mosbech et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008163M/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite the successes of the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm on large scales, significant theoretical and observational tensions remain regarding [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]]. The classic "cusp-core" and "missing satellites" problems highlight discrepancies between the steep central density profiles predicted by dark-matter-only simulations and the flatter profiles observed in dwarf galaxies [Kamionkowski et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000PhRvL..84.4525K/abstract), [Primack et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150502821P/abstract). The literature remains sharply divided on the resolution of these anomalies. One camp argues that complex baryonic feedback—such as energy injection from supernovae and stellar winds—can redistribute dark matter and sufficiently flatten central cores [Burkert et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322..489B/abstract), [Primack et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150502821P/abstract). Alternatively, these small-scale failures may necessitate modifications to the dark matter sector itself, such as Warm Dark Matter (WDM) or interacting dark matter (IDM), which naturally suppress small-scale power [Kamionkowski et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000PhRvL..84.4525K/abstract), [Mosbech et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008163M/abstract). Furthermore, a fundamental theoretical question remains open: why does the chaotic, highly asymmetric process of [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]] so consistently yield the near-universal NFW or [[einasto-profile|Einasto profile]]s seen in simulated halos, regardless of the initial conditions [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996IAUS..171..255N/abstract)? Adding to these challenges, recent studies point to potential anomalies in standard redshift models, suggesting an unexpected burst of structure formation activity occurring locally between redshifts $z=0$ and $z=0.4$ that resists simple explanation within standard $\Lambda$CDM [Nyagisera et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Astro...3...43N/abstract).

Modeling non-gravitational physics introduces severe numerical challenges. While self-similar scaling describes intermediate cluster scales well, simulations continually struggle to reproduce the thermodynamic structure of cluster cores [Bartelmann et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013SSRv..177....3B/abstract), [Planelles et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015SSRv..188...93P/abstract). This indicates that current sub-grid feedback models are likely incomplete. Future high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations must better capture microphysical processes, such as magnetic field amplification in the intracluster medium [Donnert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018SSRv..214..122D/abstract) and the dynamical impact of cosmic rays. Recent studies suggest that cosmic rays may provide a significant fraction of the total pressure support inside galaxy clusters—up to several tenths of the total pressure—which could significantly alter the thermodynamic evolution of these structures [Miniati et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..215...27M/abstract).

In the era of precision cosmology, extracting robust cosmological parameters requires modeling non-linearities with unprecedented accuracy. While N-body simulations provide brute-force numerical solutions, analytical methods have seen a renaissance through the [[effective-field-theory-of-large-scale-structure|Effective Field Theory of Large Scale Structure]] (EFTofLSS). By rigorously integrating out short-scale non-linearities to construct effective fluid equations, EFTofLSS has proven powerful in predicting the [[dark-matter-power-spectrum|Dark matter power spectrum]] and [[matter-bispectrum|Matter bispectrum]] into the mildly non-linear regime [Colas et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...06..001C/abstract), [Calderon et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250406183C/abstract). A major open challenge in this domain is disentangling primordial non-Gaussianity from the late-time non-Gaussianity induced by non-linear gravitational evolution and biased tracer relationships [Verde et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001NYASA.927...54V/abstract), [Hikage et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.385.1613H/abstract). 

Finally, next-generation observational surveys will provide stringent tests of non-linear structure formation. One highly anticipated target is the [[rees-sciama-effect|Rees-Sciama effect]]—the secondary cosmic microwave background (CMB) temperature anisotropies induced by the time-varying gravitational potentials of evolving non-linear structures. Recent forecasts suggest that by cross-correlating optimally-weighted high-redshift galaxy catalogs (e.g., from the Rubin Observatory) with high-resolution CMB maps, the Rees-Sciama effect could be detected with a signal-to-noise ratio of $6 - 8$ at multipoles up to $\ell_{\text{max}} = 6000$ [Ferraro et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220510332F/abstract). However, achieving this detection will require exquisite control over foreground-induced biases, representing a critical frontier for future cosmological experiments.
