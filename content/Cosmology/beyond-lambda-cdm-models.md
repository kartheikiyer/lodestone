The [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] currently stands as the standard cosmological paradigm, successfully describing the large-scale structure and expansion history of the universe through a combination of a static cosmological constant ($\Lambda$) and collisionless cold dark matter (CDM) [Davis et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992Natur.356..489D/abstract), [Liddle et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PhR...231....1L/abstract). However, the advent of precision cosmology has exposed persistent discrepancies between early-universe predictions and late-universe observations, motivating the **Beyond-$\Lambda$CDM** paradigm. This umbrella term encompasses a diverse array of theoretical extensions designed to resolve macroscopic anomalies—most notably the Hubble ($H_0$) and amplitude of structure growth ($S_8$) tensions—as well as the sub-galactic "small-scale crisis." 

To address these observational challenges, Beyond-$\Lambda$CDM models introduce new degrees of freedom or modify fundamental physical laws. Proposed solutions typically invoke [[alternative-dark-matter-models|Alternative dark matter models]], [[dark-energy-models|Dark energy models]] with time-varying equations of state, [[modified-gravity-theories|Modified Gravity Theories]], or the [[interaction-dark-energy-model|Interaction Dark Energy model]] framework. A central finding in the current literature is the difficulty of formulating a single consensus model: while specific extensions can successfully resolve isolated tensions, they frequently exacerbate other discrepancies or are tightly restricted by precision Cosmic Microwave Background and large-scale structure data.

## Cosmological Tensions and Theoretical Motivations

The primary drivers for extending the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] are macroscopic tensions, most notably the $H_0$ discrepancy. This tension, which currently stands at a statistical significance of $4-6\sigma$, arises between the value of the Hubble constant inferred from early-universe CMB probes (typically $H_0 \approx 67$ km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$) and late-universe local distance ladder measurements (yielding $H_0 \gtrsim 73$ km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$) [Schöneberg et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhR...984....1S/abstract), [Kable et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240311916K/abstract). Concurrently, the $S_8$ tension reflects a discrepancy in the amplitude of linear matter fluctuations, parameterized by $\sigma_8$. Weak lensing and redshift-space distortion measurements consistently favor a lower growth rate of cosmic structure than predicted by Planck CMB data under the $\Lambda$CDM framework [Basilakos et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161000160B/abstract), [Akarsu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.108b3513A/abstract).

Furthermore, on sub-galactic scales ($\lesssim 1$ Mpc), standard $\Lambda$CDM faces the long-standing "small-scale crisis." High-resolution $N$-body simulations predict dense, cusp-like dark matter density profiles and a high abundance of satellite subhalos. Conversely, observations of dwarf galaxies frequently indicate shallow central density cores and a relative scarcity of satellite companions—phenomena known as the cusp-core and missing satellites problems, respectively [Moore et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.370..629M/abstract), [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract), [de Martino et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Univ....6..107D/abstract). 

Despite extensive theoretical efforts to alleviate these tensions, the literature currently lacks a single consensus model. While specific Beyond-$\Lambda$CDM extensions can successfully resolve isolated tensions (e.g., dynamical dark energy shifting inferred $H_0$ to higher values), they frequently exacerbate other discrepancies, such as degrading fits to the $S_8$ parameter or the early integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect [Vagnozzi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104f3524V/abstract). Consequently, finding a comprehensive theoretical framework that simultaneously reconciles both large-scale cosmological tensions and small-scale structural crises remains a central objective of modern astrophysics.

## Historical Evolution: From CDM to Modified Frameworks

In the early 1980s, purely baryonic models of structure formation faced a fundamental crisis. Because baryonic density perturbations could not grow prior to the epoch of decoupling due to radiation pressure and Silk damping, these models required cosmic microwave background (CMB) temperature anisotropies on the order of $10^{-3}$ to successfully form galaxies. As observational upper limits on CMB anisotropies became increasingly stringent, this requirement proved fatal. The paradigm consequently shifted toward non-baryonic dark matter. While Hot Dark Matter (HDM)—such as light massive neutrinos—was initially considered, its relativistic free-streaming erased density fluctuations on small scales, preventing the bottom-up formation of galaxies. This cemented the dominance of Cold Dark Matter (CDM), a framework positing a non-relativistic, collisionless fluid that successfully permitted density perturbations to grow post matter-radiation equality while circumventing Silk damping. 

Despite its tremendous success in describing large-scale structure, standard CDM models normalized to early COBE data were soon found to overproduce small-scale clustering. Throughout the 1990s, several transitional variants were proposed to reconcile the theory with the observed distribution of galaxies. A prominent early extension was the [[c-hdm-model|C+HDM model]] (Cold+Hot Dark Matter), which introduced a massive neutrino fraction (e.g., $\Omega_\nu \sim 0.3$) to partially suppress small-scale power via free-streaming while preserving the large-scale success of CDM [Ma et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...471...13M/abstract), [Primack et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996NuPhS..51...30P/abstract). Concurrently, "tilted" CDM models assuming a primordial scalar spectral index of $n \simeq 0.9$ were shown to reduce central halo densities; however, they still require baryonic feedback mechanisms, such as photoionization, to successfully suppress the overabundance of dwarf satellite galaxies [Ma et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...471...13M/abstract), [Bullock et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.11005B/abstract).

As high-resolution N-body simulations advanced, however, a suite of persistent sub-galactic discrepancies solidified against the collisionless [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]]. Simulations repeatedly predicted steeply rising "cuspy" inner density profiles for dark matter halos and a vast overabundance of satellite dwarf galaxies, contradicting the flat "cored" profiles and lower satellite counts observed in the Local Group [Moore et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.370..629M/abstract), [Moore et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...535L..21M/abstract), [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract). Furthermore, the "too-big-to-fail" problem highlighted that the most massive predicted subhalos, which should readily form stars, were conspicuously absent from local observational surveys. 

While complex baryonic feedback mechanisms can flatten dark matter cores and suppress star formation in low-mass halos, an active debate remains regarding whether standard astrophysics alone can entirely resolve the crisis across all mass scales [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract), [Del Popolo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98f3517D/abstract). Consequently, the historical development of the field has largely pivoted from strictly collisionless, non-interacting CDM to models featuring a rich [[dark-sector|Dark sector]]. To suppress small-scale structure, modern beyond-CDM frameworks frequently invoke [[warm-dark-matter|Warm dark matter]] [Jing et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MPLA...16.1795J/abstract), self-interacting dark matter (SIDM) with velocity-dependent cross-sections that physically thermalize and core out halo centers [Tulin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhR...730....1T/abstract), or fuzzy dark matter characterized by an ultra-light [[dark-matter-mass|Dark matter mass]] ($m \sim 10^{-22}\text{ eV}$) that resists galactic-scale collapse via quantum wave pressure [Schive et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014NatPh..10..496S/abstract), [Bernal et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3135B/abstract). This evolution highlights a distinct paradigm shift from the minimal cold particle assumptions of the 1980s toward highly generalized interacting, modified, and wave-like dark matter extensions.

## Alternative Dark Matter Candidates

To resolve the "small-scale crisis," a variety of [[alternative-dark-matter-models|Alternative dark matter models]] relax the assumption of a cold, collisionless fluid to naturally suppress small-scale clustering.

**[[warm-dark-matter|Warm dark matter]] (WDM) and Mixed Dark Matter**
WDM scenarios posit particles with non-negligible primordial velocity dispersions, such as keV-scale sterile neutrinos. Free-streaming of these particles in the early Universe suppresses the matter power spectrum on small scales, thereby reducing the predicted abundance of low-mass satellite halos and lowering their central densities. However, WDM is subject to stringent observational limits from the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest [Strigari et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007PhRvD..75f1303S/abstract). Furthermore, constraints derived from the faint extended stellar components of galaxies force the allowed WDM particle mass to be so high that its macroscopic predictions become nearly indistinguishable from cold dark matter, with differences in satellite abundance comparable to the system-to-system variance expected in standard cosmology [Power et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..321..105P/abstract). Differences in halo assembly times between cold and warm dark matter are likely confined to present-day dwarf galaxies, limiting the ability of high-redshift observations to definitively differentiate between the two paradigms [Lovell et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.3029L/abstract). A related extension is mixed dark matter (MDM), which comprises both cold and warm phases; recent precise emulators have been developed to predict the exact power spectrum suppression for varied warm dark matter fractions [Parimbelli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...12..044P/abstract).

**Self-Interacting Dark Matter (SIDM)**
SIDM abandons the collisionless assumption by introducing a non-gravitational self-scattering cross-section. In the dense central regions of halos, these collisions allow heat transfer from the hotter outer regions to the inner halo, thermalizing the core and naturally transforming a steep density cusp into a flattened isothermal core. As a diagnostic tool, the 3D shapes of dark matter halos help differentiate these models; SIDM typically produces significantly rounder and more isotropic halo cores compared to the more triaxial profiles found in standard CDM or WDM simulations [Giocoli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215856G/abstract). While SIDM can successfully address small-scale structural issues, there is an inherent tension in the literature: the constant interaction cross-section required to fix dwarf galaxy cores tends to violate observational constraints on the shapes and densities of massive galaxy clusters. This conflict is primarily resolved by invoking velocity-dependent cross-sections, which suppress scattering in the high-velocity-dispersion environments of clusters while remaining active in low-mass dwarfs [Tulin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhR...730....1T/abstract). Among explicit realizations, specific models proposing a leptonic scalar particle with a light dilepton mediator have been shown to successfully resolve these small-scale discrepancies while strictly satisfying thermal relic density and indirect detection constraints [Kao et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.103e5021K/abstract). However, recent SIDM simulations within the TangoSIDM project suggest that velocity-dependent interactions can lead to steeper dark matter density profiles in baryon-dominated galaxies like the Milky Way, potentially creating tension with the observed Tully-Fisher relation [Correa et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240309186C/abstract). Conversely, extreme high-density environments may provide unique signatures; for instance, evidence from the strong lens system SDSS J0946+1006 reveals an overconcentrated subhalo with a steep density profile, which may indicate gravothermal collapse driven by self-interacting dark matter [Enzi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108565E/abstract).

**Fuzzy Dark Matter (FDM)**
The [[wave-dark-matter-model|Wave dark matter model]] (also known as fuzzy dark matter or Bose-Einstein Condensate Dark Matter) assumes dark matter is composed of ultra-light scalar bosons with masses on the order of $m \sim 10^{-22}$ eV. At this mass scale, the particle's de Broglie wavelength spans astrophysical distances ($\sim 1$ kpc), leading to macroscopic quantum wave interference. The Heisenberg uncertainty principle provides a quantum pressure that counters gravity below the Jeans scale. Consequently, FDM forms stable solitonic cores at the centers of galaxies and produces an extended halo of fluctuating density granules, strongly suppressing the formation of low-mass subhalos [Schive et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014NatPh..10..496S/abstract). However, recent large-volume spectral simulations incorporating full non-linear wave dynamics show that this 'quantum pressure' damping can be countered by order-unity density fluctuations at certain scales [May et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.2603M/abstract). Moreover, dynamical differences emerge in non-linear interactions; head-on subhalo collision simulations show that Fuzzy Dark Matter exhibits significantly higher kinetic energy dissipation through gravitational cooling compared to standard CDM [Koo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250700607K/abstract). Due to the initial suppression of small-scale power, typical FDM models predict a substantial delay in the onset of structure formation, with the first galaxies appearing at redshifts $z \lesssim 13$ [Schive et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014NatPh..10..496S/abstract).

**Phenomenological and Composite Dark Matter**
Beyond standard fundamental particles, some frameworks propose phenomenological or composite structures. [[modified-dark-matter|Modified Dark Matter]] (MDM) serves as a phenomenological model where dark matter mass profiles are intrinsically linked to the cosmological constant, providing consistent fits across both galactic and cluster scales [Edmonds et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180100160E/abstract). Another novel proposal invokes composite Q-balls formed in the early Universe as a unified physical mechanism that can mimic CDM on cosmological scales while exhibiting MOND-like behavior at galactic scales [Mandal et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025EPJC...85..178M/abstract). An additional layer of complexity in evaluating any dark matter candidates involves their structural scaling relations. For instance, studies have identified a flattening of the dark matter halo concentration-mass relation toward low halo masses; this flattening leads to more modest annihilation flux boosts than previously assumed, directly impacting the constraints placed on microphysical models through indirect detection [Sánchez-Conde et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442.2271S/abstract).

**Baryonic Feedback vs. Exotic Microphysics**
It remains actively debated whether the small-scale crisis genuinely necessitates new [[dark-matter-candidates-and-models|Dark Matter Candidates and Models]] with novel microphysics. A major source of ambiguity is that standard astrophysical processes, such as supernova-driven winds and dynamical friction, can drastically alter the dark matter distribution. Baryonic feedback can expel gas from potential wells, gravitationally flattening dark matter cusps into cores and suppressing star formation in the lowest-mass halos [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract), [Del Popolo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98f3517D/abstract). Consequently, distinguishing the signatures of alternative dark matter candidates from those of complex baryonic physics remains a critical open challenge in contemporary astrophysics [Brooks et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AnP...526..294B/abstract), [Zentner et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...07..031Z/abstract).

## Dynamical and Early Dark Energy

While the standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] assumes a constant vacuum energy density with an equation of state $w = -1$, the persistence of the $H_0$ and $S_8$ cosmological tensions has motivated the exploration of models where dark energy is not constant. Dynamical Dark Energy (DDE) and Early Dark Energy (EDE) paradigms introduce time-varying energy components designed to alter the cosmic expansion history at specific epochs.

**Late-Time Dynamical Dark Energy**
Late-time dynamical models are typically phenomenologized through the [[wcdm-extension|wCDM extension]], which parametrizes the dark energy equation of state as $w(a) = w_0 + w_a(1-a)$. Physically, these dynamics are often realized via [[quintessence-models|Quintessence models]], wherein a slowly rolling scalar field governs the accelerated expansion [Dinda et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..97h3506D/abstract). Within these frameworks, specific 'hilltop quintessence' scenarios have been proposed that are motivated by and satisfy theoretical constraints from quantum gravity conjectures [Bhattacharya et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241021243B/abstract).

The observational literature regarding DDE is currently complex. Earlier combinations of Planck Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) data and Dark Energy Survey (DES) clustering measurements found no significant evidence for a temporal variation in the equation of state, remaining tightly consistent with $w = -1$ [Abbott et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvD..99l3505A/abstract), [Abbott et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.107h3504A/abstract). However, recent combinations utilizing DESI DR2 Baryon Acoustic Oscillation (BAO) and Lyman-$\alpha$ forest data, alongside updated Type Ia supernovae compilations, have revealed a $2.3\sigma$ to $4.4\sigma$ statistical preference for DDE over a static cosmological constant in certain dataset combinations [Ishak et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722856I/abstract). Conversely, other analyses using slightly different pipeline compilations find a lower, statistically inconclusive preference ranging from $0.24\sigma$ to $2.60\sigma$ [Capozziello et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251021976C/abstract). For the higher-significance fits, results generally favor an evolving state where $w_0 > -1$ and $w_a < 0$. It remains actively debated whether this tension points to a genuinely dynamic equation of state, or if the data are better explained by an [[interaction-dark-energy-model|Interaction Dark Energy model]] featuring momentum exchange between the dark sectors [Li et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107361L/abstract). 

Other radical late-time propositions include a sign-switching cosmological constant. In these models, the Universe transitions from an anti-de Sitter to a de Sitter vacuum at $z \sim 2$, which phenomenological studies suggest can simultaneously relax the $H_0$, $S_8$, and supernova absolute magnitude ($M_B$) tensions [Akarsu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104l3512A/abstract), [Akarsu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.108b3513A/abstract). Another unconventional proposal involves unifying dark energy and dark matter through a negative mass fluid with continuous matter creation within a modified framework [Farnes et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...620A..92F/abstract). In a completely different approach, the Asymptotically Safe cosmology explains accelerated expansion via infrared quantum gravity modifications without requiring extra energy scales entirely [Anagnostopoulos et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...02..053A/abstract). Furthermore, the $\eta$CDM framework proposes that accelerated expansion is an emergent phenomenon resulting from the stochastic gravitational evolution of large-scale patches during structure formation [Lapi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205823L/abstract).

**Early Dark Energy**
Rather than modifying late-time acceleration, Early Dark Energy targets the pre-recombination era. By introducing a scalar field or non-linear electrodynamic component that acts briefly before decaying, EDE reduces the physical size of the sound horizon at last scattering [Benaoum et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230705917B/abstract). This reduction can naturally raise the inferred Hubble parameter to $H_0 > 72 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$, bridging the gap with local distance ladder measurements while preserving the excellent fit to primary CMB anisotropies [Kable et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240311916K/abstract).

Despite these successes, EDE models face severe theoretical and observational challenges. First, to maintain the fit to the CMB, EDE typically requires an increase in the cold dark matter density ($\omega_c$), which paradoxically exacerbates the $S_8$ tension by predicting excess small-scale structure growth at late times. Second, the presence of a dynamically significant energy component near recombination alters the decay of early gravitational potentials. This leaves a distinct imprint on the early integrated Sachs-Wolfe (eISW) effect. Without extreme fine-tuning, typical EDE models overpredict the eISW amplitude by $\approx 20\%$ compared to the baseline $\Lambda$CDM predictions, generating tension with high-precision CMB polarization data [Vagnozzi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104f3524V/abstract).

## Modified Gravity and Extensions to General Relativity

[[modified-gravity-theories|Modified Gravity Theories]] represent a class of [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]] that alter the laws of gravity on galactic or cosmological scales to account for the observed cosmic acceleration and structure formation without invoking traditional dark matter or dark energy components.

On sub-galactic and galactic scales, the most prominent framework is Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), which posits that gravity departs from the Newtonian inverse-square law at accelerations below a critical threshold:
$$a_0 \simeq 1.2 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m s}^{-2}$$
MOND naturally reproduces several empirical galactic scaling relations, such as the baryonic Tully-Fisher relation and the radial acceleration relation, without the need for an invisible dark matter halo [Cesare et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Univ....9...56C/abstract), [Wu et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446..330W/abstract). However, the literature reveals a stark conflict when extrapolating these models to larger scales. MOND-inspired theories face severe challenges when extrapolated to larger scales. For instance, relativistic formulations like $\Lambda$MOND predict overly strong Baryon Acoustic Oscillation (BAO) signals on the matter power spectrum that contradict cosmological observations [Zeng et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170100690Z/abstract), and similarly struggle with the dynamics of massive galaxy clusters [Binney et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.10219B/abstract). Furthermore, local Milky Way vertical dynamics and rotation curve data present significant challenges to theories attempting to emulate [[non-newtonian-gravity|Non-Newtonian gravity]] phenomenology. Recent analyses show that interacting models like Superfluid Dark Matter, which attempt to emulate modified gravity phenomenology via an additional fundamental force on baryons, over-predict vertical accelerations in the Milky Way compared to standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] predictions, leading to a strong statistical preference for standard collisionless dark matter profiles over these force modifications [Lisanti et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PDU....3901140L/abstract).

On cosmological scales, extensions to General Relativity (GR) attempt to mimic the background expansion history of the standard model while predicting distinct signatures in the growth of large-scale structure. A common extension is $f(R)$ gravity, which replaces the Ricci scalar $R$ in the Einstein-Hilbert action with a generic function $f(R)$. While $f(R)$ models can be tuned to reproduce cosmic acceleration, they frequently require non-linear screening mechanisms to suppress fifth forces and satisfy stringent Solar System tests. N-body simulations indicate that when incorporating [[effective-dark-matter|Effective dark matter]] density fields, $f(R)$ models produce dynamical halo properties that closely mimic those of the standard cold dark matter paradigm [He et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvL.115g1306H/abstract). Additionally, Hořava gravity has been evaluated within the effective field theory formalism; while it introduces modifications that impact the Hubble rate, the parameter space is subject to tight constraints from primary CMB and large-scale structure data [Frusciante et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PDU....13....7F/abstract).

Another extension to General Relativity is energy-momentum-squared gravity (EMSG), which alters cosmological evolution and notably predicts a decrease in galaxy cluster number counts compared to standard cosmology [Farsi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.108b3524F/abstract). Similarly, theories such as Scalar-Tensor-Vector Gravity (STVG-MOG) achieve an observational equivalence with standard $\Lambda$CDM for isotropic probes. However, this equivalence breaks down in large-scale anisotropic signals, offering potential future diagnostic tests using observables like radio-galaxy dipoles [Moffat et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260122207M/abstract). Furthermore, $f(Q)$ gravity models, which utilize metric-incompatible modifications of General Relativity, have been shown to mitigate the $S_8$ discrepancy on linear scales but struggle to fully resolve the Hubble tension [Sakr et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240503627S/abstract).

To specifically address cosmological tensions, some models propose a "cosmic glitch" in gravity, positing a sudden difference in the gravitational constant between super-horizon and sub-horizon scales, which can potentially ease both the Hubble and large-scale clustering tensions, though at the expense of spoiling fits to the baryonic acoustic oscillation (BAO) scale in galaxy surveys [Wen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...03..045W/abstract). Another approach involves [[non-local-theories|Non-local theories]] of gravity, which introduce corrections to the gravitational action of the form:
$$\delta S \sim m^2 R \Box^{-2} R$$
These models are quantum-inspired and can produce geometrically driven accelerated expansion without dark energy [Bouchè et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230101503B/abstract), [Dirian et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PhRvD..96h3513D/abstract). Notably, non-local gravity can alleviate both the $H_0$ and $\sigma_8$ tensions present in standard cosmology. However, resolving these tensions fully often requires an extended baseline that assumes a non-vanishing absolute neutrino mass of $\sum m_\nu \approx 0.21 \text{ eV}$ to correct for late-time phantom behavior [Dirian et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PhRvD..96h3513D/abstract). Like $f(R)$ gravity, these non-local models also rely on appropriate screening mechanisms to remain viable [Bouchè et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230101503B/abstract).

Finally, massive gravity and bimetric theories grant a non-zero mass to the graviton, altering the evolution of Bardeen potentials and subsequently affecting the growth of cosmic structures. The minimal theory of massive gravity contains a self-accelerating branch with linear perturbation dynamics identical to GR, as well as a normal branch that fits integrated Sachs-Wolfe-galaxy cross-correlation data over a broad parameter space [Bolis et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98b4010B/abstract). For extended frameworks like quasidilaton massive gravity, phenomenological tests utilizing galaxy rotation curves and Solar System constraints place a strict upper bound on the graviton mass of:
$$m \le 10^{-31} \text{ eV}$$
rendering direct detection well out of reach for current and near-future observatories [Martinovic et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvD.100l4016M/abstract).

Overall, while [[dark-energy-and-modified-gravity|Dark Energy and Modified Gravity]] frameworks present mathematically elegant alternatives to dark components, current consensus suggests that these models struggle to simultaneously fit precision CMB, cluster lensing, and local dynamic data with the same efficacy as the standard model.

## Dark Sector Interactions and Couplings

While the standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] assumes that the constituents of the [[dark-sector|Dark sector]] interact exclusively via gravity, extended frameworks explore the possibility of non-gravitational scattering or decay processes. These scenarios introduce an *energy-momentum coupling* between dark matter (DM), dark energy (DE), and occasionally radiation. In the [[interaction-dark-energy-model|Interaction Dark Energy model]] (IDE), the energy transfer rate $Q$ is typically parameterized as being proportional to the Hubble parameter and the energy density of the dark components, taking forms such as $Q = 3 \beta H \rho_{c}$ or $Q = \gamma \rho_{c}^{\alpha}\rho_{DE}^{\beta}$ [Li et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..93b3002L/abstract), [Cheng et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.102d3517C/abstract). Recent high-precision measurements of the baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) scale by DESI have shown robust $3\sigma$ to $5\sigma$ preference for non-vanishing DM-DE interactions, matching or exceeding the statistical performance of purely dynamical dark energy parameterizations [Li et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107361L/abstract).

Dark sector couplings imprint unique signatures on the matter power spectrum by modifying both the background expansion history and the evolution of linear matter density perturbations, $\delta_{m}$. Specifically, an exchange of energy and momentum alters the linear growth rate of structure, $f(z)\sigma_8(z)$. IDE models frequently predict a lower growth rate at low redshifts ($z < 1$) compared to standard $\Lambda$CDM predictions. This suppression can help alleviate established tensions with Redshift-Space Distortion (RSD) measurements from optical galaxy surveys [Cheng et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.102d3517C/abstract). Furthermore, interactions can modify the integrated Sachs-Wolfe (ISW) effect, as non-standard background evolution can compensate for altered high-redshift growth rates [Clemson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvD..85d3007C/abstract). Detailed one-loop corrections to the matter power spectrum using the effective field theory of large-scale structure have recently been developed to constrain these interactions on mildly non-linear scales [Silva et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251211678S/abstract).

Beyond DM-DE couplings, interactions between DM and dark radiation (DR) introduce a characteristic break in the matter power spectrum. This suppresses power for modes that enter the horizon before the DM-DR interaction decouples, offering a potential joint resolution to both the $H_0$ and $S_8$ cosmological tensions [Bagherian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240517554B/abstract). Beyond interactions explicitly with dark radiation, velocity-dependent dark matter-baryon scattering has also been shown to successfully alleviate the $S_8$ tension between the CMB and large-scale structure data by further suppressing linear matter fluctuations [He et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202636H/abstract). Another interacting framework involves [[decaying-dark-matter|Decaying dark matter]] (DCDM), where standard cold dark matter decays into dark radiation and warm dark matter over cosmic time. Recent effective field theory (EFT) analyses of BOSS data have significantly improved constraints on the CDM lifetime within these decaying models [Simon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106b3516S/abstract). Additionally, dark sectors may feature mass thresholds around $m \sim \mathcal{O}(0.1-10)\text{ eV}$. When relativistic species annihilate or become non-relativistic near the epoch of recombination, the transition can relax stringent cosmic microwave background (CMB) bounds on the effective number of relativistic species, $N_{\rm eff}$, allowing for a higher inferred Hubble expansion rate without violating CMB constraints [Allali et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.108b3527A/abstract).

A significant observational challenge in verifying dark sector interactions is their degeneracy with both standard astrophysical processes and the free-streaming effects of massive neutrinos. The suppression of small-scale clustering induced by IDE or DM-DR scattering mimics the damping caused by a non-zero neutrino mass. Analyses of RSD and weak lensing data exploring extended models have occasionally favored elevated neutrino masses—such as $\sum m_\nu \simeq 0.45\text{ eV}$ [Ruiz et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..91f3009R/abstract) or $\sum m_\nu = 0.22 \pm 0.06\text{ eV}$ [Chudaykin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220303666C/abstract)—to explain the lack of late-time structural growth. Consequently, disentangling a true energy-momentum coupling from neutrino free-streaming remains analytically difficult. Furthermore, the literature is currently conflicted on whether the small-scale suppression of dark matter halos requires exotic dark sector microphysics at all; standard baryonic feedback mechanisms, such as supernova-driven outflows and dynamical friction, can independently flatten central halo densities and suppress small-scale power [Del Popolo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98f3517D/abstract). Finally, while DM-DR interactions remain a theoretically attractive solution to the $H_0$ tension, high-resolution Lyman-$\alpha$ forest data currently present stringent constraints that significantly limit the viability of the simplest interacting radiation models [Bagherian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240517554B/abstract).

## High-Redshift Observations and the Early Universe

The standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] makes specific predictions regarding the hierarchical assembly of large-scale structure, dictating that the earliest galaxies should be relatively low-mass and morphologically unevolved. However, recent deep-space observations from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have revealed a significant excess of UV-bright, massive, and morphologically mature galaxies at high redshifts ($z > 10$) [Shen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919427S/abstract), [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.3385G/abstract). The discovery of these "impossible" early galaxies has generated substantial tension within the field, prompting investigations into whether early structure formation requires beyond-$\Lambda$CDM physics.

To reconcile these observations, some researchers have proposed modifications to the pre-recombination expansion history. Certain [[dark-energy-models|Dark energy models]], such as Early Dark Energy (EDE), can accelerate early structure formation. Cosmological hydrodynamic simulations incorporating EDE demonstrate an increased predicted abundance of massive galaxies and stellar disks at $z \simeq 6-7$ that aligns excellently with JWST measurements, while converging safely to $\Lambda$CDM predictions at lower redshifts [Shen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919427S/abstract). Other phenomenological approaches suggest models with covarying coupling constants combined with tired light (CCC+TL), which effectively stretch the inferred age of the Universe to $\sim 26.7$ Gyr, providing the necessary cosmic time to form massive galaxies at $z \sim 15$ without requiring super-Eddington accretion rates [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.3385G/abstract). Furthermore, [[generalized-dark-matter|Generalized Dark Matter]] models with time-dependent equations of state have been shown to enhance the efficiency of halo collapse in the early Universe [Davari et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231115083D/abstract).

Conversely, many [[alternative-dark-matter-models|Alternative dark matter models]] originally developed to resolve small-scale galactic anomalies tend to suppress or delay early structure formation, placing them in potential conflict with the rapid assembly implied by JWST. Even prior to JWST, the number density of faint primordial galaxies detected in the Hubble Frontier Fields provided stringent independent constraints on the masses of [[warm-dark-matter|Warm dark matter]] and sterile neutrino models, further limiting their viability to completely replace cold dark matter [Castellano et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190312580C/abstract). In [[wave-dark-matter-model|Wave dark matter model]] (FDM) scenarios, quantum wave interference below the Jeans scale delays the onset of primordial star formation to $z \sim 13$, compared to $z \sim 35$ in standard cold dark matter (CDM) scenarios [Schive et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014NatPh..10..496S/abstract), [Mocz et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494.2027M/abstract). Similarly, models evaluated within the [[ethos-framework|ETHOS (framework)]]—which incorporate relativistic dark matter couplings or self-interactions—introduce a cutoff in the linear power spectrum that delays structure formation and suppresses the abundance of galaxies below $M_\star \sim 10^8 M_\odot$ [Kurmus et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.516.1524K/abstract). 

Despite this delayed collapse, ETHOS and interacting dark matter models predict that early galaxies undergo a more vigorous initial phase of star formation, producing higher UV luminosities. Because of this compensatory effect, the predicted optical depth to cosmic reionization, $\tau$, differs from the $\Lambda$CDM baseline by only $\sim 10\%$ [Lovell et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.2886L/abstract). Therefore, current constraints on reionization are insufficient to definitively rule out these non-standard models.

The literature currently remains highly ambiguous and conflicted regarding the ultimate implications of the JWST high-redshift data. While one camp argues that the observed rapid assembly necessitates modifications to dark energy or gravity [Shen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919427S/abstract), [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.3385G/abstract), another maintains that current JWST data cannot yet definitively differentiate between CDM and alternative models like [[warm-dark-matter|Warm dark matter]] (WDM). Proponents of the latter view note that the delay in halo collapse between CDM and WDM correlates inversely with descendant mass, meaning differences are only truly manifest in present-day dwarf galaxies, not the massive high-$z$ progenitors observed by JWST [Lovell et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.3029L/abstract). Consequently, it remains an open question whether the JWST anomalies represent a breakdown of $\Lambda$CDM or simply highlight the limitations of current baryonic feedback and star-formation recipes in extreme early-Universe environments [Kurmus et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.516.1524K/abstract).

## Observational Probes and Statistical Constraints

Observational constraints on beyond-$\Lambda$CDM models rely on a synthesis of early- and late-Universe datasets designed to probe both the geometric expansion history and the growth of cosmic structure. The primary statistical challenge for any alternative theory—whether involving [[modified-gravity-theories|Modified Gravity Theories]], dynamical dark energy, or exotic [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]]—is to resolve prevailing cosmological tensions, such as the $H_0$ and $S_8$ anomalies, without degrading the exquisite fit to standard benchmark data.

**Cosmic Microwave Background**
Planck observations of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) temperature and polarization anisotropies remain the foundational anchor for cosmological parameter estimation. While models like Early Dark Energy (EDE) attempt to resolve the Hubble tension by modifying the pre-recombination expansion rate, they are highly constrained by CMB data. Specifically, EDE models often predict deviations in the early integrated Sachs-Wolfe (eISW) effect. However, consistency tests using Planck data yield an eISW amplitude of $A_{\rm eISW} = 0.988 \pm 0.027$, which is in strict agreement with the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] expectation of unity, thereby requiring extreme fine-tuning for early-time modifications to avoid over-predicting this signal [Vagnozzi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104f3524V/abstract).

**Large-Scale Structure and Expansion Probes**
Late-Universe geometric and dynamical probes, particularly Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO) and Redshift-Space Distortions (RSD), provide stringent tests of the dark sector. RSD measurements constrain the linear growth rate of structure, parametrized by $f(z)\sigma_8(z)$. The [[interaction-dark-energy-model|Interaction Dark Energy model]] framework, which couples dark matter and dark energy, often provides a better fit to RSD data than $\Lambda$CDM by predicting lower growth rates, thus naturally alleviating the $S_8$ tension [Cheng et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.102d3517C/abstract). Furthermore, recent BAO measurements from the DESI DR2 survey, combined with CMB and supernova data, have shown a $3$ to $5\sigma$ statistical preference for non-vanishing dark sector interactions [Li et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107361L/abstract), as well as modest evidence favoring a [[wcdm-cosmology|wCDM cosmology]] equation of state ($w \neq -1$) from Lyman-$\alpha$ forest data [Capozziello et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251021976C/abstract). Extended analyses excluding individual datasets persistently favor time-varying dark energy over a static cosmological constant at similar significance levels [Ishak et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722856I/abstract). Beyond these measurements, including ISW constraints from galaxy-CMB cross-correlations significantly strengthens existing large-scale structure constraints, reducing the viable parameter space for dynamical dark energy models by approximately 70% [Seraille et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240106221S/abstract). In the domain of cluster astrophysics, the thermal and kinematic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect power spectra from galaxy clusters provide unique signatures to distinguish between dynamical dark energy models and the signatures of massive neutrinos [Luchina et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250316355L/abstract). Furthermore, future surveys like Euclid and DESI will be sensitive enough to detect scale-dependent matter perturbations, allowing them to distinguish between standard $\Lambda$CDM and dynamical dark energy models that feature future singularities [Denkiewicz et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170201291D/abstract).

**Weak Lensing and the Matter Power Spectrum**
The amplitude of matter fluctuations is directly probed by weak lensing surveys (such as the Dark Energy Survey and KiDS), which map the distribution of dark matter via cosmic shear. These surveys are highly sensitive to the matter power spectrum on both linear and non-linear scales. Modifications to gravity leave distinct signatures in the lensing potential and scale-dependent growth that must be carefully suppressed via screening mechanisms to match observations [Bouchè et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230101503B/abstract); [[non-local-theories|Non-local theories]] of gravity, for example, can alleviate $H_0$ and $\sigma_8$ tensions but are heavily restricted by cluster-scale lensing data. Additionally, measurements of the small-scale matter power spectrum via the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest place severe limits on the free-streaming length of dark matter. These limits force the allowable masses of [[warm-dark-matter|Warm dark matter]] [Strigari et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007PhRvD..75f1303S/abstract) and fuzzy dark matter candidates [Hui et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PhRvD..95d3541H/abstract) to be tightly constrained, rendering their large-scale predictions highly degenerate with standard cold dark matter.

Currently, the literature reflects a landscape of conflicting statistical indicators. While specific datasets (like local $H_0$ measurements or recent high-precision BAO data) strongly favor extensions to the standard model, comprehensive joint analyses often reveal that parameters governing modified gravity or extended dark energy remain statistically consistent with general relativity and $\Lambda$CDM when all multi-probe data are evaluated simultaneously [Abbott et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvD..99l3505A/abstract), [Abbott et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.107h3504A/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The landscape of cosmology faces several critical open questions, primarily driven by the persistence of the $H_0$ and $S_8$ tensions and sub-galactic structural discrepancies. Resolving these issues requires disentangling new fundamental physics from complex astrophysical processes, a task that will rely heavily on forthcoming future surveys and advances in theoretical modeling.

One of the most pressing theoretical ambiguities is the role of baryonic physics. It remains heavily debated whether the "small-scale crisis"—which encompasses the cusp-core and missing satellites problems—can be fully resolved by standard astrophysical feedback, such as supernova-driven winds and dynamical friction, or if it necessitates alternative [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]] like [[warm-dark-matter|Warm dark matter]] (WDM) or Self-Interacting Dark Matter (SIDM) [Del Popolo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98f3517D/abstract). Current hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate that baryonic feedback can significantly flatten dark matter halos, making it exceedingly difficult to distinguish between exotic dark matter microphysics and standard astrophysics. Breaking this degeneracy requires identifying novel observational signatures, such as the precise radial distribution of subhalos in strong lensing clusters. Recent findings from multi-scale cluster lensing have revealed subhalo inner density slopes that are significantly steeper than standard CDM predictions, potentially pointing toward core-collapsed SIDM as a resolution to these emerging discrepancies [Natarajan et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107909N/abstract).

At cosmological scales, the nature of dark energy and gravity remains an open question. Recent data from DESI (the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument) has introduced striking new evidence: combinations of DESI Data Release 2 (DR2) Lyman-$\alpha$ forest measurements and baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO) show a preference for dynamical [[dark-energy-models|Dark energy models]] (featuring a time-varying equation of state $w$) or an [[interaction-dark-energy-model|Interaction Dark Energy model]] over a static cosmological constant at the $3\sigma$ to $5\sigma$ level in several analyses [Ishak et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722856I/abstract), [Li et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107361L/abstract), though other pipeline combinations yield statistically inconclusive results for the dark energy equation of state [Capozziello et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251021976C/abstract). Validating these anomalies will be a primary objective for the Euclid mission and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's LSST. By precisely mapping the growth of structure and weak gravitational lensing, the Euclid mission and LSST will probe the growth index of matter perturbations, $\gamma(z)$, providing the statistical power to distinguish between dynamical dark energy and [[modified-gravity-theories|Modified Gravity Theories]] [Basilakos et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..94l3525B/abstract), [Casas et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..97d3520C/abstract). Comprehensive pre-launch forecasts by the Euclid Collaboration emphasize its potential to robustly constrain dark energy and modified gravity, demonstrating its capability to definitively distinguish between time-varying equations of state and general relativity [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209748E/abstract).

However, analyzing data from these next-generation surveys requires overcoming significant theoretical hurdles. Most large-scale structure constraints currently rely on linear perturbation theory, but distinguishing between subtle non-standard signatures demands robust predictions extending well into the non-linear regime [Abbott et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.107h3504A/abstract). Furthermore, cosmological analyses must account for complex parameter degeneracies; for instance, the suppression of small-scale power by massive neutrinos can closely mimic the signatures of both modified gravity and interacting dark sectors [Dirian et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PhRvD..96h3513D/abstract), [Chudaykin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220303666C/abstract).

Finally, the high-redshift universe presents a new frontier for testing extensions to the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]]. JWST observations of unexpectedly massive, mature galaxies at $z > 10$ pose a challenge to standard structure formation timelines. While Early Dark Energy (EDE) models have been proposed to accelerate early structure formation and simultaneously resolve the $H_0$ tension [Shen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919427S/abstract), these models face severe constraints. Specifically, EDE is highly restricted by the early integrated Sachs-Wolfe (eISW) effect, requiring precise tuning to avoid exceeding the precise cosmic microwave background predictions of standard cosmology [Vagnozzi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104f3524V/abstract). Determining whether early-universe anomalies point toward a fundamental breakdown of the standard model or simply necessitate revised treatments of early star formation remains a defining question for the next decade of astrophysics.
