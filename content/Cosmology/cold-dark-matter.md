Cold dark matter (CDM) is a hypothetical form of [[non-baryonic-dark-matter|Non-baryonic dark matter]] characterized by its sluggish, non-relativistic velocities at the epoch of radiation-matter equality. As the primary mass component of the [[universe|Universe]], it provides the fundamental gravitational scaffolding for the standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]]. In this cosmological paradigm, CDM is modeled as a pressureless, collisionless fluid that accounts for approximately 80% of the universe's total [[mass-density|Mass density]] and over 20% of its overall [[energy-content|Energy content]]. The necessity of a dominant dark component was initially inferred from the anomalous kinematics of galaxy clusters and the flat rotation curves of spiral galaxies. Today, the CDM framework is robustly supported by a wide array of precision observations, including the power spectrum of cosmic microwave background anisotropies, the large-scale distribution of galaxies, and the mass profiles derived from strong and weak gravitational lensing [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract).

Because CDM particles possess negligible thermal velocities, their free-streaming length is exceedingly small. This kinematic property prevents the erasure of primordial density fluctuations on sub-galactic scales, dictating a bottom-up, [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]] of structure formation [Frenk et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985Natur.317..595F/abstract). Under the influence of gravity, initial overdensities decouple from the Hubble expansion and undergo violent relaxation to form virialized, [[compact-dark-matter-halos|Compact dark matter halos]]. Through continuous accretion and merging, these halos assemble into the massive clusters and interconnecting filaments of the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] [Springel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005Natur.435..629S/abstract). Gravity-only $N$-body simulations have extensively demonstrated that these bound dark matter halos exhibit a nearly universal internal structure, classically described by the [[navarro-frenk-white-nfw-profile|Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) Profile]] across an enormous range of mass scales [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..563N/abstract).

Despite its profound success in reproducing large-scale cosmological observables, the CDM paradigm faces persistent theoretical and observational challenges on sub-galactic scales. High-resolution pure dark matter simulations over-predict both the abundance of dwarf satellite galaxies and the steepness of central density cusps, leading to widely debated anomalies in [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]], such as the missing satellites and cusp-core problems [Moore et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.370..629M/abstract). Current research actively investigates whether these discrepancies can be resolved by complex baryonic physics—such as supernova feedback and gas cooling—or if they necessitate alternative [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]] like [[warm-dark-matter|Warm dark matter]] or self-interacting species [Hannestad et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph.12558H/abstract), [Sawala et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.2748S/abstract). Furthermore, the fundamental particle identity of CDM remains one of the most significant open questions in [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]]. Ongoing direct and indirect detection efforts seek to uncover the nature of this elusive mass, while recent telescope observations of early galaxies are prompting new scrutiny of the hierarchical assembly timeline in the early universe.

## Introduction and Physical Characteristics

Cold dark matter (CDM) constitutes the primary mass component of the [[universe|Universe]] in the standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract). Physically, CDM is defined as a fluid of [[non-baryonic-dark-matter|Non-baryonic dark matter]] particles that became non-relativistic early in the [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]] of the Universe, long before the epoch of matter-radiation equality. Because its initial peculiar velocities are negligible compared to the typical velocity dispersion of individual galaxies, the free-streaming length of CDM is exceedingly small. This "cold" kinematic property is essential; it ensures that primordial density fluctuations are not erased by free-streaming, allowing cosmic structure to grow hierarchically from small to large scales in a bottom-up fashion [Frenk et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985Natur.317..595F/abstract). Because it hardly interacts with ordinary matter except gravitationally, CDM clusters strongly to form the fundamental load-bearing frame of cosmic structure [Peter et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1201.3942P/abstract).

The total [[energy-content|Energy content]] and [[mass-density|Mass density]] of the Universe are typically quantified using [[omega-parameters|Omega parameters]]. Precision measurements of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) and large-scale structure indicate that the total matter density parameter is $\Omega_{m,0} \approx 0.3$ [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract). Constraints from [[big-bang|Big Bang]] nucleosynthesis and CMB anisotropies restrict the baryonic contribution to roughly $\Omega_{b,0} \approx 0.04$ to $0.05$. Consequently, ordinary baryons account for only $15\%-20\%$ of the total matter content, leaving the vast majority ($75\%-80\%$) in the form of non-baryonic CDM. This requires a precise understanding of the [[cosmic-baryon-density|Cosmic baryon density]].

Although the exact particle identity of CDM remains unknown, theoretically favored candidates include weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs) with masses in the range $1 \text{ GeV} \lesssim m \lesssim 3 \text{ TeV}$, or extremely light particles like axions that are produced without thermal velocities. Alternative macroscopic candidates also exist, such as strongly interacting composite dark matter (e.g., macroscopic quark nuggets), where the apparent weakness of interactions with ordinary matter arises from a small geometric cross-section to mass ratio rather than a fundamentally weak coupling constant [Zhitnitsky et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvD..74d3515Z/abstract). At macroscopic scales, standard CDM is modeled as a perfectly collisionless, pressureless fluid. The evolution of this fluid is governed by the collisionless [[boltzmann-equation|Boltzmann equation]] coupled with Poisson's equation for gravity. In this regime, the velocity stress tensor is negligible, and dark matter structures undergo violent relaxation during gravitational collapse to form virialized, [[compact-dark-matter-halos|Compact dark matter halos]].

## Historical Development and the Standard Model

The observational foundation for dark matter traces back to 1933, when Fritz Zwicky inferred that the velocity dispersion of galaxies in the Coma Cluster required a [[total-mass-astrophysics|Total mass (astrophysics)]] about 400 times larger than the luminous stellar mass. However, the existence of a dominant non-luminous component was not broadly accepted until the 1970s. During this period, Vera Rubin and collaborators demonstrated that spiral galaxy rotation curves remain anomalously flat at large radii, necessitating massive, extended dark halos to prevent the Keplerian decline expected if mass traced light [Battaner et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000FCPh...21....1B/abstract). 

In the early 1980s, the Hot [[big-bang|Big Bang]] framework required a theoretical shift toward non-baryonic dark matter. Calculations of primordial nucleosynthesis bounded the [[cosmic-baryon-density|Cosmic baryon density]], showing that baryons could account for only $\sim 15-20\%$ of the universe's total matter content. Early particle candidates included light, relativistic neutrinos (hot dark matter); however, their large free-streaming lengths erased small-scale density fluctuations, fundamentally failing to match observed galaxy clustering [Xiang et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992MNRAS.259..761X/abstract). Consequently, the consensus shifted to cold dark matter (CDM)—particles with negligible thermal velocities at the epoch of matter-radiation equality.

The theoretical underpinning of the CDM model was formalized by White & Rees in 1978. They proposed a two-stage mechanism for galaxy formation: dark matter halos collapse first through gravitational instability, providing deep potential wells within which baryonic gas subsequently cools, condenses, and forms stars. This bottom-up assembly process is the cornerstone of the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], wherein small initial perturbations continuously accrete and merge to form increasingly massive structures [Jenkins et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...499...20J/abstract), [van den Bosch et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.331...98V/abstract).

As computational capabilities advanced, $N$-body simulations solidified CDM as the standard cosmological paradigm. Foundational work demonstrated that collisionless, gravity-only CDM simulations naturally reproduce the observed [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] of voids, filaments, and rich clusters [Frenk et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985Natur.317..595F/abstract). Furthermore, high-resolution simulations revealed that these virialized dark matter halos follow a nearly universal density distribution across an enormous range of mass scales, now famously known as the [[navarro-frenk-white-nfw-profile|Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) Profile]] [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..563N/abstract). Today, CDM is the bedrock of the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], a spatially flat cosmology where cold dark matter comprises roughly $21-26\%$ of the universe's total energy budget. This framework accurately predicts the large-scale power spectrum, cosmic microwave background anisotropies, and the overall hierarchical growth of cosmic structure [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract), [Springel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005Natur.435..629S/abstract). 

## Hierarchical Structure Formation

The cold dark matter (CDM) paradigm posits that cosmic architecture is assembled hierarchically in a [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]] fashion [Frenk et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985Natur.317..595F/abstract). Because CDM particles possess negligible primordial velocity dispersion—meaning free-streaming is unimportant on galactic scales—gravitational instability allows density perturbations to persist and grow across virtually all astrophysically relevant mass scales. Consequently, the smallest structures collapse first, subsequently merging to assemble progressively more massive systems over cosmic time [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract).

The assembly of dark matter structures begins when linear density perturbations decouple from the cosmic expansion. Once a localized region reaches a critical overdensity, it halts its expansion and turns around to collapse. During this non-linear phase, the collisionless dark matter, governed by the collisionless [[boltzmann-equation|Boltzmann equation]], undergoes violent relaxation. This process results in a quasi-equilibrium state known as a dark matter halo. Extensive $N$-body simulations demonstrate that these virialized halos exhibit a nearly universal density profile, most notably the [[navarro-frenk-white-nfw-profile|Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) Profile]], which scales remarkably well across multiple decades in halo mass [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..563N/abstract). A virialized halo is traditionally defined as the region within which the average density is equal to $\delta_{\rm vir}$ times the critical density of the [[universe|Universe]].

The statistical properties and structural growth of these halos are elegantly described by the [[press-schechter-formalism|Press-Schechter formalism]] and its extended variants. This analytical framework models the non-linear growth of structure from a Gaussian random field of initial density fluctuations. By tracking the conditional probabilities of halo collapse at varying redshifts, the extended Press-Schechter theory allows for the construction of merger trees [van den Bosch et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.331...98V/abstract). These merger trees map the progenitor mass distributions, merger rates, and mass accretion histories of any given halo. CDM halos exhibit a remarkably universal mass accretion history, dictating how [[halo-expansion|Halo expansion]] and continuous accretion from the surrounding environment proceed [van den Bosch et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.331...98V/abstract).

On megaparsec scales, the hierarchical assembly of CDM naturally generates the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] [Springel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005Natur.435..629S/abstract). Halos cluster along dense, intersecting filaments that surround expansive voids, with the most massive and recently formed galaxy clusters residing at the nodes where large-scale filaments intersect [White et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJ...313..505W/abstract).

As smaller halos fall into the deeper gravitational potential of larger host halos, they are subjected to strong tidal forces and dynamical friction. However, because CDM is collisionless and the central densities of these accreting halos are high, many of the smaller structures survive as gravitationally bound entities. This survival results in a nested, substructure-rich population of subhalos orbiting within the host, the abundance of which is parameterized by the [[shmf-subhalo-mass-function|SHMF (Subhalo mass function)]] [Kravtsov et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AdAst2010E...8K/abstract). 

## Internal Structure of Dark Matter Halos

In the standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], initial cosmic density perturbations grow via gravitational instability, eventually breaking away from the Hubble expansion to collapse and undergo violent relaxation. This process produces gravitationally bound, virialized dark matter halos, which serve as the fundamental building blocks in the halo model of dark matter clustering. 

Collisionless $N$-body simulations have demonstrated that the spherically averaged density of these halos follows a nearly universal form across decades of mass, from dwarf galaxies to massive galaxy clusters, known as the [[navarro-frenk-white-nfw-profile|Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) Profile]] [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..563N/abstract). The NFW density profile is characterized by a logarithmic slope that is shallower than $r^{-2}$ in the inner regions (forming a central density "cusp") and steeper than $r^{-2}$ near the virial radius. The shape of the profile is largely defined by its concentration parameter, which inversely correlates with halo mass. This mass-concentration relation reflects the hierarchical nature of structure formation: lower-mass halos typically collapse at earlier cosmic epochs when the background density of the [[universe|Universe]] was higher, leading to more concentrated central regions [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..563N/abstract). Despite variations in merger histories, theoretical modeling from projects such as DREAMS has quantified that for Milky Way-mass halos, intrinsic halo-to-halo variance often dominates over variations driven by baryonic feedback in determining the overall dark matter density profile [Garcia et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251203132G/abstract). Interestingly, when baryons are included, structural scaling relations reveal that the [[central-dark-matter-density|Central dark matter density]] (defined as the core radius multiplied by the central density) remains nearly constant and independent of galaxy mass across both standard CDM and self-interacting dark matter (SIDM) models [Dalui et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251026545D/abstract).

As larger halos are assembled through the merging and accretion of smaller progenitors, they retain a vast amount of substructure. High-resolution simulations reveal that massive host halos are populated by thousands of dense, gravitationally bound subhalos that survive the violent accretion process [Kuhlen et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008JPhCS.125a2008K/abstract). This hierarchy implies that a galaxy cluster will contain hundreds of galaxy-sized subhalos, and a Milky Way-sized galaxy halo will itself contain thousands of smaller dwarf-sized subhalos, mapping out a richly nested [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]]. Even in the nonlinear dynamical regimes of these massive galaxy clusters, observations and modeling demonstrate that member galaxies remain remarkably good tracers of the underlying spatial distribution of dark matter .

## Small-Scale Cosmological Structure

Although the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] successfully reproduces the large-scale distribution of the [[universe|Universe]], pure gravity-only $N$-body [[matter-only-simulations|Matter-only simulations]] consistently produce highly concentrated dark matter halos with abundant substructure. When compared against observations on galactic and sub-galactic scales, these predictions face persistent challenges, collectively known as the [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]] crisis [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract), [de Martino et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Univ....6..107D/abstract). 

At the heart of the crisis is the "core-cusp problem." High-resolution simulations predict a nearly universal density profile characterized by a steeply rising central cusp, modeled by the [[navarro-frenk-white-nfw-profile|Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) Profile]] [Navarro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..563N/abstract). In stark contrast, observations of rotation curves in [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]]—particularly low surface brightness (LSB) galaxies and dwarf spheroidals—favor flat, constant-density central regions, suggesting the presence of [[cored-dark-matter-halos|Cored dark matter halos]] [Moore et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.370..629M/abstract), [Kuzio de Naray et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...710L.161K/abstract). To reconcile these profile shapes, recent analytical work has proposed a novel universal scaling relation that natively incorporates this cusp-to-core transition within the structural framework of CDM haloes [Kaneda et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240703614K/abstract).

A second major discrepancy involves the abundance of satellite galaxies. The hierarchical assembly of cold dark matter predicts that massive halos should be populated by a vast number of surviving subhalos. In the Local Group, this manifests as the "missing satellite problem": the predicted number of dark matter subhalos vastly exceeds the observed population of luminous dwarf galaxies [Somerville et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...572L..23S/abstract), [Kravtsov et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AdAst2010E...8K/abstract). A closely related issue is the "too-big-to-fail" problem. Cosmological simulations generate several massive, dense subhalos whose deep potential wells should have allowed them to easily retain gas and form stars. These massive subhalos are "too big to fail" at forming galaxies, yet their predicted central densities are systematically higher than the hosts of the brightest observed satellites, implying that they mysteriously remain unobserved [Navarro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019IAUS..344..455N/abstract), [Sawala et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.2748S/abstract). These small-scale discrepancies are not strictly confined to dwarf galaxies; the crisis extends even to the scale of fossil groups, where simulations predict an overabundance of massive "missing galaxies" with velocity dispersions up to $150 \text{ km/s}$ that are not observed in nature [D'Onghia et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...612..628D/abstract).

The literature remains highly divided on how to resolve these discrepancies. The two leading perspectives assert that either the complex baryonic physics of galaxy formation must be properly modeled, or the fundamental particle properties of dark matter must be modified beyond the standard collisionless, cold paradigm.

## Baryonic Feedback and Interaction

The inclusion of baryonic physics fundamentally alters the dark matter distribution on galactic scales. Because ordinary matter can radiate energy and lose angular momentum, gas cooling allows baryons to condense at the centers of halos. As this baryonic mass concentrates, the deepening of the central gravitational potential pulls the surrounding dark matter inward—a process known as adiabatic contraction—which acts to steepen the [[central-dark-matter-density|Central dark matter density]] [Tissera et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..922T/abstract).

In opposition to adiabatic contraction, energetic processes such as star formation and supernova feedback can inject massive amounts of energy into the interstellar medium. Bursty star formation drives rapid, massive outflows of gas, causing sudden fluctuations in the local gravitational potential. Because dark matter is collisionless, these non-adiabatic potential fluctuations kinematically heat the dark matter particles, causing the halo to expand. Over multiple cycles, this dark matter response can effectively flatten the central density profile, transforming the steep NFW cusp into a shallower $\rho(r) \propto r^{0}$ core [Sawala et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.2748S/abstract), [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract). Another contributing effect is the 'gravitational slingshot' mechanism, where dark matter particles gain kinetic energy from repeated encounters with massive baryonic bodies—analogous to Fermi acceleration—naturally flattening central density cusps [Chen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.2258C/abstract). High-resolution hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate that baryonic feedback significantly modifies the phase-space distribution of the halo. It leads to a broader dark matter speed distribution, an approximately constant velocity anisotropy of $\beta \sim 0.1-0.2$ in the inner halo, and reshapes the central dark matter distribution to be more oblate or rounder than in gravity-only models [Tissera et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..922T/abstract), [Nuñez-Castiñeyra et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...05..012N/abstract). Furthermore, empirical analyses of this dark matter-baryon interplay have revealed a scale-independent universal constant that describes their mutual gravitational interaction across both galactic and cluster scales [Chan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NatSR...9.3570C/abstract). This research provides insight into the potential formation of [[cored-dark-matter-halos|Cored dark matter halos]].

Baryonic processes also dramatically influence the observable subhalo population, offering a mechanism to resolve both the missing satellites and too-big-to-fail problems. While pure [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] simulations predict thousands of subhalos around a Milky Way-mass host, star formation efficiency is severely suppressed in low-mass halos. Heating from the cosmic ultraviolet background during the epoch of reionization (photoionization squelching) prevents gas from cooling and condensing in shallow potential wells, while supernova feedback ejects what little gas is accreted [Somerville et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...572L..23S/abstract), [Sawala et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.2748S/abstract). Consequently, the vast majority of low-mass subhalos are expected to be completely devoid of stars, existing as dark mini-halos rather than visible [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] [Navarro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019IAUS..344..455N/abstract).

Despite the success of baryonic feedback in reconciling the CDM framework with many small-scale observations, the literature remains divided. It is actively debated whether supernova feedback provides sufficient energy to create the large cores observed in the smallest, most dark-matter-dominated dwarf galaxies. While some researchers argue that the interplay of gas cooling and stellar feedback is sufficient to explain the observed structural properties of halos, others maintain that the persistent discrepancies necessitate alternative [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]] [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract).

## Variants and Alternatives to CDM

The ambiguity surrounding baryonic solutions has motivated extensive theoretical exploration of alternative [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]] that modify the particle physics of the dark sector to naturally resolve small-scale structural tensions.

[[warm-dark-matter|Warm dark matter]] (WDM) models, such as thermally produced relics or sterile neutrinos, assign the dark matter particle a non-negligible initial velocity dispersion. This kinetic energy allows particles to free-stream out of overdense regions in the early [[universe|Universe]], naturally suppressing the matter power spectrum on small scales and reducing the abundance of satellite halos [Lovell et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498..702L/abstract), [Parimbelli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...12..044P/abstract). However, WDM faces a severe "Catch-22" when attempting to explain galactic structure. To naturally produce $\sim 1 \text{ kpc}$ cores in dwarf galaxies, the WDM particle mass would need to be extremely light ($\sim 0.1 \text{ keV}$), which suppresses so much power that it prevents the host dwarf galaxies from forming entirely [Macciò et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.1105M/abstract). Furthermore, independent observational constraints from the high-redshift galaxy luminosity function and cosmic reionization restrict the WDM particle mass to $m \gtrsim 2-3 \text{ keV}$, a range that is also in marginal tension with Lyman-$\alpha$ forest data [Lapi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...09..003L/abstract), rendering WDM nearly indistinguishable from CDM on most galactic scales.

If dark matter particles can scatter elastically, the resulting self-interacting dark matter (SIDM) can conduct thermal energy into the dense, dynamically cold inner regions of halos. This naturally produces [[cored-dark-matter-halos|Cored dark matter halos]] without destroying the host system [Hannestad et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph.12558H/abstract), [Davé et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...547..574D/abstract). Generating appropriate cores in galaxy cluster-sized halos generally requires a [[dark-matter-self-interaction-cross-section|Dark matter self-interaction cross section]] per unit mass of $\sigma/m \sim 0.1 - 10 \text{ cm}^2/\text{g}$ [Sokolenko et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JCAP...12..038S/abstract). Because a constant cross-section of this magnitude can violate kinematic constraints from merging galaxy clusters, viable SIDM models often invoke velocity-dependent cross-sections [Davé et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...547..574D/abstract), [Adhikari et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025RvMP...97d5004A/abstract). Precise constraints from relaxed galaxy groups, such as NGC 5044, restrict the SIDM cross-section to $\sigma/m \sim 0.16 \text{ cm}^2/\text{g}$ for velocity dispersions around $300 \text{ km/s}$, providing tight bounds on these models [Gopika et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PDU....4201291G/abstract). Additionally, self-interactions during galactic infall could cause a dynamical separation between stars and their dark halos, potentially generating detectable U-shaped warps in edge-on disk galaxies [Pardo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvD.100l3006P/abstract). Specific particle implementations, such as leptonic scalar dark matter models with light mediators, can leverage the Sommerfeld enhancement to naturally achieve the required self-interaction cross-sections to resolve these small-scale discrepancies [Kao et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.103e5021K/abstract).

Further modifications to the dark sector include [[dissipative-dark-matter|Dissipative dark matter]] or atomic dark matter, where a minority subcomponent (e.g., $\sim 5\%$) of the dark sector can cool and radiate. This energy loss allows the dissipative component to undergo gravitational collapse, potentially forming dense sub-resolution clumps or a coplanar [[dark-disk|Dark disk]] aligned with the baryonic galaxy [Buckley et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvL.120e1102B/abstract), [Roy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954L..40R/abstract), [Roy et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240815317R/abstract). Another alternative is fuzzy dark matter (FDM), consisting of ultra-light scalar bosons with masses of $m \sim 10^{-22} \text{ eV}$. At this mass scale, the de Broglie wavelength becomes macroscopic, suppressing small-scale structure formation and generating central solitonic cores supported by quantum pressure rather than thermal kinematics [Calabrese et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.4397C/abstract), [de Martino et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Galax...6...10D/abstract).

Finally, some theoretical frameworks bypass the dark particle hypothesis entirely by modifying the laws of gravity or inertia. Theories such as [[modified-dark-matter|Modified Dark Matter]] (MDM) and Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) assert that the gravitational field deviates from Newtonian expectations when accelerations fall below a critical threshold of $a_0 \sim 10^{-10} \text{ m/s}^2$ [Diaferio et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1206.6231D/abstract). In particular, MDM is presented as a phenomenological model where galactic mass profiles inherently contain information about the cosmological constant [Edmonds et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180100160E/abstract). Similarly, theoretical frameworks involving macroscopic Q-balls attempt to describe cosmological and galactic dark matter unifiedly; these models mimic standard CDM at large cosmological scales while potentially reproducing MOND-like phenomenologies on galactic scales [Mandal et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025EPJC...85..178M/abstract). These modified frameworks successfully reproduce the Baryonic Tully-Fisher Relation and predict the tight Radial Acceleration Relation observed in rotationally supported galaxies without invoking a dark halo [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.106l1303M/abstract), [Chae et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...55C/abstract). Alternatively, it has been suggested that the apparent phenomenological success of MOND on galactic scales might simply be a mathematical misinterpretation of a specific isothermal dark matter density profile that naturally arises when a dark matter core is present [Chan et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PhRvD..88j3501C/abstract). However, pure modified gravity models consistently struggle to replicate lensing and dynamical mass estimates on the scale of galaxy clusters, and often require the addition of a dark mass component—such as massive sterile neutrinos—to remain observationally viable on cosmological scales [Diaferio et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1206.6231D/abstract), [Nieuwenhuizen et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ForPh..6500050N/abstract).

## Observational Evidence and Lensing

The empirical foundation for cold dark matter (CDM) spans multiple orders of magnitude in spatial scale, anchoring the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] as the prevailing paradigm of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]]. At the largest scales, measurements of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) anisotropies and the large-scale distribution of galaxies yield tight constraints on the cosmological energy budget. CMB data indicate that the total matter density parameter is $\Omega_{m,0} \approx 0.3$, while Big Bang nucleosynthesis and the CMB independently constrain the [[cosmic-baryon-density|Cosmic baryon density]] to only $\sim 15-20 \%$ of this value. This discrepancy requires that the bulk of the universe's matter is composed of [[non-baryonic-dark-matter|Non-baryonic dark matter]] [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract), [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract).

Historically, the first indications of unseen mass arose on the scale of galaxy clusters. In 1933, Fritz Zwicky applied the principle of virial equilibrium to the Coma Cluster, demonstrating that the velocity dispersion of its member galaxies required a total mass roughly $400$ times greater than the luminous mass in stars. This missing mass problem was later solidified on galactic scales by the measurement of flat galaxy rotation curves. Extensive 21 cm and optical kinematics showed that circular velocities remain constant well beyond the visible stellar disk, failing to exhibit the Keplerian decline expected if mass perfectly traced light [Battaner et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000FCPh...21....1B/abstract). These flat rotation curves necessitate the existence of extended dark matter halos, which typically dominate the gravitational potential in regions where the observed centripetal acceleration falls below $\sim 10^{-10}$ m/s$^2$ [Diaferio et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1206.6231D/abstract).

Gravitational lensing provides a powerful, purely gravitational probe of the dark matter distribution, free from the assumptions of virial equilibrium or hydrostatic balance required by dynamical and X-ray gas tracers. On cluster scales, strong and weak gravitational lensing maps reveal high mass-to-light ratios that are representative of the cosmic mean. Merging galaxy clusters, most notably the Bullet Cluster (1E 0657-56), provide stark empirical support for the collisionless nature of CDM. In these systems, gravitational lensing mass maps show that the dark matter component is spatially separated from the X-ray emitting [[hot-gas|Hot gas]], which experiences strong ram-pressure stripping during the collision [Profumo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1001.4086P/abstract), [Bogorad et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...03..067B/abstract). Furthermore, the study of gravitational lens time delays within CDM frameworks can yield measurements of the Hubble constant that are consistent with local estimates, though this agreement typically requires the cold baryon fraction in the lensing models to be larger than standard CMB-based estimates [Kochanek et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...583...49K/abstract).

Despite the success of the CDM framework in matching the large-scale structure and general lensing profiles of clusters, high-resolution observations continue to test its limits. For example, kinematic analyses indicate that the outer density profile of the Andromeda galaxy's dark matter halo appears significantly steeper (with a logarithmic slope of $\sim -3.7$) than the standard CDM prediction of $-3$, presenting localized puzzles in nearby systems [Kirihara et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PASJ...66L..10K/abstract). Detailed mass distributions within clusters also exhibit tensions with simulated dark matter substructure. Recent analyses of strong gravitational lensing in galaxy clusters have revealed that observed cluster substructures are more than an order of magnitude more efficient at producing strong lensing events than is predicted by gravity-only CDM simulations [Meneghetti et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Sci...369.1347M/abstract). To better resolve subhalo structural properties, novel techniques utilizing the substructure convergence power spectrum in strong gravitational lenses have been proposed; these methods hold the potential to definitively distinguish between the truncated NFW profiles predicted by cold dark matter and the cored profiles characteristic of self-interacting dark matter [Diaz Rivero et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..97b3001D/abstract). It remains an area of active debate whether this discrepancy highlights a need for better modeling of baryonic feedback, or if it points toward the necessity of alternative dark matter physics.

## Phenomenology and Detection Strategies

The search for the particle identity of [[non-baryonic-dark-matter|Non-baryonic dark matter]]—most prominently Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs)—relies on a combination of direct detection, indirect detection, and astrophysical kinematics. These complementary approaches attempt to measure the mass, scattering cross section, and annihilation cross section of dark matter candidates.

**Direct Detection and Local Kinematics**
Direct searches aim to measure the recoil energy of standard model nuclei struck by dark matter particles. The expected event rate in these experiments depends sensitively on the [[local-dark-matter-density|Local dark matter density]] and the local velocity distribution of the dark matter halo. Empirical determinations of local dark matter kinematics using metal-poor stars as tracers indicate deviations from the standard Maxwellian halo model; these deviations can weaken the detection bounds on the spin-independent scattering cross section for dark matter masses below $10\text{ GeV}$ [Herzog-Arbeitman et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvL.120d1102H/abstract). 

Furthermore, hierarchical clustering predicts that $1\%$ to $5\%$ of the local dark matter could consist of "cold flows" of high-velocity particles stripped from disrupted subhalos, which would produce distinctive phase signatures in the annual modulation of the direct detection signal [Stiff et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001PhRvD..64h3516S/abstract). Diffuse extragalactic dark matter falling into the Local Group may also contribute a fast-moving component ($v \sim 600\text{ km s}^{-1}$) with a narrow velocity dispersion, potentially dominating the signal in detectors that are only sensitive to highly energetic nuclear recoils [Baushev et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...771..117B/abstract). Furthermore, the late infall of cold dark matter into the Milky Way is predicted to form discrete 'caustic rings' in the galactic plane, offering unique structural signatures and potential density enhancements [Sikivie et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003PhLB..567....1S/abstract). This dynamic is important for understanding the evolution of the [[universe|Universe]].

**Indirect Detection via Gamma Rays**
Indirect detection strategies search for standard model particles—such as gamma rays, positrons, and neutrinos—produced by dark matter annihilation or decay in dense astrophysical environments. A primary target is the Galactic Center. Observations with the Fermi Large Area Telescope (Fermi-LAT) have revealed an extended gamma-ray excess peaking between $300\text{ MeV}$ and $10\text{ GeV}$. This signal is well-fitted by dark matter particles with a mass of $7\text{--}12\text{ GeV}$ annihilating to leptons, or $25\text{--}45\text{ GeV}$ annihilating to hadrons, requiring an annihilation cross section of $\langle \sigma v \rangle \sim 3 \times 10^{-26}\text{ cm}^{3}\text{ s}^{-1}$ [Hooper et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvD..84l3005H/abstract). However, recent simulation-based predictions indicate that if this Galactic Center Excess is genuinely generated by WIMP annihilation, the signal should exhibit a significantly non-spherical morphology due to the Milky Way's specific cosmological merging history [Muru et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250806314M/abstract). In addition to gamma rays, the energy losses of relativistic annihilation products diffusing through the galactic magnetic field can produce a measurable synchrotron haze, potentially explaining features like the 'WMAP haze' observed near the Galactic Center [Linden et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvD..82f3529L/abstract).

However, the literature remains highly conflicted regarding the origin of this gamma-ray excess. Searches for dark matter annihilation in the smooth Milky Way halo exclude thermal dark matter with masses below $\sim 70\text{ GeV}$ annihilating into $b\bar{b}$ at the $95\%$ confidence level, creating tension with the dark matter interpretation of the Galactic Center signal [Chang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98l3004C/abstract). Galaxy clusters provide another robust target, as their gamma-ray flux is expected to be enhanced by a factor of $\sim 5$ due to substructure [Ackermann et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010JCAP...05..025A/abstract). Theoretical models also suggest that the survival of Earth-mass 'prompt cusps' from the early Universe could significantly boost the dark matter annihilation rate outside of galactic centers [Delos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...10..008D/abstract). However, recent analyses of these prompt cusps within clusters using 15 years of Fermi-LAT data have found no gamma-ray excess, ruling out the thermal annihilation cross section to the $b\bar{b}$ channel for dark matter masses below $\sim 200\text{ GeV}$ [Crnogorčević et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250114865C/abstract). Furthermore, the potential formation of steep density spikes induced by supermassive black holes at the centers of galaxies provides another stringent testing ground; the predicted presence of a black hole-induced dark matter spike in the M87 galaxy significantly strengthens the observational constraints on thermal annihilation cross-sections across a wide range of dark matter masses [Lacroix et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..92d3510L/abstract).

**Neutrino Telescopes**
Neutrino telescopes complement gamma-ray searches by continuously monitoring the sky for high-energy neutrinos resulting from dark matter annihilations in the Sun, Earth, or Galactic center [Pérez de los Heros et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151103500P/abstract). Facilities like IceCube extend these searches to heavy [[decaying-dark-matter|Decaying dark matter]] models, probing particle masses from $10\text{ TeV}$ to $10\text{ PeV}$ in distant galaxies and clusters [Jeong et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022icrc.confE.506J/abstract). 

Additionally, interactions between dark matter and neutrinos can be constrained using the kinematics of dwarf spheroidal galaxies. If core-collapse supernova neutrinos scatter off dark matter, the energy injection would flatten the central density profiles of the host galaxies. To avoid over-depleting dark matter cores beyond observational limits, the neutrino-dark matter cross section is constrained to $\sigma_{\nu\text{-DM}} \lesssim 3.4 \times 10^{-23}\text{ cm}^{2}$ for $15\text{ MeV}$ neutrinos and dark matter masses $m_\chi \lesssim 130\text{ GeV}$, providing stringent limits on alternative [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]] [Heston et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.110b3004H/abstract).

## Stellar Streams and Local Group Probes

The Local Group serves as a stringent testing ground for the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], which predicts that massive galaxies assemble via the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]] and are consequently embedded in extended dark halos teeming with substructure [Kuhlen et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008JPhCS.125a2008K/abstract), [Navarro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019IAUS..344..455N/abstract). Recent observational campaigns rely heavily on the phase-space distribution of local stars to infer the granularity and overall morphology of the Milky Way's dark matter halo.

Cold stellar streams, formed from the ongoing tidal disruption of globular clusters and satellite galaxies, act as sensitive dynamical tracers of the local gravitational potential. Due to their extended, coherent nature in phase space, streams on eccentric orbits contain significant information regarding the shape and mass distribution of the host halo. Analytic Fisher-matrix forecasts indicate that simultaneous fitting of multiple distinct streams can constrain the structural parameters of the dark matter halo at the percent level, provided precise three-dimensional velocities are available [Bonaca et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...867..101B/abstract). In addition to extended streams, the overarching kinematics of entire globular cluster systems serve as independent dynamical tracers, providing a robust method for estimating the total dark matter halo masses of host galaxies [Spitler et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.392L...1S/abstract). The complex accretion history of galaxies also means that dark halos can be significantly misaligned, or 'tilted', relative to the central stellar disk due to past mergers or interactions; these tilted halos are long-lived and can actively contribute to the warping of galactic disks over cosmic time [Han et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957L..24H/abstract). With the advent of the *Gaia* space observatory, accurate proper motions and parallaxes have enabled tight constraints on the [[local-dark-matter-density|Local dark matter density]], strongly disfavoring scenarios that predict a highly dissipative [[thin-dark-disk|Thin dark disk]] coplanar with the Milky Way's baryonic disk [Schutz et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvL.121h1101S/abstract).

Beyond stellar streams, the intact satellite population—specifically dwarf spheroidal galaxies—provides critical probes of the central dark matter density and velocity distribution. Because these low-mass systems are highly dark matter-dominated, the kinematics of their resident stars can be used to construct the local dark matter velocity profile. For example, old, metal-poor stars deposited in the Milky Way halo by disrupted subhalos have been shown to share similar kinematics to the local virialized dark matter, which refines the empirical velocity distributions assumed for direct dark matter detection experiments [Herzog-Arbeitman et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvL.120d1102H/abstract). Observations of individual dwarf spheroidals, such as Fornax and Draco, have also been leveraged to compute single-body phase-space densities via the Jeans equation, testing the spatial correlation of dark matter mass densities on kiloparsec scales [Tyler et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhRvD..66b3509T/abstract), [Borzou et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Univ....8..386B/abstract).

The interaction between subhalos and the central host potential also illuminates the spatial segregation of baryons and dark matter during tidal disruption. Because dark matter halos are generally more extended than the stellar components they host, tidal stripping selectively removes dark matter from the outskirts of a subhalo before significantly affecting its tightly bound stars. This mechanism has been directly observed in systems like NGC 1052-DF4, where tidal interaction with a neighboring galaxy has stripped the dark matter fraction to $\lesssim 1\%$, while leaving the stellar core largely intact, with only $\sim 7\%$ of the stellar mass found in the tidal tails [Montes et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904..114M/abstract). Such findings naturally reconcile the emergence of seemingly [[dark-matter-free-galaxies|Dark matter-free galaxies]] with the predictions of cold dark matter cosmologies. Furthermore, the search for purely dark mini-halos continues, as standard cosmology predicts numerous subhalos whose potential wells were too shallow to retain gas. These completely dark subhalos may eventually be detected through their perturbative dynamical effects on cold stellar streams or through the precise mapping of extended stellar components in the galactic outskirts [Power et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..321..105P/abstract), [Navarro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019IAUS..344..455N/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] establishes a highly successful framework for large-scale structure formation, the fundamental nature of dark matter remains one of the most pressing open questions in modern astrophysics. A major frontier is determining whether the persistent discrepancies observed at sub-galactic scales—such as the cusp-core and missing satellites problems—indicate a departure from collisionless cold dark matter, or if they are simply artifacts of incomplete baryonic physics modeling. It is explicitly debated in the literature whether these small-scale issues require alternative [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]], such as [[warm-dark-matter|Warm dark matter]] or self-interacting dark matter, or if baryonic feedback mechanisms like supernova-driven outflows are sufficient to reconcile simulations with observations [Sawala et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.2748S/abstract), [O'Neil et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524..288O/abstract). This debate is complicated by strict observational constraints that render popular alternatives, such as thermal warm dark matter, nearly indistinguishable from CDM on galactic scales [Lapi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...09..003L/abstract), [Macciò et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.1105M/abstract). Recent comparative studies, such as the AIDA-TNG project, forward-model standard CDM against WDM and SIDM scenarios, finding that WDM models typically predict unexpectedly cuspier radial distributions of satellite galaxies, further complicating alternative dark matter formulations [Romanello et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215883R/abstract).

The high-redshift [[universe|Universe]] offers a vital laboratory for testing hierarchical structure formation. Recent theoretical work predicted that upcoming observations from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) mapping extremely early, massive galaxies would serve as a critical test for the standard timeline of mass assembly in CDM halos [Klypin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.504..769K/abstract). Indeed, initial comparisons of high-redshift JWST data with hydrodynamical simulations have found that the observed properties of these early, bright objects remain consistent with standard cold dark matter, as well as with warm dark matter models having $m_{\rm WDM} > 2 \text{ keV}$ [Maio et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...672A..71M/abstract). However, significant degeneracies persist; the suppression of small-scale structure predicted by alternative dark matter models can be closely mimicked by variations in star formation efficiency and baryonic feedback, complicating direct cosmological inferences [Khimey et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.4139K/abstract).

Another area of active inquiry involves the thermal and formational history of the dark sector. Models of [[late-forming-dark-matter|Late-forming dark matter]] propose that dark matter is produced via a phase transition from a relativistic species, such as [[dark-radiation|Dark radiation]], after the [[big-bang|Big Bang]]. This mechanism suppresses the growth of linear matter perturbations on small scales and leaves distinct oscillatory signatures in the matter power spectrum [Das et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.103d3517D/abstract), [Sarkar et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...03..004S/abstract). Forward modeling of Milky Way satellite galaxies places strict bounds on this scenario, requiring that the transition to dark matter occurs no later than $z > 5.5 \times 10^6$ [Das et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.103d3517D/abstract). Furthermore, while interactions between dark matter and dark radiation have been proposed to resolve tensions in the amplitude of large-scale structure fluctuations, the inclusion of Lyman-alpha data renders the statistical evidence for such interactions marginal at best [Krall et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JCAP...09..003K/abstract). More recent formulations suggest that models featuring dark sector interactions that predominantly manifest on small scales—driven by relative peculiar velocities—might still successfully alleviate tensions in the amplitude of matter density perturbations without conflicting with larger-scale bounds [Asghari et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...04..042A/abstract).

Finally, the validity of the dark matter paradigm continues to be tested against modified gravity theories. In $f(R)$ gravity models, researchers define an [[effective-dark-matter|Effective dark matter]] density field using a modified Poisson equation. Cosmological N-body simulations reveal that the power spectrum of this effective dark matter can deviate from standard $\Lambda$CDM predictions by over $150\%$ for certain parameter choices, indicating that traditional analyses assuming standard dark matter density fields may severely underestimate the impact of $f(R)$ gravity on galaxy clustering [He et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..92j3508H/abstract). Disentangling these modified gravity effects from true particle dark matter dynamics remains a critical challenge for next-generation astronomical surveys. Beyond purely gravitational modifications, broad theoretical efforts aim to ecologically link dark matter, dark energy, and modified gravity through unified frameworks, such as a single self-interacting oscillating field or a 'chameleon-like' dark fluid [Zhao et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008JPhCS.140a2002Z/abstract).
