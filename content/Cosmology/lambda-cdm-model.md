## Article

The $\Lambda$CDM (Lambda cold dark matter) model is the standard parametrization of [[big-bang|Big Bang]] cosmology and the prevailing "concordance" model of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]] [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract). It provides a minimal yet highly successful theoretical framework that describes the [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]] of the [[universe|Universe]] from a primordial hot, dense state to its current accelerated expansion. The model is defined by a spatially flat geometry governed by General Relativity, with its [[energy-content|Energy content]] dominated by two distinct, currently unidentified components: a [[positive-cosmological-constant|Positive cosmological constant]] ($\Lambda$) representing vacuum energy, and non-relativistic, collisionless [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] (CDM) [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract). Ordinary baryonic matter—the fundamental building blocks of stars, gas, and planets—comprises only about 5% of the total cosmic energy budget, while the elusive [[dark-sector|Dark sector]] accounts for the remaining 95%. 

The widespread acceptance of the $\Lambda$CDM framework is built upon its remarkable ability to simultaneously reproduce a diverse array of independent macroscopic observations. It accurately predicts the temperature and polarization anisotropies of the cosmic microwave background (CMB), the late-time accelerated expansion of the universe as evidenced by the redshift-distance relation of Type Ia supernovae, and the large-scale clustering of galaxies mapping the [[dark-matter-power-spectrum|Dark matter power spectrum]] [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract), [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract). Within this model, dark matter provides the essential gravitational scaffolding necessary for hierarchical structure formation. By driving the collapse of small, early density perturbations, CDM forms the bound halos that eventually merge and accrete baryonic matter to construct the galaxy clusters and superclusters seen today [Frenk et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985Natur.317..595F/abstract), [Liddle et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PhR...231....1L/abstract).

Despite its profound predictive power on large scales, the $\Lambda$CDM model faces significant observational tensions and theoretical challenges that drive contemporary astrophysical research. On sub-galactic scales, standard N-body CDM simulations predict steeper central density profiles and a higher abundance of satellite dwarf galaxies than are locally observed. This "small-scale crisis" has sparked intense debate over whether these anomalies can be resolved by complex baryonic feedback mechanisms or if they necessitate [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]] and novel particle physics, such as [[warm-dark-matter|Warm dark matter]] or self-interacting dark matter. Furthermore, precision measurements have revealed statistically severe discrepancies between early-universe predictions and late-time observations—most notably the "Hubble tension" regarding the expansion rate ($H_0$) and the $S_8$ tension concerning the amplitude of matter clustering. Coupled with recent discoveries of surprisingly massive, evolved galaxies at extreme high redshifts, these unresolved issues continue to motivate deep theoretical explorations into dynamical [[dark-energy-models|Dark energy models]], modified gravity, and the fundamental physical nature of the dark universe.

## Introduction and Theoretical Framework

The $\Lambda$CDM model serves as the standard "concordance" parametrization of [[big-bang|Big Bang]] cosmology and the foundation of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]] [Hamilton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract). The model provides a remarkably successful framework for tracing [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]] from a primordial hot, dense state to the presently observed large-scale structure [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract). At its core, $\Lambda$CDM assumes that the dynamics of the [[universe|Universe]] are governed by General Relativity and that the distribution of matter and energy adheres to the Cosmological principle—the theoretical assumption that the universe is homogeneous and isotropic on the largest observable scales.

Mathematically, this large-scale symmetry is described by the FLRW metric, which constitutes the exact solution to Einstein's field equations for a uniform cosmos. Within this framework, a vast array of observational evidence overwhelmingly supports a spatially flat universe. A flat geometry dictates that the total global energy density must be exceptionally close to the Critical density, defined as
$$ \rho_{\rm crit} = \frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi G} $$
where $H_0$ is the present-day Hubble parameter.

The [[energy-content|Energy content]] of the $\Lambda$CDM model is partitioned into three primary components, parameterized by their fractional contribution to the critical density, $\Omega_i = \rho_i/\rho_{\rm crit}$. The dominant constituent is a [[positive-cosmological-constant|Positive cosmological constant]] ($\Lambda$), representing a constant vacuum energy density with an equation of state $w = -1$. This component is responsible for driving the late-time accelerated expansion of the universe, and current measurements indicate that it accounts for $\Omega_\Lambda \approx 0.70$ of the global energy budget [Chakravarty et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IJMPD..2630023C/abstract), [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract). 

The remaining $\sim 30\%$ of the energy budget consists of matter ($\Omega_m \approx 0.30$). The overwhelming majority of this matter fraction is [[non-baryonic-dark-matter|Non-baryonic dark matter]] in the form of [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] (CDM)—a collisionless, non-relativistic component that interacts primarily via gravity. Because of its low thermal velocity, CDM clusters efficiently and provides the foundational scaffolding for hierarchical structure formation. Meanwhile, ordinary baryonic matter (the protons, neutrons, and electrons that make up stars and gas) accounts for only $\sim 4-5\%$ of the total cosmic inventory [Hamilton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract), [Chakravarty et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IJMPD..2630023C/abstract).

The widespread acceptance of $\Lambda$CDM represented a major paradigm shift from prior theoretical expectations of an Einstein-de Sitter universe, which assumed a critical-density, matter-only cosmos ($\Omega_m = 1$, $\Omega_\Lambda = 0$) [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract). This shift was cemented by the convergence of independent observational probes, including the temperature and polarization anisotropies of the cosmic microwave background (CMB), the distance-redshift relation of Type Ia supernovae, and the scale of baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO) [Chakravarty et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IJMPD..2630023C/abstract), [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract). The [[fiducial-cosmological-model|Fiducial cosmological model]] of $\Lambda$CDM assumes that initial density perturbations are strictly adiabatic, though analyses of CMB data, stretching back to early WMAP results, allow for a small fraction of primordial isocurvature modes [Moodley et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PhRvD..70j3520M/abstract). Historically, constraints on isocurvature components in two-field inflation models limited their contribution to less than 6% even in early analyses [Pierpaoli et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999JHEP...10...15P/abstract). By utilizing just six fundamental parameters, this minimal theoretical framework successfully links the extreme conditions of the early universe to the cosmic web seen today, establishing itself as the robust baseline against which all [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]] are compared.

## Historical Development and the Emergence of Concordance

The Hot [[big-bang|Big Bang]] model, firmly established in the 1960s, successfully described primordial nucleosynthesis and the temperature of the cosmic microwave background (CMB). However, by the early 1980s, increasingly stringent upper limits on CMB temperature anisotropies made it clear that a purely baryonic universe could not form the observed large-scale structure within the age of the [[universe|Universe]]. This realization motivated the introduction of [[non-baryonic-dark-matter|Non-baryonic dark matter]]. While neutrino-dominated Hot Dark Matter (HDM) was initially explored, its large free-streaming length was found to erase small-scale perturbations. Consequently, theoretical focus shifted to [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] (CDM) models, which predicted a hierarchical, "bottom-up" formation of structure [Frenk et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985Natur.317..595F/abstract).

The Standard CDM (SCDM) model assumed a critically dense universe ($\Omega_m = 1$, $\Omega_\Lambda = 0$) and a scale-invariant primordial fluctuation spectrum. Despite early successes in simulating galactic halos, SCDM encountered a severe crisis by the early 1990s. It failed to simultaneously match the amplitude of small-scale clustering and the relatively high degree of large-scale power observed in galaxy surveys, nor could it consistently explain the observed peculiar velocity field of galaxies [Blumenthal et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ApJ...326..539B/abstract), [Davis et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992Natur.356..489D/abstract). Furthermore, local measurements yielding a high Hubble constant ($h \approx 0.75$) in an $\Omega_m=1$ cosmology implied an expansion age uncomfortably younger than the oldest globular clusters [Primack et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996dmu..conf....1P/abstract).

To salvage an $\Omega_m=1$ universe, researchers introduced Mixed Dark Matter (MDM) or the [[c-hdm-model|C+HDM model]], typically blending $\sim 70\%$ CDM with $\sim 30\%$ HDM in the form of [[massive-neutrino-background|Massive neutrino background]]. The neutrino free-streaming suppressed the excessive small-scale power of SCDM while retaining the large-scale structure, providing an improved fit to early CMB fluctuations and galaxy clustering [Davis et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992Natur.359..393D/abstract), [van Dalen et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...398...33V/abstract), [Ma et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...471...13M/abstract). 

Concurrently, other theorists explored [[low-density-cosmological-models|Low-density cosmological models]]. By introducing a [[positive-cosmological-constant|Positive cosmological constant]] ($\Lambda$) to maintain a spatially flat geometry ($\Omega_m + \Omega_\Lambda = 1$), the matter-radiation equality epoch was shifted. This intrinsically modified the [[dark-matter-power-spectrum|Dark matter power spectrum]] to better match observations of galaxy clustering and cluster abundances without the need for an HDM component [Efstathiou et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992MNRAS.258P...1E/abstract), [Kauffmann et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992MNRAS.258..511K/abstract), [Klypin et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...466...13K/abstract). Furthermore, a low-density, $\Lambda$-dominated universe naturally resolved the cosmic age problem by extending the expansion history [Primack et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996dmu..conf....1P/abstract).

The emergence of a true "concordance" model crystallized in the late 1990s. The normalization of the power spectrum by the COBE satellite provided stringent constraints that disfavored SCDM and tightly restricted the parameter space of alternative [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]]. The decisive paradigm shift arrived with distance-redshift measurements of Type Ia supernovae, which provided direct kinematic evidence for an accelerating cosmic expansion, effectively demanding $\Omega_\Lambda > 0$ [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract), [Johri et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph..7079J/abstract), [Chakravarty et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IJMPD..2630023C/abstract). Combining these supernova data with precise CMB anisotropy maps and large-scale structure statistics demonstrated that a flat universe dominated by vacuum energy ($\Omega_\Lambda \approx 0.7$) and cold dark matter ($\Omega_m \approx 0.3$) provided the most parsimonious fit to all available data. This powerful synthesis established the $\Lambda$CDM model as the standard paradigm of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]] [Hamilton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract), [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract).

## Observational Pillars of the Model

The observational authority of the $\Lambda$CDM framework is built upon the "concordance" of three independent and complementary cosmological probes: Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) anisotropies, the redshift-distance relation of Type Ia supernovae (SN Ia), and the Baryon Acoustic Oscillation (BAO) scale. Together, these pillars break parameter degeneracies and establish a precisely constrained model of the [[universe|Universe]] [Hamilton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract), [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract).

The angular power spectrum of **CMB anisotropies** provides a direct snapshot of the photon-baryon fluid at the epoch of recombination. Precision measurements by the **Planck satellite** and its predecessors demonstrate that the Universe is spatially flat, meaning its total mass-energy density is very close to the critical density ($\rho_{\rm crit}$). Furthermore, the relative amplitudes of the acoustic peaks tightly constrain the [[cosmic-baryon-density|Cosmic baryon density]] to roughly $4\%$ and the [[non-relativistic-matter-density-parameter|Non-relativistic matter density parameter]] ($\Omega_{m,0}$) to approximately $0.30$. Because baryons only account for a small fraction of this matter, the remaining $\sim 26\%$ must be composed of [[non-baryonic-dark-matter|Non-baryonic dark matter]] [Chakravarty et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IJMPD..2630023C/abstract).

Complementing the early-Universe physics of the CMB, observations of **Supernova Type Ia** provided the first direct evidence that cosmic expansion is currently accelerating. By plotting the **Hubble diagram** of these standardizable candles, astronomers can directly map the redshift-distance relation. The SN Ia data indicate that the Universe transitioned from a matter-dominated decelerating phase to a dark energy-dominated accelerating phase at a redshift of $z \approx 0.42 \pm 0.08$ [Daly et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007NuPhS.173...19D/abstract). In the standard model, this late-time acceleration is attributed to a [[positive-cosmological-constant|Positive cosmological constant]] ($\Lambda$), which comprises roughly $70\%$ of the global [[energy-content|Energy content]] [Chakravarty et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IJMPD..2630023C/abstract).

The third pillar, **BAO**, provides a late-time "standard ruler" based on the imprint of acoustic waves frozen into the large-scale distribution of galaxies. By tracing the BAO scale across different redshifts, galaxy surveys offer an independent geometric measure of the expansion history. When BAO data are combined with SN Ia and CMB measurements in an inverse distance ladder approach, the parameters of the $\Lambda$CDM model are determined with extraordinary precision. For instance, this combination yields:
$$ \Omega_m = 0.301 \pm 0.008, \quad \Omega_k = -0.003 \pm 0.003 $$
alongside a Hubble constant of $H_0 = 67.3 \pm 1.1 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$, strongly corroborating a flat geometry [Aubourg et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..92l3516A/abstract). An additional, independent probe of the late-time expansion is the integrated [[sachs-wolfe-effect|Sachs-Wolfe effect]] (ISW). Measurements correlating CMB temperature maps with the distribution of galaxies and active galactic nuclei (AGN) from the WISE survey yield an ISW amplitude entirely consistent with [[fiducial-cosmological-model|Fiducial cosmological model]] predictions, further confirming the presence of dark energy [Ferraro et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..91h3533F/abstract).

Furthermore, the abundance of large gravitational arcs in galaxy clusters serves as a critical test of the epoch of structure formation; early studies of arc statistics suggested specific tensions with standard CDM predictions regarding cluster potentials [Bartelmann et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&A...330....1B/abstract). Despite the global success of these pillars, the literature explicitly recognizes significant and growing observational conflicts. Most notably, there is a $4-6\sigma$ tension between the value of $H_0$ inferred from the CMB and the value measured from the local distance ladder [Schöneberg et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhR...984....1S/abstract). Indeed, when employing Gaussian processes to infer the cosmic expansion history in a model-independent manner, the results show remarkable consistency with baseline $\Lambda$CDM predictions across the board, except for this glaring $H_0$ measurement discrepancy [Joudaki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..97l3501J/abstract). It remains heavily debated whether this discrepancy reflects unknown late-time astrophysical modeling errors or signals new early-Universe physics [Haridasu et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.103f3539H/abstract), [Kable et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240311916K/abstract). Furthermore, while a constant vacuum energy ($w = -1$) remains the most parsimonious baseline, recent combinations of modern BAO and SN Ia datasets have produced $2.3\sigma$ to $4.4\sigma$ statistical hints favoring dynamical [[dark-energy-models|Dark energy models]] characterized by a time-varying equation of state [Ishak et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722856I/abstract).

## Large-Scale Structure and the Halo Model

In the $\Lambda$CDM framework, the formation of large-scale structure is driven by the gravitational collapse of collisionless [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]]. These collapsed regions form bound dark matter halos, which serve as the fundamental gravitational scaffolding for the subsequent accretion of baryonic matter and the formation of galaxies [Iliev et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12427I/abstract). The growth of these structures follows a paradigm of hierarchical clustering: small-mass objects collapse first at high redshifts and subsequently merge to form progressively larger systems, such as massive galaxies and galaxy clusters [Iliev et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12427I/abstract).

The statistical properties of the initial density fluctuations are characterized by the [[dark-matter-power-spectrum|Dark matter power spectrum]], $P(k)$. To predict the abundance of bound structures analytically, cosmologists rely on the Press-Schechter formalism and its subsequent extensions (such as the [[sheth-tormen-mass-function|Sheth-Tormen mass function]]). These mathematical frameworks allow for the calculation of the halo mass function—the comoving number density of dark matter halos as a parameter of mass—as well as the merger rates and progenitor distributions of the halo population [Asgari et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..39A/abstract). 

While analytic methods offer critical insights, fully capturing the complex, non-linear dynamics of structure formation requires high-resolution N-body simulations. These simulations demonstrate that dark matter halos achieve a universal, self-similar equilibrium structure, typically parameterized by the Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) density profile, across a wide range of mass scales [Navarro et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..117..404N/abstract), [Diemand et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASL.....4..297D/abstract). While large structures follow this universal form, at the extreme low-mass end of the spectrum, Earth-mass microhalos at the CDM cutoff scale exhibit density profiles steeper than the standard NFW profile ($\rho \propto r^{-1.4}$) due to the small-scale power spectrum cutoff [Anderhalden et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013JCAP...04..009A/abstract).

Together, these theoretical and numerical tools form the basis of the "halo model," a powerful phenomenological framework that calculates the non-linear clustering of the [[universe|Universe]] by assuming all matter is partitioned into halos of varying masses and density profiles [Asgari et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..39A/abstract). While this halo model correctly describes large-scale clustering, high-resolution N-body simulations reveal persistent discrepancies on [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]] (distances $\lesssim 1 \text{ Mpc}$). These discrepancies form the basis of the so-called "small-scale crisis" of CDM, motivating deep investigations into baryonic feedback and alternative [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]].

## Small-Scale Challenges and the 'Crisis' of CDM

Discrepancies between local galactic observations and pure $N$-body [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] (CDM) simulations constitute what is often termed the "small-scale crisis" of the [[fiducial-cosmological-model|Fiducial cosmological model]]. These tensions within [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]] generally fall into three primary categories: the missing satellites problem, the cusp-core problem, and the too-big-to-fail puzzle.

The **missing satellites problem** arises from the prediction that galaxy-mass dark matter halos should host thousands of bound subhalos. However, the observed number of dwarf satellite galaxies around the Milky Way and Andromeda is orders of magnitude lower than the predicted subhalo abundance [Moore et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.370..629M/abstract), [Bullock et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55..343B/abstract). 

The **cusp-core problem** highlights a structural discrepancy in the central regions of dark matter halos. Collisionless CDM simulations predict steeply rising central density profiles, such as the Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile, where density scales as $\rho \propto r^{-1}$ at small radii. In contrast, kinematic observations of dwarf spheroidal and low-surface-brightness galaxies consistently favor flattened, constant-density central "cores" [Moore et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.370..629M/abstract), [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract).

The **too-big-to-fail** (TBTF) problem represents a related kinematic anomaly. The most massive, dense subhalos formed in CDM simulations of Milky Way-like systems are systematically too concentrated and massive to host the brightest observed Local Group satellites [Primack et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AnP...524..535P/abstract), [Bullock et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55..343B/abstract). Because these massive subhalos should theoretically be "too big to fail" at forming stars, their absence in the local observational record poses a significant challenge.

In addition to these core issues, recent observations of galaxy cluster substructures reveal them to be more than an order of magnitude more efficient at gravitational lensing than standard N-body simulations predict [Meneghetti et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Sci...369.1347M/abstract). Furthermore, satellite galaxies sometimes reside in highly flattened, kinematically coherent planes, referred to as a "Disk of Satellites." While this has been cited as a challenge to the isotropic accretion predicted by $\Lambda$CDM, recent high-resolution hydrodynamic simulations suggest that these planar structures may simply be transient phenomena or statistical selection effects resulting from small sample sizes [Maji et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170200497M/abstract), [Uzeirbegovic et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.535.3775U/abstract). Additionally, the pure structural survival of disk-dominated galaxies in a violently merging $\Lambda$CDM universe necessitates specific merger mass-ratio criteria. Spheroid formation is primarily triggered by mergers where the mass ratio exceeds 0.3, meaning massive disks can only survive if they avoid such major mergers over their history [Koda et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0711.3014K/abstract).

A major ongoing debate is whether these small-scale discrepancies expose a fundamental failure of the collisionless CDM paradigm, or if they can be resolved by properly accounting for baryonic physics. It is now widely recognized that baryonic feedback mechanisms—such as supernova explosions, stellar winds, and gas cooling—can drastically alter the dark matter distribution. Repeated episodes of gas expulsion can dynamically heat the dark matter, flattening central cusps into cores [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract), [Del Popolo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98f3517D/abstract). Furthermore, the ionizing cosmic ultraviolet background and stellar feedback can efficiently suppress star formation in low-mass halos, naturally resulting in a vast population of invisible, "dark" subhalos that could resolve both the missing satellites and TBTF problems without altering the [[dark-sector|Dark sector]] [Sawala et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.2748S/abstract), [Navarro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019IAUS..344..455N/abstract). 

If baryonic solutions prove insufficient to fully explain these phenomena, these anomalies may expose a fundamental misunderstanding of the dark sector, motivating the exploration of alternative [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]].

## Alternative Dark Matter Candidates

While the baseline $\Lambda$CDM model relying on standard [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] (CDM) excels at describing the large-scale structure of the [[universe|Universe]], the persistent small-scale challenges have motivated the search for [[viable-dark-matter-candidates|Viable dark matter candidates]] that intrinsically suppress small-scale structure or alter inner halo dynamics while preserving the large-scale successes of CDM. At a fundamental level, confirming any particle candidate requires experimental validation. However, [[dark-matter-limits|Dark matter limits]] remain elusive despite extensive, multi-pronged search strategies encompassing direct detection experiments, searches for indirect annihilation signals (such as neutrinos and gamma rays), and attempts at [[standard-model-particle-physics|Standard Model (particle physics)]] collider production [Baudis et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JPhG...43d4001B/abstract). Furthermore, the nature of the dark matter particle could alter early astrophysical processes; for instance, the "dark stars" hypothesis suggests that the first stars in the universe could have been powered by dark matter heating rather than nuclear fusion [Freese et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IJMPD..2630012F/abstract).

**Warm Dark Matter**
[[warm-dark-matter|Warm dark matter]] (WDM) posits particles with masses typically in the keV range, possessing a non-negligible free-streaming length that erases primordial density fluctuations on dwarf-galaxy scales. A widely studied WDM candidate is the $\sim 7$ keV sterile neutrino [Khimey et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.4139K/abstract). By smoothing out small-scale power, [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]] based on WDM naturally predict fewer satellite halos, addressing the missing satellites problem. However, WDM models are tightly constrained by observations. Data regarding the universal surface densities of dwarf galaxies require WDM particle masses to be $\gtrsim 3$ keV [Hayashi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...803L..11H/abstract). At these masses, the predictions of WDM become nearly indistinguishable from standard CDM [Brooks et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AnP...526..294B/abstract). Furthermore, when the ionizing background from cosmic reionization is fully accounted for, WDM models may actually under-predict the observed abundance of dwarf galaxies [Bullock et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.11005B/abstract).

**Self-Interacting Dark Matter**
To resolve the cusp-core problem without erasing small-scale halos, researchers have proposed Self-Interacting Dark Matter (SIDM). In this framework, dark matter particles undergo elastic scattering, often modeled via a light scalar mediator with Sommerfeld enhancement [Kao et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.103e5021K/abstract). These collisions transport heat from the hot, velocity-dispersion-dominated outer halo to the cooler central regions, effectively flattening the density cusp into a core. SIDM can successfully reproduce the diversity of observed galactic rotation curves [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract). Interestingly, in certain high-density environments, continuous self-interaction can trigger gravothermal collapse, locally steepening the density profile to $\gamma_{\rm 2D} \approx -2$, a phenomenon potentially observed in the subhalos of strongly lensed systems [Enzi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540..247E/abstract).

**Fuzzy and Quantum Wave Dark Matter**
The [[quantum-wave-dark-matter-model|Quantum wave dark matter model]] (also known as fuzzy, wave, or scalar field dark matter) replaces the standard particle paradigm with ultralight scalars or bosons. With particle masses on the order of $m \sim 10^{-22}$ eV, the corresponding de Broglie wavelength stretches to macroscopic astrophysical scales of $\lambda \sim 1$ kpc [Hui et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PhRvD..95d3541H/abstract). Below the Jeans scale, the quantum uncertainty principle counters gravitational collapse, preventing the formation of steep central cusps. Instead, these models predict that galaxies harbor central Bose-Einstein condensate "solitonic" cores, which transition into standard fluctuating CDM-like envelopes at larger radii [Schive et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014NatPh..10..496S/abstract), [Bernal et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3135B/abstract). Furthermore, full non-linear simulations of fuzzy dark matter demonstrate a significant reduction in the abundance of the halo mass function below a characteristic scale, which potentially alleviates the need for extreme baryonic feedback to solve small-scale anomalies [May et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.2603M/abstract). Intriguingly, macroscopic astrophysical structures, such as the observed Fermi Bubbles in the Milky Way, have been proposed as a natural manifestation of excited states within these Scalar Field Dark Matter (SFDM) halos [Matos et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220313218M/abstract).

The quantum interference inherent to these models delays early galaxy formation to redshifts $z \lesssim 13$ [Schive et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014NatPh..10..496S/abstract). However, fuzzy dark matter faces ongoing observational tensions. Fits to the Milky Way's rotation curve suggest a boson mass of $m = 2.5_{-2.0}^{+3.6} \times 10^{-21}$ eV [Maleki et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.101j3504M/abstract), but Lyman-$\alpha$ forest data prefer slightly higher masses ($m \gtrsim 10^{-21}$ eV) where the wave-like suppression becomes less effective at solving the small-scale anomalies [Hui et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PhRvD..95d3541H/abstract). Looking ahead, the 21 cm power spectrum across the Cosmic Dawn provides a promising future probe to discriminate between CDM and fuzzy dark matter based on the delayed onset of reionization [Jones et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913....7J/abstract).

**Atomic Dark Matter and Alternative Theories**
Another intriguing extension is Atomic Dark Matter (ADM), which introduces a dissipative subcomponent that can cool to form dark disks and clumps. Even constituting a small fraction of the total dark matter, this dissipative component can significantly impact galactic star formation histories and overall morphology [Roy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954L..40R/abstract). Alternatively, some [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]] dispense with dark matter halos entirely. Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), for instance, predicts a lower frequency of massive classical bulges compared to $\Lambda$CDM, because the lack of dark matter halos reduces the dynamical friction necessary for massive clumps to coalesce at galactic centers [Combes et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..413C/abstract). Another theoretical avenue introduces the possibility of Q-balls as a unified framework that mimics standard CDM at cosmological scales while exhibiting MOND-like behavior at galactic scales [Mandal et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025EPJC...85..178M/abstract).

## The Dark Sector: Dark Energy and Modified Gravity

While the baseline model of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]] assumes a [[positive-cosmological-constant|Positive cosmological constant]] ($\Lambda$) with a static equation of state $w = -1$, persistent observational tensions have motivated extensive theoretical exploration of the [[dark-sector|Dark sector]]. These extensions generally fall into two categories: dynamical dark energy models and modifications to the laws of gravity.

**Dynamical and Interacting Dark Energy**
To address discrepancies such as the Hubble tension, the [[wcdm-extension|wCDM extension]] allows the dark energy equation of state to vary with time. This is commonly parameterized within the [[w0-wa-parameter-space|w0-wa parameter space]] as $w(a) = w_0 + w_a(1-a)$ [Zablocki et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..94d3525Z/abstract). Recent combinations of DESI baryon acoustic oscillation measurements, cosmic microwave background (CMB) data, and supernovae have shown emerging statistical preferences—ranging from $2.3\sigma$ to $4.4\sigma$—for dynamical dark energy over a static $\Lambda$ [Ishak et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722856I/abstract). However, other comprehensive broad-survey constraints remain consistent with $w = -1$ at the $1\sigma$ level [Abbott et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.107h3504A/abstract), leaving the literature in a state of active debate.

Furthermore, to alleviate the "cosmic coincidence problem"—the unresolved question of why the [[non-relativistic-matter-density-parameter|Non-relativistic matter density parameter]] and the dark energy density are of the same order of magnitude at the present epoch—some theorists have proposed the [[interaction-dark-energy-model|Interaction Dark Energy model]]. In these models, dark matter and dark energy exchange energy and momentum through non-gravitational couplings, creating distinct [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]] [Tarrant et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvD..85b3503T/abstract), [Cheng et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.102d3517C/abstract). Recent analyses incorporating DESI BAO data have yielded robust evidence for non-vanishing interactions between dark matter and dark energy at the 3 to 5 sigma level [Li et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107361L/abstract). A related theoretical framework is Holographic dark energy, particularly formulations at the Ricci scale, which naturally enforce an interacting dark sector to maintain a finite ratio of matter to vacuum energy throughout cosmic history [Durán et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvD..83b3504D/abstract), [Durán et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011JPhCS.314a2058D/abstract). While these interactions can theoretically suppress the late-time growth of structures and alleviate redshift-space distortion tensions, scattering rates between dark matter and dark energy are currently tightly constrained by CMB lensing measurements [Laguë et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.110b3536L/abstract). Any interacting model must satisfy strict bounds; for instance, the decay rate of vacuum energy into cold dark matter is extremely limited, as such decay would unacceptably dilute the density fluctuation spectrum observed at low redshifts [Opher et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PhRvD..70f3529O/abstract).

**Modified Gravity and Alternative Dark Matter**
Alternatively, the missing mass and accelerated expansion of the universe can be modeled as manifestations of modified gravitational dynamics rather than new particle species or vacuum energy. Among these [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]] is f(R) gravity, which replaces the Ricci scalar $R$ in the Einstein-Hilbert action with a more general function. In f(R) gravity, the effective [[dark-matter-power-spectrum|Dark matter power spectrum]] can deviate significantly from standard $\Lambda$CDM predictions. N-body simulations demonstrate that for background field strength parameters of $f_{R0} = -10^{-4}$, the deviation in the matter power spectrum can exceed $150\%$ [He et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..92j3508H/abstract).

On galactic scales, extensions rooted in modified Newtonian dynamics (MOND) successfully explain characteristic acceleration scales without requiring [[non-baryonic-dark-matter|Non-baryonic dark matter]] halos. A prominent synthesis of these concepts is [[modified-dark-matter|Modified Dark Matter]] (MDM), a phenomenological model inspired by gravitational thermodynamics. In MDM, effective dark matter mass profiles are coupled to both the baryonic mass and the cosmological constant [Ng et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160200055N/abstract). MDM accurately reproduces galactic rotation curves and yields consistent mass profiles for galaxy clusters, suggesting that dark matter phenomenology may contain non-trivial information about the vacuum energy itself [Edmonds et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IJMPA..3250108E/abstract), [Edmonds et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180100160E/abstract). However, a fundamental challenge for many modified gravity models is their degeneracy with standard particle dark matter; many isotropic cosmological observables, including the CMB and linear power spectrum, can be equally well-described by either paradigm, requiring observations of large-scale anisotropic bulk flows to formally break the degeneracy [Moffat et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260122207M/abstract). This degeneracy persists on smaller scales as well; for instance, recent Baryonic Tully-Fisher Relation (BTFR) tests using SDSS MaNGA data show that massive spiral and elliptical galaxies are consistent with both $\Lambda$CDM and MOND predictions [Ravi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251218577R/abstract).

## Current Tensions: H0 and Sigma-8

While the $\Lambda$CDM model provides an exceptionally successful description of [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]], it is currently challenged by statistically significant discrepancies between early-universe predictions and late-universe observations. The two most prominent issues are the "Hubble tension" and the $\sigma_8$ (or $S_8$) tension.

The Hubble tension refers to the $4\sigma$ to $6\sigma$ disagreement between the present-day expansion rate, $H_0$, inferred from the cosmic microwave background (CMB) and the value measured directly in the local [[universe|Universe]] [Schöneberg et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhR...984....1S/abstract). Under the $\Lambda$CDM assumption, Planck CMB data—calibrated by the sound horizon acting as a standard ruler—predicts $H_0 \approx 67.4 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$. Conversely, local measurements utilizing the local distance ladder (primarily calibrated via Cepheid variables and the Tip of the Red Giant Branch) consistently favor higher values, typically $H_0 \gtrsim 73 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$ [Freedman et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...11..050F/abstract). Recent high-resolution observations by the James Webb Space Telescope have reduced the scatter due to Cepheid crowding by a factor of two, which will ultimately help determine whether the tension is due to photometric systematics or requires new physics [Freedman et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...11..050F/abstract).

Concurrently, the $S_8$ tension highlights a discrepancy in the late-time amplitude of matter clustering, parameterized by $\sigma_8$ (or the combination $S_8 \equiv \sigma_8 \sqrt{\Omega_m/0.3}$). Constraints from low-redshift weak lensing and galaxy clustering surveys yield structure growth amplitudes that are $2\sigma$ to $3\sigma$ lower than the expectations extrapolated from primary CMB anisotropies [Madhavacheril et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108152M/abstract). 

Theoretical efforts to resolve these tensions have proven difficult, as modifications that alleviate one discrepancy often exacerbate the other. For example, Early Dark Energy (EDE) models attempt to solve the Hubble tension by injecting a new energy component just prior to recombination, thereby reducing the sound horizon and allowing fits with $H_0 > 72 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$ [Kable et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240311916K/abstract). However, preserving the excellent fit to the CMB power spectra in EDE requires increasing the [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] density, which subsequently drives up the predicted $S_8$ value, worsening the clustering tension. Furthermore, such early-time modifications are highly constrained by the early integrated [[sachs-wolfe-effect|Sachs-Wolfe effect]] (eISW), which perfectly matches $\Lambda$CDM predictions ($A_{\mathrm{eISW}} = 0.988 \pm 0.027$) [Vagnozzi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104f3524V/abstract). 

Other proposed solutions include late-time modifications to the dark sector. Models featuring [[interaction-dark-energy-model|Interaction Dark Energy model]] mechanisms or eV-scale mass thresholds can raise the expansion rate slightly (e.g., $H_0 \approx 69 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$), but these similarly fail to substantially improve the $S_8$ tension [Allali et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.108b3527A/abstract). Another novel approach involves extending $\Lambda$CDM with a cosmological constant that undergoes a rapid sign switch at redshifts $z \sim 1.1-2.4$; this mechanism can theoretically alleviate both the Hubble tension and discrepancies in Lyman-$\alpha$ forest measurements [Akarsu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104l3512A/abstract). The literature currently reflects an ambiguous and conflicting landscape regarding the optimal path forward. While many analyses search for unknown astrophysical systematics, emerging combinations of Baryon Acoustic Oscillation data (such as those from DESI), CMB, and supernovae have shown a $2.3\sigma$ to $4.4\sigma$ statistical preference for dynamical [[dark-energy-models|Dark energy models]] over a strict cosmological constant. This potentially points toward the [[w0-wa-parameter-space|w0-wa parameter space]] as a phenomenological remedy, though a definitive consensus remains elusive [Ishak et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722856I/abstract). Alternatively, some theorists explore explanations outside standard dark energy paradigms; for example, anomalous CMB cold spots and the unexpected ISW imprint of supervoids can be explained by the AvERA model, which serves as an alternative to dark energy and attempts to offer a common explanation for the Hubble tension [Kovács et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499..320K/abstract).

## High-Redshift Frontiers and JWST Observations

In the standard $\Lambda$CDM model, the assembly of galaxies follows a strictly hierarchical process driven by the gravitational collapse of [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]]. Small dark matter halos form first and subsequently merge to build the massive halos that host contemporary galaxies. This bottom-up sequence dictates strict theoretical limits on the timing of structure formation and the evolution of the [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]]. However, recent deep-space observations have presented a formidable stress test for this timeline.

Data from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have revealed a population of exceptionally massive and structurally evolved high-redshift galaxies at $z > 10$. Existing merely $\sim 0.3$ Gyr after the [[big-bang|Big Bang]], the masses and morphologies of these systems resemble those of galaxies that have evolved for billions of years [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.3385G/abstract), [John et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230604577J/abstract). The unexpected abundance of such massive structures at early epochs has been dubbed the "Impossible early galaxy" problem, as it is in strong tension with $\Lambda$CDM predictions for the [[sheth-tormen-mass-function|Sheth-Tormen mass function]] (specifically the halo mass function). This 'Impossible early galaxy' problem possesses deep historical roots; the discovery of the unusually old, high-redshift galaxy 53W091 previously served as an early stress test for modified cold dark matter models decades prior to JWST [Kashlinsky et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...474L..81K/abstract).

To reconcile these observations within the standard cosmological framework, astrophysical processes in the early [[universe|Universe]] must have been radically different from those at lower redshifts. Explanations preserving the $\Lambda$CDM model require near-perfect star formation efficiencies, the rapid and widespread formation of massive Population III stars, or super-Eddington accretion rates onto primordial black hole seeds [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.3385G/abstract). Complicating the interpretation, simulations indicate that uncertainties in baryonic physics—such as dust attenuation and the efficiency of stellar feedback—are highly degenerate with the underlying dark matter physics. This makes it difficult to firmly rule out the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm using high-redshift luminosity functions alone [Khimey et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.4139K/abstract).

In addition to early galaxy counts, the $\Lambda$CDM model is tested by the discovery of extreme large-scale structures, such as the "Cosmic Himalayas," an immense quasar overdensity at $z \sim 2$. While traditional Gaussian statistical analyses estimated this structure to be a catastrophic $16.9\sigma$ anomaly, recent count-in-cells and nearest-neighbor analyses demonstrate that such extreme overdensities naturally arise in the heavy-tailed, non-Gaussian regime of the $\Lambda$CDM density field [Kuwayama et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251224966K/abstract). 

If standard astrophysics proves insufficient to explain the JWST observations and the rapid onset of Reionization, the data may point toward [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]]. For instance, early dark energy (EDE) models inherently predict a higher abundance of massive galaxies at $z > 10$ compared to $\Lambda$CDM, providing a better fit to the extreme high-redshift luminosity functions [Liu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533..860L/abstract). More radical proposals, such as eternal coasting models [John et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230604577J/abstract) or hybrid models combining covarying coupling constants with tired light (CCC+TL), attempt to resolve the tension by mathematically stretching the age of the Universe to provide ample time for early galaxy assembly [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.3385G/abstract). 

At present, the literature remains deeply divided over whether the high-redshift frontier represents a genuine breakdown of $\Lambda$CDM cosmology or simply a failure of current semi-analytic and hydrodynamical models to adequately capture extreme early-Universe astrophysics.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite its profound success in describing the macroscopic universe, the $\Lambda$CDM model leaves the fundamental physical nature of the [[dark-sector|Dark sector]] as one of the most pressing open questions in [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]]. Future research is heavily focused on addressing theoretical shortcomings and resolving emerging observational anomalies that challenge the standard paradigm. 

A primary driver of theoretical extensions is the $4-6\sigma$ statistical tension between local distance-ladder measurements of the Hubble constant ($H_0$) and values inferred from the cosmic microwave background (CMB) [Schöneberg et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhR...984....1S/abstract), [Freedman et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...11..050F/abstract). Proposed early-universe solutions, such as Early Dark Energy (EDE), can successfully reduce the sound horizon at recombination to resolve the $H_0$ discrepancy, but often exacerbate the $S_8$ tension regarding the amplitude of late-time matter clustering [Vagnozzi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104f3524V/abstract), [Kable et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240311916K/abstract). Late-time solutions typically invoke dynamical [[dark-energy-models|Dark energy models]] or dark sector interactions. However, the literature is currently conflicting regarding the evidence for a time-varying dark energy equation of state. While some joint analyses of DESI baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO), CMB, and supernovae data report up to a $4.4\sigma$ preference for dynamical dark energy within the [[w0-wa-parameter-space|w0-wa parameter space]] [Ishak et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722856I/abstract), other combined datasets show no significant deviation from a cosmological constant ($w=-1$) [Abbott et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.107h3504A/abstract), [Dong et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241112594D/abstract). Furthermore, models featuring kinetic scattering between dark matter and dark energy are tightly constrained; recent ACT DR6 CMB lensing measurements restrict the dimensionless interaction strength to $\Gamma_{\rm DMDE}/(H_0 \rho_c) < 0.905$ at the 68% confidence level [Laguë et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.110b3536L/abstract).

On sub-galactic scales, the standard paradigm faces a well-documented [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]] crisis (the "small-scale crisis") comprising the cusp-core, missing satellites, and too-big-to-fail problems [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract), [Bullock et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55..343B/abstract). A major ongoing debate is whether these discrepancies indicate a failure of the collisionless [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] hypothesis, or if they can be resolved by baryonic physics. Several high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations suggest that baryonic feedback—such as supernova-driven outflows and dynamical friction—can flatten central density cusps and suppress galaxy formation in low-mass subhalos, reconciling observations with $\Lambda$CDM [Sawala et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.2748S/abstract), [Del Popolo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98f3517D/abstract). Conversely, these small-scale anomalies strongly motivate alternative [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]] such as [[warm-dark-matter|Warm dark matter]] or self-interacting dark matter [Brooks et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AnP...526..294B/abstract). Another active area of research is fuzzy dark matter (or [[quantum-wave-dark-matter-model|Quantum wave dark matter model]]), which postulates an ultra-light boson of mass $m \sim 10^{-22}$ eV. This mass scale naturally suppresses small-scale structure through quantum interference, forming solitonic cores in dwarf galaxies [Schive et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014NatPh..10..496S/abstract), [Hui et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PhRvD..95d3541H/abstract).

In the realm of [[neutrino-cosmology|Neutrino cosmology]], determining the absolute mass scale of neutrinos remains a critical objective. While standard analyses provide tight upper bounds, some recent combinations of BOSS, Planck, and SPT data have yielded $4\sigma$ evidence for a non-zero sum of neutrino masses at $\sum m_\nu = 0.22 \pm 0.06$ eV [Chudaykin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220303666C/abstract). Intriguingly, nonlocal gravity models can resolve $H_0$-$\Omega_m$ tensions by predicting a non-vanishing absolute neutrino mass of approximately $0.21$ eV, aligning closely with these emerging observational hints [Dirian et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PhRvD..96h3513D/abstract). Concurrently, the standard timeline of early structure formation is being tested by high-redshift observations; JWST discoveries of massive, evolved galaxies at $z > 10$ (roughly $0.3$ Gyr after the Big Bang) are currently in strong tension with $\Lambda$CDM predictions [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.3385G/abstract), [Liu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533..860L/abstract). 

Next-generation surveys are uniquely positioned to test these [[beyond-lambda-cdm-models|Beyond-Lambda CDM models]]. The Euclid satellite will map the large-scale clustering of matter and weak gravitational lensing to place unprecedented constraints on modified gravity theories and [[generalized-dark-matter|Generalized Dark Matter]] [Tutusaus et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180506202T/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209748E/abstract). Simultaneously, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's LSST will probe the expansion history and cosmic shear, potentially reducing the uncertainty on the neutrino mass sum to $\sim 30$ meV when combined with future CMB experiments [Mishra-Sharma et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..97l3544M/abstract). Together, these observatories will rigorously determine whether current cosmological tensions necessitate new fundamental physics or simply refinements within the standard model.
