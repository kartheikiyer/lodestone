## Article

The **cosmic baryon density** represents the total mass-energy density of ordinary, baryonic matter—primarily protons, neutrons, and electrons—in the [[universe|Universe]]. It is conventionally expressed using the dimensionless [[omega-parameters|Omega parameters]] as $\Omega_b$, defined as the ratio of the mean baryonic [[mass-density|Mass density]] to the critical density. Within the standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], baryons account for approximately 4% to 5% of the total [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]] and roughly 15% to 20% of the total matter content, with the remainder composed of [[non-baryonic-dark-matter|Non-baryonic dark matter]] (specifically [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]]) and dark energy. In precision cosmology, the parameter is frequently formulated as the physical baryon density, $\omega_b \equiv \Omega_b h^2$, which isolates the matter content from the late-time [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] and directly parameterizes the primordial baryon-to-photon ratio established during the [[big-bang|Big Bang]].

The fundamental value of $\Omega_b$ is tightly constrained by independent physical processes occurring in the early universe. During primordial nucleosynthesis, the fusion of light elements—most notably deuterium and helium—served as an exquisite baryometer, directly tying the surviving isotopic abundances to the baryon density. Hundreds of thousands of years later, the acoustic physics of the tightly coupled photon-baryon fluid imprinted distinct [[temperature-anisotropies|Temperature anisotropies]] on the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] (CMB). The relative heights of the CMB acoustic peaks provide a highly precise measurement of $\omega_b$. Furthermore, the sound horizon of these primordial waves became frozen into the spatial distribution of galaxies as [[baryon-acoustic-oscillations-bao|Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO)]] [Eisenstein et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...504L..57E/abstract), [Blake et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...594..665B/abstract). The BAO scale acts as a robust standard ruler that anchors the geometry of the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]] and serves as a fundamental pillar of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]].

While early-universe observations established a rigorous baseline for the total baryon budget, local censuses of stars and cold gas historically fell short of this predicted value, leading to the "missing baryon problem." This discrepancy was a major driving force in astrophysics, eventually resolved by the understanding that the vast majority of late-time baryons reside in a diffuse, highly ionized state known as the Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM) woven throughout the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]]. Today, novel techniques such as the dispersion measures of Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) act as direct probes of the column density for this elusive gas, confirming the CMB-predicted density. On smaller scales, the [[hot-gas-mass-fraction|Hot gas mass fraction]] in massive galaxy clusters provides a localized measurement of the universal baryon-to-total mass ratio, an observation that historically provided the first compelling evidence against a matter-only universe [White et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993Natur.366..429W/abstract). 

Despite the success of the standard cosmological framework, several outstanding questions regarding the cosmic baryon density remain. The article below details the historical evolution of the baryon budget and explores persistent anomalies such as the "Lithium problem," where theoretical nucleosynthesis predictions severely overproduce lithium-7 compared to stellar observations. Furthermore, it examines how the violent redistribution of gas by active galactic nuclei and supernovae feedback alters the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] on non-linear scales. Understanding these complex baryonic effects is now recognized as essential for interpreting weak lensing data and resolving contemporary cosmological tensions, including the disparities in the Hubble constant ($H_0$) and the amplitude of structural growth ($S_8$).

## Fundamental Definitions and Parameterization

The cosmic baryon density is conventionally parameterized by the dimensionless [[omega-parameters|Omega parameters]], specifically $\Omega_b$. This parameter is defined as the ratio of the present-day mean baryonic [[mass-density|Mass density]], $\rho_b$, to the critical density of the [[universe|Universe]], $\rho_{\rm crit}$:
$$ \Omega_b = \frac{\rho_b}{\rho_{\rm crit}} $$
where $\rho_{\rm crit} = \frac{3 H_0^2}{8 \pi G}$ and $H_0$ is the Hubble constant. 

In the standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], the total [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]] is dominated by dark energy ($\Omega_\Lambda \approx 0.7$) and non-relativistic matter ($\Omega_m \approx 0.3$) [Hamilton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract). The matter component is further subdivided into baryons and [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] (CDM), such that $\Omega_m = \Omega_b + \Omega_{cdm}$. A strong consensus from multiple independent cosmological probes—including the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] (CMB), Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN), and [[baryon-acoustic-oscillations-bao|Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO)]]—converges on a value of $\Omega_b \approx 0.04$ to $0.05$ [Rees et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003RSPTA.361.2427R/abstract), [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract). Consequently, baryons constitute approximately 5% of the total mass-energy density of the Universe and account for roughly 15% to 20% of the total matter content, with the remainder existing in some non-baryonic form.

In cosmological analyses, the baryon density is frequently expressed as the physical baryon density parameter, $\omega_b \equiv \Omega_b h^2$, where $h$ is the dimensionless Hubble parameter. This quantity is independent of the late-time expansion history and is directly tied to the thermal history of the early Universe. Specifically, $\omega_b$ dictates the primordial baryon-to-photon ratio, $\eta$. Measurements of the CMB power spectrum provide high-precision constraints on this ratio; for instance, early WMAP data constrained the parameter to $\eta = (6.23 \pm 0.17) \times 10^{-10}$, a value in excellent agreement with independent constraints derived from the observed abundances of primordial light elements.

Despite tight constraints on the [[total-baryon-fraction|Total baryon fraction]] from the early Universe, local censuses of stars and cold neutral gas historically fell short, leading to the "missing baryon problem." While stars represent a highly visible component of the cosmic baryon density, they constitute less than 10% of the total baryon budget, with observations indicating that approximately half of the present-day stellar mass formed before a redshift of $z = 1.3$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Notably, the extremely high stellar masses observed in some early galaxies can be used to set lower limits on host halo number densities. For the most massive galaxies, these implied densities approach the theoretical limits of structure formation within the $\Lambda$CDM framework at $z > 4$ [Behroozi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.5382B/abstract). The overarching missing mass discrepancy has recently been resolved by observations of the diffuse intergalactic medium and Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs), which successfully recover the full cosmic baryon density predicted by the $\Lambda$CDM framework [Connor et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240916952C/abstract), [Connor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1226C/abstract).

## Historical Evolution of the Baryon Budget

The [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]] of the baryon budget is deeply intertwined with the establishment of the [[big-bang|Big Bang]] paradigm. In the late 1940s, the "perfect cosmological principle" was proposed, positing that the [[universe|Universe]] is invariant not only in space but also under displacements in time. This led to the steady-state cosmology—later modified by some into a [[quasi-steady-state|Quasi-steady state]] model—which required the continuous creation of matter to maintain a constant mean mass density as the universe expanded. Conversely, George Gamow and collaborators argued that the early universe was exceptionally hot and dense, proposing that light elements were synthesized via thermonuclear reactions during this primordial epoch. The discovery of the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] (CMB) in 1965, exhibiting a nearly perfect blackbody spectrum, proved to be an insurmountable problem for steady-state models and firmly established the Hot [[big-bang|Big Bang]] as the standard cosmological framework.

With the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] and thermal evolution of the universe established, primordial nucleosynthesis became a rigorously quantitative tool. Detailed calculations of light element abundances—most notably deuterium, helium, and lithium—demonstrated that baryons could only account for a small fraction of the critical energy density. Concurrently, dynamical measurements of galaxies and galaxy clusters indicated that the total matter density was substantially higher, implying the existence of unseen mass. 

Early theories of structure formation explored whether this missing mass could consist of primordial neutrinos, often termed "Hot Dark Matter." However, it was demonstrated that a neutrino-dominated universe possesses a free-streaming scale corresponding to a Jeans mass of $\sim 10^{16} M_\odot$. This scale is characteristic of superclusters, meaning that smaller structures like galaxies could not have formed early enough to match observations. This discrepancy prompted a paradigm shift toward [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]], which behaves as a dissipationless fluid and allows for bottom-up hierarchical clustering [Blumenthal et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982Natur.299...37B/abstract).

Despite the introduction of dark matter, the tension between the strict nucleosynthesis limits on the baryon density ($\Omega_b$) and the prevailing theoretical preference for a critical-density universe ($\Omega_m = 1$) culminated in the "Cluster Baryon Crisis" of the early 1990s. Observations of the X-ray-emitting hot gas and stellar content in rich galaxy clusters revealed that the ratio of baryonic mass to total mass was significantly higher than the universal ratio $\Omega_b/\Omega_m$ permitted by standard inflationary $\Omega_m=1$ models [White et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993Natur.366..429W/abstract). Because galaxy clusters are sufficiently massive to act as "closed boxes" that retain a representative sample of the cosmic matter inventory, this discrepancy forced the cosmological community to abandon the $\Omega_m = 1$ assumption. 

The resolution to this crisis required a low matter density, which, when combined with later supernova distance measurements and CMB acoustic peak localizations, naturally led to the modern concordance [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]]. In this framework, the universe has a flat geometry, but matter accounts for only about a quarter of its total energy density ($\Omega_m \approx 0.3$). Baryons constitute approximately $5\%$ of the total mass-energy density ($\Omega_b \approx 0.04 - 0.05$), meaning they account for only $\sim 15-20\%$ of the matter budget. The remainder of the clustering mass must necessarily exist as [[non-baryonic-dark-matter|Non-baryonic dark matter]]. While the $\Lambda$CDM model requires this dark matter, alternative frameworks such as Modified Gravity (MOG) theories suggest an alternative: instead of cold dark matter, a vector graviton field could provide the necessary energy density. In such models, the vector graviton dominates over baryons in the early universe ($\rho_\phi > \rho_b$), allowing the framework to successfully fit the CMB acoustic peaks without relying on standard non-baryonic dark matter [Moffat et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020EPJC...80..906M/abstract). Furthermore, in theories without cold dark matter, such as Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) or Modified Gravity (MOG), the highly prominent predicted baryonic acoustic oscillations in the matter power spectrum are often heavily suppressed by the statistical window functions used in processing practical galaxy samples [Moffat et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1112.4386M/abstract).

## Origins: Baryogenesis and the Early Universe

The observed mass density of baryonic matter is fundamentally rooted in the theoretical mechanisms of baryogenesis, which generated an asymmetry between matter and antimatter during the [[early-universe-epochs|Early universe epochs]]. If the [[universe|Universe]] had begun in a perfectly baryon-symmetric state, the continuous annihilation of baryons and antibaryons would have produced a vastly greater gamma-ray background than is observed, leaving a nearly empty cosmos devoid of baryonic structure. Instead, the universe possesses a non-zero, positive baryon number, yielding a tiny but critical primordial baryon-to-photon ratio of $\eta \sim 10^{-10}$.

The exact physical details of baryogenesis remain one of the major open questions in modern cosmology. While the [[standard-model-particle-physics|Standard Model (particle physics)]] contains mechanisms that violate baryon number and CP symmetry, these effects are quantitatively insufficient to produce the observed magnitude of the matter asymmetry. Consequently, baryogenesis requires new physics operating in the high-energy, radiation-dominated environment shortly after the [[big-bang|Big Bang]].

Historically, models of baryogenesis have frequently relied on Grand Unified Theories (GUTs), which explicitly permit baryon number non-conservation. In such models, the baryon asymmetry could be generated at extremely high energy scales, roughly $E \approx 10^{15}\text{ GeV}$ [Bond et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982ApJ...255..341B/abstract). An alternative and widely studied framework is leptogenesis, which proposes that an initial lepton asymmetry was generated by the out-of-equilibrium decays of heavy right-handed neutrinos and subsequently converted into a baryon asymmetry via standard electroweak processes. This mechanism naturally links the cosmic baryon asymmetry to the physics of light, sub-eV [[neutrino-mass|Neutrino mass]] [Raffelt et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002NewAR..46..699R/abstract). Other frameworks physically realize the baryon density via a combination of axion dark matter, Affleck-Dine baryogenesis, and frozen quintessence [McDonald et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013JCAP...05..019M/abstract).

The dynamics of baryogenesis also have potential implications for the initial spectrum of [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]]. If the efficiency of baryon asymmetry production varied spatially due to local fluctuations in the expansion rate or the underlying scalar fields, it could have generated isothermal (isocurvature) perturbations—fluctuations in the local baryon-to-photon ratio rather than the total energy density [Bond et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982ApJ...255..341B/abstract). Historically, models explored varying assumptions about these primordial conditions; for instance, minimal isocurvature models where baryons and radiation dominate were examined to explain the coherence length of CMB anisotropies and provide the seeds for galaxy formation [Peebles et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJ...315L..73P/abstract). Today, spatially varying mechanisms are hypothesized to generate compensated isocurvature perturbations (CIPs)—fluctuations in the relative density of baryons and dark matter that leave the total matter density unchanged. While early limits derived from galaxy-cluster baryon fractions constrained the CIP amplitude to $\lesssim 10\%$, forecasts indicate that measurements of off-diagonal CMB correlations in future polarization experiments will be sensitive to CIP amplitudes below $1\%$ [Grin et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvD..84l3003G/abstract). Ultimately, stringent constraints on isocurvature perturbations from modern cosmic microwave background measurements indicate that the primordial fluctuations are overwhelmingly adiabatic.

Once the temperature of the universe dropped below the energy scale of the relevant baryon-violating processes, the net comoving baryon number was "frozen in." This permanent asymmetry established the absolute abundance of nucleons that would later participate in primordial nucleosynthesis, ultimately fixing the [[cosmic-abundances|Cosmic abundances]] of light elements and setting the baseline for all subsequent baryonic structure formation.

## Primordial Nucleosynthesis Constraints

Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN) provides a stringent, independent probe of the physical cosmic baryon density during the first three minutes of the [[big-bang|Big Bang]]. As the [[radiation-dominated-era|Radiation-dominated era]] cooled, the hot and dense primordial plasma reached temperatures of $T \sim 10^9$ K, allowing protons and neutrons to fuse into the first [[cosmic-abundances|Cosmic abundances]]. 

The yields of BBN depend sensitively on the baryon-to-photon ratio,
$$ \eta \equiv \frac{n_b}{n_\gamma} $$
where $n_b$ is the number density of baryons and $n_\gamma$ is the number density of photons. Because the photon number density is exquisitely constrained by the present-day temperature of the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] (CMB), determining $\eta$ directly measures the physical baryon density, $\Omega_b h^2$.

Among the primordial isotopes, the deuterium abundance is the most sensitive baryometer. Because deuterium acts as an intermediate bottleneck in the formation of heavier elements, a higher baryon density increases the nuclear reaction rates that consume deuterium to form helium. Consequently, the surviving primordial deuterium fraction ($D/H$) is a monotonically decreasing function of $\Omega_b h^2$. High-resolution quasar absorption spectra, which probe pristine, high-redshift gas clouds, yield precise measurements of this ratio. Modern determinations of the primordial deuterium abundance converge to $D/H = (2.67 \pm 0.09) \times 10^{-5}$ [Coc et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1307.6955C/abstract).

When combined with precision measurements of the neutron lifetime and nuclear cross-sections from the [[standard-model-particle-physics|Standard Model (particle physics)]], the observed cosmic abundances of deuterium and helium-4 (mass fraction $Y_p = 0.2463 \pm 0.0003$) yield a physical baryon density tightly consistent with CMB anisotropy measurements [Coc et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1307.6955C/abstract). Historically, early BBN estimates favored slightly lower values, such as $\Omega_b h^2 = 0.019 \pm 0.002$ , but improvements in both CMB constraints and nuclear network data have led to a spectacular concordance. Current constraints place the total baryon fraction at $\Omega_b \approx 0.04 - 0.05$, indicating that baryons account for roughly $5\%$ of the total mass-energy density of the universe and approximately $15-20\%$ of the total [[mass-density|Mass density]] when compared against the [[dark-matter-fraction|Dark matter fraction]].

Despite the overwhelming success of BBN in determining the baryon density, a significant anomaly persists regarding the abundance of lithium-7. Standard BBN calculations, anchored by the $\Omega_b h^2$ value that perfectly predicts the deuterium and helium abundances, yield a primordial lithium abundance of $\text{Li}/H = (4.89^{+0.41}_{-0.39}) \times 10^{-10}$ [Coc et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1307.6955C/abstract). However, this prediction is approximately three times larger than the abundance observed in the atmospheres of metal-poor halo stars in our galaxy. This "Lithium Problem" remains an active area of debate. It is currently unresolved whether the discrepancy points to unmodeled stellar depletion mechanisms, errors in assumed nuclear reaction rates, or new particle physics beyond the standard [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]].

## CMB Anisotropies and the Sound Horizon

The [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] (CMB) provides one of the most rigorous and independent constraints on the cosmic baryon density. The [[temperature-anisotropies|Temperature anisotropies]] observed in the CMB power spectrum encode the acoustic physics of the tightly coupled photon-baryon fluid that existed prior to recombination, at a cosmic time of $t \sim 380,000$ years and a temperature of $T \sim 4000$ K. 

Within this primordial plasma, the competing forces of gravitational collapse and radiation pressure established standing acoustic oscillations. Baryons, having significant rest mass, altered the zero-point of these oscillations. An increase in the physical baryon density, $\Omega_b h^2$, deepens the gravitational potential wells, thereby enhancing the amplitude of the compressional phases (the odd-numbered acoustic peaks) relative to the rarefaction phases (the even-numbered peaks). By measuring the relative heights of the first and second acoustic peaks, CMB experiments precisely anchor the baryon-to-photon ratio, $\eta$. High-precision analyses of the CMB power spectrum yield a tightly constrained ratio of $\eta = 6.23 \pm 0.17 \times 10^{-10}$, which corresponds to a physical baryon density of $\Omega_b h^2 \approx 0.022$. Furthermore, precise measurements of CMB lensing break the geometric degeneracy inherent in the primary temperature power spectrum, independently favoring a universe dominated by dark energy using CMB data alone [Sherwin et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvL.107b1302S/abstract).

On angular scales larger than the horizon size at recombination, the temperature anisotropies are primarily generated by the [[sachs-wolfe-effect|Sachs-Wolfe effect]], wherein photons undergo gravitational redshift as they climb out of primordial potential wells. Conversely, on smaller, sub-horizon scales, the oscillatory features are governed by the comoving sound horizon at the epoch of decoupling. The typical length scale of this sound horizon is closely tied to the baryon density, given approximately by:
$$ r_s \approx 150 \left( \frac{\Omega_{b,0} h^2}{0.02} \right)^{-1} \text{ Mpc} $$
in comoving units.

This characteristic distance serves as a fundamental standard ruler for cosmology. Following decoupling, the radiation streamed freely, leaving the baryon distribution frozen with a slight overdensity at the sound horizon scale. As structure evolved, this scale was permanently imprinted onto the late-time matter power spectrum as [[baryon-acoustic-oscillations-bao|Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO)]] [Eisenstein et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...504L..57E/abstract). The relative amplitude of these BAO features in the galaxy distribution is highly sensitive to the universal baryon fraction, offering an independent consistency check on the CMB constraints [Sutherland et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.3128S/abstract). 

While modern CMB observations are in excellent agreement with standard Big Bang nucleosynthesis (BBN) predictions, historical measurements exhibited mild tensions. For instance, early analyses of the BOOMERanG and MAXIMA balloon experiments suggested a slightly higher baryon density of $\Omega_b h^2 = 0.030 \pm 0.005$, which conflicted with the then-standard BBN estimate of $0.019 \pm 0.002$ . Subsequent measurements from the WMAP and Planck satellites have largely resolved this discrepancy, converging firmly on $\Omega_b \approx 0.04-0.05$ for the total mass-energy budget of the [[universe|Universe]]. However, the literature notes that the exact value of the BAO amplitude and the inferred baryon fraction remain mildly sensitive to assumptions regarding non-standard radiation densities, such as the effective number of neutrino species $N_{\rm eff}$ [Burenin et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AstL...39..357B/abstract), [Sutherland et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.3128S/abstract).

## Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO) in Large-Scale Structure

In the early universe, before the epoch of recombination, baryons and photons were tightly coupled via Thomson scattering, forming a dense, hot plasma. Primordial [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]] launched acoustic waves that propagated through this medium. When the universe expanded and cooled sufficiently for neutral hydrogen to form ($z \approx 1100$), the photons decoupled from the baryons, and the acoustic waves froze in place. This process left an overdensity of baryons at a characteristic length scale defined by the comoving sound horizon at the drag epoch. In comoving units, this scale is approximately 
$$r_s \approx 150 \left( \frac{\Omega_{b,0} h^2}{0.02} \right)^{-1} \text{ Mpc}$$
where $\Omega_{b,0}$ is the present-day baryon density parameter and $h$ is the dimensionless Hubble parameter. 

Because this physical scale is imprinted by well-understood linear physics in the [[early-universe-epochs|Early universe epochs]] and is largely unaffected by complex, low-redshift astrophysical processes like galaxy bias, Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO) serve as an exceptionally robust "standard ruler" [Eisenstein et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...504L..57E/abstract). In the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]], the BAO signature appears as a series of damped oscillatory features, while in real space, it manifests as a single peak in the two-point galaxy correlation function. Furthermore, advanced wavelet analysis techniques can identify these real-space BAO structures directly as spherical shells surrounding high-density central regions traced by luminous red galaxies [Arnalte-Mur et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...542A..34A/abstract). By measuring the apparent angular size and line-of-sight redshift extent of this scale in large-scale structure surveys, cosmologists can map the angular diameter distance $D_A(z)$ and the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] $H(z)$ with high precision [Blake et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...594..665B/abstract). Alongside the BAO scale, the transition scale to cosmic homogeneity serves as a complementary large-scale structure probe that can independently constrain the matter and dark energy density ratios [Ntelis et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190406135N/abstract). 

Observationally, the BAO signal has been detected with high statistical significance ($>3\sigma$) across multiple large-scale galaxy redshift surveys. Landmark measurements from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) [Percival et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...657...51P/abstract) and the WiggleZ Dark Energy Survey [Blake et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425..405B/abstract) consistently support a flat [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] with a total matter density parameter of $\Omega_m \approx 0.25 - 0.30$. When BAO distances are combined with measurements from the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] (CMB), they break geometric degeneracies, yielding parameter constraints on $H_0$ and $\Omega_m$ at the 5% precision level [Eisenstein et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...504L..57E/abstract). 

Beyond cosmic geometry, the amplitude of the BAO feature acts as a direct probe of the cosmic baryon budget. The relative amplitude of the acoustic signature in galaxy clustering isolates the cosmic baryon fraction, $f_b = \Omega_b / \Omega_m$, independent of the sound horizon calibration [Krolewski et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240319236K/abstract). Furthermore, the exact amplitude and phase of the oscillations are sensitive to the primordial radiation density; measurements of these phase shifts offer a mechanism to constrain the [[effective-neutrino-mass|Effective neutrino mass]] and search for [[dark-radiation|Dark radiation]] or other light relics [Baumann et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JCAP...08..029B/abstract), [Sutherland et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.3128S/abstract). Additionally, the complex velocity fields associated with these scales provide further cosmological information. The Cosmic Mach Number—defined as the ratio of the mean velocity to the velocity dispersion—acts as a sensitive, scale-dependent probe capable of differentiating between the standard $\Lambda$CDM model and alternatives involving modified gravity or massive neutrinos [Ma et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JCAP...06..026M/abstract).

Although the BAO scale is considered a clean cosmological probe, it is subject to minor degradation at late times. Non-linear gravitational clustering and redshift-space distortions dampen the acoustic peaks and induce percent-level shifts in the acoustic scale due to mode coupling [Crocce et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008PhRvD..77b3533C/abstract), [Nomura et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvD..79f3512N/abstract). Furthermore, supersonic streaming velocities between baryons and dark matter at the epoch of decoupling can advect tracers and systematically shift the observed BAO peak position by roughly $0.5\%$ [Blazek et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvL.116l1303B/abstract). Increased attenuation of the power spectrum BAO can also be driven by upward fluctuations in large-scale power, which must be carefully modeled in covariance matrices [Neyrinck et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.384.1221N/abstract). 

To mitigate these non-linear effects, modern BAO analyses rely on density-field reconstruction techniques. By using the Zel'dovich approximation to estimate and reverse the bulk displacements of galaxies, the BAO peak can be effectively sharpened, restoring the precision of the standard ruler and avoiding biased cosmological constraints [Achitouv et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..92h3523A/abstract). Alternative approaches also exist; for example, analyzing higher-order statistics like the skewness of the density field can provide a cleaner detection of BAO features with lower sensitivity to galaxy bias and redshift-space distortions [Juszkiewicz et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.429.1206J/abstract). Additionally, cosmic voids offer a robust, largely pristine environment for measuring the BAO scale, as their clustering signal is less sensitive to both non-linearities and observational selection effects [Forero-Sánchez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513.5407F/abstract). Looking forward, facilities such as the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) will measure BAO with unprecedented precision; future Phase 1 and 2 SKA surveys are forecast to constrain the acoustic scale across a wide redshift range utilizing both 21cm intensity mapping and discrete galaxy catalogs [Bull et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015aska.confE..24B/abstract).

## The Missing Baryon Problem and Modern Probes

Measurements of the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] and predictions from [[big-bang|Big Bang]] nucleosynthesis consistently indicate a primordial total baryon density parameter of $\Omega_b \approx 0.04 - 0.05$. However, a direct census of the local universe—tallying the mass in stars, cold neutral gas, and molecular clouds—historically accounted for less than 10% of this expected budget [Persic et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992MNRAS.258P..14P/abstract), [Hogan et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ASPC...99...67H/abstract). Historically, it was even hypothesized that a large, unseen population of low-luminosity galaxies might recover a baryon density consistent with standard nucleosynthesis limits, long before the WHIM became the consensus target [Bristow et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994MNRAS.267...13B/abstract). This discrepancy, known as the "missing baryon problem," prompted a decades-long observational effort to locate these [[dark-baryons|Dark baryons]].

Cosmological hydrodynamic simulations have long indicated that the majority of these unaccounted-for baryons do not reside within collapsed halos, but rather in a diffuse, highly ionized plasma known as the Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM) [Kaastra et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.2324K/abstract). Driven by gravitational collapse and shock-heating of gas accreting onto the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]], as well as redistributive forces from active galactic nuclei and supernovae feedback, the WHIM reaches temperatures of $T \sim 10^5 - 10^7$ K [He et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...623..601H/abstract). Simulations predict that this intergalactic medium (IGM) contains the bulk of the missing mass, while approximately 13.8% - 14.1% of baryons remain "missing" observationally, primarily existing as undetected intracluster light (ICL) across all halos and as warm gas in low-mass halos, where baryonic feedback actively redistributes gas to the outskirts [Miller et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250420159M/abstract).

On gravitationally bound scales, massive galaxy clusters act as cosmological "closed boxes" that retain a roughly representative sample of the universal baryon fraction, $f_b = (M_{\rm gas} + M_*) / M_{\rm tot} \approx \Omega_b/\Omega_m$. To completely reconcile cluster mass censuses with early WMAP constraints on the global baryon fraction, early estimates historically suggested that clusters might contain a significant fraction (roughly $17\%$) of "other" baryons, likely hidden in a warm-hot phase [Ettori et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.344L..13E/abstract). The baryonic mass in these environments is heavily dominated by the intracluster medium, measured observationally as the [[hot-gas-mass-fraction|Hot gas mass fraction]] via X-ray bremsstrahlung emission. Because baryonic physics (such as cooling and star formation) anti-correlates the hot gas and stellar mass fractions, the total baryon mass provides a highly robust, low-scatter ($\sim 5\%$) proxy for the total halo mass, significantly improving the calibration of [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]] [Wu et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1982W/abstract). While galaxy clusters retain a representative baryon fraction, lower-mass halos do not. For instance, stacking X-ray observations of local massive spiral galaxies suggests that the hot circumgalactic medium (CGM) within the virial radius accounts for only about $8\%$ of the expected baryonic mass. This indicates that the CGM alone is highly insufficient to solve the missing baryon problem [Li et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...855L..24L/abstract).

Despite the success of cluster measurements, directly mapping the WHIM remains observationally challenging due to its extreme low density and high temperature. Direct detection of individual WHIM filaments via X-ray absorption (e.g., highly ionized O VII) is difficult, as detectable column densities typically occupy only a small fraction ($\sim 1\%$) of the filament volume [Tuominen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...671A.103T/abstract). Consequently, astronomers have turned to cross-correlation and stacking analyses. Stacking Planck Compton-$y$ maps and CMB lensing data across tens of thousands of optical galaxy filaments has yielded thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich (tSZ) detections at $>7\sigma$, suggesting that the filamentary WHIM alone accounts for $\sim 12\%$ of the total cosmic baryon budget [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250708561L/abstract). Recent measurements from DESI have further reinforced this, finding $3\sigma$ evidence for excess baryons in cosmic filaments with central overdensities reaching $\delta \sim 20$, decisively confirming the role of the cosmic web as a major baryon reservoir [Mo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250819861M/abstract). Conversely, targeted non-detections in local structures, such as the Virgo cluster filaments, have placed strict upper limits on the volume-averaged WHIM electron density of $\langle n_e \rangle < 4 \times 10^{-4} \text{ cm}^{-3}$ [Erciyes et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...675A..63E/abstract).

Recently, Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) have emerged as a transformative tool to resolve the missing baryon problem. As extragalactic radio transients, FRBs undergo frequency-dependent dispersion as they propagate through cosmic plasma. The resulting dispersion measure (DM) is directly proportional to the line-of-sight column density of free electrons, effectively acting as a precise tracer of the column density for the ionized IGM. By analyzing the DM of localized FRBs alongside foreground galaxy data, astronomers have directly measured the total late-universe baryon density, yielding $\Omega_b h_{70} \approx 0.049 - 0.051$—a value in excellent agreement with early-universe CMB predictions [Connor et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240916952C/abstract), [Connor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1226C/abstract). By mapping foregrounds using spectroscopic galaxy surveys around localized FRB sightlines, astronomers can improve constraints on the partition of baryons between the IGM and CGM by a factor of 25 [Lee et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928....9L/abstract). To better isolate these contributions from the intergalactic and circumgalactic media in contemporary FRB observations, researchers have developed detailed dispersion measure models specifically tailored for the local universe ($D < 120$ Mpc) [Huang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241022098H/abstract). Furthermore, utilizing the dispersion measures of hundreds of localized FRBs offers the potential to detect large-scale spatial variations, such as possible anisotropic distributions or dipoles in the cosmic baryon density [Lin et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ChPhC..45l5101L/abstract). These FRB sightlines indicate that the vast majority of these baryons reside in the diffuse IGM, with a global intergalactic baryon fraction of $f_{\rm igm} \approx 0.59 - 0.76$ [Khrykin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240200505K/abstract), [Connor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1226C/abstract). Cosmological tracing shows that the cosmic web's filamentary environments contribute $70\%$ to $80\%$ of the accumulated dispersion measure [Walker et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A..71W/abstract). These FRB constraints strongly support the theoretical consensus: the "missing" baryons were never absent, but simply hidden as a diffuse, ionized gas enriching the cosmic web.

## Baryonic Feedback and Matter Distribution

While the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] successfully describes the universe's large-scale structure as being driven by collisionless dark matter, the spatial distribution of matter on small and intermediate scales is significantly modified by baryonic physics. Early statistical constraints on the matter density and mass power spectrum normalization from cosmic shear analyses (such as the GaBoDS survey) frequently assumed a negligible baryon content to model initial flat-universe parameters [Hetterscheidt et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007A&A...468..859H/abstract). However, as baryons undergo [[cosmic-accretion|Cosmic accretion]] into the [[gravitational-potentials|Gravitational potentials]] of dark matter halos, they cool to form stars and provide fuel for supermassive black holes. The subsequent energetic feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei (AGN) heats and expels a substantial fraction of these baryons back into the circumgalactic and intergalactic media. For instance, models like the Simba simulation demonstrate that specific feedback mechanisms—particularly kinetic jets from AGN—profoundly alter the gas distribution specifically within the outskirts of cosmic filaments and knots, rather than just within the primary halos [Dong et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250716115D/abstract). Despite this complex feedback, studies have identified a potentially universal constant, $K$, that characterizes the dark matter-baryon interplay across both galactic and cluster scales [Chan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NatSR...9.3570C/abstract). Recent direct measurements using Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) indicate that this feedback is highly efficient, driving a large portion of the cosmic baryon budget out of galaxy halos and enriching the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] [Connor et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240916952C/abstract), [Connor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1226C/abstract). Furthermore, earlier processes such as cosmic reionization imprint additional signatures on galaxy clustering through photon-electron drag forces and the spatial modulation of the ionizing radiation field, which alters large-scale galaxy bias [Schmidt et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PhRvD..96h3533S/abstract).

This redistribution of baryons heavily impacts the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]]. Because baryons constitute roughly $15-20\%$ of the total matter density, their expulsion suppresses the total matter clustering amplitude on small scales compared to dark-matter-only predictions. Studies employing large suites of hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate that AGN feedback alters the non-linear growth of structure, suppressing the matter power spectrum by amounts far exceeding the $\sim 1\%$ precision required by next-generation weak lensing and cosmic shear surveys [Salcido et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.2247S/abstract). Consequently, researchers are continuously developing models to carefully quantify the impact of baryons on the virial halo masses of galaxy clusters, a step essential for avoiding biased cosmological constraints in upcoming missions like Euclid [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...685A.109E/abstract). If these baryonic effects are not properly modeled, they can severely bias the inferred values of fundamental cosmological parameters. 

The influence of feedback extends beyond two-point statistics, deeply affecting higher-order clustering measures such as the [[matter-bispectrum|Matter bispectrum]]. Analyses utilizing the [[bahamas-simulations|BAHAMAS simulations]] have shown that the halo bispectrum is exceptionally sensitive to the efficiencies of star formation and AGN feedback at small scales ($k \gtrsim 1\, h \, \mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$) [Yankelevich et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1448Y/abstract). Crucially, the suppression of small-scale clustering by baryonic feedback is largely separable from the suppression caused by free-streaming [[total-neutrino-mass|Total neutrino mass]], allowing higher-order statistics to simultaneously constrain both astrophysics and neutrino masses [Yankelevich et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1448Y/abstract). To parse these overlapping effects, researchers increasingly utilize novel statistical techniques; for instance, weighted density fields can act as improved probes that successfully amplify the signal of modified gravity while remaining distinguishable from standard baryonic physics and neutrino effects [Llinares et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472L..80L/abstract).

To utilize high-precision weak lensing data without introducing systematic biases, theoretical predictions must explicitly account for baryonic feedback. Current mitigation strategies involve either marginalizing over feedback parameters using hydrodynamically-calibrated models—such as modifying the power spectrum based on the mean baryon fraction of halos [Salcido et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.2247S/abstract)—or employing empirical scaling relations to map observed matter clustering back to its dark-matter-only equivalent [Zhou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110975Z/abstract). The first direct measurements of this feedback using FRB dispersion measures already strongly reject no-feedback scenarios at $>3\sigma$ confidence [Reischke et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250717742R/abstract), highlighting the absolute necessity of integrating complex baryonic processes into modern precision cosmology pipelines.

## Cosmological Tensions and Future Directions

The precise measurement of the cosmic baryon density is increasingly central to resolving major discrepancies in [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]], most notably the Hubble constant tension and the $S_8$ (amplitude of structure) tension. 

Baryon density measurements derived from [[baryon-acoustic-oscillations-bao|Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO)]] and Big Bang nucleosynthesis (BBN) provide a mechanism to measure the Hubble constant, $H_0$, independently of the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] and the local distance ladder. Analyses combining BAO and BBN consistently yield lower values of $H_0 \approx 67.6 \pm 1.1 \text{ km s}^{-1}\text{ Mpc}^{-1}$, aligning with Planck data and maintaining a strong tension with late-universe probes [Cuceu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...10..044C/abstract). Recent methodologies have sought to determine $H_0 independently$ of the comoving sound horizon by using the baryonic signature in the galaxy power spectrum to measure the mass fraction $\Omega_b/\Omega_m$, yielding $H_0 = 67.1_{-5.3}^{+6.3} \text{ km s}^{-1}\text{ Mpc}^{-1}$ [Krolewski et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240319227K/abstract). However, the literature remains ambiguous; alternative calibrations of the total energy density using late-time BAO and BBN priors favor an intermediate value of $H_0 = 69.0 \pm 2.5 \text{ km s}^{-1}\text{ Mpc}^{-1}$ [Krolewski et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251123432K/abstract).

Furthermore, the spatial distribution of baryons directly impacts the $S_8$ tension—the discrepancy between the amplitude of matter fluctuations inferred from the early universe and that measured by low-redshift weak lensing surveys. Strong baryonic feedback from active galactic nuclei and supernovae redistributes gas from the interiors of dark matter halos into the intergalactic medium (IGM), suppressing the matter power spectrum on non-linear scales. In addition to these direct probes, cross-correlations between CMB lensing and large-scale galaxy surveys play a critical role in testing theoretical structure growth predictions over cosmic time, offering an essential cross-check for addressing the $S_8$ tension [Madhavacheril et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108152M/abstract). Recent censuses using Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) indicate a highly diffuse baryon fraction in the IGM, confirming that feedback is sufficiently efficient to substantially reduce the $S_8$ tension by damping small-scale clustering [Connor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1226C/abstract), [Reischke et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250717742R/abstract). On galactic and cluster scales, baryonic physics such as dynamical friction mechanisms can lead to flat galaxy-cluster profiles, providing a solution to the core-cusp "diversity problem" and issues with galaxy rotation curves [Del Popolo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98f3517D/abstract).

Future observational campaigns aim to tighten these constraints by exploring new regions of the cosmological [[parameter-space|Parameter space]]. A major frontier is the direct detection of the Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM) via X-ray absorption and emission. The upcoming Athena mission (specifically its X-IFU instrument) will target highly ionized species such as O VII. Because these metals trace the missing baryons in the outskirts of halos and cosmic filaments, the projection and stacking of multiple sightlines will be required to characterize this exceedingly diffuse gas [Tuominen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...671A.103T/abstract). Future Large Scale Structure surveys will further test these models by providing high-significance measurements of the [[total-neutrino-mass|Total neutrino mass]], relying on precise constraints to overcome theoretical degeneracies with other parameters such as the energy density of relativistic relics [Brinckmann et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...01..059B/abstract).

Additionally, proposed missions like the Primordial Inflation Explorer (PIXIE) offer a novel pathway to constrain the global baryon density via spectral distortions of the CMB. PIXIE is designed to measure the sky-averaged thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (TSZ) effect—a distortion caused by the inverse Compton scattering of CMB photons off hot, free electrons. Theoretical forecasts predict that PIXIE could detect this global TSZ signal at an unparalleled significance of $\gtrsim 1000\sigma$. Although [[cosmic-variance|Cosmic variance]] ultimately reduces the effective signal-to-noise ratio to approximately $230\sigma$, such a measurement would place sub-percent constraints on the total thermal energy of electrons and the mean baryon density in the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]], establishing a rigid integral constraint on feedback models over cosmic time [Hill et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvL.115z1301H/abstract).
