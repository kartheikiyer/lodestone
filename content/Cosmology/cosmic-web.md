The **cosmic web** is the fundamental, interconnected [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]], comprising a vast network of [[cold-dark-matter|dark matter]] and [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|baryonic gas]] that spans megaparsec scales. Emerging from the gravitational collapse of [[strong-primordial-inhomogeneities|primordial density fluctuations]] generated in the early [[cosmological-history|Universe]] [Bond et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996Natur.380..603B/abstract), this architecture represents the quasi-linear regime of [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]]. Driven by the dynamics of [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]], matter undergoes anisotropic gravitational collapse to form a distinct morphological hierarchy: expansive, underdense [[cosmic-voids|Cosmic Voids]] are bounded by two-dimensional planar sheets (or [[zel-dovich-pancake|Zel’dovich pancake]]s), which intersect to form dense, one-dimensional [[cosmic-filaments|Cosmic filaments]]. These filaments serve as cosmic highways, funneling matter into massive, zero-dimensional nodes that host rich [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]].

Astrophysicists mathematically formalize and classify these distinct environments using topological algorithms and Hessian-based techniques, such as analyzing the eigenvalues of the tidal or velocity shear tensors across [[three-dimensional-cosmic-density-fields|Three-dimensional cosmic density fields]] [Forero-Romero et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.1815F/abstract). Beyond its dark matter skeleton, the cosmic web is inherently baryon-rich. Observational and theoretical consensus indicates that roughly 50-80% of the [[universe|Universe]]'s ordinary matter resides outside of virialized halos as a diffuse, shock-heated plasma known as the Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM) [Eckert et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.528..105E/abstract), [Connor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1226C/abstract). Furthermore, a galaxy's position within this network significantly governs its evolutionary trajectory. The cosmic web actively regulates [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] and star formation, driving environmental quenching through mechanisms like ram-pressure stripping and "cosmic web detachment" as galaxies migrate from primordial filaments into dense cluster nodes.

The cosmic web also imparts distinct kinematic signatures on the galaxies it hosts, with low-mass spirals and high-mass ellipticals exhibiting mass-dependent spin and shape alignments with their parent filaments. While these intrinsic alignments provide crucial tests for tidal torque theory, they also represent a major systematic uncertainty in [[cosmic-shear|Cosmic shear]] measurements. Current research is highly focused on resolving several critical open questions regarding the web's physical properties. Chief among these are the direct, unstacked observational mapping of the tenuous WHIM using next-generation observatories, and determining whether the large-scale geometry of the web directly induces galaxy quenching independent of local halo mass (a manifestation of halo assembly bias). Additionally, the origin of the $30-60$ nG [[supergalactic-magnetic-field|Supergalactic magnetic field]] detected within filaments, and the extreme sensitivity of simulated galaxy spin alignments to sub-grid physics, remain subjects of active, ongoing investigation.

## Introduction and Theoretical Framework

The cosmic web is the fundamental [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]], characterized by an interconnected network of [[cold-dark-matter|dark matter]] and [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|baryonic gas]] that spans megaparsec scales. Within the standard LCDM paradigm, this architecture emerges from the gravitational collapse of [[strong-primordial-inhomogeneities|primordial density fluctuations]] generated in the early [[universe|Universe]] [Bond et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996Natur.380..603B/abstract). Under the influence of gravity, [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] drives [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]], creating a vast scaffolding upon which [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|baryons]] coalesce and galaxies evolve.

The theoretical framework for the cosmic web divides the evolution of the cosmic density field into distinct mass regimes. On the largest scales ($M \gg M_*$), density fluctuations remain in the linear regime. Conversely, on small scales ($M \ll M_*$), matter undergoes complete non-linear collapse to form virialized dark matter halos. It is on the intermediate, quasi-linear mass scales ($M \sim M_*$) that the hallmark anisotropic distribution of the cosmic web manifests. In this regime, gravitational instability drives asymmetric collapse, leading to a hierarchy of morphological elements: vast, low-density [[cosmic-voids|Cosmic Voids]] are bounded by two-dimensional planar sheets (or [[zel-dovich-pancake|Zel’dovich pancake]]s), which intersect to form one-dimensional [[cosmic-filaments|Cosmic filaments]], which in turn converge at dense, zero-dimensional nodes hosting massive [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] [Bond et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996Natur.380..603B/abstract).

Mathematically, this architecture is often formalized using the local deformation of the density or velocity fields, such as the Hessian matrix of the gravitational potential (the tidal tensor, or T-web) or the velocity shear tensor ([[v-web-threshold|V-web]]) [Forero-Romero et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.1815F/abstract). The cosmic web is classified dynamically by evaluating the number of positive eigenvalues of these tensors above a given threshold, $\lambda_{\rm th}$. This specifies the dimensionality of local collapse: regions with three collapsing eigenvalues represent knots or nodes; two collapsing eigenvalues denote filaments; one collapsing eigenvalue indicates a sheet; and zero collapsing eigenvalues correspond to voids. For instance, evaluating the tidal tensor at a threshold of $0.2 \lesssim \lambda_{\rm th} \lesssim 0.4$ accurately reproduces a system of percolated voids coexisting with a network of interconnected filaments [Forero-Romero et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.1815F/abstract). While this Hessian-based classification is widely adopted, the precise choice of $\lambda_{\rm th}$ is frequently motivated phenomenologically or visually rather than being derived strictly from first principles. Consequently, different identification algorithms exhibit broad agreement on massive structures but often show significant disparities in the identification of tenuous filaments and void boundaries. 

While the LCDM paradigm serves as the consensus model for generating the web's anisotropic distribution, the literature contains occasional conflicting hypotheses regarding the physical mechanisms driving specific structures. Some alternative models propose that cosmic string loops act as seeds for globular clusters, deviating from standard hierarchical CDM fluctuations [Barton et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...06..022B/abstract), or invoke relativistic "pattern fields" as a self-organized alternative to dark matter entirely [Venkataramani et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191014649V/abstract). However, these remain outside the consensus of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]]. The overarching theoretical shift in recent decades has been the transition from viewing the [[universe|Universe]] as a collection of isolated field galaxies with occasional density enhancements to understanding it as a continuous, interconnected network where voids constitute the dominant volume and clusters act merely as the high-density convergence nodes of the web [Thompson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1109.1268T/abstract).

## Historical Development and the Zel’dovich Legacy

Historically, the spatial distribution of galaxies was viewed as a relatively uniform field punctuated by isolated density enhancements, such as [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]]. This perspective was fundamentally altered in the late 1970s with the observational discovery of vast, empty regions, now known as [[cosmic-voids|Cosmic Voids]], which implied a much more complex, interconnected organization [Thompson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1109.1268T/abstract). The suppression of galaxy formation in these voids is attributed to the synchronized action of negative sections of medium- and large-scale density perturbations [Einasto et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...534A.128E/abstract).

The theoretical foundation for this structure was laid by Yakov Zel'dovich in 1970, who modeled the anisotropic growth of primordial density inhomogeneities [Jones et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..308..219J/abstract). In this framework, the evolution of the cosmic density field is divided into distinct mass regimes. On very large mass scales ($M \gg M_*$), fluctuations remain in the linear regime and evolve strictly according to linear perturbation theory. On intermediate mass scales ($M \sim M_*$), the structure enters a quasi-linear regime. Here, anisotropic gravitational collapse naturally produces flattened, two-dimensional sheet-like structures known as the [[zel-dovich-pancake|Zel’dovich pancake]] [Wadekar et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.1333W/abstract). Subsequent collapse along the second and third principal axes yields one-dimensional [[cosmic-filaments|Cosmic filaments]] and dense nodes, respectively. Modern observational analogues of these original sheet-like pancakes have even been identified at [[low-redshift-universe|low redshift]], exhibiting distinctively low velocity dispersions on the order of $\sim 100 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Brinckmann et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JCAP...04..007B/abstract).

The modern paradigm of an interconnected "cosmic web" was formally cemented by Bond et al. [Bond et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996Natur.380..603B/abstract). They demonstrated that the large-scale filamentary network is not a late-time coincidence but is instead deterministically imprinted by the spatial distribution of rare [[density-peaks|Density peaks]] in the initial primordial density fluctuations. As the [[universe|Universe]] expands, non-linear evolution—driven by [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]]—brings this underlying skeletal network into sharper relief, funneling matter along filaments into massive cluster nodes.

This kinematic picture is inextricably linked to the [[hierarchical-clustering|Hierarchical clustering]] paradigm of [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] cosmology. On small mass scales ($M \ll M_*$), fluctuations undergo complete gravitational collapse to form virialized dark matter halos. The overall architecture of the web thereby represents a multiscale hierarchy where smaller halos and tenuous filaments continually merge and flow into larger structures, driving continuous [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]].

While early theoretical work relied primarily on analytical approximations and dark-matter-only N-body simulations, modern astrophysics explores the cosmic web through advanced high-resolution hydrodynamical models (such as the EAGLE, IllustrisTNG, and [[magneticum-simulations|Magneticum (simulations)]] suites). These modern simulations now robustly resolve not only the dark matter skeleton but also the complex thermodynamic and magnetohydrodynamic processes governing the [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|baryonic gas]] that co-evolves within the multiscale assembly of filaments and voids [Galárraga-Espinosa et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...641A.173G/abstract), [Bahé et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...702A.145B/abstract).

## Morphological Components: Nodes, Filaments, Sheets, and Voids

The [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] exhibits a pervasive, interconnected network known as the cosmic web, which is dynamically partitioned into four distinct morphological environments: nodes, filaments, sheets, and voids. On the grandest scales, this morphological hierarchy is organized into "basins of attraction" (BoA)—large-scale gravitational domains, such as the Shapley and Sloan [[great-wall-large-scale-structure|Great Wall]] BoAs, which define the primary attractors of cosmic flows [Valade et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1610V/abstract). These largest structures further assemble into massive perpendicular planes, such as the Local and Dominant [[superclusters|supercluster]] planes, which span hundreds of megaparsecs and dictate the large-scale spatial organization of superclusters and their complexes within the web [Einasto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522082E/abstract). This architecture arises from the quasi-linear regime of structure formation, where primordial density fluctuations undergo anisotropic gravitational collapse. 

A standard mathematical framework for classifying these structures relies on evaluating the eigenvalues of the tidal or velocity shear tensors across a continuous field. In the T-web approach, classification is based on the deformation tensor (the Hessian matrix of the gravitational potential); alternatively, the [[v-web-threshold|V-web]] approach utilizes the velocity shear tensor [Forero-Romero et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.1815F/abstract), [Olex et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...696A.142O/abstract). By evaluating these tensors on a spatial grid, the local environment is categorized by the number of eigenvalues that exceed a predefined threshold, $\lambda_{\rm th}$, indicating the dimensionality of local gravitational collapse:

*   **Nodes (Knots):** Regions where all three eigenvalues exceed $\lambda_{\rm th}$, indicating collapse in all three spatial dimensions. These dense nodes at cluster intersections correspond to massive [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] and serve as the convergence points of the web. The topological connectivity of a node—the number of filaments attached to it—scales positively with the halo mass, averaging $\kappa \sim 6.1$ in three-dimensional Gaussian random fields [Codis et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479..973C/abstract).
*   **Filaments:** Regions with exactly two eigenvalues above the threshold. These one-dimensional, thread-like structures channel matter into nodes and constitute the dense skeleton of the web (see [[cosmic-filaments|Cosmic filaments]]). Filaments can extend up to length scales of $\sim 130 \text{ Mpc}$ [Sarkar et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.3227S/abstract) and, while occupying a minor fraction of the cosmic volume, contain roughly half of the [[universe|Universe]]'s total matter [Gheller et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..448G/abstract). While the cosmic web occupies less than 1% of the volume, it contains roughly 45% of low-mass galaxies, largely due to the high fraction of satellite and backsplash galaxies residing within filamentary environments [Navdha et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241203438N/abstract). Detailed simulation analysis reveals that filament cores are highly substructure-dominated (up to 80%), and their gas property maps show significant heterogeneity between different hydrodynamic models like EAGLE and TNG100 [Bahe et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206484B/abstract). Internally, these filaments are often described as "pearl necklaces," where galaxy groups are distributed along the spine at regular characteristic scales of approximately $4-7 \text{ Mpc}/h$ [Tempel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...572A...8T/abstract). Additionally, observational overdensity profiles reveal that the "influence zone" of these filaments extends significantly outward, up to roughly $10 \text{ Mpc}$ from the main spine [Aguerri et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260113309A/abstract).
*   **Sheets:** Regions with a single collapsing eigenvalue. Often referred to as a [[zel-dovich-pancake|Zel’dovich pancake]], these two-dimensional planar structures form the boundaries separating voids and act as transitional zones funneling material toward filaments. Observations indicate that sheet environments are dynamically "cold," with typical velocity dispersions around $100 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Brinckmann et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JCAP...04..007B/abstract). Furthermore, asymmetric gravitational collapse caused by unbalanced voids on either side of a cosmic sheet can exacerbate the bulk flows of galaxies, such as the Local Sheet's motion away from the Local Void [Bland-Hawthorn et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1410.3845B/abstract).
*   **Voids:** Regions where zero eigenvalues exceed $\lambda_{\rm th}$, indicating expansion in all three axes. These vast, underdense voids dominate the volumetric capacity of the universe (see [[cosmic-voids|Cosmic Voids]]). 

An alternative mathematical descriptor to Hessian tensor thresholding is the "Local Dimension" metric ($D$), which quantifies the local geometry of the matter distribution. In this framework, values of $D \approx 1$, $2$, and $3$ correspond naturally to the structures of filaments, sheets, and dense clusters respectively [Sarkar et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.394L..66S/abstract).

Despite the mathematical elegance of tensor-based [[isodensity-analysis|Isodensity Analysis]], there is ongoing ambiguity in the literature regarding the precise selection of the eigenvalue threshold $\lambda_{\rm th}$. Historically, the choice of $\lambda_{\rm th}$ has been arbitrary and visually motivated rather than physically derived; for example, values between $0.2 \lesssim \lambda_{\rm th} \lesssim 0.4$ are frequently selected because they mathematically yield a state where a system of interconnected filaments coexists with large-scale void percolation [Forero-Romero et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.1815F/abstract). 

Furthermore, while diverse algorithms (such as Hessian-based methods versus topological persistence codes like DisPerSE or stochastic point processes like [[bisous-filaments|Bisous]]) show strong agreement in identifying massive nodes and prominent filaments, they exhibit significant disparities in the identification of tenuous filaments and exact void boundaries [Libeskind et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.1195L/abstract). To resolve these tensions, recent analytical efforts have sought to establish universal, physically motivated thresholds, such as normalizing the tensor outputs by the local velocity divergence field to isolate the non-linear evolution of cosmic web elements across different epochs [Olex et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...696A.142O/abstract).

## Baryonic Distribution and the Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM)

While galaxies and dark matter halos are the most visually prominent features of the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]], they account for only a small fraction of its normal matter. At low redshifts, early baryon censuses revealed a "missing baryons" problem, where the observed mass in stars, cold gas, and cluster plasma fell short of the total predicted by the cosmic microwave background. Theoretical models and hydrodynamical simulations predict that these [[dark-baryons|missing baryons]] do not predominantly reside in virialized halos, but are instead distributed throughout the Intergalactic Medium (IGM) within [[cosmic-filaments|Cosmic filaments]]. As structure forms hierarchically, diffuse gas falling into the gravitational potential wells of filaments is shock-heated to temperatures of $T \sim 10^5 - 10^7$ K, forming a highly ionized plasma known as the Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM) [Eckert et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.528..105E/abstract), [Tejos et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2662T/abstract). The exact distribution of this diffuse gas is highly sensitive to baryonic physics; for example, simulations such as Simba demonstrate that galactic jet feedback profoundly redistributes gas, significantly enhancing the density of the diffuse IGM on the outskirts of filaments and knots [Dong et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250716115D/abstract). Furthermore, information theory metrics applied to cosmological simulations suggest the periphery of clusters and filaments are the most complex environments, with a total statistical complexity for the IGM in the range of $10^{16}-10^{17}$ bits [Vazza et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.5447V/abstract).

Recent observational advances have successfully partitioned this cosmic baryon budget, providing robust support for the baryon-rich cosmic web scenario. Dispersion measures from extragalactic Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs), which are highly sensitive to the column density of ionized gas along the line of sight, have provided a late-[[universe|universe]] measurement of the total [[cosmic-baryon-density|Cosmic baryon density]], yielding $\Omega_b h_{70} \approx 0.049 - 0.051$ [Connor et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240916952C/abstract), [Connor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1226C/abstract). These FRB measurements indicate that approximately $70\% - 80\%$ of the total baryonic mass resides in the diffuse IGM rather than within the halos of [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] or individual galaxies [Connor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1226C/abstract). In fact, the filamentary WHIM is estimated to dominate the dispersion measure contribution for extragalactic sightlines, accounting for roughly $71\% - 80\%$ of the accumulated signal depending on redshift [Walker et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A..71W/abstract). Independent confirmation of the WHIM comes from ultraviolet absorption line studies using background quasars, where broad Lyman-$\alpha$ and O VI absorbers trace the kinematics and temperature of diffuse gas in [[inter-cluster-filaments|Inter-cluster filaments]].

Directly mapping the WHIM in emission remains exceedingly difficult due to its low density ($\lesssim 10^{-3} \text{ cm}^{-3}$) and extremely faint surface brightness, leaving its detailed 3D structure largely unmapped by direct observation. However, deep X-ray observations have resolved coherent filamentary structures of hot gas in a few rare cases. For instance, observations surrounding the galaxy cluster Abell 2744 detected an 8 Mpc filament of $10^7$ K gas, demonstrating that the WHIM accounts for $5\% - 10\%$ of the total mass within such structures [Eckert et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.528..105E/abstract). Where individual detections fall short, statistical stacking techniques have proven highly effective. By stacking the signals between pairs of luminous red galaxies, researchers have detected the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect and extended thermal X-ray emission bridging cluster pairs on scales $>3$ Mpc [Vernstrom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4178V/abstract), [Lokken et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933..134L/abstract). 

The WHIM is also observed to be a magnetized plasma. The same stacking techniques that reveal thermal X-ray emission have detected diffuse radio synchrotron emission along inter-cluster filaments [Vernstrom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4178V/abstract). These observations imply average magnetic field strengths in the range of $30 \le B \le 60 \text{ nG}$. The presence of such fields indicates that the intergalactic medium contains sufficient cosmic ray electrons and large-scale magnetic regularity (estimated at $5\% - 15\%$) to produce detectable synchrotron radiation, confirming that the WHIM is a dynamic, magnetized environment central to [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]].

## Galaxy Evolution and Environmental Quenching

The [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] acts as both a reservoir and a regulator for galaxy growth. Far from evolving as isolated "closed boxes," galaxies constantly exchange mass, energy, and heavy elements with the intergalactic medium. A galaxy's position within the hierarchy of [[cosmic-voids|Cosmic Voids]], sheets, [[cosmic-filaments|Cosmic filaments]], and nodes strongly correlates with its star-formation history, morphology, and chemical enrichment.

During their early assembly, galaxies accrete cold gas channeled by the cosmic web. The impact of this primordial gas supply is observed directly in extreme metal-poor galaxies (XMPs), where localized starbursts are fueled by the recent accretion of low-metallicity gas from the cosmic web [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...810L..15S/abstract). Furthermore, hydrodynamical simulations indicate that low-mass galaxies embedded within filaments experience a boosted star-formation rate (SFR) due to continuous mass inflow along connected streams [Galárraga-Espinosa et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...671A.160G/abstract).

However, as galaxies migrate along filaments toward denser nodes (clusters), they generally undergo environmental **quenching**. Observations and simulations consistently show that galaxies located in filaments and nodes are redder, more massive, and exhibit lower specific star formation rates (sSFR) than their counterparts in the field or in voids [Poudel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...597A..86P/abstract), [Xu et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.1839X/abstract). The mechanisms driving this transformation vary depending on the local cosmic web geometry and host halo mass:

*   **Cosmic Web Detachment (CWD):** The CWD model provides a unified geometric framework for galaxy starvation. In this paradigm, a galaxy initially accretes star-forming gas via a network of primordial filaments. As the [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] evolves and shell-crossing occurs on intergalactic scales, the galaxy is physically severed from these primordial streams. This detachment halts the accretion of cold gas, leading to a gradual exhaustion of fuel and subsequent quenching [Aragon Calvo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019OJAp....2E...7A/abstract). Interestingly, the specific nodes where quenching occurs can vary significantly across cosmic time. At high redshift ($z > 3$), massive quenched galaxies are found to preferentially reside in the "side-nodes" of Milky Way-sized halos rather than the most massive principal nodes of the web, indicating early, efficient quenching mechanisms in lower-mass environments before subsequent assembly [Remus et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231016089R/abstract).
*   **Cosmic Web Stripping:** Low-mass dwarf and satellite galaxies are susceptible to ram-pressure stripping as they traverse the diffuse, shock-heated gas within filaments and nodes. This "cosmic web stripping" can rapidly deplete their cold gas reservoirs without the need for internal feedback mechanisms [Benítez-Llambay et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763L..41B/abstract), [Hasan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...950..114H/abstract). A stark example of this is the dwarf galaxy AGC 727130, which provides the first direct observational evidence of cosmic web ram-pressure stripping affecting a field dwarf located precisely at the intersection of cosmic filaments [Luber et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993L..14L/abstract).
*   **Vorticity and Edge Effects:** The boundaries of filaments can also dynamically suppress star formation. At the vorticity-rich edges of filaments, high angular momentum can prevent external gas from efficiently penetrating the host halo, further starving the central galaxy [Song et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.4635S/abstract).

The cosmic web also influences the chemical evolution of galaxies. For instance, proximity to high-density nodes modulates the gas ecosystem around galaxies, driving higher gas-phase metallicities independently of the galaxy's stellar mass or local overdensity [Donnan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6..599D/abstract).

Despite these observed correlations, there is an ongoing and active debate regarding whether the large-scale geometry of the cosmic web exerts a direct, independent influence on galaxy properties, or if its effect is merely transitive. Some models suggest that the web's influence on quenching is entirely accounted for by the environmental modulation of the halo mass function and local density, with direct large-scale tidal effects playing a negligible role [Alam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.4501A/abstract). Conversely, other studies find that a direct environmental signal—particularly for satellite galaxies and low-mass centrals below $\sim 10^{12} h^{-1} M_\odot$—persists even at fixed halo mass [Song et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.4635S/abstract), [Poudel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...597A..86P/abstract), leaving the exact magnitude of the web's geometric impact a major open question in models of [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]]. Recent studies suggest that secondary galaxy bias, or assembly bias, places galaxies preferentially in more strongly clustered haloes when those haloes are closely associated with nodes or filaments, even at fixed [[cold-dark-matter|halo mass]] [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.4616W/abstract).

## Connectivity and Alignment Physics

The structural complexity of the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] is heavily influenced by how its components intersect and interact. The topology of these intersections, known as cosmic connectivity, alongside the kinematic alignment of halos with their local environment, offers critical insights into the acquisition of angular momentum and the assembly history of galaxies.

**Cosmic Connectivity and Mass Assembly**
Connectivity, denoted as $\kappa$, is defined as the number of cosmic filaments attached to a given node (typically hosting [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]]). Theoretical predictions based on Gaussian random fields suggest that nodes in a three-dimensional density field connect to an average of $\kappa \approx 6.1$ filaments [Codis et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479..973C/abstract). Observations and simulations both demonstrate that connectivity is strongly mass-dependent: the number of attached filaments correlates positively with the host halo mass, scaling approximately as $\propto \frac{10}{3} \log M$ [Codis et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479..973C/abstract). Furthermore, analysis from THE THREE HUNDRED simulation indicates that gas-traced connectivity decreases with cosmic time and is strongly correlated with the cluster's mass, rather than its dynamical state [Santoni et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...692A..44S/abstract). 

Because [[cosmic-filaments|filaments]] act as the primary conduits for matter inflow, a node's connectivity directly impacts the assembly history and properties of its resident galaxies. Higher connectivity implies multiple channels for cold gas accretion, which influences the specific star formation rate and the morphological evolution of galaxies at the nodes [Kraljic et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.4294K/abstract). However, the mapping between connectivity and galaxy properties is non-linear; at fixed halo mass, mechanisms such as active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback can reverse the relationship between connectivity and stellar mass [Kraljic et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.4294K/abstract).

**Spin and Shape Alignments**
Galaxies and [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] halos are not randomly oriented within the cosmic web. Their angular momentum (spin) vectors and shapes exhibit distinct alignments with the principal axes of their host filaments, a phenomenon rooted in anisotropic tidal torque theory [Forero-Romero et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1090F/abstract). 

Many studies suggest a mass-dependent transition in spin orientation. In models like Horizon-AGN, low-mass galaxies (typically spirals) preferentially align their spin axes parallel to the host filament, acquiring angular momentum through the smooth accretion of vorticity-rich gas from the filament's periphery [Codis et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.4753C/abstract), while massive galaxies (predominantly ellipticals) align perpendicular to the filament axis [Tempel et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.1827T/abstract), [Codis et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.4753C/abstract). However, this consensus is not universal: the EAGLE simulation, for instance, predicts that galaxy spins exhibit a propensity for perpendicular alignments across all masses, unlike their host dark matter halos [Ganeshaiah Veena et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.1607G/abstract). The orthogonal alignment observed in massive systems is driven by the [[hierarchical-clustering|Hierarchical clustering]] process, where massive halos build their angular momentum via major mergers migrating along the filament spine [Malavasi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.3817M/abstract), [Codis et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.4753C/abstract). Looking toward earlier epochs, forecasts for the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope suggest that prolate dark matter haloes retain strong shape-alignment with their host filaments at early times ($z > 1$), a signal that could be traced by future spectroscopic surveys [Hasan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250923549H/abstract). Beyond filaments, [[cosmic-voids|void]] environments also impart distinct kinematic signatures; observational findings indicate that spiral galaxies residing on the shells of [[cosmic-voids|Cosmic Voids]] have their rotation axes preferentially aligned within the plane of the void surface [Trujillo et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...640L.111T/abstract).

**Intrinsic Alignments and Observational Tensions**
These physical orientations are critical to modern cosmology because they manifest as "intrinsic alignments"—a major systematic contaminant in [[cosmic-shear|Cosmic shear]] and weak lensing measurements. While intrinsic alignments offer a unique probe of tidal torque theory, the literature is currently conflicting regarding the absolute magnitude of the alignment signal. Hydrodynamical simulations reveal that spin alignments are highly sensitive to sub-grid physics; for example, the alignment signal in the IllustrisTNG simulation is approximately 2.4 times weaker than that predicted by the Horizon-AGN simulation [Zhang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954...49Z/abstract). To quantify these alignments on large scales, researchers measure halo shape-direction (ED) and ellipticity-ellipticity (EE) cross-correlations. Observational and theoretical analyses demonstrate that ED correlations are significantly stronger than EE correlations, remaining statistically significant at large distances up to $\sim 50 \text{ Mpc}/h$ [Lee et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.389.1266L/abstract).

**Filament Kinematics**
Beyond the galaxies they host, the kinematic properties of the filaments themselves remain an area of active debate. While conventional models describe the matter flow around filaments primarily as a quadrupolar spin field resulting from tidal interactions [Forero-Romero et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1090F/abstract), recent analyses suggest that filaments may exhibit a coherent net rotation around their central axes [Xia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.1059X/abstract). In some regimes, this net rotational component is comparable to or dominates the quadrupolar flow, potentially providing a dynamo mechanism to generate or amplify the [[supergalactic-magnetic-field|Supergalactic magnetic field]] within the intergalactic medium [Xia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.1059X/abstract). Furthermore, these inter-cluster bridges are highly dynamic; recent observational evidence reveals that cosmic filaments can experience "split infall flows" directed toward cluster pairs, demonstrating their active response to competing gravitational potentials [Yao et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260118434Y/abstract).

## Identification Algorithms and Topological Analysis

Astrophysicists employ a diverse array of mathematical frameworks to objectively map the connectivity of the cosmic web and classify its morphology into nodes, filaments, sheets, and [[cosmic-voids|Cosmic Voids]]. These structure-finding algorithms generally fall into broadly defined categories: dynamic Hessian-based methods, topological and graph frameworks, stochastic models, and modern machine learning approaches.

**Hessian-Based Classifications**
Dynamic classifications, such as the T-web and [[v-web-threshold|V-web]] algorithms, partition [[three-dimensional-cosmic-density-fields|Three-dimensional cosmic density fields]] using the eigenvalues of the tidal or velocity shear tensors [Forero-Romero et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.1815F/abstract). In these methods, the local structural geometry is determined by ordering the eigenvalues:
$$ \lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \lambda_3 $$
Structures are defined by the number of eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ that exceed a critical threshold $\lambda_{\rm th}$, which corresponds to the number of dimensions undergoing gravitational collapse. A local volume is classified as a node (3 collapsing dimensions), a filament (2 dimensions), a sheet (1 dimension), or a void (0 dimensions). Historically, the exact choice of $\lambda_{\rm th}$ has been a source of tension in the literature, often being selected phenomenologically based on visual impressions rather than rigorous physical derivations [Forero-Romero et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.1815F/abstract). However, recent work demonstrates that a universal, physically motivated threshold can be derived by normalizing the shear tensor with the velocity divergence field, yielding a constant volume fraction threshold across different redshifts in the non-linear regime [Olex et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...696A.142O/abstract). Additionally, a hierarchical classification system based on the Hessian of the density contrast has been proposed to capture non-local bias terms, providing a more precise forward model for the dark matter field [Coloma-Nadal et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...07..083C/abstract). To bypass the need for an arbitrary, visually defined threshold entirely, Lagrangian classification approaches have also been developed. These methods utilize adaptive smoothing techniques (similar to smoothed-particle hydrodynamics) to classify individual halos dynamically based on the local velocity shear (V-web), directly linking the formation history of dark matter to its continuous environment [Fisher et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.458.1517F/abstract). New adaptive interpolation methods have been developed to compute Hessian-based environmental tensors for individual haloes, bypassing the resolution limitations of traditional 'Nearest Grid Point' mesh methods [Wang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NewA...8001405W/abstract). In phase-space, the caustic skeleton formalism provides a related analytic definition where filaments are formally identified as "cusp caustics"—multi-stream features emerging from the primordial deformation tensor [Feldbrugge et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240520475F/abstract), [Feldbrugge et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.539..873F/abstract).

**Topological and Graph-Theoretical Analysis**
To analyze the multiscale hierarchy of the web, researchers increasingly utilize persistent homology. This topological approach extends beyond the traditional Euler characteristic by computing Betti numbers, which provide a robust topological description of the web's connectivity at multiple scales [van de Weygaert et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011LNCS.6970...60V/abstract), [Pranav et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4281P/abstract). Betti numbers count the number of independent topological features of various dimensions (such as connected components, tunnels, and cavities), allowing for the rigorous identification of the imprint of cluster nodes, filaments, and walls across different density filtrations. To quantify the overall complexity and clustering of the cosmic web, researchers have also introduced metrics from Information Theory, such as graph entropy. Modeled as a discrete network, the graph entropy of the cosmic web evaluates to approximately $1.5$ to $3.2$ bits, providing a scalar measure of its intricate connectivity [García-Alvarado et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498L.145G/abstract). Furthermore, network centrality metrics—such as degree, closeness, and [[betweenness-centrality|betweenness]]—are employed as powerful tools to define distinct topological environments within the discrete galaxy distribution, efficiently distinguishing structural kernels, backbones, and fractures [Hong et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.1999H/abstract). Another prominent graph-based technique is the $\beta$-skeleton analysis, which identifies filamentary structures by evaluating empty geometric neighborhood constraints; notably, the $\beta$-skeleton proves highly sensitive to redshift-space distortions and variations in galaxy bias [Fang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.5276F/abstract).

A prominent application of discrete Morse theory in this regime is the Discrete Persistent Structure Extractor (DisPerSE). DisPerSE identifies the persistent skeleton of the cosmic web by computing the Morse-Smale complex directly from a discrete particle or galaxy distribution [Codis et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.4753C/abstract), [Luber et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AJ....157..254L/abstract). By tracing the ridges of the density field that connect density maxima (nodes) through saddle points, DisPerSE extracts a highly robust network of filamentary spines that is remarkably resilient to shot noise [Malavasi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...634A..30M/abstract).

**Machine Learning and Deep Learning Methods**
In recent years, the classification and mapping of the cosmic web have increasingly leveraged artificial intelligence. Deep-learning algorithms, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on advanced hydrodynamical simulations like IllustrisTNG, have demonstrated the ability to accurately reconstruct underlying dark matter density fields using the discrete positions and velocities of observed galaxies as input features, rather than producing continuous velocity maps as an output [Hong et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913...76H/abstract). Similarly, Graph Attention Networks (GAT) have been successfully applied to geometric galaxy descriptors, achieving high accuracy in mapping individual galaxies to their respective underlying dark matter environments without requiring intermediate density field reconstructions [Kololgi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251205909K/abstract).

**Stochastic Point-Process Models**
Alternatively, stochastic approaches like the Bisous model treat the extraction of filaments as a spatial pattern recognition problem. The [[bisous-filaments|Bisous]] model applies a marked point process directly to discrete [[galaxy-density-fields|Galaxy density fields]], fitting connected cylindrical segments to the data [Tempel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&C....16...17T/abstract). This approach intrinsically accounts for the network's connectivity, generating a probabilistic "visit map" and a filament orientation field rather than relying on a continuous underlying density grid. Recently, stochastic topological ranking approaches like the ASTRA algorithm have also been developed to robustly identify underdense regions and [[cosmic-voids|voids]], utilizing random catalogs to accurately map geometries that are inherently poorly sampled by galaxies [Forero-Romero et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025RASTI...4...32F/abstract).

**Algorithm Comparisons and Tensions**
Extensive comparisons of these different classification schemes reveal broad agreement on the universe's most massive structures; essentially all methods reliably identify dense nodes and prominent inter-cluster filaments [Libeskind et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.1195L/abstract). However, significant disparities exist in the identification of tenuous filaments, the exact volume-filling fractions of sheets, and the precise boundaries of voids [Libeskind et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453L.108L/abstract), [Libeskind et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.1195L/abstract). These discrepancies underscore that while the cosmic web is a robust physical reality, the characterization of its finest features remains highly sensitive to the chosen mathematical framework.

## Cosmic Web as a Cosmological Probe

The statistical and topological properties of the large-scale matter distribution serve as stringent tests for [[cosmological-parameters-and-frameworks|Cosmological Parameters and Frameworks]]. By analyzing the geometry, kinematics, and mass distribution of the web, astrophysicists can place tight constraints on [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]], dark energy, and the sum of neutrino masses.

In the standard paradigm of [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]], [[halo-clustering|Halo clustering]] is primarily dictated by total halo mass. However, theoretical and numerical studies demonstrate a strong secondary dependence on the cosmic web environment. For example, halos with masses of $\sim 10^{12} h^{-1} M_{\odot}$ residing in [[cosmic-voids|Cosmic Voids]] exhibit clustering biases that are enhanced by up to a factor of 10 compared to the average halo population [Yang et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848...60Y/abstract). While underdense regions are frequently proposed as sensitive laboratories to test dark matter particle candidates, comparative studies reveal limitations. When models of Warm Dark Matter (WDM) and [[cold-dark-matter|Cold Dark Matter (CDM)]] are abundance-matched, their respective void statistics (such as void size distributions) are practically indistinguishable, significantly challenging the use of void sizes alone as a simple probe to differentiate dark matter models [Reed et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451.4413R/abstract). The internal properties of these halos—such as their shape, concentration, and velocity dispersion—correlate strongly with the local tidal anisotropy of the web. Such correlations between internal properties and large-scale tidal anisotropy are central to understanding halo assembly bias [Ramakrishnan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.2977R/abstract). Furthermore, Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD) properties explicitly differ depending on the cosmic web component. For instance, massive nodes exhibit an unexpected excess of faint, blue galaxies residing in low-mass halos, driven by continuous mass infall from connecting filaments [Perez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.3186P/abstract).

Direct measurements of the web's structure increasingly leverage weak gravitational lensing. Stacking analyses of background galaxies around luminous red galaxy pairs have successfully isolated the weak lensing signal of inter-cluster filaments. These observations confirm that filaments are dark-matter dominated, exhibiting a mass-to-light ratio of $M/L = 351 \pm 137$ in solar units (in the $r$-band) and a total stellar mass fraction of $M_{\mathrm{stellar}}/M = 0.0073 \pm 0.0030$ [Yang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.3158Y/abstract). The cosmic web also lenses the cosmic microwave background, yielding an estimated macroscopic filament bias of $\sim 1.5$ [He et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018NatAs...2..401H/abstract). Conversely, the intrinsic alignment of galaxy shapes and spins with [[cosmic-filaments|cosmic filaments]] acts as a major systematic contaminant for [[cosmic-shear|Cosmic shear]] surveys [Codis et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.4753C/abstract).

Beyond characterizing dark matter and baryonic feedback, the detailed morphology of the web is highly sensitive to the free-streaming of massive neutrinos. Massive neutrinos actively delay structure formation and shift the volume-weighted density distribution of the [[universe|universe]]. This effect can be precisely probed using the scale-dependent edge lengths of a Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) fitted to the cosmic web, offering a powerful statistical tool to constrain neutrino masses [Simões et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251216517S/abstract). Theoretical models also propose that the nonlinear broadening of filaments—and the resulting impact on the galaxy shape-shape correlation function, $\eta(r)$—can be used to further constrain the total neutrino mass $M_{\nu}$ [Lee et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200614477L/abstract). Specifically, the $M_{\nu}$-dependence of $\eta(r)$ at scales $r \le 5\,h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ is sufficiently sensitive to distinguish between $M_{\nu} = 0.0$ eV and $M_{\nu} = 0.1$ eV. This structural metric offers a pathway to break the longstanding degeneracy between $M_{\nu}$ and the amplitude of the [[dark-matter-power-spectrum|Dark matter power spectrum]], $\sigma_8$ [Lee et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200614477L/abstract). 

Finally, global kinematic and topological metrics provide complementary cosmological constraints. The Cosmic Mach Number—the ratio of mean velocity to velocity dispersion across different scales—is highly sensitive to structure growth at wavenumbers $k \in [0.01, 0.1] \, h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$, helping differentiate between baseline and modified gravity models [Ma et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JCAP...06..026M/abstract). Similarly, topological invariants such as Betti numbers reflect the hierarchical evolution of the network [van de Weygaert et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.3640V/abstract), collectively establishing the cosmic web as a multi-scale laboratory for fundamental physics.

## Recent Advances in Mapping and Tomography

While large-scale galaxy redshift surveys effectively map the luminous skeleton of the [[universe|universe]], observing the diffuse dark matter and the Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM) that comprises the bulk of the baryonic web requires advanced observational techniques. Recent efforts have moved beyond simple galaxy tracing to utilize cross-correlation, stacking, and tomographic methods to map the previously "dark" threads of the cosmic web.

At high redshifts ($z \sim 2-3$), [[large-scale-tomography|IGM tomography]] of the Lyman-alpha forest provides a high-fidelity 3D map of the intergalactic medium. By exploiting absorption spectra from densely spaced background quasars and star-forming galaxies (with sightline spacings $\lesssim 5 \ h^{-1} \ \mathrm{Mpc}$), surveys such as CLAMATO can accurately recover the eigenvectors of the local tidal tensor and directly classify the geometry of [[cosmic-voids|Cosmic Voids]], sheets, filaments, and nodes [Krolewski et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837...31K/abstract). This tomographic reconstruction classifies roughly $70\%$ of the surveyed volume correctly relative to the underlying dark matter field, circumventing the need for impossibly dense high-redshift galaxy surveys and enabling precise studies of [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] and spin alignments during the peak of cosmic star formation [Lee et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..181L/abstract).

In the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]], the extreme faintness of the WHIM presents a significant observational hurdle. However, stacking techniques applied to large galaxy catalogs have successfully unveiled diffuse [[inter-cluster-filaments|inter-cluster filaments]]. By stacking the weak lensing shear around pairs of luminous red galaxies (LRGs), researchers have directly mapped the dark matter in filaments, confirming that these structures are highly dark-matter-dominated with a total mass-to-light ratio of $M/L = 351 \pm 137 \ M_\odot/L_\odot$ in the $r$-band [Yang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.3158Y/abstract). Furthermore, multi-wavelength stacking of LRG pairs across radio and X-ray data has revealed thermal and synchrotron emission spanning physical scales $>3 \ \mathrm{Mpc}$ [Vernstrom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4178V/abstract). These detections imply an average inter-cluster magnetic field strength of $30-60 \ \mathrm{nG}$, confirming the existence of magnetized plasma within the web [Vernstrom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4178V/abstract). Direct X-ray observations of individual massive systems, such as Abell 2744, have traced $10^7 \ \mathrm{K}$ gas filaments extending over $8 \ \mathrm{Mpc}$, confirming that $5-10\%$ of the local mass in these regions is baryonic [Eckert et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.528..105E/abstract).

To bridge the gap between discrete galaxy coordinates and continuous density fields, probabilistic and biologically inspired algorithms have gained traction. A notable example is the Monte Carlo Physarum Machine (MCPM), modeled on the growth of *Physarum polycephalum* slime mold. Applied to galaxy and absorption-line surveys, this method infers the underlying [[three-dimensional-dark-matter-maps|Three-dimensional dark matter maps]] and demonstrates that H I absorption peaks at overdensities of $\sim 200 \rho_m$ before being suppressed in the densest environments—likely due to shock heating and feedback processes [Burchett et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...891L..35B/abstract). This method was recently deployed on the SDSS DR17 to release the "Cosmic Slime Value Added Catalog," providing fully reconstructed 3D density cubes and cosmic overdensity estimates for low-redshift spectroscopic samples [Wilde et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230102719W/abstract).

Finally, the advent of space-based wide-field imaging is currently revolutionizing the scale of cosmic web mapping. Initial results from the Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1) have demonstrated the mission's capability to map the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] over $63.1 \ \mathrm{deg}^2$ at $0.5 < z < 0.9$ [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315333E/abstract). Using photometric redshifts to isolate tomographic slices of $170 \ h^{-1} \ \mathrm{Mpc}$ comoving thickness, early Euclid data has successfully extracted projected cosmic filaments, enabling robust measurements of cluster connectivity and confirming the 2D alignment of massive early-type galaxies with filament spines [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315332E/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315333E/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the fundamental morphological components of the large-scale structure are now well-established, the detailed thermodynamics, kinematics, and galaxy-environment interactions within these structures remain active areas of research. 

A central observational challenge is direct missing baryon detection. While recent studies utilizing Fast Radio Burst dispersion measures and stacked X-ray and radio observations have robustly constrained the [[total-baryon-fraction|Total baryon fraction]] in the intergalactic medium (IGM) [Connor et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240916952C/abstract), [Vernstrom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4178V/abstract), mapping the diffuse Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM) without relying on stacking techniques remains exceptionally difficult. Next-generation observatories like Athena are expected to directly characterize the thermodynamics of this elusive phase [Kaastra et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.2324K/abstract). Furthermore, the proposed Cosmic Web Explorer mission concept aims to survey up to $100\%$ of the $T > 10^6$ K diffuse gas in filaments, pushing detection limits down to overdensities of order unity [Simionescu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ExA....51.1043S/abstract). The upcoming SKA era will also revolutionize this field by providing sub-arcminute interferometric observations capable of probing neutral hydrogen column densities below $10^{18}$ cm$^{-2}$ across the vast cosmic network [Popping et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015aska.confE.132P/abstract).

Another major theoretical and observational frontier is the characterization of intergalactic magnetic fields within [[cosmic-filaments|filaments]]. Current stacking analyses of synchrotron emission from inter-cluster bridges suggest average magnetic field strengths of $30-60$ nG [Vernstrom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4178V/abstract). The mechanism generating these fields is an open question. Recent cosmological simulations indicate that filaments may possess a net coherent rotation (spin) [Xia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.1059X/abstract). Whether this macroscopic rotation serves as a primary dynamo for amplifying primordial magnetic fields, or if the flow is predominantly a quadrupolar shear field driven by [[hierarchical-clustering|Hierarchical clustering]], remains a subject of active debate [Codis et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.4753C/abstract), [Forero-Romero et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1090F/abstract).

The precise role of the cosmic web in [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] and evolution remains contested. As previously noted, it is unclear whether the web's geometric environment influences galaxy quenching directly via mechanisms like "cosmic web stripping" [Benítez-Llambay et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763L..41B/abstract) and "cosmic web detachment" [Aragon Calvo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019OJAp....2E...7A/abstract), or if these effects are entirely driven by the environmental modulation of the host [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] halo mass [Alam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.4501A/abstract).

Finally, the alignment of galaxy spins with filament axes offers a promising probe of tidal torque theory, yet predictions from hydrodynamical simulations exhibit significant tension. The magnitude of the spin-alignment signal varies substantially between different simulations (e.g., being notably weaker in IllustrisTNG compared to Horizon-AGN), highlighting a strong sensitivity to sub-grid physics such as active galactic nucleus feedback [Zhang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954...49Z/abstract). Resolving these theoretical discrepancies is critical, as intrinsic galaxy alignments represent a major systematic uncertainty in [[cosmic-shear|Cosmic shear]] measurements for future cosmological surveys.
