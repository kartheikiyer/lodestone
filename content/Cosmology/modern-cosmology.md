Modern cosmology is the branch of astrophysics dedicated to understanding the origin, evolution, and large-scale geometry of the [[universe|Universe]]. Grounded in Einstein's theory of general relativity and the Cosmological Principle—which posits that the universe is homogeneous and isotropic on large macroscopic scales—the field is mathematically underpinned by the [[friedmann-equations|Friedmann equations]] and the FLRW spacetime metric [Dodelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020moco.book.....D/abstract). By integrating these theoretical foundations with precision observational data, modern cosmology traces the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] of the cosmos from a hot, dense initial state known as the Hot [[big-bang|Big Bang]] [Zeldovich et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983reas.book.....Z/abstract), through a theorized epoch of rapid cosmic inflation, to the present-day era of [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]]. 

The contemporary cornerstone of the field is the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], a concordance paradigm that successfully describes a geometrically flat universe dominated by a dark sector. In this framework, dark energy (represented by the cosmological constant, $\Lambda$) drives the accelerated expansion of the spacetime metric, while non-relativistic [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] (CDM) governs the gravitational dynamics of mass [Liddle et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PhR...231....1L/abstract). Utilizing [[cosmological-perturbation-theory|Cosmological Perturbation Theory]], the model details how microscopic primordial quantum fluctuations evolved under gravitational instability. Through the continuous process of [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]], these initial perturbations underwent [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]] to assemble the intricate [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] of voids, filaments, and galaxy clusters that define the present-day [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract).

The transition of cosmology into a rigorous, data-driven empirical science has been propelled by a synthesis of diverse observational probes and advanced theoretical modeling. Precision measurements of the cosmic microwave background (CMB), Type Ia supernovae, baryon acoustic oscillations, and weak gravitational lensing have tightly constrained the cosmological [[parameter-space|Parameter space]] [Peacock et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph..9240P/abstract). To bridge the gap between primordial initial conditions and observable late-time structures, cosmologists rely heavily on high-resolution $N$-body and hydrodynamical simulations. These numerical models are essential for interpreting non-linear clustering, testing baryonic feedback mechanisms, and predicting the observable distribution of galaxies.

Despite its sweeping predictive successes, the standard paradigm faces increasingly significant observational challenges that motivate the next generation of research. Precision measurements have revealed persistent statistical discrepancies, most notably the Hubble tension regarding the local versus early-universe expansion rate, and the [[s8-tension|S8 tension]] concerning the amplitude of late-time matter clustering. Furthermore, recent high-redshift discoveries by the James Webb Space Telescope—such as the existence of extraordinarily massive galaxies very early in the cosmic timeline—challenge the established assembly timescales of standard cosmology. These anomalies have catalyzed intense investigation into [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]], modified theories of gravity, and interacting dark sector physics. Over the next decade, upcoming wide-field spectroscopic surveys and the advent of [[21-cm-cosmology|21-cm Cosmology]] will rigorously test these boundaries, aiming to illuminate the fundamental physical nature of the dark sector and resolve the most pressing open questions in the field.

## Foundations of the Cosmological Paradigm

The theoretical framework of modern cosmology rests upon two foundational pillars: Albert Einstein’s theory of general relativity and the Cosmological Principle. General relativity dictates that the macroscopic space-time geometry of the [[universe|Universe]] is determined by its matter and energy distribution. To solve Einstein's field equations on cosmic scales, standard cosmology introduces the Cosmological Principle, which posits that the Universe is spatially homogeneous and isotropic when averaged over sufficiently large distances (typically $\gtrsim 100$ Mpc). This principle implies the existence of a "fundamental observer" at each spatial location for whom the Universe appears locally isotropic, effectively defining a cosmological rest frame that aligns with the rest frame of the cosmic microwave background [Hamilton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract).

The mathematical synthesis of general relativity and the Cosmological Principle yields the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric. By enforcing strict homogeneity and isotropy, the generic and highly complex spacetime metric simplifies to an expression governed entirely by a time-dependent scale factor, $a(t)$, and a constant spatial curvature signature, $K$ (where $K = 0, +1, -1$ corresponds to a spatially flat, closed, or open geometry, respectively). The dynamical evolution of $a(t)$ is dictated by the [[friedmann-equations|Friedmann equations]], which link the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] of the Universe to its total energy density and pressure. This metric formulation forms the geometric backbone of the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], providing an empirically successful description of the expanding Universe [Hamilton et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract), [Lombriser et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvD..83f3519L/abstract). 

While the FLRW metric assumes perfect uniformity, the observed Universe exhibits profound complexity on smaller scales. If the Cosmological Principle held exactly at all scales, the formation of galaxies and clusters would be impossible. Consequently, the standard paradigm utilizes [[cosmological-perturbation-theory|Cosmological Perturbation Theory]] to describe how small primordial density fluctuations grew via gravitational instability into the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] observed today.

Despite the predictive success of the FLRW paradigm, the absolute validity of the Cosmological Principle remains a subject of active debate in the literature. The emergence of statistical discrepancies—most notably the $H_0$ tension—alongside unexpected large-scale bulk flows and cosmic dipoles, has prompted questions regarding whether precision cosmology has outgrown the strict FLRW symmetry assumption [Kumar Aluri et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40i4001K/abstract). Furthermore, because the Universe is demonstrably nonlinear on scales $< 200$ Mpc, a body of research is dedicated to inhomogeneous cosmologies and kinematic backreaction. To describe cosmic voids and structures without relying on averaging approximations, exact inhomogeneous solutions to Einstein's equations have been studied extensively [Krasinski et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997icm..book.....K/abstract). For instance, foundational analysis of relativistic models that do not obey the Cosmological Principle, as well as the rigorous investigation of perturbations in isotropic universes, was early on detailed by Raychaudhuri [Raychaudhuri et al. (1979)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1979thco.book.....R/abstract). More recently, the Fractal Bubble model was proposed as a specific inhomogeneous framework that attempted to explain cosmic acceleration purely via structure formation, although it faces significant challenges from modern supernova data [Kwan et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.399L...6K/abstract). Modern cosmological simulations that directly solve Einstein's equations have also been utilized to rigorously quantify the backreaction of small-scale structures on the global [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] [Macpherson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191013380M/abstract). These alternative frameworks investigate whether the mathematical process of averaging a lumpy matter distribution fails to commute with Einstein's nonlinear equations, potentially generating large-scale dynamical effects that could modify or even mimic the signal of dark energy without requiring a cosmological constant [Wijenayake et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..94h3501W/abstract), [Lane et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231101438L/abstract). While constraints on the backreaction [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]] parameter currently remain tight, evaluating the survival of the FLRW metric in the presence of [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]] remains a critical test for foundational cosmology.

## Historical Development and the Hot Big Bang

Following the formulation of general relativity, early cosmological models generally assumed a static [[universe|Universe]]. To counteract the attractive force of gravity and permit a static solution, Albert Einstein famously introduced a [[positive-cosmological-constant|Positive cosmological constant]], $\Lambda$, into his field equations. However, Alexander Friedmann (1922) and Georges Lemaître (1927) independently discovered dynamical solutions to the [[friedmann-equations|Friedmann equations]] that allowed for a time-varying scale factor, $a(t)$. The static paradigm was definitively overturned when observations of galactic redshifts established that the distance to a galaxy is proportional to its recession velocity [Kirshner et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PNAS..101....8K/abstract). This observed expansion implied that the universe must have been fundamentally smaller, denser, and hotter in the past.

In the mid-20th century, two dominant theoretical frameworks emerged to describe this [[expansion-history|Expansion history]]. George Gamow and his collaborators proposed that the early universe underwent a hot, dense phase during which the light elements were synthesized via primordial nucleosynthesis. This framework—derisively termed the [[big-bang|Big Bang]] by its critics—predicted a pervasive background of residual thermal radiation. In stark contrast, Hermann Bondi, Thomas Gold, and Fred Hoyle advocated for [[quasi-steady-state-cosmology|Quasi-steady state cosmology]]. Their model was based on the "perfect cosmological principle," which posited that the universe is not only homogeneous and isotropic in space but also invariant in time. To maintain a constant mean [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]] in an expanding volume, the Steady state model necessitated the continuous creation of new matter [McCrea et al. (1964)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1964MNRAS.128..335M/abstract).

The intense debate between these models was decisively resolved by the 1965 discovery of the cosmic microwave background (CMB). The CMB was found to exhibit an almost perfect blackbody spectrum with a temperature of a few Kelvin, precisely matching the thermal relic radiation predicted by the Hot [[big-bang|Big Bang]] scenario. The existence of this primordial radiation field, coupled with subsequent detections of strong evolution in the galaxy population at high redshifts, proved insurmountable for the Steady state cosmology [Silk et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980bbce.book.....S/abstract).

Following the validation of the Hot Big Bang, observational cosmology transitioned from a data-starved discipline into a robust empirical science. For decades, the field was famously characterized by Allan Sandage as the search for just two kinematics numbers: the Hubble constant $H_0$, which describes the present [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]], and the deceleration parameter, $q_0$, which quantifies the rate at which expansion is slowing down due to gravity [Bolotin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150200811B/abstract). Today, the framework of an expanding universe originating from a hot initial state provides the foundational bedrock for the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], seamlessly connecting the quantum origins of the universe to the mature [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] we observe today [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract).

## Cosmic Inflation and the Early Universe

The standard [[big-bang|Big Bang]] framework successfully describes a universe expanding from a hot, dense initial state, leading naturally to the existence of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) and the synthesis of light elements. However, by the 1960s and 1970s, it became clear that this classical picture suffered from severe theoretical shortcomings. Chief among these were the horizon problem—the fact that causally disconnected regions of the observable [[universe|Universe]] possess a highly uniform CMB temperature—and the flatness problem, which asks why the cosmic density parameter $\Omega$ was tuned so extraordinarily close to unity at early times.

To resolve these geometrical puzzles, the paradigm of cosmic inflation was proposed by Alan Guth in 1981, and shortly thereafter refined by Andrei Linde, as well as Andreas Albrecht and Paul Steinhardt. Inflation postulates a brief epoch of rapid, exponential expansion in the very early universe, driven by the vacuum energy of one or more scalar fields. During this phase, the scale factor $a(t)$ grew immensely, stretching the curvature radius of the universe until it became observationally indistinguishable from a [[spatially-flat-cosmological-model|Spatially flat cosmological model]]. Furthermore, regions that currently form the observable universe were in close causal contact prior to inflation, naturally establishing the large-scale homogeneity and isotropy observed today.

Beyond resolving the horizon and flatness problems, inflation provides a physical mechanism for generating the [[initial-conditions-in-astrophysics|Initial Conditions in Astrophysics]] necessary for [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]]. Quantum fluctuations in the vacuum energy of the inflaton field were stretched to macroscopic scales during the exponential expansion, freezing in as primordial density perturbations. These perturbations are generically predicted to follow a nearly scale-invariant power-law spectrum, validating the classical Harrison-Zeldovich spectrum . Modern constraints, which combine CMB polarization data with large-scale galaxy redshift surveys, have allowed the primordial curvature perturbation power spectrum to be reconstructed at subpercent levels .

Following the inflationary epoch, the scalar field decayed, transferring its energy into a hot plasma of standard model particles during a process known as reheating. This transition marked the beginning of the [[radiation-dominated-era|Radiation-dominated era]]. It is during these subsequent hot, dense phases that baryogenesis must have occurred to explain the observed matter-antimatter asymmetry. Shortly after, Big Bang Nucleosynthesis dictated the fusion of protons and neutrons into the lightest elements, yielding primordial abundances that match modern astrophysical observations. 

While inflation is firmly embedded within the standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], our understanding of the very early universe remains partly phenomenological. The exact physical mechanism of reheating is not fully understood, and the specific parameters of the scalar field potential remain loosely constrained [Brandenberger et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997hep.ph....1276B/abstract). Furthermore, while inflationary quantum fluctuations are the primary source of primordial inhomogeneities, the literature remains open to the possibility that non-adiabatic, incoherent fluctuations—such as those generated by topological defects like cosmic strings—might also contribute to the initial density field [Brandenberger et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997hep.ph....1276B/abstract), [Brandenberger et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PhRvD..69h1301B/abstract). Following these [[early-universe-epochs|Early universe epochs]], the universe continued to expand and cool, eventually leading to the epoch of recombination and, approximately one billion years later, the completion of cosmic reionization [Wise et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190706653W/abstract).

## The ΛCDM Model and the Dark Sector

The [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] ($\Lambda$CDM) is firmly established as the standard concordance paradigm of modern cosmology. It provides a highly constrained and consistent description of the universe's geometry and expansion history across a wide array of independent probes, including the cosmic microwave background (CMB), Type Ia supernovae, and baryon acoustic oscillations [Hamilton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract), [Bennett et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006Natur.440.1126B/abstract). In this framework, the [[energy-content|Energy content]] of the universe is dominated by the dark sector: $\sim 75\%$ of the energy density is due to a cosmological constant ($\Lambda$), $\sim 21\%$ is due to cold dark matter (CDM), and the remaining $4\%$ is due to baryonic matter [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract).

The dark matter hypothesis itself has a rich historical context, evolving from early studies concerning galactic disk stability to its indispensable role in the modern concordance cosmology [Sanders et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010dmp..book.....S/abstract). Cold dark matter consists of non-baryonic, collisionless particles that were non-relativistic at the epoch of decoupling. It serves as the primary driver of [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]], providing the gravitational potential wells into which baryons fall to form galaxies [Liddle et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PhR...231....1L/abstract). While the exact [[dark-matter-mass|Dark matter mass]] and particle species remain unknown, theoretical predictions and observational evidence from cosmic ray knee features suggest a potential dark matter particle mass scale at approximately $8.1$ TeV [Tomozawa et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1002.1327T/abstract). Additionally, observational limits on neutrino masses restrict the contribution of hot dark matter, and small-scale structure data constrain the allowed mass scales for warm dark matter (WDM) candidates [Hannestad et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.10133H/abstract), [Primack et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001canp.book..287P/abstract). Although standard CDM excellently reproduces large-scale structure, tensions exist on sub-galactic scales. Anomalies such as the unexpectedly strong clustering of diffuse blue dwarf galaxies [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250403305Z/abstract) and the discovery of ultra-diffuse galaxies like NGC 1052-DF2 that apparently lack dark matter [Haslbauer et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.2634H/abstract) conflict with standard CDM halo predictions, prompting ongoing investigations into self-interacting dark matter (SIDM) and baryonic feedback mechanisms. As phenomenological alternatives, [[generalized-dark-matter|Generalized Dark Matter]] (GDM) models have been developed that allow for the independent specification of the dark matter's equation of state and its clustering properties [Hu et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...506..485H/abstract).

Dark energy, represented by the cosmological constant $\Lambda$, is modeled as a uniform vacuum energy density, which drives the observed late-time accelerated expansion of the universe [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract). While $\Lambda$ is the simplest and most successful mechanism, researchers also explore dynamical [[dark-energy-models|Dark energy models]] and interacting vacuum models. Precision tests using weak lensing and galaxy clustering are only sensitive to the sound speed of dark energy perturbations if it is highly non-relativistic ($c_s \le 0.01$); for larger sound speeds, current observations cannot constrain the perturbations [Sapone et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvD..82j3535S/abstract).

Despite its predictive power, the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm faces persistent statistical discrepancies. Most notable are the $H_0$ tension (a discrepancy between the local and CMB-inferred expansion rates) and the [[s8-tension|S8 tension]] regarding the amplitude of matter fluctuations [Schneider et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3802S/abstract), [Kumar Aluri et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40i4001K/abstract). Furthermore, the recent JWST discoveries of excessively massive galaxies at redshifts $z > 7\text{--}10$ and over-abundances of massive cluster structures at $z > 2$ fundamentally challenge the structure formation timescales predicted by $\Lambda$CDM [Coley et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231214738C/abstract), [Kang et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JKAS...48...21K/abstract).

Because of these tensions, the literature exhibits active debate regarding [[dark-energy-and-modified-gravity|Dark Energy and Modified Gravity]] theories. Models such as Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) and Scalar-Tensor-Vector Gravity (MOG) can naturally explain galactic rotation curves and the dynamics of bound objects without invoking a dark matter particle [McGaugh et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015CaJPh..93..250M/abstract), [Moffat et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020EPJC...80..906M/abstract). However, these alternative models often struggle to match the CMB acoustic peaks and structure formation statistics as cleanly as $\Lambda$CDM [McGaugh et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015CaJPh..93..250M/abstract), [Zeng et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170100690Z/abstract). Consequently, whether the anomalies in the dark sector point to undiscovered particle physics, unmodeled baryonic astrophysics, or a breakdown of General Relativity on cosmological scales remains unresolved.

## Large-Scale Structure and Hierarchical Formation

According to the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] emerged from microscopic primordial density fluctuations seeded during an early inflationary epoch. These initial perturbations grew over billions of years via gravitational instability. Historically, early theoretical work also explored how collision-free particles, such as neutrinos, can dissipate small-scale density perturbations via a mechanism akin to Landau damping, a process that inherently complicates the timeline of galaxy formation if such particles dominate [Stewart et al. (1972)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1972ApJ...176..323S/abstract). During the matter-dominated era, structure growth is initially scale-independent and accurately described by [[linear-theory|Linear theory]]. However, as local overdensities cross a critical density threshold, they decouple from the background cosmic expansion and collapse, transitioning into the regime of [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]] [Liddle et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000cils.book.....L/abstract). Comprehensive reviews of structure growth demonstrate that deriving its behavior as a function of redshift, typically parameterized via [[f-8|fσ8]], serves as a powerful method to test general relativity across cosmic time [Huterer et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&ARv..31....2H/abstract). 

This non-linear evolution gives rise to the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]], a vast and complex network characterized by dense galaxy clusters connected by elongated dark matter filaments, which are separated by expansive, low-density voids [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract), [Burchett et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...891L..35B/abstract). While the universe is assumed to be statistically homogeneous on the largest scales, it exhibits profound spatial inhomogeneity on scales $< 200$ Mpc [Sylos Labini et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010JSMTE..11..029S/abstract). 

The prevailing framework for this structural evolution is [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]]. Driven by the cold, non-relativistic nature of dark matter, structure formation proceeds in a "bottom-up" fashion. Small-scale density peaks collapse first to form low-mass halos, which subsequently grow through continuous mass accretion and hierarchical merging into increasingly massive systems [Gnedin et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..270..381G/abstract). The spatial distribution, mass function, and internal density profiles of these virialized structures are centrally described by [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|Dark Matter Halo Properties and Statistics]] [Borgani et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008LNP...740..287B/abstract). In the nearby universe, isolated dwarf galaxies provide a direct test of this [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]], with observations of their number density remaining consistent with theoretical predictions down to circular velocities of approximately 50 km/s [Blanton et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...682..861B/abstract). Crucially, fitting functions for the halo mass function have proven remarkably consistent from massive cluster scales down to the micro-halo scales associated with GeV dark matter particles [Cui et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240603829C/abstract). In this paradigm, smaller progenitor halos that survive the merging process become bound subhalos, providing the gravitational potential wells necessary to host dwarf galaxies.

Despite its broad successes, the hierarchical assembly model currently faces severe observational tensions, particularly at the extremes of the mass and redshift scales. Recent data from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have revealed a population of exceptionally massive galaxies ($M > 10^{10} M_\odot$) at redshifts $z > 7\text{--}10$. These objects appear too early in the cosmic timeline to be easily accommodated by the gradual growth rates predicted in standard cosmology [Coley et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231214738C/abstract). Similarly, surveys have identified an over-abundance of massive structures (masses $> 7 \times 10^{13} M_\odot$) at $z > 2$, which conflicts with the predicted halo mass functions derived from numerical simulations, showing a probability of consistency of $< 1/2500$ [Kang et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JKAS...48...21K/abstract). To explore these extreme regions, the MAMMOTH project has pioneered methodologies utilizing Ly-$\alpha$ forest absorption to effectively map the intergalactic gas reservoirs associated with the most massive proto-clusters [Cai et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..135C/abstract).

Furthermore, the predicted small-scale distribution of dark matter frequently clashes with local observations of dwarf galaxies. Near-field cosmology reveals that star formation histories in Local Group dwarf spheroidals often exhibit extended episodes that conflict with standard reionization models, which predict an abrupt quenching of star formation [Grebel et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005nfcd.conf....1G/abstract). For example, the ultra-diffuse dwarf galaxy NGC 1052-DF2 exhibits a severe deficit of dark matter, placing its kinematics in up to $4.1\sigma$ tension with the halo expectations of the standard cosmological model [Haslbauer et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.2634H/abstract). Additionally, unexpected clustering patterns in diffuse blue dwarf galaxies deviate from standard predictions [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250403305Z/abstract). These discrepancies highlight ongoing ambiguities in the literature and suggest that either the treatment of baryonic feedback in simulations is incomplete, or alternative dark sector physics—such as self-interacting dark matter or modified gravity—may be required to fully explain the formation of the cosmic web [de Martino et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Univ....6..107D/abstract).

## Observational Probes and Precision Measurements

Historically, observational cosmology was characterized as the search for two numbers: the Hubble constant $H_0$ and the deceleration parameter $q_0$ [Bolotin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150200811B/abstract). Modern cosmology, however, operates in a precision regime where multiple independent probes tightly constrain the [[parameter-space|Parameter space]] of the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]]. Collectively, these observations confirm a geometrically flat universe dominated by dark energy and cold dark matter [Bennett et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006Natur.440.1126B/abstract), [Hamilton et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract).

To map the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] of the universe, cosmologists rely heavily on standard candles and standard rulers. Type Ia supernovae (SNIa) provide direct measurements of the luminosity distance-redshift relation, $d_L(z)$, which offered the first definitive evidence for [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]] [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract). Complementing supernovae, Gamma-Ray Bursts (GRBs) have also emerged as promising high-redshift standard candles that can constrain the dark energy equation of state deeper in the cosmic timeline [Wang et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...667....1W/abstract). Complementing these standard candles, measured via the galaxy two-point correlation function, the BAO feature serves as a standard ruler that measures the cosmic distance scale [Hansen et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519..799H/abstract). Additionally, new techniques evaluating the distribution of angles between pairs of galaxies in redshift space offer complementary tests of cosmic expansion that do not require prior knowledge of the background cosmology [Jennings et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.420.1079J/abstract). When conducting angular clustering analyses with such photometric surveys, it is critically important to use full photometric redshift [[probability-density-function-cosmology|Probability density function (cosmology)]] (PDFs) rather than single point estimates in order to substantially reduce measurement bias [Asorey et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.1293A/abstract). Beyond standard two-point statistics, redshift-space skew-spectra have been introduced as a simplified summary statistic for efficiently extracting information from the galaxy bispectrum to constrain growth rates [Schmittfull et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...03..020S/abstract). To maximize precision in modern wide-field surveys, perturbation theory has been further applied to develop second-order relativistic galaxy number counts and refined distance-redshift relations [Fuentes et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210610181F/abstract).

To measure the growth of cosmic structure, weak lensing is a primary tool. Cosmic shear—the slight distortion of background galaxy shapes due to foreground mass—directly maps the underlying dark matter density field. Tomographic weak lensing constraints provide precise measurements of the amplitude of matter fluctuations, typically parameterized by combinations of $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$ [Sapone et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvD..82j3535S/abstract). To improve precision, linear transformations of the galaxy-galaxy lensing observable are increasingly used to remove nonlocality, significantly reducing sensitivity to nonlinear small-scale modeling errors [Park et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvL.126b1301P/abstract). While highly sensitive, cosmic shear measurements are susceptible to intrinsic alignments and systematic shape measurement biases, driving the development of hybrid physical and deep learning models to correct for morphological errors [Remy et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221016243R/abstract). In a separate regime, machine learning approaches have also been proposed for slitless spectroscopic surveys to effectively mitigate redshift interloper contamination and reconstruct the true power spectrum monopole [Cagliari et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250406919C/abstract). Notably, weak lensing surveys consistently yield clustering amplitudes lower than those predicted by primary cosmic microwave background (CMB) data, a discrepancy known as the [[s8-tension|S8 tension]] [Schneider et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3802S/abstract). 

[[cluster-cosmology|Cluster cosmology]] offers an independent, complementary measure of structure growth. The Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect (tSZ)—a spectral distortion of the CMB caused by inverse Compton scattering in the hot intracluster medium—provides a redshift-independent method for cataloging massive galaxy clusters [Carlstrom et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ARA&A..40..643C/abstract), [Kitayama et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PTEP.2014fB111K/abstract). These massive structures are powerful tools for observational cosmology, tracing the nonlinear growth of primordial density fluctuations and remaining highly sensitive to the fundamental properties of dark matter and dark energy [Vikhlinin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhyU...57..317V/abstract). Facilities like the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) play a key role in measuring the CMB temperature evolution and secondary anisotropies such as the kinetic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect, setting new constraints on these structures [Messias et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015AIPC.1647...62M/abstract). Because the tSZ signal is highly non-Gaussian, advanced higher-order statistics, such as Minkowski functionals, can yield cosmological constraints significantly tighter than power spectra alone [Sabyr et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241021247S/abstract). However, the literature remains conflicted on cluster abundances; counts derived from tSZ and other observables often indicate fewer clusters than predicted by Planck primary CMB data. It is currently ambiguous whether this discrepancy points to unmodeled baryonic feedback, systematic mass calibration errors, or genuine departures from the standard model [Caldwell et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.4117C/abstract), [Schneider et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3802S/abstract).

Finally, the connection between the CMB and late-time structure is probed via the integrated [[sachs-wolfe-effect|Sachs-Wolfe effect]]. As the universe accelerates, the redshift evolution of the growth rate of the gravitational potential, $d(D/a)/dz$, leaves an imprint on the CMB [Cooray et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PhRvD..69b7301C/abstract). Cross-correlating this signal with high-redshift galaxy surveys provides a rigorous consistency check for the phenomenological validity of the $\Lambda$CDM framework and acts as a powerful discriminator for dark energy models and alternative theories of gravity [Lombriser et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvD..83f3519L/abstract).

## Numerical Simulations and Theoretical Modeling

The observed structure of the present-day universe is highly non-linear on scales smaller than $\sim 8 \, h^{-1} \mathrm{Mpc}$. To model the growth of these structures from initial density perturbations, cosmologists rely on a combination of analytical frameworks and advanced numerical simulations. 

On the analytical side, the [[press-schechter-formalism|Press-Schechter formalism]] and its extensions provide a foundational method for calculating the mass function, merger rates, and clustering properties of dark matter halos [Borgani et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008LNP...740..287B/abstract). Because of its mathematically non-rigorous nature, however, the formalism's predictions must be calibrated against numerical techniques. In the era of precision cosmology, analytical methods have been significantly upgraded. For example, the [[effective-field-theory-of-large-scale-structure|Effective Field Theory of Large Scale Structure]] allows for the rigorous calculation of the one-loop [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] in redshift space. When applied to modern survey data like BOSS DR12, this framework can constrain cosmological parameters to high precision, measuring the [[non-relativistic-matter-density-parameter|Non-relativistic matter density parameter]] $\Omega_m$ to $5.7\%$ and the Hubble parameter $H_0$ to $2.2\%$ without relying on small-scale empirical calibrations [Colas et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...06..001C/abstract). Similarly, applying frameworks like CLASS-PT alongside theoretical error approaches to BOSS data allows for precision cosmological constraints without traditional small-scale cuts [Chudaykin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220406591C/abstract). When modeling light-cone observables such as intrinsic galaxy alignments, the Zel'dovich approximation is frequently utilized to calculate unequal time correlators accurately [Chisari et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvD.100b3543C/abstract).

To accurately track the non-linear evolution of the density field, high-resolution dark matter-only N-body simulations are indispensable. Extensive suites comprising thousands of N-body realizations are used to map the non-linear clustering of dark matter over vast spatial dynamic ranges. For instance, analyses of $\sim 7000$ N-body simulations have demonstrated that the dark matter [[probability-density-function-cosmology|Probability density function (cosmology)]] follows a predictable power-law decline of $P \propto \rho^{-2}$ and can be modeled with few-percent errors across 12 orders of magnitude [Klypin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.4588K/abstract). The statistical description of clustering within these simulations has also been enriched by spatial statistics tools, such as scaling laws and lacunarity measures, which provide deeper insights into clustering [Martinez et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002SPIE.4847...86M/abstract). These simulations form the structural backbone needed to understand the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]]. Furthermore, the cosmological genus statistic has proven useful for distinguishing non-standard models, such as quintessence, from $\Lambda$CDM based on their unique large-scale structure topological properties [Watts et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468...59W/abstract).

While N-body simulations govern the dark matter skeleton, translating these structures to the observable universe requires complex hydrodynamical simulations that incorporate baryonic physics [Vogelsberger et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatRP...2...42V/abstract). The mapping between observable light and total mass is highly sensitive to subgrid processes such as gas cooling, star formation, and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback. To manage the resulting complex data structures, tools like the SOAP package have been developed; SOAP provides a modern, parallel Python environment designed for the high-resolution post-processing of hydrodynamic simulations to compute consistent properties for both halos and galaxies [McGibbon et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722669M/abstract). Projects like the BAHAMAS (BAryons and HAloes of MAssive Systems) suite explicitly calibrate stellar and AGN feedback efficiencies to reproduce the observed present-day galaxy stellar mass function and hot gas fractions in clusters [McCarthy et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2936M/abstract), [Caldwell et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.4117C/abstract). Furthermore, utilizing the Sherwood simulation suite, researchers have demonstrated that excess Lyman-$\alpha$ absorption around star-forming galaxies and quasi-stellar objects (QSOs) serves as a key observable signature of these feedback mechanisms [Meiksin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.1893M/abstract). Accurately modeling these baryonic effects is critical: strong feedback can suppress the matter power spectrum by $12-33\%$ at small scales ($k \sim 7 \, h \, \mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$), an effect which has been shown to partially alleviate the [[s8-tension|S8 tension]] between weak lensing surveys and Planck cosmic microwave background data [Schneider et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3802S/abstract).

Finally, the sheer volume and complexity of modern large-scale survey data have driven the integration of artificial intelligence and machine learning into theoretical pipelines. Neural networks have successfully been deployed to correct for redshift interloper contamination in the power spectrum to better than $1\%$ accuracy [Cagliari et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250406919C/abstract). Similarly, hybrid physical and deep learning hierarchical Bayesian models are now utilized to overcome shear estimation biases induced by complex galaxy morphologies, ensuring that measurements of the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] remain robust against theoretical systematic errors [Remy et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221016243R/abstract).

## Current Tensions and Paradigm Challenges

Despite the remarkable success of the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] in describing the universe's geometry and expansion history [Hamilton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract), precision observations have revealed several statistical anomalies that challenge the standard paradigm.

The most prominent of these is the Hubble tension, a statistically significant discrepancy between local distance-ladder measurements of the present-day [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]], $H_0$, and the values inferred from the early universe via the cosmic microwave background (CMB) [Palle et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210608136P/abstract). This discrepancy has motivated rigorous Cosmological principle tests, as variations in cosmic dipoles or line-of-sight structures could imply that the universe deviates from the foundational assumption of large-scale homogeneity and isotropy [Kumar Aluri et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40i4001K/abstract). While some alternative theories invoke inhomogeneous spacetime geometries to explain cosmic acceleration via non-linear backreaction, the backreaction density parameter is tightly constrained by Planck and large-scale structure data, with bounds such as $\Omega_A \leq 0$ at the 68% confidence level [Wijenayake et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..94h3501W/abstract).

Concurrently, the [[s8-tension|S8 tension]] highlights a discrepancy in the growth rate of cosmic structure. The amplitude of late-time matter fluctuations is frequently quantified by related parameters, such as $\Sigma_8$, parameterized as $\Sigma_8 = \sigma_8 \left( \frac{\Omega_m}{0.3} \right)^{0.58}$. Values derived from local weak lensing surveys and galaxy cluster abundances are systematically lower than the values extrapolated from Planck CMB data [Schneider et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3802S/abstract). The literature remains conflicted on whether this offset points to beyond-standard physics—such as interacting dark energy [Marcondes et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161001272M/abstract). Attempts to resolve these tensions using simple self-interacting neutrino models, which delay free-streaming, have been shown to actually exacerbate the challenges in reconciling CMB and LSS data, particularly regarding the [[s8-tension|S8 tension]]. For instance, incorporating aggressive baryonic feedback models into weak-lensing analysis pipelines has been shown to reduce the significance of the [[s8-tension|S8 tension]] from $3.8\sigma$ to $2.9\sigma$; subsequently combining this data with independent X-ray and kinematic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (kSZ) observations further reduces the tension to $2.6\sigma$ [Schneider et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3802S/abstract).

Challenges also persist in the regime of [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]]. Standard [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] predictions often struggle to match the observed internal dynamics and density profiles of dwarf galaxies [de Martino et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Univ....6..107D/abstract). Furthermore, it has been argued that the observed spatial arrangement of galaxies on scales from 100 kpc to 1 Gpc exhibits significantly more structure and regularity than is predicted by, and is thus fundamentally inconsistent with, standard dark-matter-based cosmological models [Kroupa et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161003854K/abstract). Notably, the discovery of ultra-diffuse galaxies that apparently lack dark matter entirely challenges the expected properties of standard dark matter halos in $N$-body simulations; assuming a distance of 20 Mpc, the combined existence of NGC 1052-DF2 and NGC 1052-DF4 sits in $\ge 5.8\sigma$ tension with the $\Lambda$CDM model [Haslbauer et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.2634H/abstract). 

Furthermore, early structure formation rates are being challenged by recent high-redshift observations. The James Webb Space Telescope has identified populations of massive galaxies ($>10^{10} M_\odot$) at redshifts $z > 7\text{--}10$ that appear to have assembled too rapidly for standard hierarchical clustering timelines [Coley et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231214738C/abstract). Similarly, an over-abundance of massive galaxy clusters at $z > 2$ has been observed, conflicting with $\Lambda$CDM simulation probabilities by a factor of several [Kang et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JKAS...48...21K/abstract). These compounding tensions leave it currently ambiguous whether modern cosmology requires an overhaul of its foundational gravitational theories, a modified dark sector, or merely a refined understanding of complex baryonic physics.

## Alternative Cosmological Models and Extended Physics

While the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] serves as the remarkably successful standard paradigm of modern cosmology, persistent theoretical challenges—such as the unknown fundamental nature of the dark sector and the cosmological constant problem—alongside recent observational tensions in the [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] ($H_0$) and structure growth ($S_8$) have motivated the development of [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]] [Hamilton et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract), [Lombriser et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvD..83f3519L/abstract). These frameworks attempt to explain the observed cosmic acceleration and mass discrepancies by either modifying general relativity or extending the physics of the dark sector.

**Modified Gravity and Dynamics**
A prominent class of alternatives seeks to replace cold dark matter with modifications to gravitational dynamics. Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) postulates that Newtonian gravity breaks down at a characteristic acceleration scale $a_0 \approx c H_0 \approx c^2(\Lambda/3)^{1/2}$ [Kroupa et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012IJMPD..2130003K/abstract). This characteristic acceleration scale in galaxies ($\sim 10^{-10} \text{ m/s}^2$) has been posited as a fundamental manifestation of the dark sector rather than a mere empirical parameter [Walker et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ConPh..55..198W/abstract). While highly predictive for galaxy rotation curves, standard MOND is non-relativistic and traditionally struggles to explain cosmology and structure formation [McGaugh et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015CaJPh..93..250M/abstract). To address this, relativistic generalizations such as Tensor-Vector-Scalar (TeVeS) gravity have been developed to confront large-scale data and gravitational lensing [Bekenstein et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011RSPTA.369.5003B/abstract). 

Similarly, Modified Gravity (MOG or Scalar-Tensor-Vector Gravity) extends general relativity by introducing three extra degrees of freedom: a scalar field $G$ that enhances the Newtonian gravitational constant, a massive spin-1 vector field $\phi_\mu$, and an effective mass $\mu$ [Moffat et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020EPJC...80..906M/abstract). MOG models have successfully fitted the cosmic microwave background (CMB) acoustic power spectrum, the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]], and galaxy cluster dynamics without invoking a dominant non-baryonic dark matter component [Moffat et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0710.0364M/abstract), [Moffat et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020EPJC...80..906M/abstract). Other macroscopic modifications, such as $f(R)$ gravity, remain active areas of research for explaining anomalous galactic dynamics [de Martino et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Univ....6..107D/abstract). Furthermore, measuring the decay rate of the cosmological gravitational potential provides a robust test of general relativity that is largely immune to many astrophysical uncertainties [Zhao et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251104279Z/abstract). Alternatively, mathematical reformulations of cosmology in Minkowski space can offer a reinterpretation of the cosmological constant problem and suggest new geometric origins for dark matter [Lombriser et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40o5005L/abstract). Even more radical departures exist in the form of tired-light and curvature cosmology, which are non-standard static universe frameworks that attempt to fit redshift data via energy loss rather than cosmic expansion [Crawford et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011JCos...13.3875C/abstract). Additionally, geometric alternatives such as the hyperconical model propose that the universe expands linearly; this formulation aims to resolve active mass problems while still remaining statistically compatible with current standard cosmological tests [Monjo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967...66M/abstract). To systematically evaluate these competing theories, a 'parametrized post-Friedmannian' approach has been introduced, allowing researchers to measure [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] and perturbation growth without assuming the validity of Einstein's field equations [Tegmark et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhRvD..66j3507T/abstract).

**Extended Dark Energy and Dark Sector Interactions**
Within models that retain dark matter, the cosmological constant $\Lambda$ is frequently replaced by dynamical [[dark-energy-models|Dark energy models]]. [[quintessence-models|Quintessence models]] and time-varying vacuum formulations, $\Lambda(t)$, attempt to resolve the coincidence problem of why dark matter and dark energy densities are comparable today. Such models alter the background [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] and can predict stronger small-scale dynamics, causing massive structures like galaxy clusters to form earlier than in standard cosmology [Basilakos et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.395.2347B/abstract). 

Additionally, interacting dark energy models propose a direct phenomenological coupling between the dark matter and dark energy fluids. These interactions modify the evolution of density perturbations, which can be constrained using redshift-space distortions and the [[growth-index|Growth index]] $\gamma$, parametrized via the linear growth rate $f \approx \Omega_{\mathrm{DM}}^\gamma$ [Marcondes et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161001272M/abstract). Furthermore, clustered unified dark sector models, such as the Chaplygin gas, parameterize the entire dark sector via a single clustering fraction $f$, offering another pathway to explain structure formation [Hashim et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250201751H/abstract). Alternative frameworks also reconsider the timing of accelerated expansion; for instance, conformal cosmology is an alternative model that predicts acceleration occurring at redshifts $z > 1$, contrasting with the late-time onset of standard dark energy [Mannheim et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AIPC..672...47M/abstract).

**High-Energy and Quantum Gravity Frameworks**
Other approaches derive departures from $\Lambda$CDM from high-energy physics or quantum gravity. Brane cosmology posits that cosmic acceleration arises from gravitational leakage into extra spatial dimensions; however, combined analyses of CMB and distance measurements currently tend to favor a flat $\Lambda$CDM universe over these specific braneworld scenarios [Alcaniz et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005PhRvD..71h3513A/abstract), [Pires et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvD..73l3530P/abstract). 

Asymptotically Safe cosmology offers an alternative mechanism where accelerated expansion is driven by infrared quantum gravity modifications at galactic and cluster scales. This framework operates without fine-tuning or extra energy scales, yielding statistical fits to supernovae and baryon acoustic oscillation data that are competitive with the standard model [Anagnostopoulos et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...02..053A/abstract). Finally, Einstein-Cartan cosmology introduces spacetime torsion generated by the angular momentum (spin) of matter, such as Majorana neutrinos. This torsion contributes a negative effective density that partially cancels the mass density, potentially resolving discrepancies in the large-scale CMB temperature power spectrum and explaining observed bulk flows in galaxy clusters [Palle et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014JETP..118..587P/abstract), [Palle et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210608136P/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Frontiers

The next decade of cosmological research will rigorously test the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] against unprecedented observational data. Future large-scale structure surveys will refine constraints on fundamental [[cosmological-parameters-and-frameworks|Cosmological Parameters and Frameworks]], aiming to resolve persistent statistical discrepancies such as the $H_0$ and $S_8$ tensions. By utilizing advanced wide-field spectroscopic surveys, cosmologists will probe the nature of the dark sector and the validity of the [[friedmann-equations|Friedmann equations]] at varying cosmic epochs.

A major frontier is the observation of the earliest stages of [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]]. Recent observations from JWST have identified a population of massive galaxies with stellar masses $>10^{10} M_\odot$ at redshifts $z > 7 - 10$, corresponding to less than $700$ Myr after the [[big-bang|Big Bang]] [Coley et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231214738C/abstract). The existence of such massive, early structures, along with an observed over-abundance of massive clusters at $z > 2$ [Kang et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JKAS...48...21K/abstract), challenges the assembly timescales predicted by standard simulations. The literature is currently conflicting on whether these high-redshift anomalies require modifications to our underlying cosmological framework, or if they can be reconciled through complex, revised baryonic feedback and star-formation efficiencies within the standard model.

To further illuminate the cosmic "dark ages", [[21-cm-cosmology|21-cm Cosmology]] will serve as a critical probe of the neutral intergalactic medium prior to and during the era of first galaxy formation [Furlanetto et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S..83F/abstract). Facilities like the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) will not only map the spatial distribution of neutral hydrogen but may also enable "real-time cosmology." By measuring the frequency drift of the 21-cm signal over a baseline of roughly a decade, it is theoretically possible to detect the real-time expansion rate of the universe, with an expected frequency shift of $\sim 0.1$ Hz over a 12-year observational baseline [Kloeckner et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015aska.confE..27K/abstract).

Upcoming wide-field spectroscopic and imaging surveys will significantly expand the available [[parameter-space|Parameter space]] for testing [[dark-energy-models|Dark energy models]]. These surveys will measure the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] and the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] to unprecedented precision. Such precision will allow for strict constraints on the dark energy equation of state and its sound speed, with limits potentially reaching $c_s \leq 0.01$ for clustering cold dark energy models [Sapone et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvD..82j3535S/abstract). Furthermore, combining data from the cosmic microwave background with next-generation galaxy surveys is projected to constrain the sum of neutrino masses to $\sim 14$ meV and reconstruct the primordial power spectrum at subpercent levels . To maximize this potential, $N$-body simulation forecasts for future spectroscopic galaxy surveys at $2 \le z \le 3$ demonstrate that high-redshift observations will be crucial for tightly constraining spatial curvature and the Hubble constant [Pu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241002524P/abstract).

Because these massive datasets are highly sensitive to systematic errors, the next frontier also relies heavily on advanced statistical and machine learning methods. A major wave of 21st-century statistical and computational challenges in astrophysics—including heteroscedastic error treatment and the transition to likelihood-free inference methods—must be overcome to analyze these enormous galaxy samples properly [Feigelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AnRSA...8..493F/abstract). To process vast imaging datasets, generative AI methods are being developed to automatically simplify galaxy images into skeletonized forms, dramatically streamlining the analysis [Teja Erukude et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250711692T/abstract). For example, neural networks have been demonstrated to correct for interloper contamination in the power spectrum monopole to better than $1\%$ accuracy [Cagliari et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250406919C/abstract). Concurrently, novel frameworks aim to unify cluster lensing and galaxy clustering analyses directly through the galaxy-halo connection, further minimizing systematic errors [Cao et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260116353C/abstract). These hybrid physical and AI-driven methodologies will ensure that the [[cosmological-parameters-and-frameworks|Cosmological Parameters and Frameworks]] derived from future data remain robust against instrumental and astrophysical biases, paving the way for definitive tests of alternative gravity and non-standard dark sector physics.
