## Article

**Axion stars** are theoretical, macroscopic [[compact-dark-matter-objects|Compact dark matter objects]] composed of ultra-light [[axions-and-axion-like-particles|Axions and Axion-Like Particles]] (ALPs) that have collapsed into gravitationally bound solitons. Following the initial formulation of the "invisible" axion as a cold relic in the early 1980s [Sikivie et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982PhRvL..48.1156S/abstract), [Sikivie et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983PhRvL..51.1415S/abstract), the concept evolved significantly with the introduction of "[[bose-einstein-condensate-dark-matter|Wave Dark Matter]]" or "Fuzzy Dark Matter" models. When the axion mass is extremely small (typically $m \sim 10^{-22}\text{ eV}$), the particle's de Broglie wavelength stretches across astrophysical kiloparsec scales [Marsh et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhR...643....1M/abstract). In this regime, the dark matter ceases to behave as a collection of discrete point particles and instead acts as a coherent classical wave. The resulting quantum pressure from wave interference counteracts gravitational collapse, allowing the scalar field to condense into a stable Bose-Einstein condensate dark matter core at the center of a galactic halo. This macroscopic quantum behavior elegantly resolves several small-scale structural anomalies associated with standard Cold Dark Matter (CDM), such as the cusp-core problem, by preventing the formation of singular density cusps [Hui et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ARA&A..59..247H/abstract).

The structural properties and cosmological evolution of axion stars are fundamentally dictated by the intrinsic self-interactions of the axion field. Unlike standard non-interacting models, the inclusion of an attractive or repulsive self-coupling modifies the unique mass-radius relationship of the star, strictly limiting its maximum stable mass before it becomes vulnerable to decay via number-changing interactions. Cosmologically, these condensates can nucleate early in the universe within dark minihalos, potentially yielding a vast local population of [[qcd-axion-stars|QCD axion stars]] or string axion equivalents. However, high-resolution dynamical modeling indicates that these pristine substructures rarely remain isolated. Encounters with ordinary stars subject axion miniclusters to severe tidal stripping, drawing them out into parsec-scale streams that may comprise the vast majority of the local dark matter distribution. 

While axion stars lack conventional thermal emission, they are predicted to produce distinct gravitational and electromagnetic signatures. Gravitationally, populations of dense axion minihalos can be probed through the dynamical disruption of wide binary star systems or via the high-frequency microlensing of caustic-transiting background stars. Electromagnetically, interactions between the axion condensate and strong external magnetic fields can catalyze resonant axion-to-photon conversion. Accreting axion stars that reach a critical "decay mass" undergo parametric resonance, yielding highly enhanced, monochromatic radio-line signals. Furthermore, collisions between axion stars and the magnetized atmospheres of neutron stars have been proposed as a potential progenitor for Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs), while higher-energy phenomena, such as the debated 3.5 keV X-ray line, remain investigated as potential markers of spontaneous axion decay in galaxy clusters.

Despite the elegance of [[quantum-dark-matter|Quantum dark matter]] models, the study of axion stars is characterized by significant theoretical tensions. A primary debate centers on whether these macroscopic condensates can constitute the entirety of the universe's dark matter budget. Recent analyses of galactic phase-space densities and the kinematic profiles of dwarf spheroidal galaxies suggest that ultra-light axion BECs may only account for a fractional component of the total dark matter, contradicting pure-BEC halo models. Resolving these open questions relies on next-generation observational facilities, such as the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) and the International Axion Observatory (IAXO), which will soon probe the precise parameter spaces where these elusive structures are theorized to exist.

## Theoretical Foundations and Mass-Radius Relations

Axion stars are gravitationally bound structures that arise naturally when [[axions-and-axion-like-particles|Axions and Axion-Like Particles]] (ALPs) are treated in the wave dark matter limit. For ultra-light axions with masses $m \sim 10^{-22}\text{ eV}$, the characteristic de Broglie wavelength vastly exceeds the average interparticle separation in galactic halos. The dynamic evolution of this field is governed by the Schrödinger-Poisson system. Within this framework, the gravitational attraction of the dark matter is balanced by the effective quantum pressure of the coherent wave, nucleating central, stable solitons known as axion stars [Marsh et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhR...643....1M/abstract), [Hui et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ARA&A..59..247H/abstract).

While many early analytical treatments assumed a non-interacting field due to the inherently weak coupling of axions, the inclusion of the axion field's intrinsic *self-coupling* ($\lambda$) fundamentally alters the *mass-radius relationship* and physical properties of the condensate [Eby et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JHEP...01..066E/abstract). Whether attractive or repulsive, self-interactions restrict the maximum stable mass of the axion star compared to free-field models. Quantitative constraints derived from the central mass of galaxies demonstrate that for an ultra-light scalar mass of $m \sim 10^{-22}\text{ eV}$, a self-coupling on the order of $\lambda \sim \mathcal{O}(10^{-96})$—equivalent to a scattering length $a_s \sim 10^{-82}\text{ m}$—can be observationally probed using the dark matter limits within 10 parsecs of the M87 galactic core [Chakrabarti et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...09..074C/abstract).

Beyond simple gravitational collapse, stable configurations must survive over cosmological timescales against quantum mechanical decay. Condensates are generally unstable to decay via number-changing interactions intrinsic to the axion potential, imposing a stringent lower bound on the physical size and binding energy of dynamically viable axion stars [Eby et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JHEP...01..066E/abstract). An additional, violent instability mechanism arises from the coupling between axions and photons ($g_{a\gamma\gamma}$). As an axion star accretes ambient dark matter, its mass gradually increases. If it reaches a critical threshold known as the *decay mass*, the dense axion configuration triggers parametric resonance, rapidly converting the macroscopic condensate into a burst of nearly monochromatic radio photons with energies centered at half the axion mass ($E \approx m_a/2$) [Maseizik et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240913121M/abstract), [Maseizik et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...05..033M/abstract). 

## Formation Mechanisms and Cosmological Evolution

The historical modeling of [[axions-and-axion-like-particles|axion]] dark matter depends heavily on the timing of Peccei-Quinn symmetry breaking. In models where the symmetry breaks before or during inflation, axion star formation can be triggered by gravitational Bose-Einstein condensation in virialized dark minihalos around primordial black holes well before the epoch of standard galaxy formation [Hertzberg et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.102b3013H/abstract). The resulting local abundance is highly model-dependent: theoretical predictions suggest up to $\sim 10^{17}$ [[qcd-axion-stars|QCD axion stars]] could exist within a 100-parsec radius of the Sun, whereas models involving string axions predict roughly $\sim 10^9$ such objects in the same volume [Hertzberg et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.102b3013H/abstract). 

Conversely, if the symmetry breaks after cosmic inflation, the axion field develops white-noise isocurvature fluctuations on small scales. As the universe expands, these density perturbations undergo gravitational collapse to form highly granular, sub-planetary mass halos known as miniclusters [Dai et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....159...49D/abstract). High-resolution dynamical modeling has updated the classical picture of isolated axion substructures. It is now understood that miniclusters and axion stars rarely exist purely in isolation. Encounters with ordinary stars in the galactic disk tidally strip these miniclusters, drawing their mass out into parsec-long tidal streams. This continuous disruption acts to refill the smooth dark matter distribution, and these overlapping streams are now estimated to comprise $70\%$ to $90\%$ of the local dark matter density, creating an intrinsically spiky phase-space distribution distinct from standard CDM [O'Hare et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvL.133h1001O/abstract). Furthermore, the late infall of axions is predicted to produce distinctive phase-space structures in galactic halos, such as discrete streams and caustic rings [Sikivie et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003idm..conf..216S/abstract).

On larger cosmological scales, the wave-like properties of ultra-light axions fundamentally alter structure formation. While standard wave dark matter typically suppresses small-scale structure, the kinetic misalignment mechanism can actively enhance early galaxy formation. For axion masses in the range $10^{-22} \text{ eV} < m_a < 10^{-19} \text{ eV}$, this mechanism produces density perturbations capable of explaining the unexpected excess of high-redshift, massive galaxies observed by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) [Bird et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230710302B/abstract). More broadly, within Beyond the Standard Model (BSM) cosmology, ALP physics is also proposed as a source for the stochastic gravitational wave background, complementing its role in this accelerated early galaxy formation [Khlopov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240104735K/abstract). Furthermore, related early-universe dark matter structures, such as individual high-redshift dark stars with masses below $1000 \, M_\odot$, may be detectable by JWST at $z=10$ through galaxy cluster lensing, while their supermassive variants could be visible even without magnification [Zackrisson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1101.2895Z/abstract).

## Substructure Evolution and the CDM Crisis

The standard Cold Dark Matter paradigm struggles with small-scale structural issues, notably the over-prediction of dark matter density in galactic centers (the "cusp-core" problem). Ultra-light [[bose-einstein-condensate-dark-matter|fuzzy dark matter]] provides a compelling resolution; quantum pressure prevents the formation of central density cusps, naturally producing the flattened solitonic cores observed in dark matter-dominated systems [Marsh et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhR...643....1M/abstract), [Hui et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ARA&A..59..247H/abstract).

Despite these successes, a significant tension exists in the literature regarding whether Bose-Einstein condensate dark matter can constitute the entirety of the dark matter budget. Cosmological analyses typically establish an absolute lower mass bound of $m_a \gtrsim 10^{-22}$ eV to avoid erasing observed small-scale structure [Marsh et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhR...643....1M/abstract). However, detailed Jeans modeling of dwarf spheroidal galaxies indicates a persistent tension between the boson mass required to reproduce internal stellar kinematics and the mass favored by broader cosmological data. Attempts to reconcile this discrepancy by introducing radially varying velocity anisotropies in dwarf spheroidal galaxies have proven insufficient [de Martino et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.108l3044D/abstract). 

Arguments based on observed galactic phase-space densities suggest pure-BEC dark matter halos cannot consistently reproduce observations, heavily restricting models where ultra-light scalars form 100% of the dark matter [de Vega et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Univ....8..419D/abstract), [Chakrabarti et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...09..074C/abstract). Recent cosmological inferences from the eRASS1 galaxy cluster number counts and CMB data strongly support a fractional paradigm, restricting the [[axions-and-axion-like-particles|axion]] relic density parameter ($\Omega_a$) to less than roughly $0.003$ to $0.008$, which corresponds to an axion dark matter fraction of roughly $1\%$ to $3\%$ for masses between $10^{-27}$ and $10^{-26}$ eV [Zelmer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250203353Z/abstract). Consequently, ultra-light axions may only represent a fractional component of a wider dark matter inventory.

Beyond cold substructures, ALPs have also been explored in other thermal contexts to address cosmological discrepancies. For instance, the EDGES 21cm absorption anomaly has been interpreted as a potential signal of axion-induced cooling of cosmic hydrogen, a scenario which favors an ALP mass in the $6\text{--}400 \text{ meV}$ range [Li et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021IJMPD..3050041L/abstract). Additionally, a framework for the indirect detection of axions as a hot dark matter relic has been established through their decay into photons in the eV mass range [Dror et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241214264D/abstract).

## Electromagnetic Signatures and Compact Object Interactions

Because the constituent [[axions-and-axion-like-particles|axions]] within axion stars are highly non-relativistic, the conversion of ALPs into photons—catalyzed by background magnetic fields—produces nearly monochromatic electromagnetic signatures. 

**Parametric Resonance and Photon-Axion Coupling Constraints**
When axion stars reach a critical "decay mass" determined by the axion-photon coupling constant $g_{a\gamma\gamma}$, they can efficiently convert their mass into radio photons. Axion stars that accrete dark matter from their surrounding host miniclusters can undergo parametric resonance, driving a rapid stimulated decay into a highly enhanced, narrow radio-line flux at an energy of half the axion mass ($E \simeq m_a/2$) [Maseizik et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240913121M/abstract), [Maseizik et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...05..033M/abstract). Estimates suggest that radio arrays like LOFAR, ALMA, and the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) can constrain the coupling down to $g_{a\gamma\gamma} \simeq 10^{-12} - 10^{-13} \, {\rm GeV}^{-1}$ over an axion mass range of $10^{-7}$ to $10^{-2} \, {\rm eV}$ [Maseizik et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240913121M/abstract). Broadband limits on the axion-photon coupling have also been derived by constraining the parametric growth of photon amplitudes in a background of ALP dark matter using radio to optical background data [Sigl et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190704849S/abstract). Moreover, radio data from the Bullet cluster places specific limits on the axion-photon coupling constant to $g_{a\gamma\gamma} \sim 10^{-12} - 10^{-11} \text{ GeV}^{-1}$ for masses near $20 \, \mu\text{eV}$ [Chan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatSR..1120087C/abstract). Additionally, resonant photon-to-ALP conversion in unresolved galaxy clusters could lead to a polarized spectral distortion in the CMB, detectable by future experiments like CMB-S4 [Mehta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...07..084M/abstract).

**Collisions with Stars and Compact Objects**
Radio transients can be generated when axion stars collide with the highly magnetized atmospheres of neutron stars. If the local plasma frequency matches the axion mass ($m_p \simeq m_a$), resonant axion-to-photon conversion is triggered. Theoretical models estimate that the collision of a $10^{-12} M_\odot$ axion star with a neutron star could yield a millisecond-duration burst releasing $\sim 10^{43} \, {\rm GeV}$ of radiation, making these events a potential origin for Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) [Iwazaki et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..91b3008I/abstract). However, archival searches of the Galactic Center by the Breakthrough Listen project found no evidence for resonant transitions, setting upper limits of $g_{a\gamma\gamma} \lesssim 10^{-11} \, {\rm GeV}^{-1}$ for axion masses between $15$ and $35 \, \mu{\rm eV}$ [Foster et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvL.129y1102F/abstract). Beyond neutron stars, theoretical predictions suggest that distinctive radiation bursts can also occur when axion stars collide with ordinary main-sequence (K and M type) stars in galaxies like Andromeda [Iwazaki et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhLB..82937089I/abstract).

**Black Hole Superradiance**
Axion stars and general BEC configurations are theorized to interact with black holes. While theoretical models show that the isotropic collapse of axion condensates can form supermassive black holes, realistic galactic angular momentum efficiently halts this direct collapse [Morikawa et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190302986M/abstract). Alternatively, ultra-light axions can form dense bound clouds around black holes via superradiance. In a uniform background magnetic field $B_0$, the decay rate of these superradiant clouds scales as $\Gamma \sim B_0^2 \, g_{a\gamma\gamma}^2 \, (GM)^7 \, m_a^6$. For the supermassive black hole at the Galactic Center, this photon conversion decay rate can rival the growth rate of the superradiant instability itself [Yoo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASJ...74...64Y/abstract).

**Stimulated Decay and the Axion Gegenschein**
Another unique radio signature is the axion gegenschein (counterimage). Intense radio emission from bright astrophysical sources can stimulate the two-photon decay of dark matter in the opposite direction on the sky. This produces a counterimage at a frequency corresponding to half the axion mass. A search for the axion gegenschein of the Vela Supernova Remnant using the FAST telescope constrained $g_{a\gamma\gamma} \lesssim 2 \times 10^{-10} \, {\rm GeV}^{-1}$ for masses near $10 \, \mu{\rm eV}$ [Yang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988..104Y/abstract).

**High-Energy and X-ray Emission**
Dense axion concentrations can also produce detectable high-energy signatures via the Primakoff effect in galaxy clusters. A debated signature is the anomalous 3.5 keV line observed in several galaxy clusters, which some theoretical frameworks attribute to the decay of string axions or axinos [Pei Liew et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014JCAP...05..044P/abstract), [Cicoli et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JHEP...10..192C/abstract). In related scenarios, the production of ALPs by primordial magnetic fields has been proposed as a viable mechanism for explaining both observed dark matter properties and the 3.5 keV line [Kamada et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PhRvD..96b3537K/abstract). Additionally, a cosmic axion background with $0.1\text{--}1 \text{ keV}$ energies could explain the observed soft X-ray excess in galaxy clusters through axion-to-photon conversion in cluster magnetic fields [Conlon et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PhRvL.111o1301C/abstract). At even higher energy bands, decaying ALPs in the eV mass range may produce distinct features in the extragalactic background light that are detectable in the gamma-ray spectra of distant blazars [Korochkin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...03..064K/abstract). 

In dense stellar populations, mono-energetic axions can be produced via nuclear de-excitations (e.g., the $^{57}\mathrm{Fe}$ $14.4 \, \mathrm{keV}$ transition). Searches using the NuSTAR observatory on the Virgo cluster's central galaxy M87 and M82 found no evidence of such signals, constraining the combined axion-nucleon and axion-photon coupling to $|g_{ann} \times g_{a\gamma\gamma}| \lesssim 1.1 \times 10^{-22} \, \mathrm{GeV}^{-1}$ for $m_a \lesssim 10^{-10} \, \mathrm{eV}$ [Ning et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250903569N/abstract). Furthermore, isolating pristine axion-photon signals is complicated by alternative theoretical structures like axion quark nuggets, whose signatures must be disentangled from the background thermal Bremsstrahlung and non-thermal cosmic ray synchrotron radiation emitted by the host galaxy cluster's intracluster medium [Sommer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A..38S/abstract).

## Gravitational Lensing and Dynamical Disruption

Because [[axions-and-axion-like-particles|axion]] minihalos typically have subplanetary masses ($10^{-15}$ to $10^{-8} \, h^{-1} M_\odot$), their surface densities are far below the threshold required to produce multiple images or generate high-magnification microlensing events in isolation. However, this limitation can be overcome when minihalos are superposed near the critical curves of foreground galaxy cluster lenses. As background stars cross microlensing caustics induced by intracluster stars, they experience extreme magnification factors of $\mu \gtrsim 10^3 - 10^4$. Under these magnifications, populations of axion minihalos produce surface density fluctuations with an amplitude of $\sim 10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}$ on projected length scales of $10$ to $10^4 \text{ au}$. These imprint detectable, high-frequency irregularities on the microlensing lightcurves of the caustic-transiting stars, offering a unique observational probe of wave dark matter substructures [Dai et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....159...49D/abstract).

Beyond lensing, the granularity of axion dark matter can be tested through dynamical disruption. Dense axion miniclusters and axion stars can act as gravitational scatterers, injecting kinetic energy into nearby halo-like wide binary star systems (physical separations $a \gtrsim \mathcal{O}(0.1) \text{ pc}$). As wide binaries undergo repeated encounters with massive axion minihalos, the accumulated tidal kicks accelerate the dynamical evaporation of the binary systems. Observations of the surviving wide binary population in the Milky Way by the Gaia mission place stringent upper limits on the fraction of the dark matter halo composed of dense miniclusters. These wide-binary disruption constraints firmly restrict models where the axion mass falls within $m_a \sim 10^{-15} - 10^{-12} \text{ eV}$ and $m_a \sim 10^{-19} - 10^{-16} \text{ eV}$, depending on whether the miniclusters are dense or isolated [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240902468W/abstract), [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.111d3042W/abstract).

Beyond local dynamical and lensing effects, axion-photon conversion in the intergalactic and intracluster medium can affect extragalactic observations. These conversion mechanisms can be rigorously constrained using cosmic distance measurements from Type Ia supernovae and large-scale galaxy clusters [Buen-Abad et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JHEP...02..103B/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite theoretical advances, major questions regarding [[axions-and-axion-like-particles|axion]] dark matter remain unresolved. The tension between boson masses favored by cosmological large-scale structure constraints and those required to explain galactic kinematics may point toward an "axiverse"—a framework containing multiple ALPs spanning a wide range of masses [de Martino et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.108l3044D/abstract). It also remains heavily debated whether interaction-driven observational signals—such as the origin of the $3.5 \text{ keV}$ X-ray line, the generation of Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) from axion-stellar collisions, or the JWST high-redshift galaxy excess—are definitively linked to axion dynamics or simply the result of standard astrophysical mechanisms.

The observational frontier for resolving these debates relies on next-generation facilities to probe the parameter space of [[compact-dark-matter-objects|Compact dark matter objects]] and [[qcd-axion-stars|QCD axion stars]]. In the radio band, the SKA will search for monochromatic radio-line signals emitted by surrounding axions accreting onto axion stars, expected to probe the axion-photon coupling down to $g_{a\gamma\gamma} \simeq 10^{-13} \text{ GeV}^{-1}$ [Maseizik et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240913121M/abstract). Concurrently, the International Axion Observatory (IAXO) will target the $1 \text{ meV}$ to $1 \text{ eV}$ mass range, achieving sensitivities of $g_{a\gamma} \sim 10^{-12} \text{ GeV}^{-1}$ [Armengaud et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...06..047A/abstract). For space-based gamma-ray searches, the proposed Very Large Area $\gamma$-ray Space Telescope (VLAST) aims to rigorously test the parameter space for axions below $5 \times 10^{-12} \text{ eV}$, potentially reaching peak sensitivities of $g_{a\gamma} \sim 7 \times 10^{-13} \text{ GeV}^{-1}$ at $m_a \sim 2 \times 10^{-10} \text{ eV}$ and offering a definitive test for axion-induced TeV transparency [Guo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250707786G/abstract).

On the experimental front, microwave cavity searches are actively hunting for axion dark matter. The advancement of haloscope sensitivity through the integration of SQUID microwave amplifiers has significantly reduced thermal noise backgrounds [Hotz et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PhDT.......157H/abstract). Building on this, the ADMX-HF project has served as a pathfinder for searching the $20\text{--}100 \, \mu\text{eV}$ mass range utilizing technological innovations like superconducting cavities [Shokair et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IJMPA..2943004S/abstract). Additionally, the ADMX "Sidecar" haloscope experiment utilized piezoelectrically tuned RF cavities to successfully set stringent limits on the axion-photon coupling in the $16\text{--}30 \, \mu\text{eV}$ mass range [Boutan et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PhDT........24B/abstract). Furthermore, experiments like ADMX have conducted targeted searches sensitive to nonvirialized "cold flow" axions, deliberately accounting for frequency shifts due to the detector's motion through the galactic halo [Bartram et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109h3014B/abstract).
