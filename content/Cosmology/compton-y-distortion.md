The **Compton $y$ distortion** is a characteristic spectral shift of cosmic microwave background (CMB) photons toward higher energies, generated via the inverse scattering of these photons by hot, free electrons. As the defining observational signature of the thermal [[sunyaev-zel-dovich-effect|Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect]] (tSZ), this distortion manifests as a decrease in CMB intensity at low frequencies (wavelengths $\lambda > 1.3$ mm) and a corresponding increment at higher frequencies [Zemcov et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518L..16Z/abstract). The magnitude of this [[comptonization|Comptonization]] process is quantified by the dimensionless Compton $y$ parameter, which represents the line-of-sight integral of the thermal electron pressure. Because it is directly sensitive to the product of electron density and temperature, the $y$ distortion provides a powerful, redshift-independent probe of the thermodynamic state of ionized gas in the universe, bridging the gap between primordial cosmology and late-time astrophysics.

Historically utilized for the detection and mass calibration of individual galaxy clusters, the study of Compton $y$ distortions has evolved into a premier tool for mapping large-scale structure and constraining cosmological parameters. Following the production of the first all-sky $y$-maps by the Planck satellite [Planck Collaboration et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...571A..21P/abstract), the statistical properties of the diffuse $y$-sky—originating from unresolved clusters, galaxy groups, and the warm-hot intergalactic medium (WHIM)—have been leveraged to measure the amplitude of matter density fluctuations ($\sigma_8$). Furthermore, in the hot intracluster medium of massive clusters, electrons can reach mildly relativistic velocities. These conditions introduce temperature-dependent relativistic corrections to the standard tSZ spectrum. Measuring these higher-order [[secondary-cmb-anisotropies|Secondary CMB anisotropies]] effectively breaks the traditional density-temperature degeneracy, allowing for the direct mapping of electron temperatures independent of X-ray observations.

Despite these advancements, the extraction and interpretation of the Compton $y$ signal face significant observational and theoretical challenges. Robustly isolating the $y$-distortion requires sophisticated component separation to remove severe contamination from Galactic dust, radio point sources, and the cosmic infrared background (CIB)—a dominant hurdle in the mitigation of [[foreground-secondary-anisotropies|Foreground secondary anisotropies]]. Concurrently, high-resolution measurements have revealed a striking discrepancy between the observed tSZ angular power spectrum at high multipoles ($\ell > 2000$) and the predictions of contemporary hydrodynamic simulations, raising critical questions about the modeling of energetic baryonic feedback. Looking forward, the field is poised to address fundamental physics through the pursuit of the global sky-averaged $y$-monopole—a guaranteed signature of the universe's total thermal energy budget [Hill et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvL.115z1301H/abstract)—and by exploring how exotic phenomena, such as massive neutrinos and axion-photon conversions, might uniquely imprint upon the $y$-sky.

## Physical Principles and the Comptonization Parameter

The physical basis of the Compton $y$ distortion is the inverse Compton scattering of cosmic microwave background (CMB) photons by a hot, free-electron plasma. In astrophysical contexts, this mechanism is most commonly associated with the intracluster medium (ICM) of galaxy clusters, generating the thermal [[sunyaev-zel-dovich-effect|Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect]] (tSZ) [Hill et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151006237H/abstract). During these interactions, the high-energy electrons transfer a net fraction of their thermal energy to the passing CMB radiation field. This process shifts the CMB photons to higher frequencies, creating a characteristic spectral distortion: the scattered radiation exhibits a transition from a decrement at lower frequencies ($\lambda > 1.3$ mm) to an increment at shorter wavelengths [Zemcov et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518L..16Z/abstract). Beyond cluster environments, inverse Compton scattering of photons—including the extragalactic background light (EBL)—off relativistic electrons in the lobes of radio galaxies generates a characteristic spectral imprint that can also be used to derive EBL intensities [Georganopoulos et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...686L...5G/abstract). Similarly, the Milky Way acts as a vast electron calorimeter, where the inverse Compton scattering of cosmic-ray electrons off the interstellar radiation field (ISRF) and the CMB accounts for a significant fraction of global cosmic-ray energy losses [Strong et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...722L..58S/abstract). At the highest energies, the fine structure of the cosmic-ray electron spectrum is strongly influenced by the transition to the Klein-Nishina regime during scattering events with the ultraviolet interstellar background [Evoli et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvL.125e1101E/abstract).

The amplitude of this spectral distortion is parameterized by the [[comptonization|Comptonization]] parameter, $y$. It is formally defined as the line-of-sight integral of the electron pressure, normalized by the electron rest mass energy:

$$ y = \int \frac{k_B T_e}{m_e c^2} n_e \sigma_T dl = \int \frac{P_e}{m_e c^2} \sigma_T dl $$

where $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant, $T_e$ is the electron temperature, $m_e c^2$ is the electron rest mass energy, $n_e$ is the electron number density, $\sigma_T$ is the Thomson cross-section, and $P_e = n_e k_B T_e$ is the thermal electron pressure [Pandey et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.101d3525P/abstract), [Roy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951...50R/abstract). 

Alternatively, the $y$ parameter can be expressed as the optical depth to Thomson scattering, $\tau = \int n_e \sigma_T dl$, weighted by the dimensionless electron temperature, yielding $y \approx \tau (k_B T_e / m_e c^2)$. Because $y$ scales with the product of density and temperature, standard tSZ measurements inherently suffer from a density-temperature degeneracy [Hill et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvL.115z1301H/abstract). 

While the classical non-relativistic treatment assumes the resulting spectral distortion is a universal function of frequency scaled only by $y$, electrons in massive clusters routinely reach relativistic velocities ($T_e \gtrsim 5-10$ keV). In this regime, the simple blackbody distortion framework requires relativistic corrections to the inverse Compton scattering cross-section. These relativistic corrections alter the spectral shape of the distortion in a strictly temperature-dependent manner, effectively breaking the degeneracy between optical depth and temperature [Hurier et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...596A..61H/abstract), [Butler et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932...55B/abstract). 

In addition to intensity shifts, Compton interactions also induce a $y$-type spectral distortion in the cosmic microwave background polarization. This polarized $y$-distortion is primarily dominated by scattering signals originating from the epoch of reionization [Renaux-Petel et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014JCAP...03..033R/abstract). Furthermore, numerical simulations have demonstrated that tSZ interactions in galaxy clusters also produce linear polarization signals that carry information about the local CMB quadrupole at the cluster's location [Amblard et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005NewA...10..417A/abstract). Beyond localized signals from individual halos, the aggregate of all structure formation and reionization events generates a global, diffuse $y$-sky [Pitrou et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010JCAP...07..019P/abstract). The sky-averaged $y$ monopole directly measures the total thermal energy of ionized electrons in the observable universe, placing a strict integral bound on baryonic feedback and cosmic heating [Hill et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvL.115z1301H/abstract).

## Historical Development and Observational Milestones

The historical trajectory of the Compton $y$ distortion traces a path from the foundational theoretical formulation of the [[sunyaev-zel-dovich-effect|Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect]] to the modern era of precision all-sky mapping. Originally proposed as a spectral distortion arising from the [[comptonization|Comptonization]] of cosmic microwave background (CMB) photons by hot intracluster gas, the effect is defined by its characteristic temperature decrement at long wavelengths and spectral increment at shorter wavelengths. Early theoretical work utilized the Press-Schechter formalism to predict how the mass evolution of galaxy clusters over cosmic time would impact the expected distribution of Compton $y$-values and generate observable CMB fluctuations [Bartlett et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...423...12B/abstract).

Early observational efforts were largely restricted to establishing upper limits on the global, sky-averaged $y$-distortion. The COBE-FIRAS instrument provided the first stringent bounds on this monopole signal, demonstrating that the CMB is a near-perfect blackbody and placing early limits on the $y$ parameter [Mather et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991AdSpR..11b.181M/abstract). While the low-frequency decrement was routinely observed by ground-based telescopes for decades, detecting the high-frequency spectral increment proved technically challenging due to atmospheric opacity and severe contamination from dusty star-forming galaxies. The first definitive detection of the spectral increment was achieved using the Herschel-SPIRE instrument. Observations mapping the massive Bullet Cluster (1E0657-56) successfully measured central intensity increments that perfectly matched the theoretically predicted spectral shape [Zemcov et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518L..16Z/abstract).

The field underwent a major paradigm shift with the launch of the Planck satellite. Prior to its launch, conservative early estimates suggested that the Planck HFI experiment would measure the Compton $y$ parameter to a few percent precision for individual clusters [Hansen et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005bmri.conf..199H/abstract). However, the mission exceeded these expectations, facilitating the transition from targeted cluster observations to full-sky statistical analyses of [[secondary-cmb-anisotropies|Secondary CMB anisotropies]]. By applying specialized component-separation algorithms across its multi-frequency channels, the Planck collaboration successfully produced the first all-sky Compton $y$ maps [Planck Collaboration et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...571A..21P/abstract). This milestone allowed for the robust measurement of the thermal SZ angular power spectrum over previously unexplored angular scales, transforming the $y$-parameter into a powerful cosmological probe.

The observational frontier has since expanded to extract higher-order relativistic corrections from the Compton $y$ signal. Once entirely theoretical, these temperature-dependent distortions have now been detected at high significance using statistical stacking of X-ray selected cluster samples [Hurier et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...596A..61H/abstract). Furthermore, forward-modeling of high-resolution submillimeter data for individual massive clusters has successfully isolated these relativistic signatures, providing direct electron temperature measurements in strong agreement with independent X-ray observations [Butler et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932...55B/abstract). Concurrently, recent reanalyses of archival FIRAS data utilizing improved astrophysical foreground cleaning techniques have placed even tighter constraints on the global mean $y$-distortion, directly ruling out several extreme baryonic feedback models [Fabbian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251203038F/abstract). Furthermore, the rapidly expanding catalog of SZ-detected clusters has revitalized historic cosmological measurements; recent studies have successfully updated the measurement of the CMB temperature redshift evolution, $T_{CMB}(z)$, utilizing a large sample of galaxy clusters extracted from the Planck Second Catalog (PSZ2) and the CHEX-MATE project [Luzzi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022EPJWC.25700028L/abstract).

## The Thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (tSZ) Effect in Galaxy Clusters

The thermal [[sunyaev-zel-dovich-effect|Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect]] (tSZ) is a premier tool for characterizing the intracluster medium (ICM) of galaxy clusters. Arising from the inverse [[comptonization|Comptonization]] of cosmic microwave background (CMB) photons by hot, ionized electrons ($T_e \sim 10^7 - 10^8$ K), this interaction produces a distinct spectral signature whose magnitude is parameterized by the Compton $y$ parameter. Because $y$ is directly proportional to the line-of-sight integral of the thermal electron pressure, maps of the Compton $y$ distortion provide a robust measurement of ICM pressure profiles. Cross-correlations between $y$-maps and galaxy or weak lensing catalogs allow researchers to constrain halo pressure profiles to precisions of $\sim 13\%$, serving as a powerful probe of baryonic feedback mechanisms and the thermodynamics of the circumgalactic medium [Pandey et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.101d3525P/abstract), [Roy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951...50R/abstract). Beyond thermal gas physics, interactions involving relativistic electron populations—such as those responsible for the non-thermal X-ray emission observed in the radio lobes of Pictor A—also arise from the [[non-thermal-sunyaev-zel-dovich-effect|Non-thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect]] via inverse Compton scattering of CMB photons [Migliori et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ESASP.604..645M/abstract).

For massive clusters, the standard non-relativistic approximation of the tSZ effect is insufficient because electrons in the ICM reach mildly relativistic velocities. Relativistic corrections to the tSZ spectrum depend explicitly on $T_e$, thereby breaking the density-temperature degeneracy inherent in the standard Compton $y$ parameter. Observations of individual massive clusters via spatially resolved multi-band modeling have successfully isolated this relativistic SZ signal, deriving pressure-weighted electron temperatures that match independent X-ray measurements [Butler et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932...55B/abstract).

The integrated Compton $y$ distortion of a cluster is also tightly correlated with its total mass and X-ray luminosity, establishing scaling relations that are vital for using clusters as cosmological probes [Cooray et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.307..841C/abstract). However, precision cosmology using the tSZ effect face active challenges. Observational constraints on $y$-distortions are challenged by severe foreground contamination. The extraction of the $y$ signal requires separating it from Galactic dust, radio point sources, and the cosmic infrared background (CIB). For cross-correlation studies, the intrinsic correlation between the CIB and CMB lensing potentials introduces a significant systematic bias into the measured tSZ-lensing power spectrum, complicating the robust interpretation of scaling relations for low-mass or high-redshift clusters [Hurier et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...575L..11H/abstract).

## Relativistic Corrections and Temperature Mapping

The standard thermal [[sunyaev-zel-dovich-effect|Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect]] generates a spectral distortion of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) that is typically modeled as a universal function of frequency, scaled only by the [[comptonization|Comptonization]] parameter $y$. However, in the hot intracluster medium of massive galaxy clusters, electron temperatures reach a few keV or higher. At these temperatures, the thermal velocities of the scattering electrons become mildly relativistic. These high velocities introduce temperature-dependent, frequency-dependent deviations from the non-relativistic tSZ spectrum, a phenomenon known as the relativistic SZ (rSZ) effect.

The rSZ effect modifies both the amplitude and the shape of the tSZ spectral distortion. Most notably, it alters the intensity of the high-frequency tSZ increment ($\lambda < 650\ \mu\text{m}$) and shifts the crossover frequency (the "null point") where the tSZ signal transitions from a decrement to an increment. The unique spectral signature of the rSZ effect effectively breaks the density-temperature degeneracy, allowing for the independent determination of the electron optical depth $\tau$ and the electron temperature $T_e$ directly from microwave and submillimeter observations [Hill et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvL.115z1301H/abstract), [Titarchuk et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190607060T/abstract).

Detecting the rSZ correction requires precise, multi-frequency measurements that span the tSZ decrement, null, and increment regimes. Early evidence of the finite-temperature correction was observed in the Bullet Cluster (1E0657-56) using the Herschel-SPIRE instrument, which found a $2.1\sigma$ preference for relativistic corrections [Zemcov et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518L..16Z/abstract). Advancements in multi-band observations have since enabled direct rSZ temperature mapping in individual massive clusters. For example, combined Herschel and Bolocam observations of RX J1347.5-1145 yielded a maximum a posteriori ICM electron temperature of $\langle T_{sz} \rangle_{2500} = 22.4\text{ keV}$. This independent SZ-derived temperature is highly consistent with the pressure-weighted X-ray temperature of $17.4 \pm 2.3\text{ keV}$ for the same system [Butler et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932...55B/abstract).

Beyond targeted cluster studies, the rSZ effect has also been detected statistically. A stacking analysis of Planck satellite data across a large sample of clusters yielded a $5.3\sigma$ detection of tSZ relativistic corrections, confirming on a population level that the rSZ spectral distortions are consistent with X-ray-derived ICM temperatures [Hurier et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...596A..61H/abstract). Properly accounting for the rSZ effect is now recognized as essential for unbiased cluster parameter estimation; if relativistic corrections are neglected in high-sensitivity CMB data, the derived Compton $y$-parameter and the inferred slope of tSZ scaling relations suffer from significant systematic biases [Hurier et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...604A..94H/abstract). 

## The Diffuse y-Sky and Large-Scale Structure

While the thermal [[sunyaev-zel-dovich-effect|Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect]] is most prominently observed as a discrete signal toward massive, virialized galaxy clusters, the collective emission from all ionized gas in the universe generates a diffuse $y$-sky. This background signal emerges from the non-linear evolution of cosmological perturbations and traces the thermodynamics of the warm-hot intergalactic medium (WHIM), unresolved low-mass galaxy groups, and the circumgalactic medium (CGM) [Pitrou et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010JCAP...07..019P/abstract). At extremely high redshifts ($z \sim 17$), the energetic output from the first stars and their subsequent supernova remnants could also generate significant small-scale SZ fluctuations, embedding an early-universe signature within the diffuse $y$-sky [Oh et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.342L..20O/abstract). Furthermore, Compton scattering from hot gas in early-type galaxies also contributes to the overarching $y$-distortion, though the central attenuation is generally predicted to be below $10^{-5}$ [Trester et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ApJ...347..605T/abstract). 

The angular power spectrum of the diffuse $y$-map is highly sensitive to the amplitude of matter fluctuations, parameterized by $\sigma_8$. All-sky maps from the Planck satellite successfully measured the tSZ power spectrum across intermediate angular scales, finding clear evidence of contributions from unresolved clusters and diffuse warm baryons [Planck Collaboration et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...571A..21P/abstract). However, interpreting this signal relies heavily on understanding complex astrophysical feedback mechanisms that inject energy into the intergalactic gas.

Cross-correlating the diffuse $y$-sky with external tracers of large-scale structure provides a powerful avenue for probing the intergalactic medium. However, rather than mapping the highly diffuse "missing baryons" hypothesized to reside in the vast WHIM, recent observational cross-correlations show that such measurements primarily trace the pressure of ionized gas within virialized groups and clusters over a wide range of halo masses and redshifts [Hill et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151006237H/abstract). Because the tSZ signal directly traces electron pressure rather than total gas mass, $y$-maps are often analyzed jointly with maps of the optical depth $\tau$ (extracted via the [[kinetic-sunyaev-zel-dovich-ksz-effect|Kinetic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (kSZ) Effect]]) to break the degeneracy between gas density and temperature. The cross-correlation between galaxy clustering, $y$, and $\tau$ enables independent constraints on the pressure and density profiles of halos, potentially constraining the halo gas mass fraction to $\sim 5.3\%$ precision at $z \sim 0$ [Roy et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951...50R/abstract). Furthermore, the spatial cross-correlation between the tSZ effect and the [[integrated-sachs-wolfe-effect|Integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect]] (ISW) provides a powerful method to extract the ISW signal directly from CMB data, serving as an independent cosmological probe for dark energy [Taburet et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.2207T/abstract).

Measuring these cross-correlations is fundamentally limited by astrophysical foregrounds. The cosmic infrared background (CIB), originating from dusty star-forming galaxies, spatially traces the same underlying dark matter scaffolding as the tSZ signal. Because the spatial distribution of the CIB varies across frequency bands, standard internal linear combination methods cannot perfectly null it. Residual CIB emission is a dominant systematic that heavily biases the cross-correlation between the $y$-parameter and the CMB lensing potential, requiring aggressive masking and sophisticated component separation to extract reliable cosmological constraints from the diffuse $y$-sky [Planck Collaboration et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...571A..21P/abstract), [Hurier et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...575L..11H/abstract).

## Cosmological Constraints from y-Distortion Statistics

The statistical properties of the Compton $y$-parameter map provide a sensitive probe of the late-time growth of structure and the thermodynamic history of intergalactic gas. Because the thermal [[sunyaev-zel-dovich-effect|Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect]] arises from the pressure of hot, ionized gas residing in dark matter halos, its spatial fluctuations are deeply tied to the underlying matter density field. 

**The Angular Power Spectrum and $\sigma_8$**
As one of the most prominent [[secondary-cmb-anisotropies|Secondary CMB anisotropies]], the tSZ angular power spectrum, $C_\ell^y$, is exceptionally sensitive to the amplitude of matter density fluctuations, parameterized by $\sigma_8$. Theoretical models and $N$-body simulations indicate that the amplitude of the tSZ power spectrum scales steeply with this parameter, roughly as $C_\ell^y \propto \sigma_8^{7.2-8.2}$ [Luchina et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250316355L/abstract). The Planck collaboration extracted the first all-sky $y$-map, measuring its angular power spectrum over a wide range of multipoles, but precise determination remains complicated by foregrounds like diffuse Galactic thermal dust and the clustered CIB [Planck Collaboration et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...571A..21P/abstract). Looking to the future, next-generation experiments will likely combine tSZ-selected cluster samples with velocity reconstructions to measure the cosmic growth rate to sub-1% precision, effectively breaking the degeneracy between cluster optical depth and velocity [Alonso et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..94d3522A/abstract).

**Non-Gaussianity and the Bispectrum**
Because the tSZ signal originates predominantly from rare, massive, gravitationally bound structures at low redshifts, the cosmic $y$-field is inherently highly non-Gaussian. Consequently, the power spectrum alone does not capture the full statistical information of the field. Higher-order statistics, such as the 1-point probability distribution function (PDF) and the bispectrum, must be utilized to characterize this non-Gaussianity. Analyses of the tSZ bispectrum from Planck and the Atacama Cosmology Telescope (ACT) provide strong constraints on $\sigma_8$ that are complementary to power spectrum measurements, helping break astrophysical degeneracies [Planck Collaboration et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...571A..21P/abstract), [Hill et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151006237H/abstract). Furthermore, cross-correlations between temperature and $y$-distortion anisotropies offer a unique path to measure primordial non-Gaussianity ($f_{\text{NL}}$) on scales inaccessible to standard CMB bispectra, though this signal is heavily contaminated by late-time ISW-SZ correlations [Ravenni et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JCAP...09..042R/abstract).

**CMB Temperature Evolution and CIPs**
Beyond standard structure mapping, the spectral nature of the tSZ effect allows researchers to measure the redshift evolution of the CMB temperature, $T_{CMB}(z)$. Observations of $y$-distortions in clusters at varying redshifts robustly confirm the standard adiabatic Big Bang model's prediction of a linear temperature-redshift relation ($T_{CMB}(z) = T_0(1+z)$) [Hurier et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...561A.143H/abstract). Moreover, anisotropies in the $y$-parameter can be utilized to probe compensated isocurvature perturbations (CIPs), helping to resolve theoretical degeneracies between primordial baryon and dark matter fluctuations [Haga et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JCAP...08..036H/abstract).

**Current Tensions and Baryonic Feedback**
In recent years, high-resolution measurements have revealed a significant tension between observed $y$-distortion statistics and cosmological hydrodynamic simulations. At high multipoles ($\ell > 2000$), observed tSZ power spectra from ACT and the South Pole Telescope exhibit a much lower amplitude and shallower slope than predicted by state-of-the-art $\Lambda$CDM simulations such as FLAMINGO. The literature is currently divided on the exact origin of this discrepancy. While the suppressed high-$\ell$ power could technically be accommodated by assuming a lower value for $\sigma_8$, this interpretation strongly conflicts with independent structure-growth probes (such as CMB lensing and galaxy redshift surveys) which resist a downward shift in $\sigma_8$. While [Efstathiou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250210232E/abstract) suggests baryonic feedback is systematically underestimated to explain the high-$\ell$ power suppression, recent FIRAS bounds strictly limit how much extra feedback can be invoked, directly ruling out extreme feedback models [Fabbian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251203038F/abstract). In tests of these models, measuring the [[sachs-wolfe-effect|Sachs-Wolfe effect]] and the [[integrated-sachs-wolfe-effect|Integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect]] (ISW) in conjunction with tSZ maps provides a way to further disentangle cosmological and astrophysical signals.

## Systematics and Component Separation Challenges

The extraction of the Compton $y$-parameter from multi-frequency microwave maps is fundamentally limited by the presence of bright astrophysical foregrounds that obscure the faint thermal [[sunyaev-zel-dovich-effect|Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect]]. The scale and physical origin of these contaminants dictate the technical difficulties involved in robust component separation.

At large angular scales ($\ell < 60$), the dominant contaminant is diffuse thermal dust emission from the Milky Way. At smaller scales ($\ell > 500$), the clustered cosmic infrared background (CIB) and unresolved radio and infrared point sources become the primary sources of contamination [Planck Collaboration et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...571A..21P/abstract). The CIB is a particularly challenging systematic because it arises from the cumulative thermal dust emission of star-forming galaxies across a wide range of redshifts, meaning its spatial distribution varies as a function of observation frequency. Consequently, standard frequency-based foreground cleaning methods are often insufficient to completely remove CIB fluctuations from reconstructed $y$-maps [Hurier et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...575L..11H/abstract). 

If solely frequency dependence is used for component separation, systematic errors resulting from residual infrared and radio sources can exceed statistical uncertainties by a factor of $2$ to $5$ for Planck-like surveys. This unremoved emission severely biases the estimation of both the cluster [[comptonization|Comptonization]] parameter and the intra-cluster electron temperature [Aghanim et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...439..901A/abstract). CIB contamination is especially problematic for cross-correlation studies; residual CIB-lensing correlations introduce a significant bias into the measured tSZ-lensing power spectrum [Hurier et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...575L..11H/abstract). Similarly, residual dust emission currently limits the precision of tSZ stacking analyses on low-redshift luminous red galaxies [Liu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250208850L/abstract). At very small angular scales ($\ell \gtrsim 3000$), infrared emission also becomes a limiting factor in efforts to map relativistic corrections to the tSZ effect [Hurier et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...604A..94H/abstract).

To overcome these challenges, modern component separation increasingly relies on algorithms that exploit both spatial and spectral morphologies. Blind source separation techniques, such as General Morphological Component Analysis (GMCA), were initially developed to extract images of the cosmic microwave background from Planck data. Demonstrating the versatility of these algorithms, they are now being adapted beyond CMB studies to disentangle complex components in X-ray astronomy, such as analyzing supernova remnants [Picquenot et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...627A.139P/abstract). In targeted cluster studies, forward-modeling approaches have proven highly effective; by simultaneously fitting the SZ signal, CIB galaxies, and Galactic cirrus dust while accounting for their non-Gaussian spatial and frequency-dependent covariances, researchers can accurately extract the average Comptonization parameter without standard biases [Butler et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932...55B/abstract). 

The necessity of exquisite component separation extends to measurements of the global sky-averaged $y$-distortion, where improved astrophysical foreground cleaning techniques have been essential for tightening legacy constraints and ruling out extreme models of baryonic feedback [Fabbian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251203038F/abstract). Note that the Compton $y$-maps can also be used as an independent measure to extract and validate the CMB dipole, as the observer's motion modulates temperature anisotropies with the same frequency dependence as the tSZ effect [Sullivan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv211112186S/abstract).

## The Global y-Monopole and Cosmic Energy Feedback

The global $y$-monopole, denoted as $\langle y \rangle$, represents the all-sky average of the thermal [[sunyaev-zel-dovich-effect|Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect]]. Because the [[comptonization|Comptonization]] parameter $y$ is proportional to the line-of-sight integral of the electron pressure, its sky-averaged monopole serves as a direct, unbiased measure of the total thermal energy contained in ionized gas throughout the observable universe. Consequently, $\langle y \rangle$ imposes a fundamental integral constraint on the cosmic energy budget, capturing the accumulated heat injected by gravitational structure formation, the epoch of reionization, and baryonic feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei (AGN) over cosmic time [Pitrou et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010JCAP...07..019P/abstract), [Hill et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvL.115z1301H/abstract).

Theoretical models predict that the emergence of non-linear structure and the reionization of the intergalactic medium produce a diffuse, guaranteed $y$-distortion signal roughly one order of magnitude below the historic upper limits set by the COBE-FIRAS instrument [Pitrou et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010JCAP...07..019P/abstract), [Hill et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvL.115z1301H/abstract). An improved reanalysis of archival FIRAS data utilizing advanced foreground cleaning techniques has recently constrained the mean $y$-distortion to $\langle y \rangle = (1.2 \pm 2.0) \times 10^{-6}$, yielding a 95% confidence upper bound of $\langle y \rangle \lesssim 5.2 \times 10^{-6}$ [Fabbian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251203038F/abstract). While falling short of a definitive detection, this bound is already stringent enough to rule out several extreme baryonic feedback models implemented in cosmological hydrodynamical simulations, particularly those governing gas in galaxy groups and clusters with masses $M \lesssim 10^{14} M_\odot$ [Fabbian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251203038F/abstract).

Definitively mapping the cosmic energy budget requires a high-precision measurement of the global $y$-monopole by next-generation space spectrometers, such as the proposed Primordial Inflation Explorer (PIXIE). Forecasts predict that such a mission could detect the sky-averaged tSZ signal with a statistical significance of $\gtrsim 1000\sigma$, reducing to an effective signal-to-noise ratio of $\sim 230\sigma$ when limited by cosmic variance [Hill et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvL.115z1301H/abstract). Furthermore, a PIXIE-like instrument would be sensitive enough to detect relativistic corrections to the sky-averaged signal at $30\sigma$. Because standard $y$-distortions suffer from an inherent degeneracy between electron density and temperature, measuring these relativistic "distortions of distortions" is critical. Isolating the relativistic signature breaks this degeneracy, allowing a direct and independent inference of the mean baryon density and the temperature distribution of the intracluster medium at low redshift [Hill et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvL.115z1301H/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the mapping of the Compton $y$-parameter has matured into a precision cosmological tool, several theoretical and observational challenges remain at the forefront of [[secondary-cmb-anisotropies|Secondary CMB anisotropies]] research. 

A major unresolved question is the discrepancy between the observed tSZ power spectrum at high multipoles ($l > 2000$) and the predictions from current hydrodynamic simulations [Efstathiou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250210232E/abstract). Resolving this tension will require a deeper understanding of the thermodynamic state of the circumgalactic and intergalactic medium, and whether models systematically underestimate the expulsion of gas via AGN and supernovae feedback.

Observationally, the definitive detection of the sky-averaged $y$-distortion monopole remains a primary target. While differential measurements across clusters are routine, determining the absolute global $y$-distortion requires a dedicated space-based absolute spectrometer to measure the total thermal energy of ionized gas in the observable universe [Hill et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvL.115z1301H/abstract).

Another compelling frontier is the study of the anisotropic thermal [[sunyaev-zel-dovich-effect|Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect]]. This variation of the tSZ effect arises from the scattering of anisotropic CMB radiation—specifically the low-order multipoles ($\ell=1, 2, 3$)—by the hot plasma within galaxy clusters. Measuring these specific spectral distortions provides a unique, independent method for evaluating the local CMB dipole, quadrupole, and octupole. By utilizing signals from nearby clusters, this technique allows researchers to separate the [[sachs-wolfe-effect|Sachs-Wolfe effect]] from the [[integrated-sachs-wolfe-effect|Integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect]] (ISW) effect, offering fresh pathways to test cosmological models [Edigaryev et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98l3513E/abstract). To this end, recent methodologies have demonstrated the feasibility of extracting these low-order CMB multipoles ($\ell=1,2,3$) using a modified Least Response Method applied to anisotropic SZ signals from galaxy clusters [Novikov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251207441N/abstract). In related work, hypothetical large-scale [[magnetic-sunyaev-zel-dovich-effect|cosmic magnetic fields could align electron orbits in hot plasmas, potentially imprinting an aligned pattern of inverse Compton scattering]] that might explain low-order CMB multipole anomalies like the 'Axis of Evil' [Longo et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007astro.ph..3694L/abstract). Furthermore, gravitational scattering of the CMB by dark (non-luminous) galaxy clusters could induce distinct dipole patterns in the CMB, providing a method to detect such objects independent of standard thermal $y$ distortion signatures [Aso et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...576L...5A/abstract).

Future high-resolution ground-based observatories, including the Simons Observatory and CMB-S4, will dramatically improve the fidelity of $y$-maps. These facilities will enable researchers to probe fundamental physics, including the sum of the neutrino masses ($\sum m_\nu$). Recent modeling of the SZ effect in the presence of massive neutrinos indicates that the mean of the logarithmic Compton-$y$ distribution decreases linearly with the neutrino fraction. Additionally, the thermal SZ power spectrum exhibits a strong scaling with both $\sum m_\nu$ and the structure growth parameter $\sigma_8$, presenting a novel method to break cosmological degeneracies [Luchina et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250316355L/abstract). Complementing these SZ efforts, specific distortions in the neutrino density field caused by structure formation (neutrino winds) produce bispectrum signatures in the spatial cross-correlations of matter and galaxies, which can further help break degeneracies between neutrino mass and the optical depth to reionization [Nascimento et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...11..036N/abstract). High-resolution surveys will also search for subtle early-universe primordial chemical potentials ($\mu$-distortions) that modify the incident CMB blackbody, subsequently altering the observed SZ spectrum in massive clusters [Zegeye et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.109f3503Z/abstract).

Beyond standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology, the $y$-sky is emerging as a laboratory for exotic particle physics, specifically axion-photon conversion. The resonant conversion of CMB photons into axion-like particles in the presence of large-scale magnetic fields induces a "patchy screening" of the CMB. Cross-correlations of this expected signal with large-scale structure have already constrained the axion-photon coupling to $g_{a\gamma\gamma} \lesssim 2 \times 10^{-12}~{\rm GeV}^{-1}$ for masses $10^{-13}~{\rm eV} \lesssim m_a \lesssim 10^{-12}~{\rm eV}$ [Goldstein et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240910514G/abstract). Furthermore, theoretical work demonstrates that turbulence in the intracluster magnetic field and electron density can heavily modulate this conversion, producing highly non-Gaussian, spatially anisotropic spectral distortions that deviate markedly from standard [[comptonization|Comptonization]] signatures [Mehta et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250305302M/abstract). Disentangling these turbulence-induced non-Gaussian axion signals from standard astrophysical foregrounds represents a critical future direction for CMB spectroscopy.
