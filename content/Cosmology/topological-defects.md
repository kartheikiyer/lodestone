In cosmology and particle physics, **topological defects** are stable, non-perturbative configurations of matter or energy fields that are theorized to have formed in the early universe. According to the Kibble mechanism, as the primordial universe expanded and cooled, it underwent a series of spontaneous symmetry-breaking phase transitions. When a field relaxes into a lower-energy vacuum state, causally disconnected regions of space can settle into topologically distinct degenerate vacua. As these regions expand and intersect, the field is unable to smoothly interpolate between them, trapping localized pockets of high energy density in the unbroken symmetric state [Vilenkin et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985PhR...121..263V/abstract). The dimensionality of the resulting defect is dictated by the topology of the vacuum manifold, resulting in zero-dimensional [[monopoles|Monopoles]] (including [[global-monopoles|Global monopoles]]), one-dimensional [[cosmic-strings|Cosmic strings]], two-dimensional [[domain-walls|Domain walls]], and three-dimensional unwinding configurations such as [[non-topological-textures|Non-topological textures]].

Historically, topological defects played a central role in early theories of structure formation. In the [[cosmic-string-scenario|Cosmic string scenario]], the immense localized energy of these macroscopic networks was proposed as the primary gravitational seed for galaxy formation [Vilenkin et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981PhRvD..24.2082V/abstract). However, because defect networks evolve actively and non-linearly, they continually generate non-Gaussian and isocurvature density perturbations. By the late 1990s, high-precision measurements of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) revealed an almost perfectly Gaussian, adiabatic primordial density field. This starkly contradicted the predictions of standard scaling defect models, leading the astrophysical community to strongly favor the single-field inflationary paradigm and effectively ruling out standard topological defects as the primary source of cosmic structure [Albrecht et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PhRvL..79.4736A/abstract), [Peacock et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999coph.book.....P/abstract). Furthermore, defects associated with discrete symmetries or exact spherical topologies, such as standard domain walls and monopoles, were shown to scale poorly with the expansion of the universe, leading to a disastrous overclosure ($\Omega \gg 1$) unless they decayed very early.

Today, topological defects are primarily investigated as highly constrained, subdominant relics that offer a unique observational window into Beyond-Standard-Model (BSM) physics, including Grand Unified Theories (GUTs) and string theory. Because the energy scale of a defect is tied directly to the temperature of its parent phase transition, their macroscopic properties encode the fundamental physics of the early universe. Modern observational efforts place stringent bounds on their abundance through multi-messenger channels. For example, the decay of cosmic string loops produces a stochastic gravitational wave background that is actively hunted by pulsar timing arrays, while large-scale defect networks are constrained by searches for polarization in the CMB and ultracompact minihaloes in the dark matter distribution. Additionally, the decay of superheavy topological relics is continually evaluated as a potential, albeit debated, source of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays.

Despite these strict modern constraints, defect models have recently experienced a theoretical revival driven by observational anomalies in the high-redshift universe. Early data from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has revealed an unexpected overabundance of massive galaxies at redshifts $z > 7$. This has reopened the question of whether "biased" domain walls—which decay before overclosing the universe—or low-tension cosmic strings might provide the supplementary, non-linear gravitational seeds required to explain this early mass excess. Distinguishing these subtle non-Gaussian topological signatures from standard non-linear gravitational clustering remains a critical open challenge. Consequently, next-generation galaxy surveys are increasingly employing Topological Data Analysis (TDA) to map the "topological bias" of the cosmic web, searching for the geometrical fingerprints of these primordial phase transitions.

## Theoretical Foundations and the Kibble Mechanism

According to the standard paradigm of early universe cosmology and particle physics, matter at extremely high temperatures is described by quantum fields whose dynamics are invariant under certain continuous or discrete symmetry groups. As the universe expanded and cooled, it underwent a series of spontaneous symmetry-breaking phase transitions [Vilenkin et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985PhR...121..263V/abstract), [Avelino et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Symm....8...70A/abstract). Andrew Liddle's comprehensive review of early-universe physics provides a foundational synthesis that situates topological defects alongside inflation and baryogenesis as key structural consequences of these high-energy symmetry-breaking phase transitions [Liddle et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..126...31L/abstract). During such a transition, a field makes a phase transition from its initially symmetric, high-energy state into a final configuration with a lower minimum energy. 

If there are more than one topologically distinct vacuum states for the final configuration, different regions in the universe can end up in different vacuum states after the phase transition. These regions are separated by topological defects, which are still on the original field configuration. The dynamics of this process are governed by the Kibble mechanism [Hendry et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.368..315H/abstract). As these separate domains expand and intersect, the field cannot smoothly interpolate between topologically distinct ground states. Consequently, the field is forced out of the vacuum manifold at the boundaries between these regions, trapping localized pockets of high energy density in the original, unbroken symmetric state. These stable, localized energy configurations are topological defects [Vilenkin et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985PhR...121..263V/abstract).

The dimensionality and nature of the resulting defects are strictly determined by the symmetry of the field and its topologically distinct vacuum states:
*   **[[domain-walls|Domain walls]]**: Arise when a discrete symmetry is broken.
*   **[[cosmic-strings|Cosmic strings]]**: One-dimensional line defects formed when a continuous symmetry is broken.
*   **[[monopoles|Monopoles]]**: Zero-dimensional point defects.
*   **[[non-topological-textures|Non-topological textures]]**: Three-dimensional unwinding configurations.

The characteristic energy scale and mass of these defects are dictated by the temperature of the universe at the epoch of the phase transition. Grand Unified Theory (GUT) phase transitions, which occurred at temperatures $T_{\text{GUT}} \sim 10^{15} \text{ GeV}$, would produce extremely massive defects. For instance, the expected mass of a GUT-scale magnetic monopole is $m \sim T_{\text{GUT}}$, and the Kibble mechanism predicts a primordial abundance of roughly one such monopole per horizon volume at the time of the transition. Conversely, defects formed during the electroweak phase transition would be characterized by a mass scale of $\sim 10^2 \text{ GeV}$, rendering their cosmological impacts far less severe [Vilenkin et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981PhRvD..24.2082V/abstract). 

While the extreme energies of cosmological phase transitions are inaccessible to direct experimentation, the Kibble mechanism relies on universal statistical and topological principles that can be tested in the laboratory. For example, the rapid adiabatic expansion of liquid $^4\text{He}$ through its critical density induces a superfluid phase transition that copiously produces quantized vortices. These vortices serve as an exact laboratory analogue for the cosmological formation of [[cosmic-strings|Cosmic strings]], providing empirical support for the Kibble mechanism's predictions regarding early-universe defect generation [Hendry et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.368..315H/abstract).

## Taxonomy of Cosmological Defects

The classification of cosmological defects is determined by the topology of the vacuum manifold $\mathcal{M}$ following a spontaneous symmetry-breaking phase transition. According to the Kibble mechanism, as the early universe cooled, distinct regions settled into different, topologically distinct vacuum states. The nature of the boundaries between these regions depends on the dimensionality of the broken symmetry, producing zero-dimensional [[monopoles|Monopoles]], one-dimensional [[cosmic-strings|Cosmic strings]], two-dimensional [[domain-walls|Domain walls]], and three-dimensional [[non-topological-textures|textures]] [Vilenkin et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985PhR...121..263V/abstract), [Hendry et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.368..315H/abstract).

**Zero-Dimensional: Monopoles**
Point-like defects, such as magnetic monopoles and [[global-monopoles|Global monopoles]], form based on the symmetry of the field and its topologically distinct vacuum states. For a Grand Unified Theory (GUT) phase transition, monopoles are predicted to have a mass comparable to the GUT temperature scale, $m \sim T_{\rm GUT}$. However, standard topological monopoles would be produced with an energy density that implies a present-day density parameter $\Omega_0 \gg 1$. Because this would vastly overclose the universe, standard monopoles are definitively ruled out as primary seeds for cosmic structure.

**One-Dimensional: Cosmic strings**
Line-like defects arise when a continuous symmetry is broken. The cosmological impact of cosmic strings depends heavily on their energy scale. Strings formed at the electroweak scale ($\sim 10^2$ GeV) have a negligible cosmological effect, whereas GUT-scale strings ($\sim 10^{15}$ GeV) can form massive oscillating loops capable of seeding gravitational instabilities [Vilenkin et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981PhRvD..24.2082V/abstract). Recent theoretical extensions also suggest that heterotic cosmic strings arising from M-theory can carry charged zero modes, potentially providing the primordial seed fields necessary to explain galactic-scale magnetic fields [Gwyn et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvD..79h3502G/abstract). Recent studies of ultraviolet luminosity functions from JWST and HST place a stringent upper bound on the dimensionless string tension at $G\mu \lesssim 10^{-8}$ (95% credibility) [Blamart et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209980B/abstract). 

**Two-Dimensional: Domain walls**
Sheet-like defects form when a discrete symmetry is broken. Like monopoles, standard domain walls are traditionally considered cosmologically disastrous because their energy density scales poorly with the expansion of the universe, rapidly leading to $\Omega_0 \gg 1$. However, the literature presents a significant caveat: "biased" domain walls. In potentials with a slight non-degeneracy between vacua, the wall network becomes unstable and decays before dominating the universe [Lalak et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008PhRvD..78h5020L/abstract). The energy released by the collapse of these biased domain walls has been recently proposed as a mechanism to seed massive non-linear structures, potentially explaining the anomalous abundance of bright galaxies at $z \gtrsim 7$ observed by JWST [Winckler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722812W/abstract).

**Three-Dimensional: Textures**
Textures (including Non-topological textures) are delocalized, three-dimensional topological field configurations. Rather than forming stable localized structures, textures dynamically unwind and collapse, leaving behind density fluctuations and producing distinctive non-Gaussian signatures in the cosmic microwave background [Spergel et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992SciAm.266c..52S/abstract). 

**Composite Defects**
In some symmetry-breaking schemes, hybrid structures can form, such as monopoles connected by strings (monopolonia). The cosmological viability of these composites remains an area of active debate. Some analyses suggest that such topologically unstable networks annihilate and disappear long before producing appreciable galactic-scale density fluctuations [Vilenkin et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982NuPhB.196..240V/abstract). Conversely, other researchers argue that late-decaying relics from these composite networks could survive in the galactic halo, potentially serving as the source of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays (UHECRs) with energies exceeding $10^{11}$ GeV [Berezinsky et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999NuPhS..75..119B/abstract).

## Historical Evolution: From Structure Seeds to Precision Constraints

In the early development of modern cosmology, topological defects—such as [[cosmic-strings|Cosmic strings]], [[domain-walls|Domain walls]], and [[non-topological-textures|textures]]—were considered leading candidates for the primordial density perturbations that seeded galaxy formation [Vilenkin et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981PhRvD..24.2082V/abstract), [Vilenkin et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985PhR...121..263V/abstract). Theorized to form via the Kibble mechanism during symmetry-breaking phase transitions in the early universe, the energy trapped within these network structures was expected to draw in surrounding matter via gravitational instability, thereby acting as the primary driver of large-scale structure formation. For example, second-order phase transitions and the subsequent formation of domain walls were theorized to lead to the clustering of massive black holes, potentially serving as the primordial nuclei for galaxy formation [Khlopov et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002astro.ph..2505K/abstract).

A central theoretical and observational conflict soon emerged in the debate of inflation vs. defects. In addition, the energy scales intrinsic to these two paradigms often differ substantially; the inflationary energy scale can be markedly lower than the extreme energy scales typically required to produce structure-forming topological defects [Liddle et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992PhLB..279..244L/abstract). Pure topological defect scenarios inherently generate "active" perturbations that are strongly non-Gaussian and isocurvature in nature. In contrast, standard single-field inflationary models predict a "passive" spectrum of highly adiabatic, Gaussian fluctuations. The paradigm shifted definitively in the late 1990s with precision measurements of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB). When standard active scaling defect models were calibrated using COBE normalization, they predicted a matter power spectrum and CMB anisotropy multipoles that were in severe conflict with observational data [Albrecht et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PhRvL..79.4736A/abstract), [Albrecht et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998PhRvD..59b3508A/abstract). Subsequent high-resolution CMB and large-scale redshift surveys confirmed that the initial density field was characterized by almost-perfect Gaussian random phase distributions [Parihar et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...796...86P/abstract). This solidified the inflationary paradigm within the Standard Model of Cosmology and effectively ruled out topological defects as the primary source of cosmic structure.

During this transition, the literature reflected an active effort to salvage defect models. In addition to theoretical conflicts, the literature from the 'golden age' of CMB research describes the active process of 'killing' and 'resurrecting' defect theories as they were tested against combined CMB and large-scale structure datasets [Bond et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997mba..conf...15B/abstract). Some researchers argued that defects could still match galaxy clustering data if the universe were open ($\Omega \sim 0.2-0.4$) rather than flat [Pen et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995PhRvD..51.4099P/abstract), or if the defect networks exhibited broken scale invariance and decayed at the radiation-matter transition [Pen et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..4083P/abstract). Ultimately, however, the astrophysical consensus shifted toward treating topological defects not as an alternative to inflation, but as tightly constrained, subdominant relics that could coexist within an inflationary universe [Brandenberger et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PhRvD..69h1301B/abstract). Modern precision bounds from CMB anisotropies and pulsar timing arrays have severely restricted their abundance; for example, the dimensionless tension of cosmic strings is now bounded at $G\mu \lesssim 10^{-8}$ [Blamart et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209980B/abstract).

Despite these strict bounds, defect models have recently experienced a theoretical revival, driven by observational puzzles in the high-redshift universe. Early data from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has revealed an unexpectedly high abundance of massive, UV-luminous galaxies at redshifts $z > 7$. While the literature remains unresolved as to whether this mass excess stems from standard astrophysical processes (such as variable star-formation efficiencies) or new cosmology, subdominant topological defects offer a compelling physical mechanism. Recent studies suggest that incorporating non-linear seeds from low-tension cosmic strings [Blamart et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209980B/abstract) or decaying "biased" domain walls [Winckler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722812W/abstract) can accurately reproduce the observed early-universe ultraviolet luminosity functions without violating existing CMB constraints.

## Observational Signatures in the Cosmic Microwave Background

Topological defects leave distinct imprints on the cosmic microwave background (CMB), offering a direct observational window into the high-energy physics of the early universe. Unlike the standard inflationary paradigm—which predicts predominantly adiabatic, Gaussian density fluctuations—topological defects actively seed structure through localized concentrations of stress-energy. This continuous generation mechanism natively sources both non-Gaussianity and isocurvature perturbations [Brandenberger et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997hep.ph....1276B/abstract). However, there are nuances in combined models; certain inflationary phase transitions can themselves generate topological defects that provide adiabatic density perturbations featuring a sharp maximum between galactic and horizon scales [Linde et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992PhLB..284..215L/abstract).

Historically, topological defects such as [[cosmic-strings|Cosmic strings]] and [[non-topological-textures|Non-topological textures]] were investigated as the primary candidates for seeding cosmic structure. However, calculations of the CMB power spectrum for standard "active" scaling defect models revealed a critical mismatch with observational data. Pure defect models struggle to simultaneously match the large-scale galaxy distribution and the amplitude of CMB anisotropies initially measured by COBE [Albrecht et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PhRvL..79.4736A/abstract), [Albrecht et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998PhRvD..59b3508A/abstract). While some researchers noted that defect models could be temporarily reconciled with early data by assuming an open universe with $\Omega \sim 0.2-0.4$ [Spergel et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...412L...5S/abstract), [Pen et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995PhRvD..51.4099P/abstract). In open universe models, the generation of large-scale cosmic background radiation fluctuations from defects is suppressed, potentially offering better agreement with galaxy survey data compared to flat universe models [Spergel et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994NuPhS..35...17S/abstract). However, the advent of high-precision acoustic peak measurements confirmed a flat universe, effectively ruling out pure defect scenarios in favor of inflation. 

Despite this, topological defects may still exist as a subdominant component of the universe's energy density. Modern constraints from the Planck mission and other high-resolution CMB experiments tightly restrict their fractional contribution. Because defect networks evolve actively and non-linearly, they continually generate vector metric perturbations in addition to scalar modes, which can significantly influence observables like redshift space distortions [Bonvin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JCAP...02..028B/abstract). General limits on temperature anisotropies and polarization anomalies from the CMB continue to place stringent upper bounds on the energy scales of cosmological models [Avelino et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Symm....8...70A/abstract). More recently, by analyzing early-universe ultraviolet luminosity functions (UVLFs) rather than CMB data, the dimensionless tension of cosmic strings has been constrained even more tightly to $G\mu \lesssim 10^{-8}$ at 95% credibility [Blamart et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209980B/abstract). Limits are similarly prohibitive for [[domain-walls|Domain walls]] and [[monopoles|Monopoles]], which would overclose the universe ($\Omega \gg 1$) unless they decay very early or form highly biased, unstable networks [Vilenkin et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982NuPhB.196..240V/abstract), [Lalak et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008PhRvD..78h5020L/abstract).

The localized nature of defects also provides a unique theoretical source of primordial non-Gaussianity. Because density perturbations seeded by defects do not originate from random quantum fluctuations with random phases, their statistical properties deviate significantly from a pure Gaussian random field. Studies propose utilizing the trispectrum—the connected four-point correlation function in Fourier space—as a sensitive quantitative probe to discriminate these topological signatures from standard Gaussian models [Verde et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...553...14V/abstract). This distinct defect-induced non-Gaussianity can also be contrasted with other alternative scenarios, such as the peculiar momentum-dependent primordial bispectrum predicted by quasi-single field inflation models [Sefusatti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JCAP...08..033S/abstract). To date, analyses of the genus topology of the large-scale structure remain consistent with Gaussian random phase initial conditions [Parihar et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...796...86P/abstract). 

Consequently, the prevailing consensus treats topological defects not as an alternative to inflation, but as a potential complementary phenomenon that could have occurred after or alongside it. Modern cosmological parameter bounds remain robust even when relaxing the assumption of purely adiabatic fluctuations to allow for minor contributions from the incoherent, isocurvature modes that topological defects would produce [Brandenberger et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PhRvD..69h1301B/abstract).

## Large-Scale Structure and Topological Bias

Although standard active scaling models of topological defects are strongly excluded as the primary source of primordial density fluctuations [Albrecht et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PhRvL..79.4736A/abstract), [Albrecht et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998PhRvD..59b3508A/abstract), subdominant defect populations inject localized, non-Gaussian perturbations into the matter density field. These non-linear seeds can distinctly influence the architecture of the cosmic web. The departure from Gaussianity induced by defects can be statistically quantified using higher-order correlation functions, such as the trispectrum of the galaxy density field [Verde et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...553...14V/abstract). Statistical physics analyses of galaxy distributions and CMB fluctuations suggest that while amplitudes are small, the complex structure of these fields remains central to resolving the Harrison-Zeldovich spectrum debate [Pietronero et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhyA..306..395P/abstract).

Modern topological data analysis, particularly persistent homology and Betti curves, reveals that the structural connectivity of the cosmic web—characterized by nodes, filaments, and [[domain-walls|sheet-like walls]]—exhibits a "topological bias." This is an environmental structure bias that is independent of standard two-point halo clustering. Observations and simulations demonstrate a mass-dependent linear scaling relation wherein dark matter haloes of different masses preferentially trace environments with distinct topological signatures [Bermejo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract). The presence of non-Gaussian initial conditions from defects alters these multiscale geometrical environments, providing a novel structural signature that upcoming large-scale redshift surveys aim to constrain. Furthermore, topological data analysis of the cosmic web can accurately recover characteristic cosmological scales, such as the $\sim 150\ {\rm Mpc}$ baryon acoustic oscillation signal, even in sparsely sampled data sets [Kono et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200602905K/abstract).

On smaller scales, topological defects influence structure formation by seeding ultracompact minihaloes (UCMHs). Because defects generate large-amplitude isocurvature perturbations, they can trigger the early gravitational collapse of dark matter into dense, highly compact structures that survive to the present era. The abundance of UCMHs serves as a purely gravitational probe of small-scale cosmology; current pulsar timing arrays place stringent limits on the density of UCMHs, which in turn constrains the parameter space of [[cosmic-strings|Cosmic strings]] independent of the particle nature of dark matter [Clark et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.1402C/abstract).

Recently, defect models have experienced a theoretical revival in the context of the high-redshift universe. Observations from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) indicate an unexpected abundance of massive galaxies at redshifts $z > 7$. Cosmological models suggest that the localized collapse energy injected by "biased" domain walls [Winckler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722812W/abstract) or the non-linear perturbations from low-tension cosmic strings (with tensions bounded by $G\mu \lessapprox 10^{-8}$) [Blamart et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209980B/abstract) can significantly boost the ultraviolet luminosity function at high redshifts. This mechanism provides the necessary non-linear seeds to resolve the high-redshift galaxy abundance puzzle without requiring modifications to standard star-formation astrophysics.

Beyond their role as seeds for density perturbations, topological defects can be observed via their direct gravitational influence on light and galactic dynamics. For instance, "pattern field" theory proposes that defects couple to the baryonic matter distribution to account for the gross structure of cold disk galaxies and their dark halos as self-organized patterns [Venkataramani et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191014649V/abstract). General relativistic models of galaxies demonstrate that topological structures embedded within a halo can impact its average rotational dynamics, potentially altering the transition between the rising and flat regimes of galactic rotation curves [Galoppo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025EPJC...85..382G/abstract). Additionally, topological defects act as distinct gravitational lenses. Milky Way-like galaxies harboring such defects are predicted to exhibit a noticeable increase in their gravitational deflection angles [Galoppo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025EPJC...85..382G/abstract). On a cosmological scale, the prospective detection of standard, universe-spanning cosmic strings has long been proposed through their distinct gravitational lensing of distant background quasars and galaxies [Pacyński et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986Natur.319..567P/abstract). Conversely, fossil remnants of low-tension cosmic strings are theorized to cluster locally within the Milky Way, where they could potentially be detected by the optical microlensing of background stars in upcoming surveys [Chernoff et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IJMPD..2430010C/abstract).

## Probes of Beyond-Standard-Model Physics

Topological defects offer a unique observational window into energy scales far beyond the reach of terrestrial colliders. According to the Kibble mechanism, discrete symmetry-breaking phase transitions in the early universe—such as those predicted by Grand Unified Theories (GUTs)—can generate networks of [[domain-walls|Domain walls]], [[cosmic-strings|Cosmic strings]], and [[monopoles|Monopoles]] [Vilenkin et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985PhR...121..263V/abstract), [Hendry et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.368..315H/abstract). For a GUT phase transition occurring when the universe cooled through $T \sim 10^{27}$ K (roughly $10^{-34}$ s after the Big Bang), the resulting defects are expected to have characteristic mass scales of $m \sim 10^{15}$ GeV [Vilenkin et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981PhRvD..24.2082V/abstract), [Hendry et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.368..315H/abstract). In addition to standard string and monopole configurations, primordial black holes (PBHs) can also arise from these extreme early-universe transitions, acting as macroscopic cosmological defects that serve as universal probes of superhigh-energy BSM physics [Khlopov et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IJMPA..2830042K/abstract). In supersymmetric inflationary models, heavy magnetic monopoles (mass $\sim 10^{13}$ GeV) can survive the expansion and remain present in the galactic halo at potentially observable levels [Lazarides et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996PhLB..372...20L/abstract).

While standard grand unification strings were once favored in the [[cosmic-string-scenario|Cosmic string scenario]] for galaxy formation, their predicted massive loops and subsequent black hole production remain vital theoretical probes of the very early universe [Vilenkin et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981PhRvD..24.2082V/abstract). Furthermore, decaying superheavy relics such as monopolonia and cosmic necklaces have been proposed as sources of Ultra-High Energy Cosmic Rays (UHECRs), potentially injecting particles with energies exceeding $10^{11}$ GeV and bypassing the GZK cutoff [Berezinsky et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999NuPhS..75..119B/abstract). However, this interpretation remains debated, with some researchers arguing that known astrophysical accelerators, such as the radio galaxy Centaurus A, are more likely sources for UHECRs than distributed topological defects [Farrar et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.10370F/abstract).

The framework of string theory provides another rich source of topological relics. In models featuring the flux compactification of extra dimensions, fundamental strings can manifest on cosmological scales with tensions substantially below the Planck scale [Chernoff et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IJMPD..2430010C/abstract). Recent analyses of ultraviolet luminosity functions from the Hubble and James Webb Space Telescopes (JWST) have set an upper bound on this dimensionless string tension of $G\mu \lesssim 10^{-8}$ at 95% credibility [Blamart et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209980B/abstract). A distinct signature could arise from heterotic cosmic strings in M-theory, which stem from M5-branes wrapped around four compact internal dimensions. Because these strings carry charged zero modes, their scaling solutions could generate coherent primordial seed magnetic fields on galactic scales [Gwyn et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvD..79h3502G/abstract).

Topological defects are also intimately linked to the dark sector and dark matter candidates. Models involving the spontaneous breaking of global U(1) symmetries, such as simplified axion models, produce strings connected by domain walls that typically decay before they can dominate the cosmic energy density [Vilenkin et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982PhRvL..48.1867V/abstract). Defect networks can act as seeds for ultracompact minihaloes (UCMHs), the abundance of which can be heavily constrained by pulsar timing array data [Clark et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.1402C/abstract). In the context of axion cosmology, ultralight bosons such as axion-like particles (ALPs) are leading dark matter candidates that can form stable, macroscopic field configurations [Marsh et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151007633M/abstract). Direct detection efforts have probed the hypothesis that the Earth routinely passes through these macroscopic domain walls. For instance, the Global Network of Optical Magnetometers for Exotic physics searches (GNOME) monitored coordinated atomic magnetometer data but found no statistically significant signals during month-long trials, placing strict experimental constraints on ALP domain wall scenarios [Afach et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatPh..17.1396A/abstract).

Despite historical tensions with the Cosmic Microwave Background that marginalized active scaling defect models [Albrecht et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PhRvL..79.4736A/abstract), [Albrecht et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998PhRvD..59b3508A/abstract), subdominant defect populations have seen a recent revival. The unexpected abundance of massive, high-redshift galaxies ($z \gtrsim 7$) observed by JWST has reinvigorated interest in non-linear seeds. It is currently actively debated whether "biased" domain walls—which decay before they overclose the universe—or sparse populations of cosmic strings can account for this early mass excess without modifying standard star-formation physics [Winckler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722812W/abstract), [Blamart et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209980B/abstract). If confirmed, these BSM topological relics would provide a crucial missing link between early-universe phase transitions and late-time structure formation.

## Gravitational Wave Background and Pulsar Timing

The cosmological evolution of topological defects, particularly networks of [[cosmic-strings|Cosmic strings]], provides a persistent source of gravitational radiation. Following their formation in early-universe symmetry-breaking phase transitions, cosmic string networks produce a population of surviving closed string loops of cosmological sizes that oscillate relativistically and decay primarily through gravitational wave emission [Chernoff et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IJMPD..2430010C/abstract). The superposition of these decay signals, integrated over cosmic history, generates a stochastic gravitational wave background that spans a sufficiently broad range of frequencies to be an observational target through gravitational wave emission and pulsar timing arrays.

The amplitude of the resulting gravitational wave spectrum is heavily parameterized by the dimensionless string tension, $G\mu$ (where $G$ is the gravitational constant and $\mu$ is the mass per unit length), which dictates the energy scale of the underlying symmetry breaking. While early theories posited that defects might act as the primary drivers of cosmic structure formation, modern consensus dictates that they can only exist as a subdominant component of the universe's energy density. Recent analyses—incorporating cosmic microwave background limits alongside ultraviolet luminosity functions of high-redshift galaxies—place a stringent upper bound on the string tension:
$$ G\mu \lessapprox 10^{-8} $$
at a $95\%$ credibility level [Blamart et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209980B/abstract). Low-tension strings of this nature, which may arise naturally from the warped geometries of extra spatial dimensions in string theory, could persist as fossil remnants within galaxies and remain detectable through their continuous gravitational wave emission [Chernoff et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IJMPD..2430010C/abstract). Furthermore, specialized measurements of gravitational wave polarization intensities have been proposed as a means to test other defect-mediated phase transitions, such as those involving late-universe neutrino condensates [Dvali et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..93k3002D/abstract).

In the nanohertz frequency band, pulsar timing provides a highly sensitive, independent probe of topological defects. Beyond direct detection of the stochastic gravitational wave background, pulsar timing can constrain the secondary, non-linear structures seeded by defects in the early universe. Large-amplitude primordial perturbations and defects such as cosmic strings can induce the formation of ultracompact minihaloes (UCMHs)—dense clumps of dark matter that are expected to survive into the present epoch [Clark et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.1402C/abstract). Pulsar timing arrays place limits on UCMHs based on their gravitational effects. Current pulsar timing data has established rigorous limits on the cosmic abundance of UCMHs, which subsequently constrains the parameter space for generic small-scale non-Gaussianity and defect models. Because these constraints rely entirely on macroscopic gravitational interactions, they offer the significant advantage of remaining independent of the specific particle nature of dark matter [Clark et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.1402C/abstract).

## High-Energy Phenomena and Cosmic Rays

While the primary cosmological focus on topological defects centers on their role in the early universe, their late-time evolution and decay can act as powerful "top-down" engines for high-energy astrophysical phenomena. Specifically, the annihilation or interaction of macroscopic defects can inject massive amounts of energy into the intergalactic and galactic medium, potentially offering an alternative to conventional shock-driven particle acceleration.

One of the most prominent high-energy signatures theorized for topological defects is the production of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays (UHECRs). Standard astrophysical acceleration mechanisms struggle to explain the origin of cosmic rays with energies well in excess of $10^{10}$--$10^{11}\text{ GeV}$ [Berezinsky et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999NuPhS..75..119B/abstract). This is particularly challenging due to the energy threshold imposed by the Greisen-Zatsepin-Kuzmin (GZK) cutoff. Berezinsky proposed that specific defect configurations—such as cosmic necklaces or monopolonia—could act as local, non-thermal UHECR sources. If these superheavy relics survive and cluster within the dark matter halo of the Milky Way, their late-time decays would yield UHECRs that evade the GZK cutoff entirely, since the particles originate locally rather than traversing cosmological distances [Berezinsky et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999NuPhS..75..119B/abstract).

However, the literature is actively divided on the viability of defects as UHECR sources. Farrar argues strongly against distributed sources like topological defects, noting that any proposed mechanism must simultaneously account for the unbroken spectral smoothness and isotropy of events down to at least $E \sim 10^{18.7}\text{ eV}$. This observational constraint leads some researchers to disfavor topological defect models, attributing UHECRs instead to discrete, highly energetic extragalactic accelerators (such as the nearby radio galaxy Centaurus A) propagating through an estimated extragalactic magnetic field of $\sim 0.3\ \mu\text{G}$ [Farrar et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.10370F/abstract). 

In addition to the UHECR regime, specific classes of lower-energy defects could leave distinct signatures in the galactic cosmic-ray antimatter spectrum. Topological incarnations within extensions of the standard electroweak model, such as Z-strings and electroweak [[monopoles|Monopoles]], have been analyzed for their decay products. It has been demonstrated that light superconducting [[cosmic-strings|Cosmic strings]] could serve as a continuous non-thermal source of antimatter, potentially injecting a significant and observable antiproton flux into the galactic medium [Kephart et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996PhLB..388..481K/abstract).

Beyond direct decay, surviving macroscopic defects can generate high-energy phenomena through direct hydrodynamic interaction with the astrophysical environment. A cosmic string moving supersonically through a collisional gas—such as the hot intracluster medium of galaxy clusters—produces a wake enclosed by two planar shocks. The shock compression ratio, which depends on the string's speed and its geometric angle defect, results in localized heating and gas over-densities. Under favorable conditions, these string-crossing events can induce sustained particle acceleration. Because the consequences of such a collision persist over cosmological timescales, these acceleration sites could potentially be identified observationally as unusual, highly planar large-scale radio structures [Beresnyak et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...804..121B/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Although topological defects are firmly excluded as the primary generators of primordial density fluctuations [Albrecht et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PhRvL..79.4736A/abstract), their potential existence as a subdominant component of the universe's energy density remains a vibrant area of research. Currently, the literature is focused on two major frontiers: identifying defect signatures in high-redshift galaxy surveys and developing sophisticated mathematical tools to isolate non-Gaussian topological features in the cosmic web.

Recent observations by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have revealed an unexpectedly high abundance of massive galaxies at redshifts $z > 7$, challenging standard $\Lambda$CDM structure formation models. This JWST galaxy abundance puzzle has revived interest in defects as supplementary, highly non-linear seeds for early galaxy formation. Recent semi-analytical modeling demonstrates that the non-Gaussian perturbations generated by [[cosmic-strings|Cosmic strings]] can sufficiently boost the early-galaxy abundance to match observed ultraviolet luminosity functions (UVLFs) from $z = 4$ to $z = 17$, without requiring modifications to standard star-formation astrophysics [Blamart et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209980B/abstract). Fitting these UVLFs establishes a competitive upper bound on the cosmic string tension of $G\mu \lessapprox 10^{-8}$ at $95\%$ credibility [Blamart et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209980B/abstract). Similarly, biased [[domain-walls|Domain walls]]—which evade the stringent cosmological overclosure limits that rule out standard domain walls—can release significant collapse energy to seed dark matter halos massive enough to explain the $z \gtrsim 7$ mass excess [Winckler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722812W/abstract). However, the literature remains explicitly conflicted on whether this high-redshift excess is definitively driven by defects or if it can be resolved through refined astrophysical models of early star formation [Blamart et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209980B/abstract).

On the data analysis frontier, distinguishing the subtle, non-Gaussian signatures of topological defects from standard non-linear gravitational clustering remains a central challenge [Bonvin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JCAP...02..028B/abstract). To this end, researchers are increasingly applying Topological Data Analysis (TDA) to large-scale structure datasets. TDA, specifically through the algebraic framework of persistent homology, quantifies the multiscale topology of the universe by classifying spatial features by their homology dimensions (e.g., 0-dimensional point components, 1-dimensional loops, and 2-dimensional shells) [Kono et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200602905K/abstract). Persistent homology has successfully identified the baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) scale as a statistically significant 2-dimensional hollow shell at $\sim 150 \ {\rm Mpc}$, even in sparse quasar samples [Kono et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200602905K/abstract). 

Furthermore, TDA has revealed the existence of a "topological bias," demonstrating that dark matter halos trace different geometrical environments of the cosmic web (filaments versus walls) depending on their mass. This environmental structural bias scales linearly with halo mass and operates independently of the standard two-point correlation clustering bias [Bermejo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract). Accounting for topological bias will be critical for interpreting data from next-generation surveys such as DESI, Euclid, and the Vera Rubin Observatory. 

Ultimately, fully resolving these observational questions will require theoretical advancements in the non-perturbative physics that governs spontaneous symmetry breaking and defect formation in the early universe [Marsh et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv151007633M/abstract). Accurately predicting the macroscopic relics of these phase transitions remains an essential step in bridging fundamental particle physics with the next decade of precision cosmology.
