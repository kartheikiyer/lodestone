## Article

Warm dark matter (WDM) is a class of hypothetical [[non-baryonic-dark-matter|Non-baryonic dark matter]] candidates characterized by intermediate, keV-scale masses. Positioned kinematically between the highly relativistic particles of hot dark matter and the massive, sluggish particles of standard [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] (CDM), WDM particles decouple from the primordial plasma while still retaining a non-negligible thermal velocity dispersion. This intrinsic motion yields a characteristic free-streaming length that allows the particles to stream out of small gravitational potential wells in the early Universe. Consequently, WDM heavily suppresses the linear matter power spectrum on sub-galactic scales, delaying the formation of the first luminous objects and truncating the hierarchy of structure formation at low masses, while preserving the well-documented successes of the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] on macroscopic cosmic scales.

The primary theoretical motivation for WDM lies in its potential to elegantly resolve the small-scale anomalies of the pure CDM paradigm, specifically the "missing satellites" and "cusp-core" problems [Jing et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MPLA...16.1795J/abstract). By washing out high-frequency primordial density fluctuations, WDM inherently predicts a reduced abundance of low-mass subhalos. Furthermore, the thermal phase-space packing limits of WDM particles theoretically inhibit the formation of steeply cusped central density profiles, naturally yielding the [[cored-dark-matter-halos|Cored dark matter halos]] favored by kinematic observations of dwarf galaxies [Hogan et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph.12549H/abstract). The most prominent WDM candidate is the [[sterile-neutrino|Sterile neutrino]], frequently modeled with a [[dark-matter-mass|Dark matter mass]] of $\sim 7$ keV to simultaneously account for the appropriate relic abundance and unexplained $3.5$ keV X-ray emission lines observed in galaxy clusters [Abazajian et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvL.112p1303A/abstract). 

Despite its theoretical appeal, modern astrophysical observations have placed the WDM framework under severe tension, a central theme explored throughout this article. Stringent lower mass limits derived from the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest, Milky Way satellite counts, and high-redshift James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) data consistently demand particle masses $m \gtrsim 3-12$ keV [Viel et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvL..97g1301V/abstract), [Dekker et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106l3026D/abstract), [Liu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968...79L/abstract). At these higher masses, the free-streaming length is drastically reduced, rendering WDM dynamically indistinguishable from CDM on galactic scales and mitigating its ability to fully solve the core-cusp problem. This article examines the physical mechanisms of the leading WDM particle candidates and the multi-wavelength observational constraints shaping the field. It also investigates the profound degeneracy between WDM free-streaming and baryonic feedback processes, explores hybrid scenarios such as mixed [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]] and [[alternative-dark-matter-models|Alternative dark matter models]], and highlights the open questions regarding how upcoming measurements of [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]] might definitively constrain the thermal history of the [[dark-sector|Dark sector]].

## Background and Physical Mechanism

Warm dark matter represents a class of [[non-baryonic-dark-matter|Non-baryonic dark matter]] candidates characterized by a keV-scale mass. Positioned between the macroscopic free-streaming scales of hot dark matter and the negligible thermal velocities of [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]], WDM particles decouple from the primordial plasma while still relativistic or semi-relativistic. Consequently, they retain a non-negligible velocity dispersion in the early Universe. This intrinsic thermal motion gives rise to a characteristic free-streaming length, which defines the spatial scale below which dark matter particles can freely escape from gravitational potential wells before undergoing gravitational collapse.

The primary cosmological consequence of this free-streaming length is the erasure of primordial density fluctuations on sub-galactic scales. In the linear regime of structure formation, this manifests as a sharp linear power spectrum suppression at high wavenumbers compared to the standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] [Colombi et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...458....1C/abstract). Because WDM behaves identically to CDM on spatial scales larger than $\sim 100$ kpc [de Vega et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1304.0759D/abstract), it preserves the well-documented successes of CDM in describing the large-scale structure of the Universe and the cosmic microwave background. However, on small scales, the free-streaming effectively suppresses the formation of low-mass [[compact-dark-matter-halos|Compact dark matter halos]] and delays the epoch of early galaxy formation.

Furthermore, the maximum phase-space density of WDM is bounded by Liouville's theorem, which restricts the fine-grained phase packing of particles. This thermal limit mathematically bounds the maximum density achievable at the center of a collapsed structure, differentiating it from the infinitely deep potential wells theoretically predicted by pure CDM and offering a mechanism to naturally generate [[cored-dark-matter-halos|Cored dark matter halos]] [Hogan et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph.12549H/abstract).

## Historical Context: From Hot to Cold and Back

In the early 1980s, theoretical preferences for a flat universe—coupled with tightening constraints on cosmic microwave background anisotropies—drove cosmologists to search for non-baryonic dark matter candidates. Neutrinos initially emerged as a natural choice, spurred by tentative experimental claims in 1980 of an electron neutrino mass of $\sim 30$ eV. Because such light particles remain relativistic long after decoupling, they constituted "Hot Dark Matter" (HDM). In HDM cosmologies, large thermal velocities smooth out density perturbations on scales smaller than tens of megaparsecs via free-streaming. Consequently, structure formation must proceed "top-down," with massive Zel'dovich pancakes forming first and fragmenting into galaxies.

The HDM paradigm, however, quickly encountered fatal theoretical and observational challenges. Tremaine and Gunn (1979) demonstrated via the Pauli exclusion principle that individual galaxy halos could not be densely packed enough if composed of $30$ eV neutrinos. Furthermore, early $N$-body simulations in 1984 demonstrated that an HDM-dominated universe could not produce the observed distribution of galaxies without generating excessively strong clustering on large scales. 

Following the HDM failure, the community rapidly shifted toward [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]]. Groundbreaking calculations of the linear transfer function for adiabatic CDM models, such as those by Peebles in 1982, showed that heavier, weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs) with masses in the range of $1 \text{ GeV} \lesssim m \lesssim 3 \text{ TeV}$ would decouple with negligible thermal velocities. This lack of free-streaming allowed for bottom-up, hierarchical structure formation, cementing the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] as the cosmological standard.

Despite the triumphs of the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] on large cosmological scales, high-resolution $N$-body simulations in the 1990s and early 2000s revealed significant discrepancies in [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]] [Moore et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.370..629M/abstract). To resolve these tensions, theorists revisited the intermediate "warm" dark matter regime. A WDM particle retains a non-negligible free-streaming length that washes out primordial density fluctuations on sub-galactic scales, naturally suppressing the formation of low-mass dark matter halos [Jing et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MPLA...16.1795J/abstract).

In recent years, the concept of WDM has evolved to focus on specific keV-scale particle candidates, most notably the [[sterile-neutrino|Sterile neutrino]] [de Vega et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1304.0759D/abstract). However, modern astrophysical constraints from the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest and high-redshift galaxy observations have pushed the allowable mass limit for thermal WDM relics into a regime where WDM behaves so similarly to CDM that it struggles to solve the core-cusp problem completely [Macciò et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.1105M/abstract), [Lapi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...09..003L/abstract). Consequently, it remains highly contested whether the small-scale discrepancies strictly necessitate a departure to [[alternative-dark-matter-models|Alternative dark matter models]], or if complex baryonic feedback processes are sufficient to mimic WDM effects within a pure CDM framework [Tissera et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..922T/abstract), [Brooks et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AnP...526..294B/abstract). In parallel, abandoning dark matter entirely in favor of modified gravity faces historical hurdles; for instance, lensing and X-ray data for massive galaxy clusters like A1689 suggest that modified gravity models (e.g., MOND, MOG) struggle to explain dynamics without additional dark matter components, such as eV-mass neutrinos [Nieuwenhuizen et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ForPh..6500050N/abstract).

## Leading Particle Candidates: Sterile Neutrinos and Gravitinos

The theoretical framework of warm dark matter relies on particles with masses typically in the keV range. Because these particles decouple with non-negligible thermal velocities, their subsequent free-streaming suppresses the matter power spectrum on sub-galactic scales. The prominent particle candidates in the literature include the [[sterile-neutrino|Sterile neutrino]], the gravitino, and particles arising from specific portal mechanisms. 

**Sterile Neutrinos and the Shi-Fuller Mechanism**
The [[sterile-neutrino|Sterile neutrino]] is arguably the most intensely studied WDM candidate. Interest in a sterile neutrino with a mass of $m \approx 7$ keV surged following the detection of an unidentified X-ray spectral line at $3.51-3.56$ keV in galaxy clusters, which can be interpreted as the radiative decay of a $7.1$ keV sterile neutrino into an active neutrino and a photon [Abazajian et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvL.112p1303A/abstract), [de Vega et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1304.0759D/abstract). 

Standard non-resonant thermal production of such particles tends to violate structural bounds, requiring masses $\gtrsim 10$ keV. However, sterile neutrinos can be produced efficiently via the Shi-Fuller mechanism, a lepton-number-driven resonant Mikheyev-Smirnov-Wolfenstein (MSW) conversion of active neutrinos [Shi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999PhRvL..82.2832S/abstract). A unique physical feature of this resonant production is that the adiabaticity condition favors the conversion of lower-energy active neutrinos. Consequently, the resulting sterile neutrinos possess a non-thermal (colder) momentum distribution. This shifts their kinematics to a non-relativistic regime at an earlier cosmological epoch, significantly reducing their free-streaming length compared to a standard thermal relic of the same mass, and allowing them to preserve structures on the scales of dwarf galaxies [Shi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999PhRvL..82.2832S/abstract), [Abazajian et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvL.112p1303A/abstract).

**Gravitinos and Thermal Relics**
In supersymmetric extensions of the Standard Model, the gravitino frequently emerges as a WDM candidate. Gravitinos can be produced non-thermally, such as through the late decay of the inflaton field, yielding a population with a free-streaming length capable of suppressing small-scale power [Takahashi et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008PhLB..660..100T/abstract). 

For generic thermal relics that decouple while still relativistic, the [[relic-density|Relic density]] scales roughly proportionally to the particle mass. While literature historically associated thermal WDM with masses of order $1$ keV, modern observational constraints have heavily restricted this parameter space. Observations of the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest, Milky Way satellite counts, and recent high-redshift UV luminosity functions from the James Webb Space Telescope place stringent lower bounds on the thermal relic mass, requiring $m > 3.2 - 4.4$ keV [Liu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968...79L/abstract), [Dekker et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106l3026D/abstract).

**The Supersymmetric Higgs Portal**
Beyond standard thermal production and sterile neutrinos, alternative physics can also produce appropriate keV-scale candidates. For example, WDM can be generated via the supersymmetric Higgs portal mechanism. In these models, a suitable WDM particle with a mass in the $0.3-4$ keV range is naturally produced, with the mass inherently determined by the required relic density and the reheating temperature of the early Universe [McDonald et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvD..79j3523M/abstract).

## The Small-Scale Crisis of ΛCDM

While the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] achieves remarkable success in predicting the large-scale structure of the Universe, it faces persistent observational challenges on galactic and sub-galactic scales. Specifically, pure [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] $N$-body simulations exhibit significant discrepancies with observations of dwarf galaxies on length scales below $\sim 1$ Mpc and halo mass scales below $\sim 10^{11} M_\odot$ [Bullock et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55..343B/abstract). These discrepancies, collectively referred to as the small-scale crisis of CDM, are primarily categorized into three distinct but related issues: the missing satellites problem, the cusp-core problem, and the too-big-to-fail problem [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract).

The missing satellites problem describes the dramatic overprediction of low-mass subhalos in CDM simulations compared to the observed number of dwarf satellite galaxies in the Local Group. The cusp-core problem arises from the radial density profiles of these halos: CDM models predict a steeply rising central density (a "cusp"), whereas kinematic observations of dwarf galaxies strongly favor flattened central densities, or [[cored-dark-matter-halos|Cored dark matter halos]] [Moore et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.370..629M/abstract). Finally, the too-big-to-fail problem highlights that the most massive, dense subhalos produced in CDM simulations are kinematically incompatible with the observed brightest Milky Way satellites, which inhabit significantly less dense halos than predicted. For instance, detailed modeling shows that candidate host subhalos for the Fornax dwarf spheroidal galaxy in CDM tend to be more concentrated and less massive at $z=0$ compared to those in WDM and [[decaying-dark-matter|Decaying dark matter]] (DDM) models, further highlighting kinematic tensions [Wang et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457.4248W/abstract).

Furthermore, fine-grained details of hierarchical structure formation complicate the expected dark matter distribution. For instance, high-resolution models predict the formation of "prompt cusps" at initial density maxima. If these dense microstructures survive to the present day without being tidally disrupted, they could significantly boost the expected [[dark-matter-annihilation|Dark matter annihilation]] signal outside the centers of halos, adding another layer of complexity to resolving dark matter dynamics at small scales [Delos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...10..008D/abstract).

Warm dark matter was initially proposed as an elegant, purely dark-sector solution to the bulk of these issues. By introducing a non-negligible thermal velocity dispersion, WDM particles suppress the formation of [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]] via free-streaming. This physical mechanism inherently reduces the abundance of low-mass subhalos, directly addressing the missing satellites and too-big-to-fail problems by washing out density perturbations on sub-galactic scales. Additionally, phase-space packing constraints (governed by Liouville's theorem) impose a maximum phase-space density, theoretically preventing the formation of arbitrary density cusps and leading to flatter central profiles [Hogan et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph.12549H/abstract). 

However, other pure dark-sector solutions also exist. For instance, observations have provided evidence for a universal core-halo transition at $\sim 1.0$ kpc in Local Group dwarf galaxies, a structural feature that precisely matches predictions for [[bose-einstein-condensate-dark-matter|Bose-Einstein condensate dark matter]] (or "Fuzzy" dark matter) composed of ultra-light bosons with a mass of $\sim 1.5 \times 10^{-22}$ eV [Pozo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.110d3534P/abstract). Similarly, models invoking strong [[dark-matter-self-interaction-cross-section|dark matter self-interactions]] can efficiently flatten central density cusps, though if these interactions are too strong, they can result in unstable halos that are susceptible to dramatic particle ejection over a Hubble time [Hannestad et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph.12558H/abstract).

Ultimately, the literature demonstrates that resolving the small-scale crisis strictly through WDM is problematic due to a fundamental tension with large-scale cosmological constraints. Numerical simulations indicate that thermal WDM candidates with masses $m \gtrsim 1-2$ keV—necessary to satisfy constraints from the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest—produce central density cores of only $\sim 10-20$ pc in $10^{10} M_\odot$ halos, which is orders of magnitude smaller than the $\sim 1$ kpc cores observed in nature [Macciò et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.1105M/abstract). Conversely, lighter WDM particles ($m < 0.75$ keV) that could theoretically produce sufficiently large cores are unable to simultaneously reproduce the observed galaxy stellar mass function and the Tully-Fisher relation [Kang et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...767...22K/abstract). Furthermore, observations of individual unusual systems play a key role in testing the crisis and dark matter alternatives: for instance, the case study of the dark-matter-deficient ultra-diffuse galaxy NGC1052-DF2 provides a robust test for modified gravity theories (such as DHOST). Rather than being an obstacle, the kinematics of NGC1052-DF2 can be effectively matched by DHOST modified gravity theories, demonstrating that such dark-matter-deficient galaxies do not necessarily rule out alternatives to General Relativity [Laudato et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022EPJC...82..935L/abstract).

## Observational Constraints and the 3.5 keV Line

Searches for warm dark matter—particularly in the form of a [[sterile-neutrino|Sterile neutrino]]—often leverage X-ray observatories to look for the signature of [[decaying-dark-matter|Decaying dark matter]]. If a sterile neutrino has a mass in the keV range, it can undergo two-body radiative decay into an active neutrino and a photon:
$$ \nu_s \rightarrow \nu + \gamma $$
Conservation of momentum dictates that the emitted photon will manifest as a monochromatic X-ray emission line at an energy $E_\gamma \approx m_s/2$.

A major focal point in these multi-wavelength searches is the controversial unidentified X-ray spectral line observed between $3.51$ and $3.56$ keV in several local galaxy clusters and the Andromeda galaxy. If interpreted as a dark matter decay signature, this line corresponds to a sterile neutrino with a [[dark-matter-mass|Dark matter mass]] of $m_s \approx 7.1$ keV and a mixing angle of $\sin^2 2\theta \sim 7 \times 10^{-11}$ [de Vega et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1304.0759D/abstract). Such a particle, if produced via the resonant Shi-Fuller mechanism, could naturally yield the correct relic abundance and provide a mechanism to alleviate small-scale structural issues, potentially matching the Milky Way satellite abundance [Abazajian et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvL.112p1303A/abstract). Alternatively, the 3.5 keV line has also been modeled using "exciting dark matter" scenarios where [[heavier-dark-matter|Heavier dark matter]] upscatters and decays to produce the line [Finkbeiner et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..94h3002F/abstract). Historically, earlier direct X-ray constraints from the Andromeda galaxy placed strict limits on sterile neutrino warm dark matter, bounding its mass to $m_s < 3.5$ keV at 95% confidence [Watson et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvD..74c3009W/abstract).

But the dark matter origin of the 3.5 keV line remains highly ambiguous and contested. Deep sky observations with NuSTAR covering empty fields (such as COSMOS and ECDFS) aimed at the Milky Way halo have constrained decaying dark matter in the $6-40$ keV range. While NuSTAR data did exhibit lines near 3.51 keV, these have been attributed either to instrumental systematic effects (such as calibration uncertainties) or to conventional astrophysical origins [Neronov et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..94l3504N/abstract). Similarly, high-resolution Chandra observations of gas-poor dwarf spheroidal galaxies, such as Draco, reveal X-ray spectra that leave very little room for line emission, placing stringent constraints on the mass-mixing angle parameter space of sterile neutrinos [Riemer-Sorensen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0901.2569R/abstract). 

In addition to decay, observational bounds also constrain potential annihilation channels. Recent all-sky survey data from the SRG/ART-XC telescope have established strict limits on the velocity-independent [[dark-matter-annihilation|Dark matter annihilation]] cross-section for keV-scale mass dark matter in the Milky Way and Local Group dwarfs, further narrowing the available parameter space for interacting models in this mass regime [Zakharov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.110l3026Z/abstract). Similarly, broader searches for dark matter annihilation in the smooth Milky Way halo with the Fermi-LAT have excluded thermal dark matter masses below $\sim 70$ GeV annihilating to $b\bar{b}$, enforcing stringent bounds on heavier relics that complement the keV-scale limits [Chang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..98l3004C/abstract). At even higher energy scales, observatories like HAWC have evaluated sensitivity to [[multi-tev-dark-matter|Multi-TeV dark matter]] annihilation signals in rich environments such as the Andromeda galaxy (M31) and the Virgo cluster, further constraining massive relic scenarios that might act as heavy counterparts in mixed models [Abeysekara et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvD..90l2002A/abstract).

These multi-wavelength null results are compounded by severe structural constraints on the WDM particle mass. Subhalo modeling based on the abundance of Milky Way satellites severely restricts the available parameter space, ruling out thermal relic WDM lighter than 4.4 keV and generic sterile neutrinos lighter than 12 keV at 95% confidence [Dekker et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106l3026D/abstract). Recently, new thermal relic mass limits have pushed even higher; evaluating the kinematic influence of prompt dark matter cusps in the faintest Milky Way satellites, such as Triangulum II, has established that thermal relics must exceed $m_x > 5.8$ keV to remain consistent with observations [Delos et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251204156D/abstract). Conversely, other structural observations suggest potential systemic issues in standard CDM modeling; for example, observed galaxy cluster substructures have been found to be more efficient gravitational lenses than CDM simulations predict by more than an order of magnitude, indicating that small-scale structure within standard frameworks is not yet fully understood [Meneghetti et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Sci...369.1347M/abstract). Consequently, the lowest WDM masses capable of cleanly resolving the small-scale anomalies of the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] are in stark tension with both X-ray decay limits and modern structure formation bounds.

## Interplay Between Dark Matter Nature and Baryonic Feedback

A major motivation for considering warm dark matter and [[alternative-dark-matter-models|Alternative dark matter models]] is the failure of collisionless [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] to reproduce observed small-scale galactic structures. However, the inclusion of baryonic physics in modern hydrodynamical simulations has demonstrated that astrophysical processes can mimic or obscure the structural signatures of WDM, creating a profound model degeneracy [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract), [Brooks et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AnP...526..294B/abstract).

Baryons alter the underlying dark matter distribution through two competing mechanisms. Initially, gas cooling and baryonic condensation deepen the central gravitational potential, leading to the adiabatic contraction of the dark matter halo and yielding a steeper, more concentrated density profile [Tissera et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..922T/abstract), [Nuñez-Castiñeyra et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...05..012N/abstract). Conversely, violent potential fluctuations driven by stellar feedback and supernovae can transfer energy to the dark matter via purely gravitational interactions. This dynamical heating can flatten primordial density cusps into cores, effectively achieving the same structural end-state as WDM free-streaming [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract). Recent efforts within the pure CDM paradigm have even derived theoretical scaling relations between surface mass density and scale radius that seamlessly incorporate a universal cusp-to-core transition driven by these mechanisms [Kaneda et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240703614K/abstract). Furthermore, the inclusion of baryons systematically drives halos to become more spherical in their inner regions and shifts the dark matter velocity phase-space distributions toward greater isotropy [Tissera et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..922T/abstract), [Kelso et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JCAP...08..071K/abstract).

To circumvent the theoretical uncertainties of baryonic feedback, alternative dark-sector modifications have been proposed. For instance, specific self-interacting dark matter (SIDM) benchmarks utilizing a $10-100$ MeV mediator have been shown to reliably produce core sizes consistent with dwarf galaxy observations while remaining mathematically compatible with strict constraints derived from major cluster mergers like the Bullet Cluster [Kaplinghat et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1308.0618K/abstract). Conversely, complex models such as endothermic SIDM—where kinetic energy is lost during up-scattering to excited states—can actually increase central halo densities, a process that competes with the traditional coring effects of elastic scattering [O'Neil et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524..288O/abstract). Additionally, effective drag forces in SIDM models can lead to distinct observable signatures, such as stellar disk offsets, warps, and thickening in cluster environments, offering novel ways to constrain these interactions astrophysically [Secco et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...860...32S/abstract).

The interplay between the dark matter particle mass and baryonic feedback parameters produces a "Catch-22" when interpreting observational data. To systematically tackle this degeneracy, new numerical frameworks like the AIDA-TNG project have been introduced. This suite of high-resolution cosmological magnetohydrodynamic simulations is explicitly designed to disentangle the simultaneous effects of baryons and alternative dark matter models, such as WDM and SIDM, on galaxy properties [Despali et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250112439D/abstract). For instance, recent analyses utilizing the AIDA-TNG suite demonstrate that SIDM models consistently produce rounder and more isotropic halo inner regions compared to standard CDM, even after fully accounting for baryonic effects [Giocoli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215856G/abstract). Semi-empirical models targeting high-redshift galaxies have similarly shown that uncertainties in subgrid baryonic parameters are highly degenerate with dark matter free-streaming lengths. For instance, a CDM model with a low star formation efficiency ($\epsilon$) and a WDM model—such as a $7\text{ keV}$ [[sterile-neutrino|Sterile neutrino]]—with a high $\epsilon$ can provide equally good fits to the $z=4$ UV luminosity function and cosmic star formation rate densities [Khimey et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.4139K/abstract). In simulated systems with $M_{\rm halo} \approx 10^{10} M_\odot$, WDM halos form later and are intrinsically less centrally dense than their CDM counterparts. While this shallower potential reduces the absolute efficiency of supernova feedback, the combination of delayed halo formation and energy injection still leads to lower overall dark matter densities and a broader diversity of star formation histories [Bozek et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.4086B/abstract).

The literature remains strictly conflicted on whether stellar feedback alone is sufficient to resolve all discrepancies in [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]] without invoking a modification to the CDM paradigm. Observations of the faintest dwarf galaxies—where baryonic feedback is definitively insufficient to reshape the potential well—remain the most robust regime for breaking the degeneracy between complex astrophysics and the fundamental nature of the dark matter particle [Brooks et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AnP...526..294B/abstract).

## Large-Scale Structure and the Lyman-Alpha Forest

On scales larger than $\sim 100$ kpc, warm dark matter and [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] are dynamically indistinguishable and yield identical results for the large-scale matter power spectrum [Colombi et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...458....1C/abstract), [de Vega et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1304.0759D/abstract). In both scenarios, the horizon scale at matter-radiation equality introduces a characteristic scale in the transfer function, which dictates the shape of the power spectrum at late times. However, on sub-galactic scales, the non-negligible thermal velocities of WDM particles result in free-streaming that severely suppresses the growth of density perturbations. 

Remarkably, despite this severe suppression of low-mass halo formation in WDM scenarios, the macroscopic cosmic web remains extraordinarily robust. When utilizing self-abundance-matched catalogs, the resulting spatial distributions of galaxies in WDM and CDM models are virtually indistinguishable from each other via standard clustering statistics on large cosmological scales [Reed et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451.4413R/abstract).

The Lyman-$\alpha$ forest—the series of absorption lines in the spectra of distant quasars caused by neutral hydrogen in the intergalactic medium—serves as one of the most sensitive probes of the WDM free-streaming suppression at high redshifts. Because WDM washes out small-scale structure, it alters the distribution of neutral gas, directly impacting the Lyman-$\alpha$ flux power spectrum. By combining measurements of the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) Lyman-$\alpha$ forest at $2.2 < z < 4.2$ with cosmic microwave background (CMB) and galaxy clustering data, researchers have placed stringent lower bounds on the WDM particle mass. For early decoupled thermal relics, these combined probes set a lower limit of $m_{\rm WDM} \gtrsim 2$ keV at $2\sigma$ confidence [Viel et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvL..97g1301V/abstract). For a [[sterile-neutrino|Sterile neutrino]] produced via standard mechanisms, the limit is even more restrictive, requiring masses of $m_{\rm WDM} \gtrsim 10$ keV [Viel et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvL..97g1301V/abstract).

The literature presents explicit tensions regarding the optimal WDM particle mass. While early theoretical models and some recent astrophysical fits favor a particle mass of $2-3$ keV to alleviate small-scale anomalies in the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] [Lapi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...09..003L/abstract), masses at the lower end of this range are in marginal tension with the stringent constraints imposed by the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest. Conversely, WDM particles heavy enough to comfortably satisfy large-scale constraints behave so similarly to CDM that their free-streaming lengths are drastically reduced, preventing them from fully explaining the kpc-scale cores observed in dwarf galaxies [Macciò et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.1105M/abstract).

## WDM in the Epoch of Reionization

During the Epoch of Reionization, the free-streaming of warm dark matter particles suppresses small-scale density perturbations, delaying the gravitational collapse of the earliest, lowest-mass halos. Consequently, the formation of the first stars and galaxies is shifted to later cosmic epochs compared to the standard [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] paradigm [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...852..139V/abstract). 

High-resolution radiation-hydrodynamic simulations, such as the THESAN-HR suite, demonstrate that this suppression manifests predominantly as a deficit at the faint end of the rest-frame UV luminosity function and the low-mass end of the halo mass function at $z \gtrsim 6$ [Shen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.2835S/abstract). Despite this initial delay, strong non-linear effects enable WDM models to partially "catch up" to CDM reionization histories. In WDM scenarios, halos below the half-power mass exhibit enhanced specific star formation rates driven by increased gas abundances, reduced gas depletion times, and more compact galaxy sizes [Shen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.2835S/abstract). While these structural differences can theoretically help disentangle WDM signatures from purely baryonic uncertainties, the delayed reionization generally alters the expected Gunn-Peterson optical depth distribution, retaining a strong signature of the WDM free-streaming scale [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...852..139V/abstract).

Early high-redshift data from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have provided stringent new constraints on these models. Because JWST observations at $z > 9$ predominantly select the brightest and rarest galaxies, their physical properties and number densities remain broadly consistent with both CDM and WDM models, provided the thermal relic mass is $m_{\rm WDM} > 2 \text{ keV}$ [Maio et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...672A..71M/abstract). However, by combining JWST measurements of the UV luminosity function at $z = 9-14.5$ with archival Hubble Space Telescope (HST) blank-field surveys at $z = 4-8$, the allowable parameter space for WDM has narrowed. Simultaneous fitting of astrophysical parameters and the dark matter particle mass yields a new lower bound of $m_{\rm WDM} \geq 3.2 \text{ keV}$ at the $95\%$ confidence level [Liu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968...79L/abstract). This builds upon earlier pre-JWST analyses combining high-$z$ galaxy counts and Planck optical depth limits. Those analyses indicated that for standard cold dark matter and heavy WDM masses ($m_{\rm WDM} \gtrsim 3 \text{ keV}$), one must invoke extreme astrophysical assumptions to suppress star formation in halos below $M_{\rm H} \sim 10^8 M_\odot$ to avoid overproducing satellites while satisfying Planck constraints, whereas lighter WDM inherently suppresses structure formation at those scales without the need for extreme astrophysics [Lapi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...09..003L/abstract). 

To definitively differentiate between CDM and WDM—and to avoid degeneracies with assumptions regarding star formation efficiency—observations must probe the extremely faint end of the galaxy population. Current constraints on the free-streaming cutoff wavenumber $k_{\rm fs}$ derived from JWST rest-frame UV luminosities $L_{\rm UV}$ continue to test the boundaries of these [[dark-matter-models|Dark matter models]] [Hoeneisen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024IJAA...14...45H/abstract). Upcoming JWST imaging of deep gravitationally lensed fields holds the potential to uncover this faint, [[low-mass-dark-matter|Low-mass dark matter]]-hosted galaxy population and firmly establish the free-streaming length of dark matter in the early Universe [Shen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.2835S/abstract).

## Recent Advances: Mixed and Late-Forming Models

To reconcile the stringent lower bounds on warm dark matter particle mass with the persistent small-scale structural issues of the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], recent literature has increasingly explored hybrid scenarios. In these [[alternative-dark-matter-models|Alternative dark matter models]], the suppression of structure on sub-galactic scales is achieved either by mixing a warm component into a cold [[dark-sector|Dark sector]] or by dynamically altering the properties of dark matter over cosmic time.

### Mixed Dark Matter

In Mixed Dark Matter (MDM or CWDM) scenarios, the dark sector comprises both a dominant [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] component and a sub-dominant warm or hot component. This hybrid approach preserves the large-scale successes of CDM while suppressing the matter power spectrum on small scales. High-resolution $N$-body simulations indicate that the suppression of the matter power spectrum in mixed models is largely independent of other cosmological parameters at the 2% level for scales $k \lesssim 10\ h/\text{Mpc}$ up to redshift $z \le 3.5$ [Parimbelli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...12..044P/abstract). 

The parameter space for these models is defined by the mass of the warm component, $m_{\rm WDM}$, and its fractional contribution to the total dark matter abundance, $f_{\rm WDM}$. Recent analyses utilizing Milky Way satellite galaxy counts from the Dark Energy Survey and Pan-STARRS1 have placed stringent constraints on these parameters. While pure thermal relic WDM is excluded for masses below $6.6\text{ keV}$, mixed models permit significantly lighter WDM particles provided their fraction is capped. Specifically, observational constraints plateau at $f_{\rm WDM} \lesssim 0.45$ for $m_{\rm WDM} \lesssim 1.5\text{ keV}$ [Tan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240918917T/abstract). Similar fractional limits ($f_{\nu_s} \lesssim 0.45$) apply to a $7\text{ keV}$ [[sterile-neutrino|Sterile neutrino]] produced via the resonant Shi-Fuller mechanism [Tan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240918917T/abstract). 

Despite these bounds, mixed models remain highly compelling; $N$-body simulations demonstrate that a warm fraction of 20-60% can successfully reproduce the cumulative maximum circular velocity functions of satellite galaxies in the Milky Way and Andromeda without over-erasing structure [Harada et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JCAP...01..031H/abstract). To rigorously probe these scenarios with upcoming galaxy surveys, the Effective Field Theory of Large Scale Structure has recently been extended to treat the non-cold component as a perfect fluid with a finite sound speed [Verdiani et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250708792V/abstract). Other mixed scenarios introduce more complex dark sector physics. For instance, atomic dark matter (ADM) introduces a dissipative subcomponent that can cool and condense to form a rotating "dark disk" aligned with the baryonic stellar disk, directly impacting galactic star formation histories and structural evolution [Roy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954L..40R/abstract).

### Late-Forming and Dynamic Dark Matter

A distinct theoretical pathway to achieving WDM-like small-scale suppression is [[late-forming-dark-matter|Late-forming dark matter]] (LFDM). In these models, dark matter originates as highly relativistic [[dark-radiation|Dark radiation]] that transitions into a cold, non-relativistic state at a characteristic redshift $z_T$, which falls between Big Bang Nucleosynthesis and the epoch of matter-radiation equality. This phase transition dynamically suppresses the growth of linear matter perturbations and imprints oscillatory signatures on small scales, analogous to dark acoustic damping [Das et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.103d3517D/abstract).

The cutoff scale in the linear matter power spectrum is determined by the transition redshift $z_T$. Initial constraints derived from the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest required the transition to occur at $z_T > 9 \times 10^5$ if the entirety of the dark matter is late-forming [Sarkar et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...03..004S/abstract). However, more recent forward-modeling of the Milky Way satellite population has significantly tightened this bound. For neutrinolike LFDM with strong self-interactions, the abundance of observed satellite galaxies demands that $z_T > 5.5 \times 10^6$ at 95% confidence [Das et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.103d3517D/abstract). This stringent limit implies that if dark matter is created by a transition from [[dark-radiation|Dark radiation]], the bulk of its formation must be complete no later than approximately one week after the Big Bang.

Other dynamic or decaying scenarios can emulate these structural benefits. For example, meta-cold dark matter (mCDM) posits a hybrid model where dark matter originates from late decays ($z < 1000$). This mechanism naturally produces the observable constant-density cores required by small-scale observations while safely maintaining a standard CDM-like power spectrum on large spatial scales [Strigari et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007PhRvD..75f1303S/abstract). Furthermore, interacting quintessence models, where dark energy decays into dark matter, induce a damping effect on the matter power spectrum that mathematically mimics the free-streaming signature of ultrarelativistic neutrinos, providing yet another novel avenue to bridge small-scale discrepancies [Olivares et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvD..74d3521O/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

A persistent open question in astrophysics is whether the small-scale challenges of the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] strictly require a departure from [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]], or if they can be resolved entirely by baryonic physics. Because both warm dark matter and baryonic feedback suppress the observable subhalo abundance and alter inner halo profiles, a significant degeneracy exists between the underlying dark matter particle physics and subgrid baryonic processes [Tissera et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..922T/abstract), [Khimey et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.4139K/abstract).

Upcoming and current observational facilities offer promising avenues to break this degeneracy. Future JWST observations must probe the faint end of the rest-frame UV luminosity function (e.g., $M_{\text{UV}} \approx -12$) and the precise timing of reionization, as WDM significantly delays the formation of the first stars in low-mass halos [Lapi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...09..003L/abstract), [Liu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968...79L/abstract). On macroscopic scales, upcoming wide-field surveys from the Euclid mission are forecasted to significantly improve sensitivity to mixed dark matter and decaying models, potentially tightening current parameter bounds by up to two orders of magnitude [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240618274E/abstract).

In addition to luminous tracers, strong gravitational lensing provides a purely gravitational method to measure the subhalo abundance, bypassing the uncertainties of whether these low-mass subhalos host visible galaxies. The power spectrum of dark matter substructure in lenses, as well as flux ratio statistics from lensed quasars, are highly sensitive to the free-streaming cutoff in the matter power spectrum. This allows for stringent, astrophysics-independent limits on WDM models [Diaz Rivero et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..97b3001D/abstract), [Zelko et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvL.129s1301Z/abstract). Anomalous lensing events can also point toward new physics; for example, the recent discovery of a highly concentrated dark subhalo in the strong lens SDSS J0946+1006 features a remarkably steep density profile, which may indicate dark matter self-interactions leading to gravothermal collapse [Enzi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108565E/abstract). Furthermore, on cluster scales, macroscopic kinematic signatures such as the 'wobbling' of the brightest cluster galaxy (BCG) inside cored SIDM halos—as described by simulations like the DARKSKIES suite—provide unique dynamical tests not present in collisionless CDM frameworks [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919842H/abstract).

Finally, the microphysical nature of the WDM particle remains unresolved. The most prominent candidate is the [[sterile-neutrino|Sterile neutrino]], a model motivated in part by the detection of an unidentified X-ray spectral line at $\sim 3.5 \text{ keV}$ in several galaxy clusters and the Andromeda galaxy [Abazajian et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvL.112p1303A/abstract). However, the origin of this line is hotly debated; the literature conflicts over whether it constitutes a genuine signature of dark matter decay or is merely the result of astrophysical backgrounds and instrumental systematics [Neronov et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..94l3504N/abstract). Breaking these deadlocks will ultimately require complementary approaches, merging high-resolution kinematic surveys, deep high-redshift observations, and new results from both indirect and direct detection via upcoming [[dark-matter-search-experiments|Dark matter search experiments]]. In these indirect searches, environmental factors must be carefully modeled; for example, SIDM is known to produce shallower density spikes ($r^{-7/4}$) around supermassive black holes compared to CDM, which significantly relaxes indirect detection constraints derived from objects like the M87 galaxy or the Draco dwarf spheroidal [Alvarez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104d3013A/abstract). Additionally, extreme astrophysical environments offer powerful, entirely novel laboratories; for instance, the accumulation of self-interacting dark matter in neutron stars can serve as a high-density probe capable of constraining dark matter self-interaction cross-sections many orders of magnitude more stringently than bounds derived from the Bullet Cluster [Güver et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014JCAP...05..013G/abstract). Similarly, the requirement that energy injection from core-collapse supernova neutrinos must not excessively flatten dark matter cusps has been utilized to place stringent upper limits on the fundamental neutrino-dark matter cross-section [Heston et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.110b3004H/abstract). Additionally, novel techniques are continuously emerging; for instance, if the dark sector includes an ultralight axion component, the oscillating gravitational potential of its solitonic cores produces a time dilation of pulsar pulse arrival times that may be detectable by pulsar timing arrays [de Martino et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Galax...6...10D/abstract).
