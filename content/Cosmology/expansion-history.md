## Article

In [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]], the **expansion history** of the [[universe|Universe]] describes the evolution of the cosmic scale factor $a(t)$ over time. Rooted in General Relativity, this dynamic space-time framework dictates how distances between co-moving observers increase as the universe evolves from the hot [[big-bang|Big Bang]] to the present day [Kirshner et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PNAS..101....8K/abstract). The fundamental kinematics of this expansion are governed by the [[friedmann-equations|Friedmann equations]], which directly relate the macroscopic [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]]—quantified by the Hubble parameter $H(t)$—to the total [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]] and spatial curvature. Because different components of the cosmic [[energy-content|Energy content]] (such as relativistic radiation, non-relativistic matter, and vacuum energy) dilute at different rates as space expands, the universe naturally transitions through distinct dynamical epochs that fundamentally shape its physical properties [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

The overarching [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]] is characterized by these successive energy transitions. Following the [[early-universe|Early universe]], the cosmos underwent a rapid, decelerating expansion during the [[radiation-dominated-era|Radiation-dominated era]] [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract). As the universe cooled and expanded, it passed through [[matter-radiation-equality|Matter-radiation equality]] to enter a matter-dominated epoch. This phase was critical for the assembly of the cosmic web: the background expansion was slow enough to allow primordial [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]] to undergo steady gravitational collapse. This delicate balance between local gravitational attraction and the kinematic "Hubble drag" of expanding space dictates the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] of structures, inextricably linking the background expansion history to the timeline of [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|Galaxy and Universe evolution]]. Eventually, as the [[mass-density|Mass density]] diluted further, the universe entered its current phase of [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]], a phenomenon widely attributed to a cosmological constant or dark energy. 

This article explores the theoretical frameworks and historical observational milestones that established the expanding universe paradigm, detailing the physical mechanisms and scaling relations that define each cosmic epoch. It reviews how independent geometric and kinematic probes in [[cosmography|Cosmography]]—such as Type Ia supernovae standard candles and Baryon Acoustic Oscillation standard rulers—are utilized to precisely map the expansion history. Finally, the text examines how the rigid theoretical relationship between expansion and structure growth is used to distinguish between material dark energy models and [[modified-gravity-theories|Modified Gravity Theories]], highlighting modern open questions such as the unresolved [[hubble-expansion-and-tension|Hubble Expansion and Tension]] and the future prospect of measuring the expansion rate in real-time via the [[redshift|Redshift]] drift of distant galaxies.

## Theoretical Framework and Friedmann Dynamics

The theoretical foundation of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]] rests upon the cosmological principle, which posits that the [[universe|Universe]] is homogeneous and isotropic on large scales. Within the framework of General Relativity, this spatial geometry is described by the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric, which serves as the foundational mathematical basis for modeling complex cosmological observables, including acoustic peaks, gravitational lensing, and the Einstein-Boltzmann equations governing perturbations [Dodelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020moco.book.....D/abstract). The dynamic evolution of this space-time is entirely characterized by the scale factor $a(t)$, which parameterizes the relative expansion of space, and the spatial curvature $K$. The time derivative of the scale factor defines the [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]], or Hubble parameter, $H(t) \equiv \dot{a}/a$. The foundational distance-redshift relation originally established by Hubble in 1929 constitutes the primary observational basis for this expanding framework [Kirshner et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PNAS..101....8K/abstract).

The evolution of $a(t)$ is governed by the [[friedmann-equations|Friedmann equations]], which relate the geometry of the universe to its [[energy-content|Energy content]]. The first Friedmann equation dictates the expansion rate in terms of the total [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]], $\rho$:

$$ H^2 = \left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - \frac{K c^2}{a^2} + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3} $$

where $G$ is the gravitational constant, $c$ is the speed of light, and $\Lambda$ is the cosmological constant.

In the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm, the total energy density comprises distinct fluid components that evolve differently as the universe expands. Normalizing the scale factor such that $a(t_0) = 1$ today, these components scale predictably: non-relativistic matter $\rho_m \propto a^{-3}$, radiation $\rho_r \propto a^{-4}$, and vacuum energy $\rho_\Lambda \propto a^0$. Using the dimensionless [[omega-parameters|Omega parameters]] ($\Omega_i \equiv \rho_{i,0} / \rho_{\rm crit}$), the Friedmann equation is conventionally rewritten to describe the expansion history as a function of [[redshift|Redshift]] $z$, where $a = (1+z)^{-1}$:

$$ H^2(z) = H_0^2 \left[ \Omega_{r,0}(1+z)^4 + \Omega_{m,0}(1+z)^3 + \Omega_{k,0}(1+z)^2 + \Omega_{\Lambda,0} \right] $$

This formulation elegantly maps out the [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]]: an early [[radiation-dominated-era|Radiation-dominated era]] gives way (at [[matter-radiation-equality|Matter-radiation equality]]) to a matter-dominated epoch, and finally transitions to a vacuum-dominated phase in the late universe. Historically, the critical developments in modeling this temperature-density history relied heavily on analyzing Friedmann cosmological models containing both matter and radiation components simultaneously [Jacobs et al. (1967)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1967Natur.215.1156J/abstract).

The second Friedmann (or acceleration) equation reveals how the pressure $P$ of these components drives the change in the expansion rate:

$$ \frac{\ddot{a}}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho + \frac{3P}{c^2}\right) + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3} $$

The competition between attractive gravity ($\rho, P > 0$) and the repulsive cosmological constant dictates whether the cosmic expansion decelerates or accelerates. This dynamic is quantified by the deceleration parameter $q(t) = -\ddot{a}a/\dot{a}^2$. High-precision distance measurements from Type Ia supernovae, baryon acoustic oscillations, and the cosmic microwave background consistently demonstrate that $q(t)$ transitioned from positive to negative at a redshift of $z \approx 0.6$, indicating that the [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]] is occurring [Zhu et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...602...12Z/abstract), [Blake et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.1725B/abstract).

While a static cosmological constant ($\Lambda$) provides a robust fit to current observational data, the literature reflects an active debate over the exact physical mechanism driving this acceleration. Theoretical alternatives often challenge standard Friedmann dynamics directly. Some models introduce a dynamic dark energy fluid with a time-varying equation of state. Others consider scenarios where the cosmological constant varies with the expansion factor, the Hubble constant, or the matter density, dynamic changes which can significantly alter the theoretical size of the cosmic particle horizon [Öztaş et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.2228O/abstract). Furthermore, at smaller scales, alternative inhomogeneous frameworks like Swiss-cheese models propose the existence of small anti-gravity sources within galaxies, potentially explaining cosmic acceleration via an infrared fixed point for the cosmological constant [Mitra et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Univ....7..263M/abstract). In fact, exact inhomogeneous solutions to Einstein's equations provide alternative frameworks for describing structures like galaxy clusters and cosmic voids without relying on standard macroscopic approximations [Krasinski et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997icm..book.....K/abstract). To bridge the gap between local structures and global cosmology in these models, researchers have utilized McVittie spacetime scalar invariants (such as the Ricci, Weyl, and Kretschmann scalars) to carefully define the physical scale where dark energy dominance begins [Benisty et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250403009B/abstract). Alternatively, models invoke [[modified-gravity-theories|Modified Gravity Theories]]—such as Modified Gravity (MOG) [Moffat et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0710.0364M/abstract) or [[cardassian-models|Cardassian models]] [Zhu et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...585...52Z/abstract)—which replace the dark energy component with a phenomenologically modified Friedmann equation [Frusciante et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhR...857....1F/abstract). Despite these ongoing theoretical critiques, standard Friedmann dynamics remain the indispensable cornerstone for modeling the macroscopic evolution of the universe.

## Historical Development of the Expansion Paradigm

The formulation of [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]] began with the realization that general relativity inherently describes a dynamic space-time. Following the publication of his field equations, Albert Einstein favored a static model of the [[universe|Universe]] and introduced a cosmological constant, $\Lambda$, to exactly counterbalance gravitational attraction. However, during the 1920s, Alexander Friedmann and Georges Lemaître independently solved the [[friedmann-equations|Friedmann equations]] to show that an isotropic and homogeneous universe naturally undergoes metric expansion or contraction.

In 1929, Edwin Hubble provided the empirical foundation for the expanding universe by demonstrating that the recession velocities of galaxies are linearly proportional to their distances [Kirshner et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PNAS..101....8K/abstract). This relationship, now known as Hubble's Law, fundamentally redefined our understanding of the cosmic expansion history. Recognizing that the universe was not static, Einstein famously dismissed his original, finely tuned introduction of the cosmological constant as his "biggest blunder."

Following Hubble's discovery, a major mid-century theoretical divide emerged regarding the physical evolution of the cosmos. Proponents of the "perfect cosmological principle" developed the Steady State cosmology, which posited that the continuous creation of matter maintained a constant mean [[mass-density|Mass density]] as the universe expanded. However, the subsequent discovery of the perfectly thermal cosmic microwave background (CMB) and observations of evolving galaxy populations presented insurmountable contradictions for Steady State models. This definitively established the Hot [[big-bang|Big Bang]] as the prevailing cosmological paradigm [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract).

For decades thereafter, the consensus held that the universe was matter-dominated and that its expansion must be slowing down due to the attractive force of gravity. In this framework, the cosmic deceleration parameter, defined as $q_0 = -\ddot{a}a/\dot{a}^2$ (where $a$ is the scale factor), was assumed to be strictly positive ($q_0 > 0$) [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). This paradigm was abruptly overturned in 1998 when independent teams utilized the luminosity distances of Type Ia supernovae to measure the expansion rate at high redshifts. They discovered that the expansion of the universe is currently accelerating, corresponding to a negative deceleration parameter ($q_0 < 0$) [Tegmark et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhRvD..66j3507T/abstract), [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

This [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]] necessitated the re-introduction of a fluid with strong negative pressure, reminiscent of Einstein's $\Lambda$, which today dominates the total [[energy-content|Energy content]] of the universe [Bludman et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009JPhCS.161a2026B/abstract). While the resulting $\Lambda$CDM framework is robustly supported by a diverse array of precision cosmological probes [Aubourg et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..92l3516A/abstract), the literature maintains a small minority of [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]]. Historically, these have included "tired-light" hypotheses [Laviolette et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...301..544L/abstract) that attempt to explain redshift without metric space expansion, as well as modified redshift models based on the spatial distribution of luminous matter that attempt to account for the apparent acceleration without invoking dark energy [Nyagisera et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Astro...3...43N/abstract). Though such static frameworks consistently fail modern observational tests, the transition from a static cosmos to an accelerating, expanding spacetime continues to represent one of the most profound observational triumphs in astrophysics.

## Cosmic Epochs and Energy Transitions

The [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]] of the [[universe|Universe]] is defined by successive epochs in which different components dominate the total [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]]. According to the [[friedmann-equations|Friedmann equations]], the [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] $H(t) \equiv \dot{a}/a$ of a spatially flat universe is determined by the sum of these energy densities:
$$H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3} \left( \rho_r + \rho_m + \rho_\Lambda \right)$$
where $\rho_r$, $\rho_m$, and $\rho_\Lambda$ represent the densities of radiation, non-relativistic matter, and dark energy, respectively. Because these components scale differently as the scale factor $a(t)$ increases, the universe experiences distinct dynamical phases [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract).

**Radiation-Dominated Era**
The [[early-universe|Early universe]] was governed by a dense, thermal bath of relativistic particles, characterizing the [[radiation-dominated-era|Radiation-dominated era]]. The radiation energy density scales as $\rho_r \propto a^{-4}$, which accounts for both the volumetric expansion ($\propto a^{-3}$) and the [[gravitational-redshift|Gravitational redshift]] of photon energies ($\propto a^{-1}$). During this epoch, the scale factor evolves as $a(t) \propto t^{1/2}$ [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). The extreme temperatures and densities of this phase are the hallmark of the hot [[big-bang|Big Bang]] model, which establishes the thermal history and drives the primordial nuclear physics of the cosmos [Liddle et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..126...31L/abstract), [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract).

**Matter-Dominated Epoch**
Because radiation density dilutes more rapidly than the non-relativistic [[mass-density|Mass density]], the expansion eventually reached a transition point known as [[matter-radiation-equality|Matter-radiation equality]] ($z_{\rm eq}$). This marked the onset of the matter-dominated epoch. Non-relativistic matter density—comprising both baryonic and cold dark matter—scales strictly with volume, $\rho_m \propto a^{-3}$, leading to an expansion rate governed by $a(t) \propto t^{2/3}$. Shortly after $z_{\rm eq}$, at a temperature of $T \sim 4000$ K (corresponding to a cosmic time of $t \sim 2 \times 10^5$ years), the universe underwent recombination. Free electrons bound to nuclei, causing the cosmic plasma to become transparent and allowing photons to free-stream [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Subsequently, the universe underwent reionization as the first luminous sources emerged; the timeline of this epoch is increasingly reconstructed using complementary probes such as the global 21 cm signal and the kinetic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (kSZ) effect [Bégin et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.105h3503B/abstract). The matter-dominated epoch is critical for cosmic structure formation: without the overwhelming damping effect of radiation pressure, [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]] were able to undergo steady, power-law growth via gravitational instability [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

**Dark Energy Dominance**
In the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]], the continuing expansion causes the matter density to fall below the density of dark energy (typically modeled as a cosmological constant $\Lambda$). For a cosmological constant, the energy density remains uniform as space expands, $\rho_\Lambda \propto a^0$. As $\rho_m$ dilutes, dark energy inevitably comes to dominate the cosmic energy content [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract). As observations confirm, the universe transitioned from a decelerating phase to [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]] at a [[redshift|Redshift]] of $z \approx 0.6$ [Zhu et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...602...12Z/abstract). Deep into this era of dark energy dominance, the scale factor asymptotically approaches an exponential growth phase, $a(t) \propto \exp(\Omega_{\Lambda,0}^{1/2} H_0 t)$, unretarded by the attractive force of matter gravity [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

## Observational Probes and Cosmography

[[cosmography|Cosmography]] seeks to map the universe's expansion history and scale factor $a(t)$ directly from observations using geometric and kinematic tests, largely independent of assumed gravitational dynamics. These observational probes tightly constrain the [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] $H(z) = \dot{a}/a$ and the deceleration parameter $q(z)$, which evaluates whether the expansion is accelerating or decelerating [Bolotin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150200811B/abstract):
$$ q(z) = -\frac{\ddot{a}a}{\dot{a}^2} $$

The most prominent kinematic probe utilizes Type Ia supernovae as standard candles. By mapping the luminosity distance as a function of [[redshift|Redshift]], observers in 1998 demonstrated that the present-day universe is undergoing [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]] ($q_0 < 0$). Model-independent reconstructions of this supernova distance-redshift relation demonstrate that the expansion history curve possesses a shape that strictly requires a dark energy component or [[modified-gravity-theories|Modified Gravity Theories]] to explain [Tegmark et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhRvD..66j3507T/abstract). In addition to these geometric distances, cosmographers combine galaxy ages with Type Ia supernovae to constrain spatial curvature in a model-independent way by directly comparing luminosity distance and [[look-back-time|Look-back time]]. Weak lensing has also emerged as a powerful tool to constrain cosmology, although weak lensing constraints on dark energy remain notably sensitive to survey parameters like redshift depth and systematic uncertainties in shear measurement [Das et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JCAP...11..011D/abstract).

To complement standard candles, cosmologists employ standard rulers, the most robust being Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO). BAO imprints a characteristic physical scale—the sound horizon at the recombination epoch—onto the spatial distribution of galaxies. By measuring the BAO scale in both transverse and line-of-sight directions, galaxy surveys extract the angular diameter distance and the Hubble parameter $H(z)$. High-precision measurements from the SDSS-III Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey (BOSS), utilizing both large-scale galaxy clustering and the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest, provide a high-confidence detection of dark energy. When combined with [[big-bang|Big Bang]] relic radiation (CMB) and supernovae, BAO anchors an "inverse distance ladder" that yields a highly precise expansion rate of $H_0 = 67.3 \pm 1.1 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$ [Aubourg et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..92l3516A/abstract). However, the literature notes a moderate $2-2.5\sigma$ tension between Lyman-$\alpha$ forest BAO measurements and flat $\Lambda$CDM model predictions [Aubourg et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..92l3516A/abstract).

A purely geometric probe of the expansion history is the Alcock-Paczynski test. This technique evaluates the ratio of the observed angular size to the radial redshift size of an object or clustering pattern known to be statistically isotropic [Lopez-Corredoira et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ffp..confE..85L/abstract). Because assuming an incorrect expansion history distorts standard spheres into ellipsoids in redshift space, this test constrains the product of the angular diameter distance and $H(z)$. Applying the Alcock-Paczynski test to large-scale clustering in the WiggleZ Dark Energy Survey yielded a robust, non-parametric measurement of $H(z)$ with $10-15\%$ precision across redshifts $0.1 < z < 0.9$, directly demonstrating cosmic acceleration independent of specific dark energy models [Blake et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.1725B/abstract).

Alternative standard rulers leverage the topological properties of the [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]] within the large-scale structure. For instance, the two-dimensional genus of the galaxy distribution acts as a standard ruler that is intrinsically insensitive to non-linear gravitational evolution [Park et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...715L.185P/abstract). Beyond the genus, researchers have extended the use of Minkowski Tensors to analyze anisotropic signals in redshift space, providing a powerful tool for connecting non-Gaussian density fields to the underlying cosmological parameters [Appleby et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250710091A/abstract). Genus amplitude measurements have independently constrained the dark energy equation of state to $w_{de} = -1.05_{-0.12}^{+0.13}$ and the [[non-relativistic-matter-density-parameter|Non-relativistic matter density parameter]] to $\Omega_m = 0.303 \pm 0.036$ [Appleby et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...907...75A/abstract).

Collectively, these cosmographic probes strongly favor an accelerating, spatially flat $\Lambda$CDM cosmology. Nonetheless, when these weakly model-dependent geometric measurements of the intermediate universe are compared against local distance ladder measurements, there remains a persistent and unresolved [[hubble-expansion-and-tension|Hubble Expansion and Tension]] [Joudaki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..97l3501J/abstract).

## Expansion History and the Growth of Structure

The formation of galaxies and large-scale structure is inextricably linked to the background expansion history of the universe. In the standard cosmological paradigm, structure forms from small primordial [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]] seeded in the [[early-universe|Early universe]]. The amplification of these initial fluctuations is governed by gravitational instability, a process wherein overdense regions attract surrounding matter and become increasingly dense. 

In a static universe, gravitational instability would cause the density contrast $\delta = \delta\rho/\rho$ to grow exponentially. However, in an expanding [[universe|Universe]], the kinematic expansion of space acts as a damping term on the accretion flows, an effect commonly referred to as Hubble drag. Consequently, the competition between gravitational attraction and Hubble drag dictates that the growth rate is typically a power law in time, $\delta \propto t^\alpha$, rather than an exponential run-away process [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

In the linear regime ($\delta \ll 1$), the evolution of sub-horizon cold dark matter perturbations is described by the [[linear-growth-factor|Linear growth factor]] $D(a)$, defined such that $\delta(a) \propto D(a)$. During the matter-dominated epoch (well-approximated as an Einstein-de Sitter universe), the expansion rate allows the linear growth factor to scale proportionally with the scale factor, $D(a) \propto a$. Correspondingly, the Newtonian gravitational potential $\Phi \propto D(a)/a$ remains constant [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). However, as the universe transitions into an epoch dominated by dark energy or spatial curvature, the accelerated expansion rate enhances the Hubble drag. This suppresses the growth of structure, causing $D(a)$ to grow more slowly than $a$ and the gravitational potentials to decay over time. 

This delicate interplay between expansion and collapse shapes the cosmic web, separating the density field into distinct dynamical regimes. On the largest scales, fluctuations remain linear; on intermediate scales, they enter the [[quasi-linear-regime|Quasi-linear regime]] and form filaments and pancakes; and on small scales, they undergo [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]] to form bound, virialized dark matter halos. To rigorously model these intricate dynamics and connect them to galaxy survey data, theoretical efforts heavily utilize 1-loop Standard Perturbation Theory and Effective Field Theory to accurately predict the matter power spectrum and redshift space multipoles [Gomez-Navarro et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240216239G/abstract).

Because General Relativity predicts a rigid, specific relationship between the background expansion history $H(z)$ and the growth of large-scale structure, observations of structure formation serve as a stringent test of the underlying cosmological model. The rate of structure formation is often parameterized by the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] $f(a) = d \ln D / d \ln a$. This is frequently approximated using the [[growth-index|Growth index]] $\gamma$, such that $f \simeq \Omega_m(a)^\gamma$ [Huterer et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&ARv..31....2H/abstract). In the standard $\Lambda$CDM model, $\gamma \approx 0.55$ [Joudaki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..97l3501J/abstract). Measurements of $f$ (often combined with the amplitude of mass fluctuations as $f\sigma_8$) from galaxy clustering and weak lensing can be used to distinguish a cosmological constant from dynamic [[modified-gravity-theories|Modified Gravity Theories]] or coupled dark matter-dark energy scenarios. In these alternative models, the growth of structure can deviate from the standard prediction even if they possess identical background expansion histories [Bean et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvD..81h3534B/abstract), [Lopez Honorez et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010JCAP...09..029L/abstract). Additionally, the mean pairwise streaming velocities of galaxy clusters—measured via the kinetic Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect—offer a promising future test to distinguish between General Relativity and modified gravity models by mapping the velocity field of cosmic structures [Kosowsky et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvD..80f2003K/abstract).

While the standard expansion history accurately predicts the broad strokes of structure formation, it faces localized tensions. For instance, comparing the theoretical mass accretion histories driven by the background cosmology against high-redshift observations occasionally reveals discrepancies, such as the over-prediction of stellar mass densities at $z \sim 3$, sometimes termed the "missing galaxy problem" [Nagamine et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...610...45N/abstract). Resolving such discrepancies remains a focus of ongoing research, bridging the gap between global expansion dynamics and local astrophysical feedback.

## Dark Energy vs. Modified Gravity

The phenomenon of [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]] requires either the existence of a novel, negative-pressure fluid—broadly termed dark energy—or a fundamental breakdown of General Relativity on cosmological scales, often referred to as Dark Gravity or [[modified-gravity-theories|Modified Gravity Theories]] [Bludman et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009JPhCS.161a2026B/abstract). A comprehensive review of the theoretical and phenomenological analysis of modified gravity, including inflation-based models, underscores the sheer diversity of proposed theoretical extensions designed to match modern observational constraints [Avelino et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Symm....8...70A/abstract).

In material dark energy scenarios, the acceleration is driven by a substance with an equation of state $w \equiv P/\rho < -1/3$. Beyond the static cosmological constant ($\Lambda$), dynamical frameworks such as [[quintessence-models|Quintessence models]] introduce a time-evolving scalar field. Further variations include the [[oscillating-dark-energy-model|Oscillating dark energy model]], which attempts to resolve the cosmological coincidence problem by allowing the dark energy density to periodically track the matter density. However, the observable imprints of such oscillations are often smoothed out in integrals over the expansion history, making them observationally difficult to distinguish from a standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology [Pace et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.1186P/abstract). Other material approaches include holographic dark energy models, which allow for non-gravitational interactions between the dark sectors to maintain a finite ratio of matter to dark energy throughout the expansion [Durán et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvD..83b3504D/abstract).

Alternatively, geometric modifications alter the [[friedmann-equations|Friedmann equations]] without invoking exotic fluids. For example, [[cardassian-models|Cardassian models]] modify the expansion dynamics directly to produce acceleration, but they have historically struggled to reconcile their required parameter space with observational constraints on the non-relativistic [[mass-density|Mass density]] parameter $\Omega_m$ [Zhu et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...585...52Z/abstract). Alternatively, Debye Gravitational Theory (DGT) offers a novel geometric modification that can explain phenomena such as falling galaxy rotation curves at high redshifts ($z > 0.77$) without needing dark matter [Navia et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180405914N/abstract). More broadly, scalar-tensor theories introduce additional degrees of freedom that couple to gravity. These modifications produce distinct signatures in the non-linear regime, which can be constrained using the galaxy bispectrum and second-order perturbation indices [Yamauchi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PhRvD..96l3516Y/abstract). 

A fundamental challenge in modern cosmology is that purely kinematic measurements of the background [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] $H(z)$ and the deceleration parameter $q(z)$ cannot unambiguously distinguish between material dark energy and modified gravity [Bludman et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007astro.ph..2085B/abstract). Because both frameworks can be engineered to yield identical expansion histories, breaking this degeneracy requires probing the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] and the evolution of [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]]. While a coupled dark fluid might mimic a modified geometry at the background level, modifications to gravity generally alter the Poisson equation and introduce a "slip" between the two Newtonian [[gravitational-potentials|Gravitational potentials]]. This leads to divergent, scale-dependent predictions for the [[growth-function|Growth function]] and the assembly of large-scale structure [Bean et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvD..81h3534B/abstract), [Lopez Honorez et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010JCAP...09..029L/abstract).

To systematically navigate this theoretical landscape, researchers increasingly rely on the effective field theory of dark energy. This Lagrangian-based formalism captures both material fluids and geometric modifications, classifying the myriad of proposed models according to their non-minimal couplings to gravity and the specific imprints they leave on [[cosmological-perturbation-theory|Cosmological Perturbation Theory]] [Frusciante et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhR...857....1F/abstract). By isolating these perturbation-level deviations, effective field theory provides a rigorous framework for using future galaxy surveys to finally distinguish between a dark fluid and modified gravity. Furthermore, the expanding space paradigm itself faces conceptual critiques, including arguments that the 'expansion of space' is essentially an unobservable concept when analyzed using radar ranging within FLRW models [Chodorowski et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.378..239C/abstract), alongside perceived violations of energy conservation in local comoving volumes within general relativity [Baryshev et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AIPC..822...23B/abstract). Radical alternatives to both dark components and standard modified gravity have even hypothesized the existence of alternative time-flows for observers inside the universe, which could theoretically mimic velocity dispersions and cosmic acceleration without requiring dark matter or dark energy [Magain et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.1110M/abstract).

## Impact on Galaxy Evolution and Star Formation

The interplay between the cosmic expansion rate and gravitational instability fundamentally governs [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|Galaxy and Universe evolution]]. In a static universe, density perturbations would grow exponentially; however, in an expanding [[universe|Universe]], the growth of structures is suppressed by Hubble drag. Consequently, in the linear regime, the density contrast $\delta \rho / \rho$ evolves as a power law in cosmic time $t$:
$$ \frac{\delta \rho}{\rho} \propto t^\alpha $$
where $\alpha > 0$ depends on the dominant energy component [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). The late-time accelerated expansion, driven by dark energy, further suppresses the [[linear-growth-factor|Linear growth factor]], freezing the growth of large-scale gravitational potentials.

The assembly of dark matter halos acts as the primary driver for galaxy formation. The mass accretion history of these cold dark matter halos follows a universal profile whose scaling parameters are tightly linked to the background expansion and cosmology [van den Bosch et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.331...98V/abstract). For massive galaxies, both secular accretion and the merger history contribute approximately equally to mass assembly between $z=1$ and $z=3$ [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract). Additionally, environmental feedback played a crucial role in shaping luminous evolution; for example, radio galaxy lobes expanding into the cosmic web are thought to have triggered extensive star formation during the quasar era around $z \sim 2$ [Barai et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004BASI...32..385B/abstract).

As gas cools and falls into these expanding potential wells, its conversion into stars dictates the global cosmic star formation history. Because star formation is directly tied to the rate of matter infall into halo cores, the stellar mass density at early epochs ($z > 5$) serves as a sensitive probe of the underlying expansion history [Robinson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...539...89R/abstract). Furthermore, observing the evolution of the Fundamental Plane (FP) of early-type galaxies serves as a robust tool for tracing passive stellar evolution and determining their formation redshifts across cosmic time [Pahre et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001Ap&SS.276..983P/abstract).

The coupling between baryonic matter and dark matter during galaxy assembly can also alter internal halo structures. While standard models often assume adiabatic contraction of the dark matter during disk assembly, alternative models suggest that disk formation and associated feedback can instead induce [[halo-expansion|Halo expansion]]. This halo expansion lowers the ratio of the disk circular velocity (measured at $2.2$ disk scale lengths) to the virial velocity down to $V_{2.2} \sim 1.2 V_{\rm vir}$. This expanded configuration provides a better simultaneous fit to observed size-luminosity relations and the galaxy luminosity function than strongly contracted halos [Dutton et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...654...27D/abstract).

Despite the broad success of the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm, reconciling the predicted expansion and structure growth with observations reveals significant tensions. Hydrodynamic simulations and semi-analytic models have historically predicted up to a $50\%$ surplus of stellar mass density at $z \sim 3$ relative to observations, a discrepancy frequently termed the "missing galaxy problem" [Nagamine et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...610...45N/abstract). More recently, James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observations have uncovered massive, highly evolved galaxies at redshifts corresponding to just a few hundred million years after the [[big-bang|Big Bang]]. These discoveries suggest that standard hierarchical models of [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]] may require refinement, or that early-universe expansion histories and [[formation-epoch|Formation epoch]] timescales might differ from standard theoretical assumptions [Karapetian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250910856K/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the six-parameter $\Lambda$CDM paradigm successfully describes a vast array of observations, precision constraints on the cosmic expansion history have revealed significant physical and theoretical anomalies that remain subjects of active research.

One of the most pressing issues in [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]] is the [[hubble-expansion-and-tension|Hubble Expansion and Tension]]. This tension manifests as a statistically significant discrepancy between the present-day expansion rate, $H_0$, measured locally via the distance ladder (e.g., using Type Ia supernovae calibrated by Cepheids) and the value inferred from the early-universe cosmic microwave background (CMB) assuming a standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology [Joudaki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..97l3501J/abstract). Model-independent reconstructions of the expansion history confirm that this discrepancy is robust and unlikely to be solely an artifact of cosmological parameterization [Joudaki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..97l3501J/abstract). Proposed resolutions often invoke "new physics" such as early dark energy or dark radiation. However, modifying the effective number of relativistic species ($N_{\mathrm{eff}}$) to include dark radiation does not fundamentally alleviate the $H_0$ tension in general expansion histories [Joudaki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..97l3501J/abstract). For reference, combinations of baryon acoustic oscillation, supernova, and CMB data constrain the relativistic degrees of freedom to $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 3.43 \pm 0.26$ in flat $\Lambda$CDM frameworks [Aubourg et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..92l3516A/abstract).

Theoretical difficulties also persist regarding the driver of late-time acceleration. The cosmological coincidence problem asks why the energy density of dark energy and the matter energy density are of the same order of magnitude precisely at the present epoch [Bludman et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007astro.ph..2085B/abstract). To avoid the fine-tuning required by a static cosmological constant, various dynamic [[dark-energy-and-modified-gravity|Dark Energy and Modified Gravity]] models have been proposed. Oscillating dark energy models naturally allow matter and dark energy to be periodically comparable, though current metrics of the [[linear-growth-rate|Linear growth rate]] and cosmic shear struggle to distinguish these from standard $\Lambda$CDM [Pace et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.1186P/abstract). Alternatively, holographic dark energy models coupled at the Ricci scale propose non-gravitational interactions between dark sectors, ensuring their ratio remains finite throughout cosmic history [Durán et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhRvD..83b3504D/abstract), [Durán et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011JPhCS.314a2058D/abstract). 

In the high-redshift regime, the expansion history governs the competition between gravitational collapse and Hubble drag, dictating the timeline of [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|Galaxy and Universe evolution]]. Recently, this timeline has been challenged by the JWST early galaxy problem. Observations have identified massive, evolved galaxies existing only $\sim 300$ million years after the [[big-bang|Big Bang]], at odds with the gradual assembly predicted by standard hierarchical structure formation. Reconciling these discoveries may challenge standard hierarchical models, with some researchers proposing alternative local expansion mechanisms, such as active outflows from galactic centers [Karapetian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250910856K/abstract). Another persistent challenge emerges locally: observations of the physical nature and remarkable preservation of the Hubble flow within the Local Volume (distances $< 10$ Mpc) are difficult to fully reconcile with the anticipated velocity perturbations from nearby mass structures [Karachentsev et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Afz....46..399K/abstract). At even smaller scales, the $\Lambda$CDM framework faces notable challenges on galactic scales, particularly regarding dark matter distribution in halo cores, which has spurred proposed solutions like fuzzy or self-interacting dark matter [de Martino et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Univ....6..107D/abstract).

Looking forward, next-generation observatories will probe the expansion history dynamically. A highly anticipated technique is the measurement of redshift drift (the Sandage-Loeb effect), which provides a direct, real-time observation of the change in the cosmic expansion rate. By monitoring the redshifts of a large statistical sample of galaxies over a baseline of approximately a decade, facilities like the Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) and the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) aim to detect minute temporal changes in redshift, $\dot{z}$ [Roos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008741R/abstract). Because redshift drift is directly proportional to the average change in the expansion velocity between emission and absorption, it provides a purely kinematic, model-independent test capable of testing the concordance $\Lambda$CDM model against alternative [[modified-gravity-theories|Modified Gravity Theories]] [Roos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008741R/abstract).
