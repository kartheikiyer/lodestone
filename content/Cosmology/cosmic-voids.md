**Cosmic voids** are vast, underdense regions that constitute the majority of the volume in the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]]. Characterized by significantly lower densities of galaxies and dark matter compared to the cosmic mean, these [[low-density-environments|Low-density environments]] are bounded by the dense nodes, [[cosmic-filaments|Cosmic filaments]], and [[walls-astronomy|Walls (astronomy)]] that compose the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]]. Before the late 1970s, the universe was generally assumed to be populated by a relatively uniform distribution of galaxies. The advent of wide-angle redshift surveys revealed instead that galaxies are highly clustered, leaving immense intervening expanses nearly empty [Thompson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1109.1268T/abstract). The subsequent mapping of colossal underdensities, alongside successful replications in early cold dark matter models, cemented voids as a natural and fundamental consequence of hierarchical clustering and gravitational instability [White et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJ...313..505W/abstract), [Geller et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989Sci...246..897G/abstract).

Far from being static or purely empty expanses, modern astrophysics treats voids as active, dynamical entities that evolve from primordial negative density perturbations. As the universe expands, matter flows outward from these basins toward denser surrounding structures, resulting in predictable kinematic signatures and a universal inverse top-hat density profile that holds across various tracer types [Hamaus et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvL.112y1302H/abstract). Voids also exhibit a complex, nested hierarchy; large macroscopic voids are often partitioned by tenuous interior filaments into networks of smaller [[subvoids|Subvoids]]. Since they lack rigid physical boundaries, detecting and characterizing voids relies on sophisticated algorithmic methodologies—ranging from spherical underdensity finders to topological watershed transforms—which mathematically delineate these structures from the surrounding matter field [El-Ad et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...491..421E/abstract). 

Observationally, voids serve as dual-purpose laboratories for both astrophysics and fundamental cosmology. Astrophysically, their interiors provide highly isolated environments for studying galaxy evolution away from the complex tidal interactions and merger histories typical of dense clusters. Galaxies residing in these regions tend to be less massive, bluer, and more gas-rich, exhibiting a retarded evolutionary state heavily influenced by [[galaxy-void-biases|Galaxy-void biases]]. Cosmologically, because void interiors remain in the linear or mildly non-linear regime of gravitational collapse, their statistical and morphological properties—such as the [[void-abundance|Void abundance]] and large-scale shapes—are exceptionally sensitive to diffuse cosmic components. They provide tight, complementary constraints on the dark energy equation of state, the sum of neutrino masses, and the growth rate of structure, while also leaving distinct secondary imprints on the cosmic microwave background via the Integrated Sachs-Wolfe (ISW) effect and on background galaxies through weak gravitational lensing.

Despite their theoretical utility, several open questions and observational tensions remain active areas of research. Significant anomalies exist between standard $\Lambda$CDM predictions and current data, including a notable $3\sigma$ deficit of deep voids in recent spectroscopic surveys, anomalously high ISW signals, and potential ISW "sign-flips" at low redshifts. Furthermore, high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations challenge the assumption that voids are strictly pristine, revealing that ongoing matter accretion and baryonic feedback can significantly alter their mass profiles and gas content. To address these complexities, the field is increasingly relying on machine learning emulators and simulation-based inference, laying the groundwork to leverage the massive datasets from next-generation spectroscopic surveys and unlock the full potential of voids as precision cosmological probes.

## Historical Discovery and the Changing Paradigm

Prior to the late 1970s, the standard cosmological model assumed a universe populated predominantly by a uniform background of "field galaxies." While density enhancements such as rich galaxy clusters and superclusters were recognized, they were viewed as isolated, [[localized-overdensities|Localized overdensities]] superimposed on an otherwise homogeneous distribution of matter [Thompson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1109.1268T/abstract). Large, cohesive regions of empty space were neither observationally established nor theoretically expected.

This paradigm was fundamentally challenged in 1978 with the publication of two seminal papers based on early wide-angle redshift surveys. Extending deep enough to capture a representative sample of the universe beyond the [[local-cosmic-domain|local supercluster environment]], Gregory & Thompson (1978) and Jõeveer, Einasto, & Tago (1978) independently reported the existence of massive regions nearly devoid of galaxies [Thompson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1109.1268T/abstract). Displayed as radial cone diagrams, the redshift data revealed that galaxies were highly clustered into narrow boundaries, leaving vast intervening expanses completely empty.

The discovery of voids initially met resistance from theoretical cosmologists, who struggled to reconcile these immense features with [[primeval-large-scale-structures|structure formation]] in a seemingly homogeneous early universe [Thompson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1109.1268T/abstract). However, as wider and deeper redshift surveys mapped the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]], the ubiquity of voids became undeniable. Observations consistently identified voids with local density minima falling to $\sim 20\%$ of the mean cosmic density and typical diameters scaling to $5000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ in redshift space [Geller et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989Sci...246..897G/abstract). The discovery of colossal individual structures, such as the expansive [[bo-tes-void|Boötes Void]] and the massive [[great-wall-large-scale-structure|Great Wall (large-scale structure)]] measuring at least $60 h^{-1} \times 170 h^{-1} \text{ Mpc}$, cemented a new morphological picture of the universe [Geller et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989Sci...246..897G/abstract). Even in these early stages, theoreticians sought to bound the maximum extent of these features; early theoretical upper limits on void diameters (e.g., $< 80 \ h^{-1}\text{Mpc}$) were derived from CMB isotropy constraints via the Sachs-Wolfe effect [Blumenthal et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...388..234B/abstract).

The universe was no longer viewed as a uniform field, but rather as a cellular [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] composed of [[sheet-filamentary-structure|thin sheets, filaments]], and dense nodes bounding vast void regions. By the late 1980s, numerical $N$-body simulations of cold dark matter (CDM) successfully reproduced these large-scale features, demonstrating that voids are a natural consequence of gravitational instability and hierarchical clustering [White et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJ...313..505W/abstract).

Over time, the conceptual understanding of voids has undergone a secondary paradigm shift. Originally defined purely by the observational absence of luminous tracers or treated as mere survey artifacts, modern astrophysics treats voids as active, dynamical entities. Voids are now recognized as having intrinsic physical properties shaped by tidal forces, featuring universal inverse top-hat density profiles and complex internal kinematics that can be modeled via linear theory [Hamaus et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvL.112y1302H/abstract). Furthermore, they are not strictly empty; instead, they exhibit a nested hierarchical arrangement composed of tenuous [[cosmic-filaments|Cosmic filaments]] and [[subvoids|Subvoids]] populated by faint, low-mass galaxies [Lindner et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995A&A...301..329L/abstract).

## Formation, Dynamics, and Hierarchical Structure

The formation and evolution of cosmic voids are driven by the gravitational amplification of [[primeval-large-scale-structures|primordial negative density perturbations]]. As the universe expands, matter flows outward from these underdense regions toward the denser structures of the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]]. Historical estimates have suggested that approximately 15% of the total matter in the universe resides within these underdense regions [Einasto et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...429..465E/abstract). The kinematics of this evacuation process are well-described by linear theory, which yields velocity profiles that show excellent agreement with numerical simulations [Hamaus et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvL.112y1302H/abstract). Dynamically, voids can be defined as regions of negative velocity divergence when treating them as sinks of back-in-time streamlines, a criterion that offers a robust alternative to purely density-based identification [Elyiv et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.448..642E/abstract). Interestingly, voids can also be understood through the theory of void-cluster correspondence, where they are treated as the dynamical counterparts of massive clusters based on density-inverted mirror simulations [Shim et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908..211S/abstract). Furthermore, similar to dark matter halos, voids possess spin angular momentum; their spin axes show strong spatial alignments with neighboring voids, an effect driven by large-scale tidal fields [Lee et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...652....1L/abstract), and the minor axis of a given dark matter halo tends to align with the line joining it to the void center [Patiri et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...652L..75P/abstract). Beyond their internal expansion, voids as a whole experience global motion toward neighboring overdense superstructures, with galaxies in void shells exhibiting differential infall velocities depending on their orientation relative to these superstructures [Lares et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470...85L/abstract).

Analytically, the emergence and statistical distribution of these negative density fluctuations can be modeled using the excursion set formalism. This framework successfully predicts the characteristic comoving scales of voids at high redshifts—yielding diameters approaching $40 \text{ Mpc}$ at $z \sim 5$—and provides a theoretical baseline for understanding [[void-abundance|Void abundance]] across cosmic time .

Far from being simple, empty basins, voids exhibit a complex hierarchical buildup. The "void hierarchy" principle dictates that large voids, defined by the distribution of bright galaxies, are internally partitioned by tenuous [[cosmic-filaments|Cosmic filaments]] of fainter galaxies into smaller [[subvoids|Subvoids]] [Lindner et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997euvl.conf..402L/abstract), [Lindner et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997astro.ph.11046L/abstract). This nesting hierarchy consists of locally expanding subdomains where the broader velocity field breaks into expanding subvoids [Aragon-Calvo et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.3409A/abstract). This nested structure means that the typical diameter of a void, $D$, obeys a linear scaling relation with the mean separation of the tracer galaxies, $\lambda$, expressed as:
$$D = D_0 + \nu \lambda$$
where $D_0$ and the slope $\nu$ depend on the underlying cosmology and [[dark-matter-halo-scales|dark matter halo mass thresholds]] . This environmental partitioning is also evident in halo distributions; findings using the SpineWeb method show that the majority of low-mass halos ($< 10^{12} M_\odot$) reside in voids, whereas more massive halos are predominantly distributed within [[walls-astronomy|cosmic walls]] [Jaber et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.4087J/abstract).

The evolutionary trajectory of a void is heavily dictated by its large-scale environment, broadly dividing the population into two dynamical classes: "void-in-void" (R-type) and "void-in-cloud" (S-type) systems. R-type voids are embedded in larger underdense regions, causing them to asymptotically converge to the mean cosmic density and continually expand. In contrast, S-type voids are locally underdense but are embedded within larger large-scale overdense environments , [Montero-Dorta et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250414616M/abstract).

As voids evolve and evacuate matter, the displaced material accumulates at their boundaries. This outward expansion eventually drives the local density field into the non-linear regime, leading to shell crossing at the void ridges where expanding material intersects with the static or collapsing cosmic web [Curtis et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251214825C/abstract). Despite this outward flow, the interiors of voids are not entirely isolated or pristine. High-resolution hydrodynamical simulations reveal that void replenishment is a significant factor in their evolution; over cosmic history, $10\%$ to $35\%$ of a void's mass can actually be accreted inward from surrounding overdense regions (such as clusters and walls). Much of this pre-processed material can linger within the void for up to $\sim 10 \text{ Gyr}$, challenging the paradigm that void interiors are strictly composed of unpolluted, primordial gas [Vallés-Pérez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...920L...2V/abstract). Additionally, void interiors may uniquely retain primordial magnetic field properties, while galaxies evolving within them acquire correspondingly weaker magnetic fields compared to those in denser environments [Rodríguez-Medrano et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023BAAA...64..175R/abstract).

## Identification and Characterization Methodologies

Because cosmic voids lack a rigid physical boundary, their identification in observational data and $N$-body simulations is inherently algorithmic. The delineation of these underdense regions depends heavily on the chosen mathematical criteria, leading to a proliferation of distinct void-finding methodologies. Comparative studies of various algorithms, such as the Aspen-Amsterdam void finder comparison project, have shown that while different methods successfully agree on the deepest large-scale features—typically identifying void centers where the local density drops below $10\%$ of the cosmic mean ($\delta \lesssim -0.9$)—they diverge significantly in how they define void boundaries and handle complex geometries [Colberg et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.387..933C/abstract).

Historically, one of the first robust computational approaches was the spherical underdensity finder, pioneered by algorithms such as VoidFinder [El-Ad et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...491..421E/abstract). This method typically begins by classifying the tracer distribution into "wall" and "field" galaxies. It then grows maximal spheres within the field distribution, eventually merging overlapping spheres to define the void volume. Because they enforce a strict geometric and density threshold, spherical finders excel at isolating purely underdense, pristine environments.

An alternative, topological approach leverages Voronoi tessellation to estimate the [[three-dimensional-cosmic-density-fields|three-dimensional density field]] without imposing a fixed smoothing scale or geometry [Gaite et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.10328G/abstract). This technique forms the basis of ZOBOV (ZOnes Bordering On Voidness) and its modern implementations, such as the VIDE toolkit [Sutter et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442.3127S/abstract). After the Voronoi tessellation assigns a local density to each tracer, a watershed transform is applied. The algorithm identifies local density minima and merges adjacent basins, effectively pouring "water" into the density field until it reaches the ridges of the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]]. While watershed algorithms capture the highly non-spherical, hierarchical nature of voids, their lack of a rigid density threshold introduces systematic challenges. Notably, ZOBOV-based finders are prone to "leaking" into denser regions; their defined boundaries can extend up to the density maxima of surrounding [[walls-astronomy|Walls (astronomy)]] [Zaidouni et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...978....3Z/abstract). 

Beyond density-based metrics, some algorithms rely on dynamical criteria. Because void interiors are characterized by an expanding velocity field, identifying regions of negative velocity divergence ($ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} < 0 $) can mitigate the impact of sparse sampling and shot noise. Using the Zel'dovich approximation to trace back galaxy orbits, dynamical finders define voids as the sinks of back-in-time streamlines, generating divergence profiles with significantly less scatter than conventional overdensity profiles [Elyiv et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.448..642E/abstract). By incorporating kinematic information from resources such as the CosmicFlows-3 catalog, researchers have demonstrated that true void emptiness should be assessed directly through matter underdensity kinematics rather than relying solely on discrete galaxy counts [Courtois et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...673A..38C/abstract). 

Beyond these traditional approaches, researchers are increasingly exploring biologically inspired methodologies. For instance, the Monte Carlo Physarum Machine (MCPM) algorithm is a slime-mold-inspired technique used to elegantly reconstruct the [[three-dimensional-cosmic-density-fields|3D continuous density field]] and identify the cosmic web from discrete galaxy surveys [Wilde et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230102719W/abstract). To systematically classify the varying environments mapped by these diverse algorithms, frameworks such as the "Local Dimension" ($D$) are commonly utilized. The Local Dimension evaluates the topological features of the density field, where $D \approx 1, 2,$ or $3$ formally characterizes the local shape as a filamentary, planar, or cluster-like structure within the cosmic web [Sarkar et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.394L..66S/abstract).

Regardless of the chosen algorithm, it is important to note that characteristic void sizes and volumes are significantly influenced by the sampling density of the tracer survey and the presence of redshift-space distortions [Schmidt et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...546..609S/abstract). Void identification is highly sensitive to observational systematics overall. Survey boundaries and masked regions truncate the continuous density field, which can artificially truncate voids or falsely identify edge effects as underdensities. Uncorrected survey masks have been shown to reduce the inferred void number density by a factor of $3$ across all scales, while simultaneously skewing the relative size distribution and artificially increasing the mean void ellipticity by approximately $30\%$ [Sutter et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442.3127S/abstract). Furthermore, in photometric surveys where line-of-sight position errors routinely exceed $50 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$, traditional 3D finders fail; researchers must instead project galaxies into 2D slices to confidently identify photometric voids [Sánchez et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..746S/abstract).

## Statistical and Morphological Properties

The statistical and morphological properties of cosmic voids provide essential constraints on the formation of the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] and the fundamental parameters of cosmology. By treating voids as dynamical components rather than merely empty space, researchers utilize their density distributions, size frequencies, and shapes to probe the underlying dark matter field. 

**Universal Density Profile**
Simulations using watershed algorithms demonstrate that the average density profile of voids follows a universal functional form. This "universal density profile" resembles an inverse top-hat function and can be accurately described across a wide range of scales and redshifts using just two free parameters: a scale radius and a central density [Hamaus et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhRvL.112y1302H/abstract). While this empirical profile is robust across different tracer types (such as halos or galaxies) via simple rescalings, literature highlights a tension between simulations and observations. Observed void profiles traced by luminous galaxies are often much steeper than the underlying dark matter profiles [Ricciardelli et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440..601R/abstract), [Curtis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962...58C/abstract). This discrepancy indicates that galaxies act as biased tracers within underdense regions, and it is now understood that halo bias exhibits a distinct scale-dependence along the void profile [Verza et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...940L..16V/abstract). However, observational studies such as those from the Dark Energy Survey indicate that the relative bias between galaxies and galaxy clusters remains linear within and around voids, allowing void-centric profiles of different tracers to be linked by a single multiplicative constant [Pollina et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.2836P/abstract). Tracking the density profiles of TNG300 voids across cosmic time from $z=3$ to $z=0$ reveals that the underlying dark matter ridges and interiors evolve significantly more than the profiles traced by galaxies [Curtis et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985..244C/abstract).

**Void Size Function and Scaling Relations**
The Void size function (VSF)—which quantifies [[void-abundance|Void abundance]] as a function of effective radius—is highly sensitive to the dark energy equation of state and the sum of neutrino masses $\Sigma m_\nu$ [Bayer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919...24B/abstract), [Verza et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...12..044V/abstract). Phenomenologically, the VSF can be approximated by a three-parameter log-normal distribution [Pycke et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...821..110P/abstract). The sizes of voids obey strict scaling relations with the mean galaxy separation $\lambda$, typically parameterized as $D = D_0 + \nu \lambda$ [Müller et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000MNRAS.318..280M/abstract), [Arbabi-Bidgoli et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.332..205A/abstract). Studying these structures demonstrates that universal cellular scaling relations hold on physical scales up to nearly 30 orders of magnitude larger than previously studied non-cosmological cellular systems, underscoring the nested, hierarchical nature of the density field [Aragon-Calvo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200900977A/abstract). While $\Lambda$CDM models generally reproduce the observed VSF, there is an unresolved $>3\sigma$ deficit of the deepest voids in BOSS survey data compared to standard theoretical predictions [Nadathur et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461..358N/abstract). To rigorously assess the presence of unexpectedly massive underdensities, extreme-value statistics can be employed as a framework to predict the size and frequency of the largest expected voids within any given cosmological volume [Chongchitnan et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...05..062C/abstract).

**Morphology, Ellipticity, and Topology**
Voids are rarely perfectly spherical; their morphological features are heavily influenced by local tidal fields. The intrinsic ellipticity of voids is utilized in Alcock-Paczynski tests, where the cosmologically induced "stretch" of stacked voids along the line of sight constrains the expansion history of the universe [Biswas et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvD..82b3002B/abstract), [Sutter et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761..187S/abstract). Additionally, the evolution of mean void ellipticity serves as a highly sensitive probe for the matter density and the dark energy equation of state [Bos et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426..440B/abstract). However, observational systematics severely impact measured morphology. Survey masks and boundary effects can increase the mean measured ellipticity by roughly 30% and artificially reduce the inferred number density by a factor of 3 [Sutter et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442.3127S/abstract). 

To quantify the complex spatial configuration of voids beyond simple axial ratios, researchers employ Betti numbers derived from alpha shapes and Delaunay tessellations. Betti numbers formalize the topological information content of the discrete galaxy distribution, successfully capturing the multiscale hierarchy wherein large voids are partitioned by tenuous filaments into networks of smaller [[subvoids|Subvoids]] [van de Weygaert et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011LNCS.6970...60V/abstract), [van de Weygaert et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.3640V/abstract).

## Galaxy Evolution in Pristine Environments

Cosmic voids represent extreme [[low-density-environments|Low-density environments]] within the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]], offering a unique astrophysical laboratory to study galaxy formation in relative isolation. Because they experience fewer mergers and reduced tidal interactions, **void galaxies** are broadly hypothesized to undergo a "retarded evolution" compared to those in denser regimes [Pustilnik et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..235..238P/abstract). Observational and simulated data generally indicate that galaxies residing in voids are preferentially less massive, younger, bluer, and less metal-enriched than their counterparts in filaments and clusters [Curtis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962...58C/abstract). Observational efforts like the Lynx-Cancer void survey have specifically identified unusual very metal-poor and gas-rich dwarf galaxies, serving as direct evidence of these slower, pristine evolutionary tracks [Pustilnik et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022muto.confE..26P/abstract). Furthermore, void galaxies tend to exhibit higher spin parameters and later formation epochs, characteristics that favor the formation of extended, low-surface-brightness disks [Peper et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.1223P/abstract). Furthermore, within these sparse regions, low-mass and high-redshift galaxies are statistically more likely to exhibit prolate morphologies and align with their nearest filamentary boundaries [Hasan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250923549H/abstract). This [[galaxy-void-biases|environmental biasing]] manifests most strongly in host halos with masses below $\sim 10^{13.5} M_\odot$, above which the local halo environment dominates over the large-scale underdensity [Rodríguez-Medrano et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521..916R/abstract). The environmental impact of these underdensities also extends outward; the fraction of late-type and star-forming galaxies increases in the proximity of larger voids, suggesting a "region of influence" extending up to twice the void radius [Ricciardelli et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...846L...4R/abstract). Furthermore, comparisons of [[local-cosmic-domain|Local Volume voids]] with $\Lambda$CDM simulations suggest that dark matter halos with circular velocities below approximately $45 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ may remain 'dark' or devoid of luminous galaxies entirely in these regions [Tikhonov et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..244..152T/abstract). It should be noted, however, that the assumption of a strictly "pristine" environment is challenged by cosmological simulations showing that up to $35\%$ of void mass can be accreted from surrounding overdense regions over cosmic time, bringing pre-processed material into the void interior [Vallés-Pérez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...920L...2V/abstract).

To rigorously model the statistical distribution of these populations, formalisms inspired by Excursion Set Theory have been successfully applied to map the environmental and redshift dependence of the galaxy luminosity function within voids [Ashurikisomi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250701626A/abstract). A central question in void galaxy evolution is the state of their cold gas and their **star formation rate (SFR)**. Surveys such as the Void Galaxy Survey (VGS) characterize these objects as possessing substantial HI gas reservoirs, but rather than being perfectly unperturbed, they exhibit ongoing assembly driven by gas dynamics between interacting systems and kinematic misalignments during slow cold-gas accretion [Kreckel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..308..591K/abstract). In addition to cold gas within galaxies, the broader intergalactic medium within voids harbors significant neutral hydrogen. Observations indicate that approximately $65\%$ of Lyman-alpha absorbers actually reside in voids, where they follow a more uniform radial distribution compared to the edge-concentrated galaxy population [Watson et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220406708W/abstract). While absolute star formation rates are generally lower in these sparse environments, void galaxies exhibit higher specific star formation rates (sSFR), indicating more efficient star formation relative to their stellar mass [Habouzit et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493..899H/abstract); simulations suggest these sSFRs can be elevated by $\sim 20\%$ compared to non-void field galaxies [Curtis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962...58C/abstract). However, the literature presents a direct conflict regarding molecular gas and star formation efficiency. The CO-CAVITY pilot survey, measuring CO emission in void galaxies with stellar masses between $10^{8.5}$ and $10^{10.3} M_\odot$, found no statistically significant difference in molecular gas mass, atomic gas mass, or sSFR when compared to control samples in filaments and walls [Domínguez-Gómez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...658A.124D/abstract). Where quenching does occur in voids, it is thought to be driven primarily by host dark matter halo mass and early formation times leading to rapid initial gas consumption, rather than environmental stripping [Shojaei et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250116545S/abstract). Additionally, controlling for local density, void galaxies exhibit a surprisingly high active galactic nucleus (AGN) fraction, measured to be $\sim 80\%$ higher than non-void galaxies at $z=0$ [Curtis et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251214825C/abstract).

**Dwarf galaxies** in voids are particularly sensitive tracers of their sparse surroundings. Because they are isolated from the strong tidal forces typical of clusters, classical galaxy formation theory predicts that void dwarfs should retain dynamically cold, rotationally supported disks. However, UV background photo-heating suppresses baryon accretion in low-mass halos, significantly reducing the expected number of observable dwarf galaxies in these deep underdensities [Hoeft et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AdAst2010E..87H/abstract). Interestingly, integral field spectroscopy from the Dwarfs in Void Environments (DIVE) survey—targeting low-mass systems ($M_\star = 10^7 - 10^9 M_\odot$)—reveals unexpected internal dynamics. These isolated dwarf galaxies exhibit low ratios of rotational velocity to stellar velocity dispersion, $v_{\rm rot}/\sigma_\star \lesssim 2$, indicating "puffy," dispersion-supported structures [de los Reyes et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951...52D/abstract). This finding demonstrates that dwarf galaxies can evolve into dispersion-dominated systems independently of environmental tidal stirring, suggesting an intrinsic, mass-dependent evolutionary pathway rather than a purely environmental one.

## Cosmological Probes: Dark Energy and Neutrino Mass

Cosmic voids within the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] provide highly sensitive, complementary constraints on fundamental cosmological parameters. Because they represent the unscreened, linear to mildly non-linear regime of the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]], voids are uniquely responsive to diffuse components such as dark energy and massive neutrinos [Contarini et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260114362C/abstract). 

**Dark Energy and Expansion History**
Void shapes and sizes are sensitive probes of the expansion history of the universe. By assuming that voids are, on average, spherically symmetric in real space, stacking them in redshift space allows for the application of the Alcock-Paczynski (AP) test. This technique measures the cosmologically induced geometric "stretch" along the line of sight, providing constraints on the matter density ($\Omega_m$) and the dark energy equation of state ($w$) [Sutter et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761..187S/abstract), [Lavaux et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...754..109L/abstract). Forecasts indicate that adding void shape statistics to standard probes can improve the Dark Energy Task Force figure of merit by up to two orders of magnitude for surveys like Euclid [Biswas et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvD..82b3002B/abstract), while Roman Space Telescope forecasts rely on the [[void-abundance|void size function]] and cross-correlation function to provide complementary constraints [Verza et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..227V/abstract). Additionally, the geometric properties of voids can constrain the amplitude of matter fluctuations; the use of the giant void ellipticity function has served as a tool to normalize the matter power spectrum, yielding estimates of $\sigma_8 = 0.90 \pm 0.04$ [Lee et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0907.4453L/abstract). In certain survey regimes, AP tests using stacked voids are projected to outperform standard Baryon Acoustic Oscillation (BAO) measurements by an order of magnitude [Lavaux et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...754..109L/abstract). When extracting geometric information, void-based BAO measurements remain robust to moderate survey incompleteness, though they require local-density-dependent thresholds under severe selection effects to avoid bias [Forero-Sánchez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513.5407F/abstract).

**Neutrino Mass**
Massive neutrinos suppress the growth of structure, which leaves a distinct imprint on the void size function and density profiles. Combining void statistics with traditional halo and matter power spectra effectively breaks parameter degeneracies between the sum of neutrino masses ($\Sigma m_\nu$) and the amplitude of matter fluctuations ($\sigma_8$) [Massara et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...11..018M/abstract), [Verza et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...12..044V/abstract). Fisher matrix analyses of $N$-body simulations demonstrate that integrating voids with halo clustering can yield a marginalized error of $\sigma(\Sigma m_\nu) = 0.018$ eV for a $1\,(h^{-1}\mathrm{Gpc})^3$ volume, entirely independent of cosmic microwave background (CMB) priors [Bayer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919...24B/abstract). Advanced simulation-based inference techniques, such as implicit likelihood inference applied to BOSS data, yield upper bounds on $\Sigma m_\nu$ of $0.35$ eV when the void size function and void-galaxy cross power spectrum are included [Thiele et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969...89T/abstract). To handle the computational expense of these parameter space explorations, neural network emulators are increasingly utilized to accurately model void summary statistics directly from theory [Kreisch et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935..100K/abstract), [Lehman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205262L/abstract).

**Growth of Structure and Modified Gravity**
The dynamics of galaxies around void centers probe the growth rate of structure and offer a unique environment to test modifications to General Relativity in [[low-density-environments|Low-density environments]]. Redshift-space distortions (RSD) in the void-galaxy cross-correlation function provide precise measurements of the linear growth rate. Using BOSS data, the anisotropic distortion parameter $\epsilon$ was constrained to percent-level precision ($\epsilon = 1.003 \pm 0.012$), yielding a linear growth rate of $f/b = 0.417 \pm 0.089$ (where $b$ is the galaxy bias) at a median redshift of $z=0.57$ [Hamaus et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvL.117i1302H/abstract). Additionally, local dynamics within these expanses generate observable relativistic effects; second-order Hubble terms driven by void expansions contribute significantly to the gravitational redshift signatures observed in galaxies associated with voids [Sakuma et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170905756S/abstract). These dynamic signatures extend even to the high-redshift universe; using the Lyman-alpha forest at $z = 2.49$, researchers have achieved a $10\sigma$ detection of matter flows (RSD) around voids [Ravoux et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220311045R/abstract).

While current kinematic constraints from void dynamics show no significant deviations from Einstein's gravity [Hamaus et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvL.117i1302H/abstract), but a comprehensive theoretical framework has been established for testing modified gravity models using a combination of void counts, weak lensing, RSD, and ISW imprints [Cai et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IJMPD..2748007C/abstract). There remains an active tension regarding void abundances. Analyses of the BOSS survey have reported a deficit of the deepest voids relative to standard $\Lambda$CDM predictions at a $>3\sigma$ significance [Nadathur et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461..358N/abstract). The physical cause of this discrepancy—whether a signature of alternative gravity, varying dark energy, or systematic survey biases—remains an open question in the literature.

## Gravitational Lensing and CMB Imprints

Cosmic voids exert measurable gravitational effects on background light, providing direct probes of the underlying matter density field independent of [[galaxy-void-biases|Galaxy-void biases]]. Because the gravitational potential of an individual void is relatively shallow, observational studies predominantly rely on stacked weak lensing to achieve high signal-to-noise ratios. Looking to the future, Stage IV surveys like Euclid and the Rubin Observatory (LSST) are expected to detect void lensing with high enough precision to constrain their mass density profiles entirely independent of galaxy bias [Krause et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...762L..20K/abstract).

For massive voids with radii $\sim 90\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ and density contrasts $\delta \approx -0.9$, analytical models predict weak lensing shear up to $\sim 10^{-2}$ in the outer regions, alongside magnitude fluctuations of $\sim 0.05\,\mathrm{mag}$ [Chen et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...804..130C/abstract). Observationally, the stacked weak lensing signature of voids has been robustly confirmed. The Dark Energy Survey (DES-SV) detected void lensing at a $4.4\sigma$ significance, confirming that photometrically identified voids trace true matter underdensities rather than Poisson noise [Sánchez et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..746S/abstract). Similarly, the lensing of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) by BOSS voids has been detected at $5.3\sigma$ in CMB lensing convergence maps, yielding a lensing amplitude parameter of $A_L = 1.10 \pm 0.21$ [Raghunathan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...890..168R/abstract). Methodological advances have shown that identifying voids directly from weak lensing convergence maps—rather than from galaxy distributions—can amplify the measured tangential shear signal by up to a factor of 40 [Davies et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480L.101D/abstract).

Counterintuitively, relativistic Doppler terms associated with local void dynamics can overwhelm the standard weak lensing dimming effect. At low redshifts, this results in brightness magnification or "antilensing," where background objects appear brighter rather than dimmer. Non-linear corrections to this effect can alter linear theory predictions by over 20% for deep voids, representing a crucial systematic for supernova cosmology [Bolejko et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PhRvL.110b1302B/abstract).

Voids also leave distinct imprints on the CMB via the Integrated Sachs-Wolfe (ISW) effect. As CMB photons traverse the decaying gravitational potentials of expanding voids in a dark energy-dominated universe, they lose energy, creating characteristic temperature decrements (cold spots). Additionally, the presence of voids causes "void-wiggling" of primary CMB temperature gradients, introducing fluctuations of $\sim 15\,\mu\mathrm{K}$ for large voids at $z \approx 0.5$ [Chen et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...804..130C/abstract). However, the environmental context of the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] is critical: while large, isolated voids produce cold spots, small voids embedded in contracting overdense regions can actually generate hot spots [Cai et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...786..110C/abstract).

The literature presents notable tensions regarding the amplitude of the ISW signal. Stacking analyses of SDSS DR7 voids have measured an average temperature decrement of $2.6-2.9\,\mu\mathrm{K}$, which is higher than standard $\Lambda$CDM predictions at a $\sim 2\sigma$ significance [Cai et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...786..110C/abstract). Furthermore, the hypothesis that the famous CMB Cold Spot is solely caused by a foreground supervoid has been heavily scrutinized. Observations rule out a $100\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ radius void with $\delta = -0.3$ at $0.5 < z < 0.9$ as the primary source, finding that local density fluctuations in that region are well within the range of cosmic variance [Granett et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714..825G/abstract).

Recent observations have uncovered a potential sign flip in the ISW effect in the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]] ($z < 0.03$). CMB photons passing through these nearby voids appear anomalously hot at a $2.7-3.6\sigma$ significance, exhibiting a mean temperature difference of $41\,\mu\mathrm{K}$ compared to galaxies—a discrepancy $6.5\sigma$ larger than simulated expectations [Hansen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250608832H/abstract). This anomaly is corroborated by the analysis of "Cosmic Tunnels" (2D void-like line-of-sight underdensities), which yield a highly significant ($3.6\sigma$) overall ISW detection; however, their evidence for the low-redshift sign flip specifically remains a tentative detection [Davies et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251016799D/abstract). The presence of such hot voids challenges the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm and remains an active area of debate, with proposed solutions invoking dynamical dark energy or modified gravity.

## Open Questions, Machine Learning, and Future Surveys

Despite their proven utility as cosmological probes, the application of cosmic voids in precision cosmology faces several unresolved theoretical and observational tensions. In the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]], data from the Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey (BOSS) has revealed a $3\sigma$ deficit of the deepest voids relative to $\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}$ expectations [Nadathur et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461..358N/abstract). Furthermore, measurements of the Integrated Sachs-Wolfe (ISW) effect around voids show amplitudes significantly higher than predicted by standard models, alongside recent evidence for an unexpected sign-flip in the ISW signal at $z < 0.03$, suggesting that local voids appear hotter than expected [Cai et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...786..110C/abstract), [Hansen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250608832H/abstract), [Davies et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251016799D/abstract). The fundamental assumption that voids are pristine, isolated environments is also challenged by modern hydrodynamical simulations. These models indicate that **baryonic feedback effects** (such as supernovae and active galactic nuclei) can heat and expel gas into void interiors, reducing the predicted [[void-abundance|Void abundance]] in hydrodynamical simulations by approximately 20% compared to dark matter-only models, though the overall effects on void statistics are ultimately deemed minimal [Paillas et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.4434P/abstract). Cosmological voids also experience ongoing matter accretion from overdense regions of the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]], further complicating their evolutionary history [Vallés-Pérez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...920L...2V/abstract).

To accurately model void statistics while accounting for complex non-linear dynamics and baryonic physics, the field is increasingly turning to machine learning and **simulation-based inference**. Traditional analytical models rely on simplifying assumptions that often apply only to a subset of the true void population. To bypass these limitations, massive synthetic catalogs like the **GIGANTES dataset**—which contains over one billion voids extracted from the QUIJOTE $N$-body simulations—have been developed expressly for training machine learning algorithms [Kreisch et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935..100K/abstract). Utilizing these large training suites, researchers have constructed **neural network emulators** capable of predicting void summary statistics, such as the void size function and density profiles, across a broad cosmological parameter space [Lehman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205262L/abstract). These emulators offer orders-of-magnitude acceleration in parameter estimation over traditional simulations. Crucially, tests incorporating hydrodynamic simulations demonstrate that cosmological posteriors derived from emulated void statistics remain highly robust to baryonic physics, cementing voids as a theoretically clean probe [Lehman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205262L/abstract).

These computational advancements are laying the necessary groundwork for the massive data volumes expected from next-generation spectroscopic surveys, reinforcing the broad case for void science as a key decade-level tool for constraining both modified gravity and galaxy evolution [Pisani et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019BAAS...51c..40P/abstract). Upcoming missions such as **Euclid and Roman Space Telescope** will map the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] with unprecedented tracer density and volume, identifying tens of thousands of individual voids [Kreisch et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935..100K/abstract), [Verza et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..227V/abstract). Likelihood-free inference applied to these datasets is forecast to yield highly competitive, independent constraints on fundamental physics. For instance, combining the void auto-correlation function with halo statistics is projected to constrain the sum of neutrino masses, $\Sigma m_\nu$, to an error of $0.09\ \mathrm{eV}$ for a $1\ h^{-3}\ \mathrm{Gpc}^3$ volume, completely independent of cosmic microwave background priors [Kreisch et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935..100K/abstract). The exceptional tracer density of the Roman Space Telescope will additionally leverage void size functions and void-galaxy cross-correlations to tightly constrain the dark energy equation of state ($w$) and break historical degeneracies between dynamical dark energy and massive neutrinos [Verza et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...12..044V/abstract), [Verza et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..227V/abstract).
