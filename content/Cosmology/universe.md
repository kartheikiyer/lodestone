In [[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]], the **Universe** is defined as the totality of space-time, matter, and energy. Its evolution and large-scale geometry are primarily understood through the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], which serves as the concordance framework for an expanding space-time continuum originating from a hot, dense initial state known as the [[big-bang|Big Bang]] [Sharov et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ehdb.book.....S/abstract). Governed by General Relativity and the Cosmological Principle—which posits that the Universe is statistically homogeneous and isotropic on large scales—this framework dictates that the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] and thermal evolution of the cosmos are fundamentally determined by its [[energy-content|Energy content]] and [[mass-density|Mass density]]. The early Universe underwent a series of phase transitions, evolving from a primordial plasma during the [[radiation-dominated-era|Radiation-dominated era]] to a state where neutral atoms could form, releasing the relic photons observed today as the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]].

Precision observations have established that the present-day energy budget of the Universe is heavily dominated by a "dark sector." Roughly 70% of the total density is attributed to dark energy, a poorly understood vacuum energy driving [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]], while approximately 25% consists of collisionless [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract). Luminous baryonic matter constitutes only about 5% of the cosmos. As the Universe expanded and cooled, primordial [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]] grew via gravitational instability, resulting in [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]] [Peebles et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981lssu.book.....P/abstract). This process organized the underlying [[mass-density|Mass density]] into a complex, cellular morphology known as the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]], characterized by dense galaxy clusters and interconnected filaments separated by vast voids [Zeldovich et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982Natur.300..407Z/abstract). 

This article explores the [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]] of the Universe, tracing its physical evolution from [[early-universe-epochs|Early universe epochs]] through the epoch of reionization and the late-time assembly of the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]]. It details the mathematical and statistical tools used to quantify structure, such as the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]], and examines the cosmography of the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]]. Despite the immense predictive success of the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm, the advent of high-precision data has exposed critical anomalies. The following sections review active debates surrounding these observational tensions—such as the statistically significant discrepancy in the local [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] ($H_0$ tension), the discovery of massive superstructures challenging the assumed [[homogeneity-scale|Homogeneity scale]], and unexpected properties of high-redshift galaxies. Consequently, the article concludes by evaluating ongoing research into [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]], modified gravity theories, and the unresolved physical nature of dark matter and dark energy.

## Fundamental Principles and the Cosmological Framework

[[modern-cosmology|Modern cosmology]] provides the foundational space-time framework necessary to describe the origin, evolution, and [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]]. This framework is anchored in Einstein’s theory of General Relativity, which posits that the space-time continuum is dynamic and its geometry is determined by the [[mass-density|Mass density]] and [[energy-content|Energy content]] distributed within it. 

At the core of this standard framework is the Cosmological Principle: the hypothesis that, on sufficiently large scales, the Universe is statistically homogeneous and isotropic. Originally proposed as a generalized Copernican principle—suggesting our location in the Universe is typical and indistinguishable from any other—this assumption strictly eliminates the possibility of a self-similar, fractal structure on the largest scales. Under the Copernican Principle, the vanishing of the low-order multipoles (dipole, quadrupole, and octupole) in the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] serves as a primary observational test to impose homogeneity on large scales [Maartens et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011RSPTA.369.5115M/abstract). Furthermore, while the Universe is highly isotropic, observations of the cosmic microwave background place strict upper limits on any large-scale rotation or distortion of the cosmos [Collins et al. (1973)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1973MNRAS.162..307C/abstract). Additionally, while the FLRW metric defines the local geometry, the global topology of the Universe remains an active area of study; searches for multiple images of cosmic objects, such as distant galaxy clusters, can be used to constrain models of the Universe's physical shape and finite size [Gomero et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002IJMPA..17.4281G/abstract).

The mathematical realization of General Relativity combined with the Cosmological Principle yields the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric. The FLRW geometry is completely specified by two quantities: the spatial curvature $K$, and the scale factor $a(t)$, which describes the time evolution of the physical length scale of the Universe. The [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] of the Universe is quantified by the Hubble parameter, defined at the present epoch $t_0$ as:
$$H_0 = \left. \frac{\dot{a}}{a} \right|_{t_0}$$
where $\dot{a} > 0$ denotes an expanding universe. The fact that the Universe is expanding implies it must have been smaller, denser, and hotter at earlier times. For a comoving volume $V \propto a^3(t)$, the application of thermodynamic principles to this expanding background allows cosmologists to predict the thermal history and particle interactions during [[early-universe-epochs|Early universe epochs]].

While the Cosmological Principle has historically been well-supported by the isotropy of the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] and the large-scale spatial distribution of galaxies, recent high-precision data have sparked active debate regarding its validity at all observable scales. The consensus literature states that statistical isotropy and homogeneity are typically assumed to hold on scales $\gtrsim 100$ Mpc [Payne et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.102j2004P/abstract), [Hamilton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract). However, observations have revealed significant anomalies that challenge this paradigm within the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]]. For instance, the discovery of massive superstructures within the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]], such as the $400$ Mpc "Quipu" superstructure, presents features that are an order of magnitude larger than those predicted by standard simulations [Boehringer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250119236B/abstract), [Dupuy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...678A.176D/abstract). Additionally, analyses of fractal and cellular models have contested the existence of a $130 \, h^{-1}$ Mpc [[homogeneity-scale|Homogeneity scale]] [Zaninetti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JApA...33..399Z/abstract), [Sarkar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240703042S/abstract). 

Furthermore, statistically significant discrepancies in measurements of the Hubble constant ($H_0$ tension) and anomalous large-scale bulk flows hint at potential deviations from the exact symmetries assumed by the FLRW metric. The literature is currently ambiguous and conflicting as to whether these tensions represent underappreciated observational systematics, the nonlinear backreaction of small-scale structures on large-scale dynamics [Macpherson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191013380M/abstract), or a fundamental breakdown of the Cosmological Principle, suggesting that precision cosmology may have outgrown the standard FLRW approximation [Kumar Aluri et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40i4001K/abstract).

## Historical Development of Cosmological Thought

The early twentieth century marked a profound shift in our understanding of the cosmos, transitioning from a static, Milky Way-centric view to a dynamic and expanding framework. A pivotal moment in this transition was the 1920 "Great Debate" at the Smithsonian Institution between Harlow Shapley and Heber Curtis regarding the scale of the Universe [Horvath et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200100159H/abstract). Shapley argued that the Universe consisted of a single, massive galaxy with the Sun located far from its center, while Curtis contended that the Universe was populated by many relatively small, independent "island" galaxies similar to our own [Horvath et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200100159H/abstract). 

This debate was observationally resolved by Edwin Hubble, whose measurements of the velocities and redshifts of distant galaxies proved that the Milky Way is merely one of countless galaxies [Sharov et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ehdb.book.....S/abstract). Furthermore, Hubble discovered that the Universe is expanding, establishing the foundational kinematics of cosmology (often expressed via the Hubble law) [Sharov et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ehdb.book.....S/abstract), [Baryshev et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AIPC..822...23B/abstract). In the decades that followed, the observational study of the Universe was largely defined as the search for two numbers: the Hubble parameter $H_0$, governing the linear [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]], and the deceleration parameter $q_0$, describing its non-linear dynamical evolution over time [Bolotin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150200811B/abstract). Furthermore, independent measures such as the age estimates of globular clusters in the Milky Way have yielded a 95% confidence lower limit of 11.2 billion years for the Universe, providing critical constraints on cosmology independent of cosmic expansion parameters [Krauss et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Sci...299...65K/abstract).

Following the acceptance of cosmic expansion, a theoretical schism emerged regarding the [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]] of the Universe. In 1948, Bondi, Gold, and Hoyle proposed the Steady State Theory. This model was based on the "perfect cosmological principle," which hypothesized that the Universe remains invariant not only under spatial displacement but also under time displacement, necessitating the continuous creation of matter to maintain a constant mean density. In stark contrast, the competing [[big-bang|Big Bang]] model posited that the Universe emerged from a significantly denser, hotter state and expanded over time. The subsequent discovery of the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] radiation, and the demonstration that it possesses a perfect blackbody spectrum, proved to be an insurmountable problem for the Steady State Theory, cementing the hot Big Bang as the consensus paradigm.

In the late twentieth century, the field underwent another major paradigm shift—from qualitative morphological descriptions of galaxy distributions to rigorous statistical quantification—inaugurating the era of Precision Cosmology [Strauss et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.395A...9S/abstract). A critical revelation during this period was the discovery that the expansion of the Universe is accelerating, a finding that overturned the long-held assumption that gravity would inevitably decelerate the cosmic expansion [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract), [Blake et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.1725B/abstract). 

Today, precise measurements from the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] and extensive galaxy redshift surveys have established the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] as the premier theoretical framework [Hamilton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract). In this concordance model, the Universe is spatially flat and its energy budget is dominated by dark energy ($\sim 70\%$) and cold dark matter ($\sim 25\%$), with baryonic matter making up only $\sim 5\%$ [Rees et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003RSPTA.361.2427R/abstract), [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract). While Precision Cosmology has successfully codified the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] and large-scale geometry of the Universe, it has also revealed new tensions—such as statistically significant discrepancies in the exact value of $H_0$—leading some researchers to question whether the field's precision has finally outgrown its foundational assumptions [Kumar Aluri et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40i4001K/abstract).

## The ΛCDM Paradigm and Energy Budget

The standard concordance framework of modern astrophysics is the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]]. Rooted in General Relativity and the Cosmological Principle, this [[fiducial-cosmological-model|Fiducial cosmological model]] describes a spatially flat expanding universe whose dynamics and structure are governed by three primary components: baryonic matter, [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] (CDM), and dark energy [Hamilton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract). 

The relative contributions of these components to the total [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]] are quantified using [[omega-parameters|Omega parameters]], which express the density of a component as a fraction of the critical density required to halt the expansion. In the modern epoch, the radiation density is negligible, and the total energy budget is overwhelmingly dominated by dark components, satisfying the flat-geometry relation:
$$ \Omega_{\rm tot} = \Omega_b + \Omega_c + \Omega_\Lambda \approx 1 $$
where $\Omega_{\rm tot}$ represents the total energy density [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract).

Baryonic matter—comprising the protons, neutrons, and electrons that make up all luminous stars, gas, and dust—accounts for a remarkably small fraction of the universe. Observations consistently constrain the baryonic contribution to roughly 5% ($\Omega_b \approx 0.05$) [Rees et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003RSPTA.361.2427R/abstract). The remainder of the matter budget is composed of [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]], which constitutes approximately 25% ($\Omega_c \approx 0.25$). Although non-luminous and yet to be directly detected in the laboratory, CDM is essential for hierarchical structure formation; its collisionless gravitational influence dictates the deep potential wells of galaxy halos and cluster environments [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract).

The largest component of the energy budget is dark energy, which accounts for roughly 70% of the total density ($\Omega_\Lambda \approx 0.70$) [Rees et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003RSPTA.361.2427R/abstract). Typically modeled as a cosmological constant ($\Lambda$) associated with the vacuum of space, dark energy exerts a negative pressure that drives the [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]] [Bennett et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006Natur.440.1126B/abstract). This accelerated [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] has been robustly constrained with 10-15% precision using multiple independent probes, including Type Ia supernovae, baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO), and the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] (CMB) [Blake et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.1725B/abstract). However, current observational data do not yet definitively rule out dark energy models where the equation of state parameter $w$ is less than $-1$ [Melchiorri et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph..6652M/abstract). Furthermore, the small positive value of the cosmological constant is often discussed within an anthropic context, though empirical tests involving the mechanisms of planet formation in high-redshift dwarf galaxies have been proposed to critically evaluate these claims [Loeb et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006JCAP...05..009L/abstract).

Despite its immense predictive success across diverse observables, the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm is not without significant theoretical and empirical challenges. The fundamental physical nature of both dark matter and dark energy remains unidentified [Hamilton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract). Furthermore, the advent of high-precision data has revealed persistent tensions in measurements of the local [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] and the growth rate of structure. In response, a subset of the literature actively explores [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]]. Extensions and modifications to General Relativity, such as Modified Gravity (MOG) or Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), attempt to replicate galactic rotation curves and cosmic expansion without invoking exotic dark components [Moffat et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0710.0364M/abstract). Other proposals, such as the stochastic $\eta$CDM framework, introduce dynamic equations of state to simultaneously resolve the $H_0$ and structure growth tensions [Lapi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205823L/abstract). However, the literature remains conflicting on these alternatives; modified gravity theories often struggle to simultaneously match the comprehensive success of the $\Lambda$CDM model across all global probes, such as CMB isotropy and the matter power spectrum. Consequently, $\Lambda$CDM remains the consensus standard model.

## Thermal History and Phase Transitions

Because the Universe is expanding, its comoving volume scales as $V \propto a^3(t)$, dictating a thermal history in which the early cosmos was significantly denser and hotter. The [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]] traces back to a highly energetic state a fraction of a second after the [[big-bang|Big Bang]], a hot, dense phase theorized to have been preceded by a period of accelerated expansion known as Cosmic Inflation [Liddle et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..126...31L/abstract). Inflation stretched microscopic quantum fluctuations to macroscopic scales, seeding the primordial [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]] that later evolved into the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] under gravitational instability [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract).

Following inflation, the Universe entered the [[radiation-dominated-era|Radiation-dominated era]]. During this epoch, it was filled with a primordial soup of quarks and leptons [Turner et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RvMPS..71..145T/abstract). A key unresolved consequence of the Big Bang is the apparent baryon asymmetry; despite predictions of early matter-antimatter equilibrium, gamma-ray astronomy places severe constraints on the presence of substantial baryonic antimatter in the observable Universe [von Ballmoos et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014HyInt.228..100V/abstract). As adiabatic cooling progressed, the Universe's thermal history was punctuated by specific particle and nuclear reactions. Big Bang Nucleosynthesis occurred when the temperature dropped sufficiently to allow protons and neutrons to fuse, yielding a primordial chemical composition consisting primarily of hydrogen (H), deuterium ($^2$H), and helium ($^3$He, $^4$He), alongside trace amounts of lithium ($^7$Li) [Bonifacio et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..265...81B/abstract), [Galindo Uribarri et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220805518G/abstract).

For the first several hundred thousand years, the Universe remained a hot, opaque plasma. When the temperature fell to $T \sim 4000$ K (roughly $t \sim 2 \times 10^5$ years), the [[energy-density-cosmology|Energy density (cosmology)]] of relativistic particles dropped below that of non-relativistic matter. During this epoch, known as Recombination, electrons and protons combined to form neutral hydrogen atoms at a redshift of $z \approx 1100$ [Zaroubi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1002.2667Z/abstract). The corresponding drastic drop in free electron density decoupled photons from baryonic matter, making the Universe transparent. These freely streaming photons are observed today as the highly isotropic [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] [Hamilton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SHPMP..46...70H/abstract).

The period following Recombination is termed the "Dark Ages," defined by a lack of luminous astrophysical sources. To directly study this unlit era and the subsequent Cosmic Dawn, researchers are increasingly turning to HI (21-cm) cosmology, mapping the abundance and distribution of neutral hydrogen to probe the onset of early structures [Padmanabhan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108113P/abstract). This era ended with the gravitational collapse of the earliest overdensities into the first stars. Theoretical models predict that the very first "zero-metal" (Population III) stars may have formed as early as $z \sim 65$, roughly 30 million years after the [[big-bang|Big Bang]] [Naoz et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.373L..98N/abstract). However, despite extensive multi-decade searches, these primordial zero-metal stars have yet to be directly detected. As a result, there remains ambiguity in the literature regarding the exact formation mechanisms and mass distributions of the earliest stellar populations [Bonifacio et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..265...81B/abstract).

The radiation emitted by these first stars and the earliest galaxies triggered the Epoch of Reionization, marking the second major phase transition of cosmic hydrogen [Zaroubi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1002.2667Z/abstract). Occurring around redshift $z \sim 10$, the intergalactic medium was progressively ionized by escaping ultraviolet photons. Studies suggest that energetic feedback from low-mass galaxies in the early Universe was a critical driver for allowing this ionizing radiation to escape into the intergalactic medium [Erb et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.523..169E/abstract). By the time the Universe was approximately 500 million years old, this phase transition was largely complete, leaving the diffuse intergalactic medium in the highly ionized state that persists today. Following reionization, cosmic star formation continued to evolve; observations of gamma-ray attenuation by the Extragalactic Background Light (EBL) have successfully reconstructed this star formation history, revealing that it peaked around $z \sim 2$ [Fermi-LAT Collaboration et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Sci...362.1031F/abstract). During these active epochs, galaxy morphology also evolved significantly over time; for instance, at redshifts near $z \approx 1$, approximately 30% of galaxies appear too peculiar to fit into standard classification systems [Abraham et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001Sci...293.1273A/abstract). Over cosmic time, the chemical evolution of the Universe has resulted in an overall mean metal abundance that is remarkably close to the solar value [Edmunds et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997MNRAS.292..733E/abstract).

## Expansion Dynamics and the Deceleration Parameter

The kinematics of the Universe's [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] are governed by the time evolution of the cosmic scale factor, $a(t)$. To characterize this evolution independently of a specific dynamic model, cosmologists parameterize the expansion using two fundamental observables: the Hubble parameter $H(t) \equiv \dot{a}/a$, where $\dot{a} > 0$ designates an expanding universe, and the deceleration parameter $q_0$, which relies on higher time derivatives of the scale factor to quantify the deviation from a linear [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] [Bolotin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150200811B/abstract). Historically, theoretical frameworks assumed that the mutual gravitational attraction of matter—both baryonic and [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]]—would continually retard the expansion, necessitating an ultimately decelerating cosmos ($q_0 > 0$). However, multiple independent probes, including Type Ia supernovae, [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] (CMB) anisotropies, and baryon acoustic oscillations, have demonstrated that the expansion of the Universe is currently accelerating, implying $q_0 < 0$ [Bennett et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006Natur.440.1126B/abstract), [Blake et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.1725B/abstract).

Within the standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], the Universe underwent a transition from a decelerating, matter-dominated phase to an accelerating, dark-energy-dominated phase at late cosmic times. Dark energy acts as a ubiquitous fluid with negative pressure, driving the scale factor to accelerate ($\ddot{a} > 0$). Utilizing geometrical analyses of large-scale galaxy clustering, such as the Alcock-Paczynski test, the [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] has been measured with 10-15% precision across the redshift range $0.1 < z < 0.9$. These measurements solidly confirm the reality of [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]] and are fully consistent with a cosmological constant [Blake et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.1725B/abstract). Despite this phenomenological success, the physical nature of the accelerating agent remains undetermined, and the literature actively debates whether this phenomenon requires a new vacuum energy component or a modification to General Relativity [Avelino et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Symm....8...70A/abstract). Furthermore, high-precision local measurements of the expansion rate ($H_0$) exhibit a statistically significant tension with the value inferred from early-universe CMB data, reinforcing doubts about the completeness of the base model [Kumar Aluri et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40i4001K/abstract).

While cosmic expansion is conventionally inferred from statistical snapshots at various redshifts, next-generation observatories will enable "real-time cosmology" by directly measuring the change in the [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] over human timescales. Known as the redshift drift, this technique tracks the secular change in the redshift of a distant galaxy as the Universe expands between the time of light emission and its eventual observation [Roos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008741R/abstract). Because the redshift drift is directly proportional to the difference between the Hubble parameter at the source's redshift and $H_0$, it offers a strictly kinematic, model-independent measurement of the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]].

In a standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], the expected frequency shift is exceedingly small. For neutral hydrogen (HI) 21-cm emission from distant galaxies, the anticipated drift is on the order of $0.1$ Hz over a baseline of $12$ years [Kloeckner et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015aska.confE..27K/abstract). Despite the extreme instrumental stability required to measure such a minuscule signal, facilities like the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) and upcoming Extremely Large Telescopes (ELTs) are projected to achieve the necessary sensitivity and survey volumes to statistically detect this drift [Kloeckner et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015aska.confE..27K/abstract), [Roos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008741R/abstract). Such a direct measurement will serve as a definitive test of cosmic kinematics and the temporal stability of the expansion rate.

## The Cosmic Web and Large-Scale Structure

On scales much larger than individual galaxies, the spatial distribution of matter in the Universe exhibits a highly organized, cellular morphology known as the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] [Zeldovich et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982Natur.300..407Z/abstract). This large-scale structure consists of dense galaxy clusters (knots) connected by elongated filaments and flattened sheets, all of which frame vast, severely underdense regions called voids [Zeldovich et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982Natur.300..407Z/abstract), [van de Weygaert et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999mdrg.conf..341V/abstract). Detailed analyses of the cosmic web reveal that its statistical complexity is not uniform; the highest complexity is localized in the peripheries of galaxy clusters and filaments, quantifying the immense theoretical difficulty of predicting energy evolution within the intragalactic medium [Vazza et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.5447V/abstract). To map these complex structures, comparison between point-process algorithms like the Bisous model and kinematic techniques such as the velocity shear web shows good agreement in tracing the filamentary scaffolds of the local Universe [Libeskind et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453L.108L/abstract).

The prevailing paradigm for the origin of this network is [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]], a process driven by gravitational instability. Small, primordial [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]] generated in the early Universe grow over time. In the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]], [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] dictates the dynamics of this process because it is collisionless and dominates the cosmic mass budget. As the Universe expands, overdense regions collapse under their own gravity to form virialized dark matter halos [White et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987Natur.330..451W/abstract), [Springel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005Natur.435..629S/abstract). Baryonic matter falls into these deepening potential wells, eventually cooling and condensing to form stars and galaxies. In addition to gravity and dark matter, the role of astrophysical magnetic fields and magnetohydrodynamics is increasingly recognized as a vital factor in shaping the evolution of galaxies and the thermodynamics of the wider intergalactic medium [Shukurov et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021amff.book.....S/abstract).

Massive redshift surveys, such as the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey and the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, alongside high-resolution $N$-body simulations, have confirmed that the statistical properties of this clustering—often quantified via the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] and the two-point correlation function—are broadly consistent with a universe dominated by dark energy and cold dark matter [Colless et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004mmu..symp..196C/abstract), [Springel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005Natur.435..629S/abstract). While luminous galaxies reside primarily in the dense knots and filaments, a substantial fraction of the [[cosmic-baryon-density|Cosmic baryon density]] is distributed along the filaments in the form of a diffuse, highly ionized plasma known as the Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM) [Kaastra et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.2324K/abstract). Recent X-ray power spectrum analyses have provided high-confidence indirect detections of this intergalactic gas, confirming its critical role in tracing the dark matter scaffolding [Cerini et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230714411C/abstract). Complementing these high-energy views, upcoming facilities like the next-generation Very Large Array (ngVLA) will trace the cold molecular gas reservoirs in the high-redshift circumgalactic medium, illuminating the cold baryon cycle critical to structure evolution [Emonts et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ASPC..517..587E/abstract).

The largest coherent features within the cosmic web are superclusters, which can be defined dynamically as "basins of attraction" that govern local matter flows. For instance, mapping of the local velocity field delineates the [[laniakea-supercluster|Laniakea Supercluster]], spanning a volume of approximately $2 \times 10^6 \, (h^{-1} \mathrm{Mpc})^3$ [Valade et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1610V/abstract), [Dupuy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...678A.176D/abstract). The spatial distribution of rich superclusters frequently exhibits a quasi-regular pattern, with a characteristic separation distance of $120-140 \, h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Einasto et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997Natur.385..139E/abstract), [Einasto et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522082E/abstract). Conversely, the vast voids framing these structures can be dynamically understood through the concept of effective gravitational repulsion, where primordial density 'dimples' act to evacuate matter and carve out the large-scale underdense regions observed today [Piran et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997GReGr..29.1363P/abstract).

However, the literature presents an active and growing tension regarding the maximum scale of these superstructures. The [[fiducial-cosmological-model|Fiducial cosmological model]] predicts an upper limit to the size of gravitationally bound structures based on the [[homogeneity-scale|Homogeneity scale]] dictated by the cosmological principle. Yet, recent observations have identified hyper-scale structures that are an order of magnitude larger than those predicted by standard $\Lambda$CDM simulations [Dupuy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...678A.176D/abstract). A notable example is the newly discovered "Quipu" superstructure, which spans over $400 \ \mathrm{Mpc}$ in length with an estimated mass of $\sim 2 \times 10^{17} \ M_\odot$ [Boehringer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250119236B/abstract). The existence of such immense features—along with others like the Sloan Great Wall—raises unresolved questions about whether these structures represent extreme but permissible statistical outliers, or if they signify a genuine breakdown of large-scale spatial homogeneity [Kumar Aluri et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40i4001K/abstract), [Sarkar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240703042S/abstract).

## Clustering Statistics and Cosmological Probes

The transition from qualitative morphology to precision statistical cosmology has driven the development of rigorous tools to analyze the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] [Strauss et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.395A...9S/abstract). The standard paradigm assumes that the observed [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] arose from primordial [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]] that were amplified by gravitational instability over cosmic time [Springel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005Natur.435..629S/abstract). 

To quantify this structure, cosmologists rely on second-order spatial statistics: the two-point correlation function in real space and its Fourier transform, the [[cosmological-power-spectrum|Cosmological power spectrum]] [Martínez et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009LNP...665..269M/abstract), [Bernardeau et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhR...367....1B/abstract). These statistics measure the amplitude of clustering as a function of spatial scale, allowing for precise constraints on the [[non-relativistic-matter-density-parameter|Non-relativistic matter density parameter]] ($\Omega_m$) and the linear bias parameter ($b$) that relates the luminous galaxy distribution to the underlying mass. Furthermore, information entropy is increasingly used as a statistical tool to quantify phase correlations, allowing cosmologists to disentangle the linear effects of initial ripples from the nonlinear interactions that drive the formation of filaments and clusters [Coles et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000Natur.406..376C/abstract). Measurements of redshift-space distortions, which manifest as anisotropies in the clustering, can further constrain the growth rate of structure. For instance, data from the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey yielded a distortion parameter:
$$ \beta = \frac{\Omega_m^{0.6}}{b} = 0.43 \pm 0.07 $$
favouring a low-density Universe where matter makes up only a fraction of the critical density [Peacock et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001Natur.410..169P/abstract).

A critical feature embedded within the cosmological power spectrum is the signature of Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO). These oscillations reflect the sound horizon at the epoch of recombination and provide a robust "standard ruler" in the late-time Universe [Nichol et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008GReGr..40..249N/abstract). By scaling a template for the two-point correlation function to find the best-fitting dilation parameter ($\alpha$), surveys can independently constrain the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] of the Universe and test models of dark energy [Hansen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519..799H/abstract), [Blake et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.1725B/abstract). Another powerful statistical and observational tool is the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect, which serves as a probe of the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] and the distributions and dynamics of hot intracluster plasma [Kitayama et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PTEP.2014fB111K/abstract). Measurements of the SZ effect can be used to reconstruct the distance-redshift relation and map the spatial distribution of hot gas within deep cluster potential wells [Kitayama et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PTEP.2014fB111K/abstract).

Because galaxies are biased tracers of the underlying [[mass-density|Mass density]], weak gravitational lensing has emerged as an essential complementary probe. By measuring the coherent distortion (shear) of background galaxy shapes caused by foreground mass distributions, weak lensing maps the dark matter potential directly, independent of the complexities of galaxy formation [Bahcall et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.439.2505B/abstract), [Bernardeau et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhR...367....1B/abstract). Weak lensing observations on scales up to $\sim 22 \ h^{-1} \text{ Mpc}$ indicate $\Omega_m \approx 0.26 \pm 0.02$, closely aligning with the $\sim 25\%$ [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] fraction expected in the standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] [Bahcall et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.439.2505B/abstract), [Rees et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003RSPTA.361.2427R/abstract).

To bridge linear perturbation theory and the highly nonlinear, late-time structure of the Universe, researchers rely heavily on large-volume N-body simulations. Groundbreaking efforts, such as the Millennium simulation (following $2160^3$ particles in a volume 2.23 billion light-years on a side) [Springel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005Natur.435..629S/abstract) and the MareNostrum Universe (over 2 billion particles in a $500 \ h^{-1} \text{ Mpc}$ box) [Gottloeber et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..8289G/abstract), demonstrate that numerical evolutions of dark matter architectures successfully reproduce observed filamentary networks, voids, and halo populations. To bridge these dark matter halo merger trees with luminous observations, researchers frequently employ empirical models, such as the UniverseMachine framework, which apply constrained galaxy formation physics directly to simulated dark matter architectures [Behroozi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ascl.soft02011B/abstract). 

Despite the broad success of these statistical and observational probes, the literature reflects emerging and unresolved tensions. Precise local measurements of the [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] (the $H_0$ tension) and the amplitude of mass fluctuations ($f\sigma_8$) occasionally diverge from predictions calibrated by early-Universe data. While the cause remains highly ambiguous, these anomalies have prompted continuous scrutiny of the standard model, inspiring debate over whether they stem from unresolved observational systematics or require fundamentally new physics governing the growth of structure [Kumar Aluri et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40i4001K/abstract), [Lapi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205823L/abstract).

## Cosmography of the Local Universe

The cosmography of the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]] relies heavily on the framework of near-field cosmology, which aims to reconstruct the three-dimensional mass distribution and the [[cosmic-velocity-field|Cosmic velocity field]] from observations of nearby galaxies. In the linear and quasi-linear regimes of structure formation, the peculiar velocities of galaxies are directly coupled to the underlying matter [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]]. Because perturbations are amplified by attracting matter, mapping the velocity field allows cosmologists to infer the total [[mass-density|Mass density]]—including non-baryonic dark matter—without relying exclusively on luminous tracers. Using extensive distance and redshift compilations like the Cosmicflows catalogs, researchers employ Hamiltonian Monte Carlo algorithms and constrained numerical models to reconstruct the quasi-linear density and velocity fields of our local volume [Hoffman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018NatAs...2..680H/abstract), [Valade et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1610V/abstract).

This dynamical approach allows cosmologists to define superclusters not merely as static overdensities, but as "basins of attraction" within a watershed framework defined by the gravitational velocity field [Dupuy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...678A.176D/abstract). Historically, local mass flows were attributed primarily to the Great Attractor, a massive density peak dominating the kinematics of the immediate cosmic neighborhood [Dressler et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991Natur.350..391D/abstract). Modern mapping has largely subsumed this structure into the [[laniakea-supercluster|Laniakea Supercluster]], our home supercluster, which spans an estimated volume of $2 \times 10^6 (h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc})^3$ [Dupuy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...678A.176D/abstract). 

However, boundaries in the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] are highly sensitive to the depth and precision of the velocity data. Recent analyses utilizing the Cosmicflows-4 catalog, which probes out to a redshift of $z \approx 0.1$ (velocities up to $\sim 30,000\ \mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}$), suggest a slight probabilistic preference for Laniakea actually operating as a sub-region of the much larger Shapley basin of attraction [Valade et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1610V/abstract). 

The same velocity field inversions that identify attractors also locate divergent regions, or "repellers," which correspond to vast cosmic voids where matter is flowing outward. Analyses of the local field have dynamically identified the central repellers of the [[bo-tes-void|Boötes Void]] and Sculptor void. Furthermore, these kinematic maps reveal that previously distinct underdense regions, such as the Dipole and Cold Spot repellers, may actually constitute a single, gigantic contiguous void entity [Dupuy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...678A.176D/abstract).

The empirical mapping of the local Universe presents a critical test for the standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]]. While constrained simulations (such as the CLUES project) demonstrate that the standard framework can successfully reproduce many local features [Yepes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014NewAR..58....1Y/abstract), the immense scale of dynamically defined superclusters raises outstanding theoretical questions. Several watershed superclusters observed in the local velocity field appear to be an order of magnitude larger than the theoretical superstructures typically predicted by $\Lambda$CDM cosmological simulations, highlighting a potential tension between local cosmography and standard hierarchical structure formation [Dupuy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...678A.176D/abstract).

## Alternative Cosmological Models and Modified Gravity

The standard [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] successfully describes a wide array of cosmological observations but requires that roughly 95% of the Universe's mass-energy budget consists of unidentified dark matter and dark energy [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract), [Tortora et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JPhCS.354a2021T/abstract). The lack of direct detection of these components, combined with persistent observational tensions such as the $H_0$ discrepancy, has motivated the development of [[alternative-cosmological-models|Alternative cosmological models]] that modify General Relativity or relax the strict spatial symmetries of the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric [Kumar Aluri et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40i4001K/abstract), [Avelino et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Symm....8...70A/abstract).

**Modified Gravity and Low-Acceleration Dynamics**
To explain galactic rotation curves and the [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]] without invoking a dark sector, several modifications to Newtonian dynamics and General Relativity have been explored. Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) and related relativistic extensions, such as Modified Gravity (MOG), alter the laws of gravity at low accelerations. MOG formulations have been shown to provide competitive fits to the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]] temperature anisotropies, the [[angular-power-spectrum|Angular power spectrum]] of galaxies, and supernova luminosity-distance data without the need for exotic dark matter, while simultaneously predicting a vacuum energy term that drives an accelerating expansion [Moffat et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0710.0364M/abstract).

However, translating MOND into a rigorous cosmological framework presents challenges. Cosmologies based on MOND reproduce the standard thermal history and primordial nucleosynthesis during the [[radiation-dominated-era|Radiation-dominated era]], but diverge significantly once matter dominates. These models predict non-uniform cosmic expansion and substantial inhomogeneities out to a large fraction of the Hubble radius [Sanders et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.296.1009S/abstract). Furthermore, the stability of MOND-based galaxies has been questioned. Alternative frameworks like Modified Gravitational Attraction (MOGA) suggest that replacing Newton's inverse-square law with a simple inverse attraction term ($\propto 1/r$) naturally stabilizes galactic disks and yields the observed flat rotation curves [Toxvaerd et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024EPJP..139..395T/abstract). Other approaches avoid modifying the fundamental gravitational action entirely, instead proposing that graviton-graviton interactions could sufficiently increase the gravitational binding of large systems to mimic dark matter effects [Deur et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhLB..676...21D/abstract).

**Inhomogeneous Cosmological Models and Backreaction**
A distinct class of alternatives retains General Relativity but discards the assumption of large-scale homogeneity. Historically, topological defects such as cosmic strings were also extensively explored as an alternative or supplementary mechanism for generating the primordial [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]] required for structure formation, before inflation became the standard paradigm [Kibble et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986RSPTA.320..565K/abstract). Similarly, global field ordering and the breaking of global symmetries, such as in the nontopological texture scenario, were historically investigated as alternative mechanisms to inflation for generating scale-invariant primordial perturbations [Pen et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994PhRvD..49..692P/abstract). Because Einstein's equations are highly nonlinear, the presence of small-scale structures—such as galaxies, clusters, and voids—theoretically "backreacts" on the large-scale dynamics. In standard simulations, this small-scale structure is smoothed out. However, solving Einstein's equations directly on inhomogeneous distributions suggests that the backreaction of these nonlinearities might mimic a [[positive-cosmological-constant|Positive cosmological constant]], potentially explaining the accelerating expansion without dark energy [Macpherson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191013380M/abstract), [Krasinski et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997icm..book.....K/abstract), [Bolejko et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009suem.book.....B/abstract). 

Recent theoretical efforts have also explored stochastic inhomogeneous models to address observational tensions. The $\eta$CDM model, for example, introduces a stochastic evolution of large-scale patches (with characteristic sizes of $10-50 \ h^{-1}$ Mpc) to capture the complex gravitational processes of structure formation. This ensemble-averaged evolution produces an "emergent" dark energy with a time-dependent equation of state, which can simultaneously alleviate both the $H_0$ and the $f\sigma_8$ structure growth tensions [Lapi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205823L/abstract), [Lapi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...04..015L/abstract). Other radical departures, such as models redefining the cosmological time flow for local observers, have been shown to fit distant supernova data and galaxy cluster velocity dispersions without a dark sector, while predicting an older [[cosmological-age|Cosmological age]] of 15.4 to 16.5 Gyr [Magain et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.1110M/abstract).

Ultimately, the literature remains conflicting. While modified gravity and inhomogeneous models often succeed in reproducing local rotation curves or specific subsets of data, constructing a single alternative framework that simultaneously matches all global precision probes—such as CMB isotropy and the full [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]]—remains a major open challenge in modern cosmology.

## Open Questions and Future Frontiers

While the [[lambda-cdm-model|Lambda CDM model]] successfully describes a spatially flat universe dominated by dark energy and cold dark matter, the transition into the era of precision cosmology has revealed statistically significant discrepancies between theoretical predictions and observational data. A primary theoretical challenge lies in understanding the Dark Sector; the exact physical properties of the particles constituting [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] and the fundamental physics driving late-time cosmic acceleration remain completely unidentified [Spergel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...347.1100S/abstract).

Beyond the composition of the [[energy-content|Energy content]] of the universe, several observational anomalies suggest that the standard model may require modification. The most prominent of these is the Hubble Tension, which describes the statistically significant discrepancy in the [[expansion-rate|Expansion rate]] ($H_0$) when derived from early-universe probes, such as the [[cosmic-microwave-background|Cosmic microwave background]], versus direct local measurements using late-universe distance ladders [Kumar Aluri et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40i4001K/abstract). Accompanying this is the $f\sigma_8$ tension, which highlights a mismatch in the amplitude of [[density-perturbations|Density perturbations]] and the growth rate of cosmic structure between the early and late universe [Lapi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205823L/abstract).

The foundational assumption of large-scale homogeneity and isotropy is also facing renewed scrutiny. The discovery of massive spatial features, such as the $400 \text{ Mpc}$ "Quipu" superstructure, presents cosmic structures that are an order of magnitude larger than those typically predicted by standard $\Lambda$CDM simulations [Boehringer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250119236B/abstract). The literature currently lacks a consensus on whether these massive formations and the associated cosmological tensions reflect unrecognized observational systematics or a genuine breakdown of the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) paradigm [Kumar Aluri et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40i4001K/abstract).

Furthermore, the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has opened a new frontier in the study of the early universe, yielding results that strain the standard timeline of [[hierarchical-structure-formation|Hierarchical structure formation]]. JWST has identified massive, highly evolved galaxies at high redshifts ($z \sim 15$) that existed mere hundreds of millions of years after the [[big-bang|Big Bang]] [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.3385G/abstract). The existence of such systems has led to vigorous debate. While some researchers argue that the rapid formation of massive Population III stars, super-Eddington accretion rates, modified power spectra, or primordial black hole seeds can accommodate these galaxies within the standard framework [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.3385G/abstract), others propose radical solutions to resolve the conflict, such as covarying coupling constants, tired-light hybrid models, or a dramatically revised [[cosmological-age|Cosmological age]] of $26.7 \text{ Gyr}$ [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.3385G/abstract). 

To resolve these open questions, cosmologists are relying on next-generation observatories. The Euclid Mission represents a major leap forward in this effort. Designed to survey $15,000 \text{ deg}^2$ of the darkest sky and reconstruct the three-dimensional distribution of over 10 billion sources, Euclid will provide unprecedented constraints on the [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] of the universe and the evolution of the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] [Tutusaus et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023chep.confE..95T/abstract). By mapping dark matter through weak gravitational lensing and measuring galaxy clustering, the Euclid Mission will strictly test the $\Lambda$CDM framework against alternative physical mechanisms—such as modified gravity or stochastic $\eta$CDM models—potentially uncovering the true nature of the Dark Sector and the ultimate fate of the universe [Tutusaus et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023chep.confE..95T/abstract) [Lapi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205823L/abstract). Additionally, upcoming massive spectroscopic redshift surveys, such as those planned for the Maunakea Spectroscopic Explorer (MSE), will provide dense mapping of the cosmos to place unprecedented constraints on the [[total-neutrino-mass|Total neutrino mass]] and early-universe inflationary physics [The MSE Science Team et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190404907T/abstract). Concurrently, the frontier of "The Gravitational Universe" will be expanded by future space-based gravitational wave observatories like eLISA, which will be capable of probing the merger dynamics of massive seed black holes at redshifts up to $z \sim 20$, offering entirely new windows into the earliest epochs of structure formation [eLISA Consortium et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1305.5720E/abstract).
