A **galaxy redshift survey** is an observational program in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] designed to map the [[three-dimensional-galaxy-surveys|three-dimensional spatial distribution]] of matter in the universe. By systematically measuring the [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]] or estimating the photometric redshift of thousands to millions of galaxies, astronomers can infer their radial distances via [[galaxy-clustering-and-redshift-estimation|Galaxy Clustering and Redshift Estimation]]. Because galaxies act as biased luminous tracers of the underlying [[galaxy-density-fields|Galaxy density fields]], converting their two-dimensional angular positions and redshifts into a three-dimensional coordinate system provides a direct [[galaxy-mapping|map]] of the cosmos. These [[three-dimensional-galaxy-surveys|Three-dimensional galaxy surveys]] are the primary empirical tool for quantifying the [[low-redshift-large-scale-structure|Low-redshift large-scale structure]], allowing researchers to trace the cosmic web, chart cosmic expansion, and constrain fundamental cosmological parameters.

Historically, redshift surveys revolutionized cosmology by revealing that galaxies are not distributed randomly. Foundational efforts like the [[cfa-redshift-survey|CfA Redshift Survey]] demonstrated that the universe exhibits a "bubble-like" topology, with galaxies residing in dense clusters and elongated filaments that enclose vast, empty voids [de Lapparent et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...302L...1D/abstract). Subsequent massive campaigns, such as the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]] [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract) and the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]], provided the statistically representative volumes required to rigorously measure the power spectrum of the galaxy distribution. Furthermore, the observed radial positions in these surveys encode local peculiar velocities induced by gravitational infall. These non-Hubble motions create [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]] [Kaiser et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987MNRAS.227....1K/abstract), which modern surveys leverage to break degeneracies between the matter density ($\Omega_m$) and the linear growth rate of structure, thereby providing stringent tests of General Relativity and the nature of dark energy.

Beyond cosmological parameter estimation, galaxy redshift surveys are essential for understanding galaxy assembly and environmental quenching. By characterizing the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|Galaxy Bias and Clustering Statistics]] across different epochs, researchers track how [[galaxy-property-distributions|galaxy properties evolve]] over time and vary with local density. While next-generation experiments and novel techniques like [[intensity-mapping|Intensity mapping]] continue to push the boundaries of survey volume and depth, several critical tensions remain unresolved. Chief among these open questions is the true [[homogeneity-scale|Homogeneity scale]] of the universe; recent analyses of modern massive catalogs suggest persistent structural power and extreme-value density fluctuations at scales up to $400 \ h^{-1} {\rm Mpc}$, challenging standard assumptions of rapid spatial homogenization [Sylos Labini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251121585S/abstract). Additionally, researchers face ongoing theoretical and observational challenges in determining whether environmental quenching is fundamentally causal or a reflection of underlying mass assembly, and in successfully disentangling intrinsic [[redshift-distribution|redshift distributions]] from galaxy bias when using [[cross-correlation-analysis|Cross-correlation analysis]] to calibrate faint photometric datasets.

## Cosmological Foundations

Galaxy redshift surveys form the cornerstone of [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] and cosmology by mapping the three-dimensional distribution of matter in the universe. The foundational premise of these surveys rests on Hubble's Law, which relates the recession velocity $v$ of a galaxy to its radial distance $r$ via the Hubble constant $H_0$:
$$v = H_0 r$$

Observationally, this expansion velocity is derived from the [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]] $z$ of the source. For relatively nearby galaxies, the radial distance can be approximated linearly as $r \approx c z / H_0$, while at cosmological distances, full relativistic distance-redshift relations are required to trace comoving coordinates. Unlike intrinsic distance indicators (such as the Tully-Fisher relation or [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|stellar velocity dispersions]]), which suffer from absolute uncertainties that scale proportionally with distance, redshifts can be measured efficiently and accurately for massive samples of galaxies. This makes redshift inference the optimal tool for 3D [[galaxy-mapping|Galaxy mapping]].

While redshift provides a vital proxy for distance, the observed redshift is actually a combination of the cosmological expansion and the galaxy's peculiar velocity $v_{\rm pec}$, which is induced by local gravitational fields:
$$cz = H_0 r + v_{\rm pec}$$

This additive velocity component displaces galaxies along the line of sight in redshift space. Because these peculiar velocities are correlated with the underlying matter density field, their inclusion systematically distorts the three-dimensional spatial distribution of galaxies observed in surveys. These kinematic signatures, known as [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]], act as robust probes for estimating fundamental cosmological parameters and are treated in detail below.

## Historical Evolution: From Pencil Beams to All-Sky Maps

The systematic mapping of the three-dimensional [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]] began with targeted pencil-beam and shallow wide-angle surveys. The [[cfa-redshift-survey|CfA Redshift Survey]] marked a watershed moment in extragalactic astronomy by providing the first extensive three-dimensional maps of the local galaxy distribution. Early results from this survey revealed that galaxies are not uniformly distributed but are instead organized into a "bubble-like" topology, residing on thin sheets and filaments that surround vast underdense [[2d-void-definitions|voids]] with diameters of $\sim 25-50 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [de Lapparent et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...302L...1D/abstract). However, these pioneering surveys highlighted a fundamental limitation: the large-scale structures discovered, such as the CfA Great Wall, were comparable in scale to the survey volume itself [de Lapparent et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ApJ...332...44D/abstract). Consequently, these early catalogs did not constitute "fair samples" of the universe, rendering statistical measures like the [[redshift-space-correlation-function|Redshift-space correlation function]] highly sensitive to [[sample-variance|Sample variance]]. Serving as a bridge between early shallow wide-angle surveys and deeper pencil beams, the ESO Slice Project (ESP) galaxy redshift survey mapped the galaxy distribution at intermediate depths, detecting significant large-scale structures at $z \sim 0.1$ [Vettolani et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997A&A...325..954V/abstract). 

To probe beyond local anomalies, the community transitioned toward deeper, densely sampled slice geometries. The [[las-campanas-redshift-survey|Las Campanas Redshift Survey]] (LCRS) represented a significant technological and observational leap. Utilizing fiber-fed multi-object spectroscopy, the LCRS obtained over 26,000 galaxy redshifts out to a median velocity of $\sim 30,000 \ \mathrm{km \ s^{-1}}$ [Shectman et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...470..172S/abstract). The LCRS confirmed that the alternating pattern of voids and walls persisted on scales of $5000 \ \mathrm{km \ s^{-1}}$, while also demonstrating that the [[galaxy-density-fields|galaxy density field]] begins to appear smoother on scales $\gg 100 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Tucker et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph..2023T/abstract). Parallel to these optical surveys, early infrared initiatives like the IRAS Faint Source Survey (FSS) provided a full-sky view of clustering that preferentially traced spiral galaxy populations [Oliver et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.280..673O/abstract).

The true paradigm shift from pencil-beam and slice geometries to representative [[all-sky-surveys|All-sky surveys]] arrived with the advent of massively multiplexed spectrographs. The Two-Degree Field Galaxy Redshift Survey (yielding the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]]) and the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (SDSS) revolutionized the field by measuring hundreds of thousands to over a million [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]]s in a single program [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract). The 2dFGRS utilized an adaptive tiling algorithm to achieve a highly uniform sampling rate over $2000$ square degrees, ultimately obtaining spectra for $\sim 220,000$ galaxies with a median depth of $z \approx 0.11$ [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract). Following the success of optical all-sky mapping, the [[6df-galaxy-survey|6dF Galaxy Survey]] extended these efforts into the near-infrared, providing a comprehensive catalog of the local universe out to $z \sim 0.15$ with over 150,000 redshifts [Jones et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.355..747J/abstract). Synthesis efforts like the 2M++ galaxy redshift catalogue later combined these disparate datasets, integrating 2MASS, 6dFGS, and SDSS data to comprehensively map the local density field out to $200 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Lavaux et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2840L/abstract).

By encompassing statistically representative volumes, these massive [[redshift-catalogs|Redshift catalogs]] overcame the limitations of early surveys. They provided the necessary statistical power to compute the power spectrum with unprecedented precision and measure the redshift-space distortion parameter $\beta = \Omega_m^{0.6}/b$ [Peacock et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001Natur.410..169P/abstract). Analysis of the 2dFGRS dataset using higher-order statistics yielded an independent measurement of a low-density universe with $\Omega_m \approx 0.27 \pm 0.06$ [Verde et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002astro.ph.12311V/abstract), helping to firmly establish the $\Lambda$CDM standard model and cementing the role of large-scale surveys as fundamental cosmological probes.

## Survey Design and Redshift Measurement Techniques

[[target-selection|Target selection]] is a critical first step in designing a galaxy redshift survey. Most surveys of the local universe are flux-limited, meaning they target all galaxies brighter than a specified apparent magnitude in a given photometric band. Because such surveys inherently suffer from [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Malmquist bias]], researchers often extract [[volume-limited-samples|Volume-limited samples]]—selecting only galaxies brighter than a specific absolute magnitude limit—to ensure a uniform [[selection-function|Selection function]] across the survey volume. At higher redshifts ($z > 1$), [[target-selection|Target selection]] frequently relies on broad-band color cuts to identify candidates prior to spectroscopic follow-up, such as the selection of Lyman-break galaxies at $z \sim 3$ [Steidel et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..146..428S/abstract).

Maximizing the efficiency of multi-object spectrographs requires sophisticated tiling algorithms to assign optical fibers or slits to target galaxies. Modern surveys employ adaptive or heuristic algorithms to ensure high completeness while overcoming instrumental constraints like fiber collisions. For example, the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]] utilized an adaptive tiling algorithm to achieve a highly uniform sampling rate of 93% over its 2000 square degree survey region [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract). Similarly, the [[gama-survey|GAMA survey]] implemented a heuristic greedy algorithm for optimal tiling, which rapidly improved spatial uniformity and corrected for spatial biases introduced by the 2dF spectrograph [Robotham et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PASA...27...76R/abstract).

For [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]] determination, automated pipelines calculate the radial velocity of a galaxy by identifying emission and absorption features. Typically, observed spectra are cross-correlated against a library of high signal-to-noise empirical or synthetic templates. A prominent example is the [[autoz-cross-correlation-algorithm|AUTOZ cross-correlation algorithm]], developed for the [[gama-survey|GAMA survey]] [Baldry et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441.2440B/abstract). AUTOZ measures redshifts by cross-correlating both absorption- and emission-line spectra, clipping large deviations in high-pass-filtered data to ensure robustness against uncorrected artifacts. This method yields a single figure of merit for template matches, substantially improving reliability and reducing velocity uncertainties to a median of $33 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Baldry et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441.2440B/abstract). 

Beyond broad cosmic mapping, deep multi-wavelength surveys such as the AGN and Galaxy Evolution Survey (AGES) target specific regions like the Boötes field, providing dense spectroscopic coverage to study the interplay between active galactic nuclei (AGN) and galaxy evolution [Kochanek et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJS..200....8K/abstract). Furthermore, archival datasets remain fertile ground for ongoing searches and new discoveries; for example, theoretical studies predict that surveys like the 2dFGRS contain gravitationally lensed quasars—with estimates of around 10 lenses expected in the dataset—identifiable from overlapping emission lines, provided that selection effects are carefully modeled [Mortlock et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.321..629M/abstract), and recent visual inspections of the 6dFGS have identified thousands of broad-line AGN after carefully correcting for instrumental fiber cross-talk [Jeat Hon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008536J/abstract).

When spectroscopic follow-up is computationally or financially prohibitive, photometric redshifts provide an alternative by estimating distances from broad-band photometry. The accuracy of photometric redshifts depends strongly on the number of filters and the wavelength coverage; for instance, the [[combo-17-survey|COMBO-17 survey]] achieved an average accuracy of $\sim 3\%$ using 17 narrow optical wavebands. Recent advancements have integrated machine learning into this process; algorithms like NetZ use convolutional neural networks directly on galaxy images to predict redshifts with high precision, especially at $z > 1$ [Schuldt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...651A..55S/abstract).

To calibrate photometric [[redshift-distribution|redshift distributions]] and mitigate their inherent uncertainties, researchers increasingly employ clustering redshifts. This technique infers the redshift distribution of a photometric sample by spatially cross-correlating it against an overlapping reference sample with known spectroscopic redshifts [Schulz et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724.1305S/abstract). Tools such as the-wizz [Morrison et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467.3576M/abstract) and the stochastic order redshift technique (SORT) [Kakos et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.1857K/abstract) have been developed to robustly estimate these distributions. While clustering redshifts can effectively bypass the need for representative spectroscopic follow-up, the method is sensitive to the degeneracy between the source redshift distribution and [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy bias]] [Schulz et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724.1305S/abstract), [Johnson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4118J/abstract).

## Statistical Analysis of Galaxy Clustering

To extract cosmological parameters and describe the large-scale structure of the universe, galaxy redshift surveys employ a suite of mathematical tools that treat the spatial distribution of galaxies as a realization of a stochastic point process. Because galaxies are biased tracers of the underlying dark matter, these statistics are essential for quantifying [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|Galaxy Bias and Clustering Statistics]] and probing the physical laws governing structure formation.

**Two-Point Statistics and the Power Spectrum**
The most fundamental measure of galaxy clustering is the two-point correlation function, $\xi(r)$, which quantifies the excess probability of finding a pair of galaxies separated by a comoving distance $r$ compared to a uniform, unclustered random distribution. In practice, it is estimated by comparing the counts of galaxy pairs in the observational data, $DD(r)$, to the expected counts in a random catalog, $RR(r)$, which can be expressed conceptually as:
$$ \xi(r) = \frac{DD(r)}{RR(r)} - 1 $$
On scales smaller than roughly $10\ h^{-1}$ Mpc, the real-space correlation function of optically selected $L^*$ galaxies is well described by a power law, $\xi(r) = (r/r_0)^{-\gamma}$, with a correlation length $r_0 \approx 5.0\ h^{-1}$ Mpc and an exponent $\gamma \approx 1.7$ [Hawkins et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.11188H/abstract), [Guzzo et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998wfsc.conf...85G/abstract). 

Because peculiar velocities perturb observed radial distances, surveys directly measure the [[redshift-space-correlation-function|Redshift-space correlation function]], $\xi(s)$, which appears anisotropic. To mitigate these [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]], researchers often compute the projected correlation function, $w(r_p)$, by integrating over the line-of-sight separation $\pi$. Alternatively, the Fourier conjugate of the correlation function—the power spectrum $P(k)$—is extensively utilized to measure large-scale periodic features such as baryon acoustic oscillations and to constrain the overall matter density [Blake et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..803B/abstract).

**Higher-Order Statistics**
While two-point statistics fully characterize a Gaussian random field, gravitational instability and non-linear galaxy biasing introduce significant non-Gaussianities into [[galaxy-density-fields|Galaxy density fields]]. These deviations are captured by [[higher-order-clustering-statistics|Higher-Order Clustering Statistics]]. The [[three-point-correlation-function|Three-point correlation function]] and its Fourier equivalent, the bispectrum, provide critical independent constraints on cosmology. For instance, analysis of the bispectrum in the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey successfully broke the degeneracy between the linear galaxy bias $b$ and the matter density $\Omega_m$ (which are otherwise coupled in the redshift-distortion parameter $\beta \approx \Omega_m^{0.6}/b$), yielding an independent measurement of the matter density of $\Omega_m \approx 0.27$ [Verde et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.335..432V/abstract), [Verde et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002astro.ph.12311V/abstract). Additionally, counts-in-cells analyses have been used to robustly measure the skewness and kurtosis of the galaxy distribution [Szapudi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000MNRAS.318L..45S/abstract). Moreover, large-scale structure data from redshift surveys can be optimized to constrain early universe physics. For instance, the application of optimal redshift-dependent weights can significantly improve the signal-to-noise ratio when measuring primordial non-Gaussianity from the galaxy distribution [Mueller et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.4160M/abstract).

**Topology, Geometry, and the Homogeneity Scale**
Beyond traditional correlation functions, the geometric and topological properties of the cosmic web are rigorously quantified using Minkowski Functionals. These metrics describe the volume, surface area, mean curvature, and Euler characteristic of structural features, allowing researchers to parameterize the morphological properties—such as the shapes and sizes—of superclusters, filaments, and voids via "Shapefinders" [Sheth et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..2105S/abstract). 

A persistent debate in the statistical analysis of galaxy surveys involves the [[homogeneity-scale|Homogeneity scale]] of the universe. Standard cosmological models predict that the transition to spatial homogeneity occurs at scales around $100\ h^{-1}$ Mpc, a result supported by early deep redshift surveys [Shanks et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...274..529S/abstract). Modern massive surveys continue to test these assumptions across vastly larger volumes; for instance, analyses utilizing over a million galaxies from the SDSS DR7 have confirmed spatial homogeneity on scales reaching $\sim 2 \ h^{-1} \mathrm{Gpc}$ [Longo et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1305.0464L/abstract). However, recent analyses of the [[desi-bright-galaxy-survey|DESI Bright Galaxy Sample]] demonstrate that the conditional average density continues to exhibit power-law decay out to scales approaching $400\ h^{-1}$ Mpc. On these scales, the distribution of density fluctuations follows extreme-value statistics rather than Gaussianity, directly challenging the assumption that the local galaxy distribution rapidly transitions to homogeneity [Sylos Labini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251121585S/abstract).

## Redshift-Space Distortions and Peculiar Velocities

In observational cosmology, the measured redshift of a galaxy is not determined solely by the Hubble expansion. It also contains a Doppler component from the galaxy's peculiar velocity along the line of sight, driven by the gravitational pull of the surrounding large-scale structure. The inferred distance to a galaxy in velocity units, $c z_{\rm obs}$, is related to its true proper distance $r$ by
$$ c z_{\rm obs} = H_0 r + v_{\rm pec, \parallel} $$
where $v_{\rm pec, \parallel}$ is the line-of-sight component of the peculiar velocity. Because these peculiar velocities are correlated with the underlying matter density field, their inclusion systematically distorts the three-dimensional spatial distribution of galaxies observed in surveys. These [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]] (RSD) induce an artificial anisotropy in the otherwise isotropic real-space clustering of galaxies, offering a powerful method to probe the dynamics of cosmic structure formation.

When treating RSD in surveys covering large swaths of the sky, the traditional plane-parallel approximation breaks down. This necessitated the development of specialized coordinate systems and mathematical formalisms to accurately express wide-angle redshift distortions in arbitrary survey geometries [Szalay et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...498L...1S/abstract). Observationally, RSD manifests in two distinct regimes depending on the spatial scale observed. On small, highly non-linear scales ($r \lesssim 10 \ h^{-1} \, \mathrm{Mpc}$), the rapid, randomized virialized motions of galaxies within massive dark matter halos cause clusters to appear elongated along the line of sight in redshift space. This phenomenon, which creates stretched radial structures colloquially known as [[fingers-of-god|Fingers of God]], smears out the small-scale clustering amplitude.

Conversely, on large, linear to mildly non-linear scales, galaxies exhibit coherent infall toward massive overdensities (such as superclusters) and outflow from voids. As theoretically derived by Nick Kaiser, this large-scale infall compresses the apparent structures along the line of sight, artificially amplifying the clustering amplitude—a phenomenon known as the Kaiser effect [Kaiser et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987MNRAS.227....1K/abstract). The degree of this large-scale anisotropy in the [[redshift-space-correlation-function|Redshift-space correlation function]] is quantified by the distortion parameter $\beta$, defined as:
$$ \beta = \frac{f(\Omega_m)}{b} \approx \frac{\Omega_m^{0.6}}{b} $$
where $b$ is the linear galaxy bias and $f(\Omega_m)$ is the logarithmic growth rate of cosmic structure.

Because $\beta$ depends on both the matter density parameter $\Omega_m$ and the bias $b$, RSD provides a critical tool for breaking degeneracies inherent in galaxy clustering measurements. Historically, measuring this clustering anisotropy has allowed surveys to directly constrain the total mass density. For instance, an early analysis of the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey measured $\beta = 0.43 \pm 0.07$, favoring a low-density universe with $\Omega_m \approx 0.3$ [Peacock et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001Natur.410..169P/abstract).

In modern cosmological analyses, RSD is predominantly used to measure the parameter combination $f\sigma_8$, where $\sigma_8$ characterizes the root-mean-square amplitude of matter fluctuations. Measuring $f\sigma_8$ provides a direct, model-independent test of the growth rate of structure, offering a means to test General Relativity against modified gravity models. The [[6df-galaxy-survey|6dF Galaxy Survey]] employed K-band selected galaxies at an effective redshift $z_{\rm eff} = 0.067$ to measure $f(z_{\rm eff})\sigma_8(z_{\rm eff}) = 0.423 \pm 0.055$. This yielded a growth index of $\gamma = 0.547 \pm 0.088$, which is highly consistent with the theoretical predictions of General Relativity [Beutler et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.3430B/abstract).

At higher redshifts, the [[wigglez-survey|WiggleZ survey]] traced the evolution of the growth rate across $0.4 < z < 0.8$, demonstrating a close match to the self-consistent predictions of the standard $\Lambda$CDM model [Blake et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..803B/abstract). Similar precision mapping has been achieved by the [[2dflens-survey|2dFLenS Survey]], which constrained $\beta$ across multiple redshift bins out to $z = 0.7$ [Blake et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.4240B/abstract). Additionally, alternative methods for deriving the growth rate have been developed, such as constraining the growth rate by minimizing the spread of absolute magnitudes inferred from peculiar velocity fields derived using linear perturbation theory, further refining cosmological constraints [Nusser et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...744..193N/abstract). Furthermore, linear redshift distortions also significantly affect other cosmological probes, such as the Integrated Sachs-Wolfe (ISW) signal. If these RSD effects are omitted in cross-correlation analyses, they can potentially lead to an overestimation of the dark energy density [Rassat et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0902.1759R/abstract). By decoupling the universe's expansion history from the growth of its structural web, peculiar velocity measurements extracted from RSD remain one of the most robust cosmological probes available. Historically, mapping these local kinematic fields relied on foundational datasets like the Mark III Catalog of Galaxy Peculiar Velocities, which enabled early, rigorous analyses of [[cosmicflows|Cosmicflows]] and large-scale peculiar velocities by providing a homogeneous velocity-distance baseline [Willick et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJS..109..333W/abstract).

## The Galaxy Luminosity Function and Bias

An essential statistical descriptor of the galaxy population measured by redshift surveys is the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] (GLF), which characterizes the number density of galaxies per unit volume as a function of their intrinsic luminosity. The GLF is typically well-fitted by a [[schechter-function|Schechter function]]:
$$ \Phi(L) dL = \phi^* \left(\frac{L}{L^*}\right)^\alpha \exp\left(-\frac{L}{L^*}\right) d\left(\frac{L}{L^*}\right) $$
where $L^*$ is the characteristic luminosity, $\alpha$ is the faint-end slope, and $\phi^*$ is the normalization. Measurements from early surveys established standard optical parameters; for instance, field galaxies were found to be well-described by $\alpha \approx -1.07$ and a characteristic absolute magnitude $M^*_B \approx -19.7$ [Efstathiou et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988MNRAS.232..431E/abstract), [Loveday et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...390..338L/abstract). 

Estimating the true GLF from a redshift survey requires accounting for [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Malmquist bias]]. Because most surveys are flux-limited, intrinsically luminous galaxies can be detected out to much larger distances than faint ones. If uncorrected, this [[selection-function|Selection function]] skews the observed distribution toward brighter galaxies. To recover the intrinsic GLF, researchers often employ statistical corrections such as the [[v-vmax-test|V/Vmax test]] or construct [[volume-limited-samples|Volume-limited samples]] by restricting the analysis to a subset of galaxies intrinsically brighter than a chosen absolute magnitude limit over a specific redshift range.

The GLF not only describes galaxy abundances but also intersects with the study of large-scale structure through the concept of galaxy bias. Galaxies are not perfect tracers of the underlying dark matter distribution; rather, they form preferentially in the dense peaks of the dark matter halo field. In the linear regime, this relationship is expressed as $\delta_g = b \delta_m$, where $\delta_g$ and $\delta_m$ are the galaxy and matter density contrasts, respectively, and $b$ is the linear bias parameter. 

Detailed analyses of the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]] and similar surveys have demonstrated that optically selected $L^*$ galaxies are nearly unbiased tracers of the mass distribution on large scales, with linear bias parameters consistent with unity ($b_1 = 1.04 \pm 0.11$) and non-linear quadratic bias consistent with zero [Verde et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.335..432V/abstract). However, [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy bias]] is a strong function of [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]], particularly luminosity and color. Luminous galaxies are more strongly clustered than their fainter counterparts. For example, the relative bias scales with luminosity approximately as $b/b^* = 0.85 + 0.15(L/L^*)$ [Peacock et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AIPC..666..275P/abstract), and sub-$L^*$ galaxies can be up to three times less biased than $L^*$ galaxies [Loveday et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...468....1L/abstract). 

Clustering also depends on rest-frame color and [[galaxy-morphological-classification|Galaxy morphological classification]]. Red, early-type galaxies exhibit a higher bias and reside in denser environments than blue, late-type, star-forming galaxies [Hawkins et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.11188H/abstract). This differential clustering highlights that the physical processes quenching star formation are strongly tied to the host dark matter halo mass and local overdensity.

At higher redshifts, the bias and luminosity function evolve significantly. While the total stellar mass density in blue galaxies has remained roughly constant since $z \sim 1$, the mass density of the red sequence has approximately doubled. Furthermore, [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|high-redshift populations]] such as Lyman-break galaxies at $z \sim 3$ are observed to be very strongly biased relative to the mass distribution, confirming their status as early tracers of the most massive dark matter halos at those epochs [Steidel et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..146..428S/abstract). Standardizing these luminosity- and type-dependent biases is critical for breaking degeneracies when using redshift surveys to constrain fundamental cosmological parameters.

## High-Redshift Surveys and Galaxy Assembly

The advent of 10-meter class telescopes equipped with highly multiplexed spectrographs has extended the reach of [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]] to $z > 1$, observing the Universe when it was a fraction of its current age. These high-redshift campaigns trace [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] and the assembly of the cosmic web over the past 12 billion years. 

Because galaxies at high redshift are exceptionally faint, many deep surveys rely on multi-band photometric pre-selection to efficiently identify targets. A prominent example is the selection of the Lyman-break galaxy (LBG) population. LBGs are actively star-forming systems characterized by a relatively flat spectral energy distribution that drops sharply shortward of the rest-frame Lyman limit at $\lambda \approx 912$ \AA, owing to absorption by neutral hydrogen in the galaxy and the intergalactic medium. By targeting these color drop-outs, surveys have confirmed that LBGs at $z \sim 3$ are strongly clustered. They exhibit a comoving correlation length similar to that of present-day galaxies, demonstrating that they are highly biased tracers residing in the most massive dark matter halos at those early epochs [Steidel et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..146..428S/abstract).

The [[deep2-redshift-survey|DEEP2 Redshift Survey]] pioneered dense, high-precision spectroscopic mapping at $z \sim 1$. Utilizing the Keck DEIMOS spectrograph at a resolution of $R \sim 6000$, DEEP2 secured over 38,000 [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]] measurements down to a limiting magnitude of $R_{AB} = 24.1$ over an area of 2.8 square degrees [Newman et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJS..208....5N/abstract). Early foundational work also includes the [[cnoc-survey|CNOC2 Field Galaxy Redshift Survey]], which was one of the first major efforts to investigate the evolution of large-scale structure and galaxy populations at intermediate redshifts ($0.1 \lesssim z \lesssim 0.6$) [Yee et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJS..129..475Y/abstract). Efforts to bridge the gap between local and high-redshift regimes also include the PRIsm MUlti-object Survey (PRIMUS), which stands as the largest faint galaxy survey to $z \sim 1$. By utilizing low-dispersion prisms, PRIMUS efficiently measured approximately 130,000 redshifts [Coil et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741....8C/abstract). 

Parallel efforts on the Very Large Telescope (VLT) adopted a more inclusive target selection. The VIMOS VLT Deep Survey (VVDS) utilized a purely magnitude-limited approach ($I_{AB} \le 24.75$) to map approximately 35,000 galaxies out to $z \sim 6.7$ [Le Fèvre et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...559A..14L/abstract). Similarly, the K20 survey compiled a complete near-infrared selected sample that utilized deep VLT spectroscopy to constrain galaxy formation scenarios up to $z \sim 2$ [Cimatti et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...392..395C/abstract). This effort was subsequently expanded by the VIMOS Ultra-Deep Survey (VUDS), which targeted $\sim 10,000$ very faint galaxies ($i_{AB} \le 27$) specifically designed to bridge the historical "redshift desert" and probe the main phase of galaxy assembly between $2 < z < 6$ [Le Fèvre et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...576A..79L/abstract). At even higher redshifts, the VANDELS ESO public survey provides ultra-deep, high signal-to-noise ratio spectra for galaxies at $1 < z < 6.5$. These exquisite data are critical for determining stellar population ages and metallicities during the universe's most active star-forming epochs [Garilli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...647A.150G/abstract). Complementing these deep optical efforts, near-infrared surveys like UltraVISTA have pushed selection limits to unprecedented depths, providing accurate measurements of stellar mass functions and tracking the mass assembly of galaxies out to $z \sim 4$ [McCracken et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Msngr.154...29M/abstract).

These massive datasets have rigorously quantified the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and its dependence on rest-frame color across cosmic time. Observations reveal a strong differential evolution in the galaxy population: the total stellar mass density contained within red, passive galaxies has roughly doubled since $z \sim 1$, whereas the mass density of the blue, star-forming population has remained relatively constant. This indicates a steady quenching of star formation over time, though near-infrared selected samples indicate that a large fraction of early-type galaxies were already structurally in place by $z \sim 1.2$, exhibiting only modest space density evolution thereafter . Environmental drivers of this evolution are also manifest in the [[butcher-oemler-effect|Butcher-Oemler effect]], where intermediate-redshift clusters display a markedly increased fraction of blue, star-forming galaxies compared to their local counterparts.

While the broad strokes of large-scale structure evolution are well established, accurately measuring the precise evolution of galaxy clustering at intermediate epochs ($z \sim 0.5$) requires careful theoretical interpretation. For instance, it is important to note that the observed weak structural growth scaling as $(1+z)^{-0.3 \pm 0.2}$ when evaluated in comoving coordinates at $r < 10/h$ Mpc is mathematically equivalent to "declustering" scaling as $(1+z)^{0.6 \pm 0.4}$ when measured in proper coordinates, representing the exact same clustering evolution expressed in different coordinate systems [Carlberg et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RSPTA.357..167C/abstract). Determining the physical evolution of galaxy bias also necessitates disentangling the observational [[selection-function|Selection function]] inherent to flux-limited high-redshift surveys.

## Environmental Dependence and Galaxy Clusters

The spatial distribution of galaxies is highly heterogeneous, organized into a cosmic web of filaments and sheets that intersect at dense galaxy clusters. Because basic [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] correlate strongly with their local environments, redshift surveys are essential tools for isolating the physical mechanisms that drive galaxy evolution.

By providing three-dimensional positions, a [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]] survey reliably identifies bound structures that are otherwise obscured in 2D photometric catalogs. Traditional optical cluster searches relied on a [[richness-class|Richness class]] criterion—such as Abell's requirement of at least 50 member galaxies within a specific magnitude range—but line-of-sight projection effects routinely cause $\sim 10-15\%$ contamination in purely photometric catalogs [Collins et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.274.1071C/abstract). With spectroscopic data, interloping galaxies can be removed and [[galaxy-cluster-properties-and-scaling-relations|cluster dynamics]] can be quantified. The velocity dispersion of cluster members, $\sigma$, serves as a robust proxy for the total dynamical mass. High-redshift surveys, such as VIPERS, have successfully extended scaling relations to $z \sim 1.2$, confirming relationships such as $\log(\sigma_{200}) \propto 0.36 \log(M_{200})$ across a wide range of halo masses [Abdullah et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251209303A/abstract). 

While optical surveys rely on galaxy counts or spectroscopic velocity dispersions, [[radio-surveys|X-ray surveys]]—such as the extended ROSAT-ESO flux limited X-ray galaxy cluster survey (REFLEX II)—provide an independent statistical census of the galaxy cluster population via thermal emission from the intracluster medium [Böhringer et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...555A..30B/abstract). Many modern optical cluster searches bypass simple counts by leveraging the characteristic colors of old stellar populations. The Blanco Cosmology Survey (BCS), for example, successfully identified galaxy clusters out to $z < 0.75$ by pinpointing red-sequence over-densities [Bleem et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..216...20B/abstract). Similarly, the next-generation [[red-sequence-cluster-survey|Red-sequence Cluster Survey]]-2 (RCS-2) targets roughly 10,000 clusters over 900 square degrees, using these massive structures to constrain fundamental cosmology via the cluster mass function [Gilbank et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AJ....141...94G/abstract). As upcoming space-based stage-IV surveys demand extreme purity and completeness, the Euclid Cluster Finder Challenge has provided a rigorous benchmark for evaluating modern cluster detection algorithms, such as [[amico-galaxy-cluster-catalogue|AMICO]] and PZWav, ensuring robust identification across wide photometric datasets [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...627A..23E/abstract). Even within the clusters themselves, the central dominant galaxies display strong environmental dependence; for example, the radio luminosity function of Brightest Cluster Galaxies (BCGs) is sensitive to the dynamical relaxation state and the optical luminosity of their host clusters [Yuan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.3669Y/abstract). Additionally, galaxy clusters serve as robust tracers for intrinsic alignments, providing cleaner cosmological signals compared to individual galaxy alignments, which is valuable for refining weak lensing models [Vedder et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.5561V/abstract). Beyond individual galaxies and clusters, the tenuous filaments connecting them in the cosmic web have been directly detected via their imprint on cosmic microwave background (CMB) gravitational lensing. These measurements show that filaments trace the underlying matter distribution with a specific bias of approximately 1.5 [He et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018NatAs...2..401H/abstract).

To quantify the continuous density field outside of rich clusters, surveys often utilize local density estimators, such as counts within a fixed cylindrical aperture or projected $n$th-nearest neighbor distances. These specific 3D metrics are uniquely suited to spectroscopic data; even optimistic photometric redshift uncertainties ($\sigma_z \approx 0.02$) smear out the line-of-sight galaxy distribution too severely to cleanly resolve small-scale environments [Cooper et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...634..833C/abstract). 

Armed with robust density estimators, massive local datasets like the [[gama-survey|GAMA survey]] and the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] have extensively mapped the morphology-density relation. In the local universe, dense group and cluster environments are preferentially populated by massive, early-type galaxies with red [[galaxy-color-distributions|Galaxy Color Distributions]] and low specific star-formation rates. However, because these intrinsic parameters are tightly correlated with one another, identifying the fundamental driver of environmental quenching remains a subject of active debate. [[mark-correlation-function|Mark correlation function]] analyses yield conflicting results: some studies identify stellar mass as the most direct tracer of the local environment [Sureshkumar et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A..35S/abstract), whereas others find that optical colors exhibit the strongest correlation [Joy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250510429J/abstract). Consequently, the literature remains ambiguous regarding which relationships with the environment are strictly causal and which are secondary reflections of underlying mass assembly.

Tracking [[cluster-candidates|Cluster candidates]] at higher redshifts allows observers to witness environmental quenching in action. A hallmark of this evolution is the [[butcher-oemler-effect|Butcher-Oemler effect]], originally identified as a dramatic increase in the fraction of blue, star-forming galaxies in intermediate-redshift clusters ($0.3 \lesssim z \lesssim 0.5$) compared to their local counterparts. In addition to elevated blue fractions, intermediate-redshift clusters exhibit higher proportions of morphologically disturbed spirals and post-starburst galaxies (characterized by strong Balmer absorption). Global luminosity function measurements confirm that while the total stellar mass density in blue galaxy populations has remained relatively constant since $z \sim 1$, the mass density in red populations has approximately doubled. This rapid evolution underscores the role of dense environments in actively quenching star formation and building the quiescent red sequence over the latter half of cosmic history.

## Systematic Errors, Completeness, and Selection Effects

In any [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|extragalactic astronomical survey]], the observed sample is a distorted representation of the true galaxy distribution due to observational constraints. The most prominent systematic effect in a magnitude-limited survey is [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Malmquist bias]], where intrinsically faint galaxies are systematically missed at large distances. To reconstruct the continuous physical density field, one must model the radial [[selection-function|Selection function]], $S(z)$, which describes the probability that a galaxy at redshift $z$ is included in the survey. The selection function is derived from the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] $\Phi(L)$ via:
$$ S(z) = \frac{\int_{L_{\rm min}(z)}^{\infty} \Phi(L) dL}{\int_{L_{\rm min}(0)}^{\infty} \Phi(L) dL} $$
where $L_{\rm min}(z)$ is the minimum absolute luminosity observable at redshift $z$ given the survey's apparent magnitude limit. To measure clustering statistics such as the [[redshift-space-correlation-function|Redshift-space correlation function]], observed galaxy pair counts must be weighted by the inverse of the selection function. Alternatively, one can construct [[volume-limited-samples|Volume-limited samples]] by applying a stringent absolute magnitude limit such that all included galaxies are luminous enough to be detected throughout the entire survey volume. While this approach sidesteps radial weighting, it necessitates discarding a large fraction of the observed data. To systematically model these observational biases, modern analysts often employ specialized open-source software tools like GLACiAR, which estimate completeness and selection functions using artificial galaxy recovery simulations [Carrasco et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...22C/abstract).

### Redshift Completeness and Target Selection
Even within a strictly defined flux limit, not all targeted galaxies yield secure spectroscopic measurements. [[redshift-completeness|Redshift completeness]] varies as a function of apparent magnitude, surface brightness, and the presence of spectral emission lines. For instance, in the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey]], completeness dropped from 99% for the brightest targets to 90% near the survey limit, yielding an overall average of 91.8% [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract). To rigorously evaluate such boundaries, statistical measures such as the $T_c$ and $T_v$ tests have been developed; these metrics provide powerful, objective tools for assessing magnitude [[catalogue-completeness|completeness]] and identifying underlying [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|systematic errors]] in redshift survey catalogs [Johnston et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.376.1757J/abstract). Furthermore, multi-object spectrographs suffer from fiber collisions, where physical constraints prevent the placement of adjacent optical fibers on closely separated galaxies. This induces a systematic under-sampling of dense environments, such as [[cluster-candidates|Cluster candidates]], which must be corrected by assigning statistical weights to successfully observed galaxies based on their local angular density.

### Cosmic Variance and Homogeneity
The finite volume of a survey introduces [[sample-variance|Sample variance]] (often termed **cosmic variance**), which describes the statistical uncertainty arising from observing a localized patch of the universe rather than a truly representative "fair sample." Early surveys, such as the [[cfa-redshift-survey|CfA Redshift Survey]], mapped volumes where the sizes of the largest structural features were comparable to the survey extent itself, rendering estimates of the mean galaxy density highly uncertain [de Lapparent et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ApJ...332...44D/abstract). 

While modern surveys like the [[gama-survey|GAMA survey]] and the [[desi-bright-galaxy-survey|DESI Bright Galaxy Survey]] span vastly larger volumes, achieving true statistical convergence depends on the underlying [[homogeneity-scale|Homogeneity scale]] of the universe. Some analyses of the local universe suggest the existence of a $\sim 300 \, \mathrm{Mpc}$ scale local under-density that could systematically bias global cosmological parameter estimates [Keenan et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...775...62K/abstract). Furthermore, as noted in recent DESI analyses, the lack of a clear transition to spatial homogeneity on scales up to $\sim 400 \, h^{-1} \mathrm{Mpc}$ implies that [[sample-variance|cosmic variance]] remains a significant systematic challenge even in the era of massively multiplexed surveys [Sylos Labini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251121585S/abstract).

### Photometric Redshift Systematics
For deep surveys that rely on [[photometry-and-redshift-estimation|photometric redshift estimation]] rather than spectroscopy, redshift uncertainties introduce a severe smearing effect along the line of sight. Even optimistic photometric redshift errors of $\sigma_z \approx 0.02$ can irretrievably wash out small-scale line-of-sight clustering, severely limiting the measurement of local galaxy environments [Cooper et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...634..833C/abstract). To mitigate this, cross-correlation techniques (clustering redshifts) have been developed to calibrate the true redshift distribution of photometric samples using overlapping spectroscopic surveys [Schulz et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724.1305S/abstract), [Ruggeri et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215963R/abstract). However, the accuracy of this method is often limited by degeneracies between the inferred redshift distribution and the redshift-dependent [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|galaxy bias]], as well as uncorrected selection functions in the calibration data [Schulz et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724.1305S/abstract), [McMahon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220708657M/abstract).

## Recent Advances and Future Directions

The transition to Stage IV cosmological experiments marks a significant leap in the mapping of the universe. Projects like the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) and the future Roman Space Telescope are designed to probe massive cosmic volumes with unprecedented density. For example, the [[desi-bright-galaxy-survey|DESI Bright Galaxy Survey]] aims to obtain a nearly magnitude-limited sample of low-redshift galaxies ($0.05 \le z \le 0.4$), yielding highly precise measurements of baryon acoustic oscillations and [[redshift-space-distortions|Redshift-space distortions]] [Ruiz-Macias et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020RNAAS...4..187R/abstract). Space-based missions will further extend this frontier; forecasts for the Roman Space Telescope's High Latitude Spectroscopic Survey predict that $H\alpha$-selected galaxies will enable measurements of the angular diameter distance to within $2\%$ uncertainty over the redshift range $1 < z < 2$ [Zhai et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.3490Z/abstract). Next-generation [[spectroscopy-surveys|Spectroscopy surveys]] hold immense promise for disentangling dark energy from modified gravity; they are projected to simultaneously constrain the dark energy equation of state and modified gravity models without having to assume standard General Relativity [Daniel et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013JCAP...02..007D/abstract). Additionally, high-redshift surveys will play a pivotal role in fundamental physics by detecting neutrino mass through the measurement of the scale-dependent suppression of galaxy clustering caused by neutrino free-streaming [Takada et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvD..73h3520T/abstract).

With modern imaging surveys detecting billions of sources, obtaining individual spectroscopic follow-up for every target is unfeasible. Consequently, the calibration of photometric redshifts using [[cross-correlation-analysis|Cross-correlation analysis]]—often termed "clustering redshifts"—has emerged as a vital technique [Morrison et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467.3576M/abstract), [Kakos et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.1857K/abstract). By cross-correlating a photometric sample with a well-characterized spectroscopic tracer population, researchers can reconstruct the underlying [[redshift-distribution|Redshift distribution]]. However, the literature is explicitly conflicted regarding the ultimate reliability of this method in the absence of a highly representative spectroscopic sample. While some studies demonstrate that clustering redshifts can successfully calibrate weak lensing analyses and reduce [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|systematic errors]] [Ruggeri et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215963R/abstract), others highlight that the method is severely weakened by the inherent degeneracy between the redshift distribution and the galaxy bias $b(z)$. Furthermore, uncorrected [[target-selection|Target selection]] functions in the spectroscopic reference sample can induce significant biases that are difficult to mitigate [Schulz et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724.1305S/abstract), [McMahon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220708657M/abstract).

To address these limitations, modern analytical pipelines are increasingly adopting [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]] to model photometric redshift distributions. This approach constructs a joint posterior distribution of galaxy types and redshifts by combining broadband photometric fluxes with spatial clustering priors. Crucially, it allows for the full propagation of [[source-redshift-uncertainty|redshift uncertainties]] into cosmological parameter estimation. By overcoming the biases introduced by simple template-fitting or point-estimate approaches, this framework yields a statistically robust reconstruction of the underlying [[galaxy-density-fields|density field]] [Leistedt et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.4258L/abstract), [Sánchez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.2801S/abstract).

A major paradigm shift in observational cosmology is the rapid development of [[intensity-mapping|Intensity mapping]]. Traditional redshift surveys rely on detecting and thresholding individual galaxies, which is observationally expensive for faint, high-redshift targets. In contrast, intensity mapping utilizes the aggregate, unresolved emission from all sources—such as 21-cm emission from neutral hydrogen or $H\alpha$ line emission—within a three-dimensional spatial voxel to trace the cosmic density field [Cheng et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877...86C/abstract). This technique circumvents the need to detect faint objects individually, allowing surveys to map large-scale structure much more efficiently. Furthermore, cross-correlating line intensity maps with traditional galaxy redshift surveys provides a novel method to measure clustering bias and emission intensity while cleanly evading the fundamental limits of [[sample-variance|Sample variance]] [Oxholm et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104h3501O/abstract).
