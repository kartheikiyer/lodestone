## Article

The **Mapping Nearby Galaxies at Apache Point Observatory (MaNGA)** survey is a cornerstone program of the fourth-generation [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (SDSS-IV) that has fundamentally transformed the study of local galaxy evolution [Yan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..197Y/abstract). Historically, large-scale [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] campaigns relied on single-fiber measurements that captured only the central regions of galaxies, potentially biasing the classification of their internal dynamics, star formation rates, and chemical abundances [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract). Building on the legacy of early [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] (IFS) pathfinders like the [[atlas3d-survey|Atlas3D survey]] and the [[califa-survey|CALIFA survey]] [Delgado et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..300D/abstract), [Falcón-Barroso et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...597A..48F/abstract), MaNGA scaled spatially resolved spectroscopy to an unprecedented statistical size. By utilizing 17 variable-size fiber bundles, the survey successfully mapped the two-dimensional structural, kinematic, and compositional properties of approximately 10,000 nearby galaxies (median $z \sim 0.03$) out to 1.5 or 2.5 effective radii ($R_e$) [Law et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152...83L/abstract).

To process this massive volume of high-dimensional data, the MaNGA collaboration developed rigorous data reduction pipelines and specialized analysis toolkits that produce rectified 3D data cubes and derived maps of physical properties [Albareti et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJS..233...25A/abstract). A critical component of this framework was the simultaneous execution of the [[mastar|MaStar]] program, which compiled an empirical library of over 10,000 stellar spectra to provide a highly optimized basis for stellar population synthesis fitting. Together, these tools enable astronomers to systematically separate the stellar continuum from ionized gas emission, yielding precise, spatially resolved maps of star formation histories, gas-phase metallicities, and both stellar and gas kinematics across the Hubble sequence. 

The primary findings of the MaNGA survey have solidified and challenged various paradigms in extragalactic astronomy. The survey provides robust, spatially resolved evidence for the "inside-out" growth and quenching of massive galaxies, demonstrating that they typically possess older, metal-rich bulges surrounded by younger, actively star-forming disks. Furthermore, MaNGA observations have challenged the textbook assumption of a universal stellar Initial Mass Function (IMF) by revealing "bottom-heavy" centers in massive early-type galaxies. In the realm of active galactic nuclei (AGN), the survey has mapped the complex internal kinematics of [[seyfert-galaxies|Seyfert galaxies]] and "changing-look" AGNs, characterizing the asymmetric gas inflows and wide-spread outflow shocks that govern black hole accretion and host-galaxy feedback.

Despite these comprehensive insights, MaNGA has also highlighted several persistent open questions and theoretical tensions. For instance, while local stellar surface mass density is a recognized driver of chemical enrichment, the exact physical mechanisms—such as pristine gas accretion, galactic fountains, or radial migration—that cause unexpected metallicity increases in the outer disks of low-mass galaxies remain highly debated. Additionally, observations of [[radio-selected-galaxies|Radio-selected galaxies]] within the sample indicate that radio AGN activity is not linearly correlated with the instantaneous quenching of star formation, challenging simplistic models of AGN feedback. Finally, researchers continue to investigate the role of central dark matter distributions in early-type galaxies that exhibit unexpectedly flat velocity dispersion profiles, ensuring that MaNGA's rich dataset will drive dynamical modeling efforts for years to come.

## Historical Context and Technical Evolution

The transition of galaxy spectroscopy from single-aperture measurements to wide-field two-dimensional mapping represents a major paradigm shift in extragalactic astronomy. In the early 2000s, massive [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] campaigns, such as the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]] and early iterations of the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (SDSS), established the standard for statistically powerful investigations of the local Universe. However, these programs relied on single-fiber spectroscopy—typically a 3-arcsec fiber placed at the photometric center of the target. This approach, while efficient, was fundamentally limited because it treated galaxies as unresolved point sources, resulting in incomplete or biased classifications of their ionization states, chemical abundances, and internal dynamics . 

To address these limitations, the community increasingly turned to [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] (IFS). Early IFS programs like SAURON, the [[atlas3d-survey|Atlas3D survey]], and the [[califa-survey|CALIFA survey]] successfully demonstrated the necessity of resolving internal spatial variations. CALIFA, for instance, mapped $\sim 600$ nearby galaxies across the Hubble sequence, providing spatially resolved star formation histories and integrated velocity dispersion profiles that served as crucial local benchmarks [Delgado et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..300D/abstract), [Falcón-Barroso et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...597A..48F/abstract). The CALIFA instrumentation legacy continues today with contemporary efforts like the CAVITY survey, which utilizes the same PMAS/PPak integral-field setup to specifically characterize galaxy populations residing in the most under-dense regions, or voids, of the local Universe [García-Benito et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A.161G/abstract).

Despite the scientific success of these predecessor instruments, a persistent challenge in spectroscopic survey history was scaling IFS to the sample sizes of traditional redshift surveys. The SAMI Galaxy Survey provided a crucial stepping stone by demonstrating multiplexed IFS, eventually observing 3,400 galaxies on the [[anglo-australian-telescope|Anglo-Australian Telescope]] [Bryant et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.2857B/abstract), [Bland-Hawthorn et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..309...21B/abstract). Building upon these foundations, the Mapping Nearby Galaxies at Apache Point Observatory (MaNGA) survey was designed as a core program of SDSS-IV to transition spatially resolved spectroscopy to an unprecedented statistical scale of 10,000 galaxies [Yan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..197Y/abstract). By coupling high spectrophotometric accuracy with multiplexed IFU hardware, MaNGA successfully bridged the gap between detailed, single-object physical studies and large-scale demographic surveys. Furthermore, careful studies of spatial resolution effects on IFU data—particularly from the CALIFA perspective—validated MaNGA's observational strategy, confirming its ability to accurately map physical properties like gas-phase metallicity and stellar ages at its target resolution [Mast et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...561A.129M/abstract).

## Survey Design and Sample Selection

MaNGA's sample selection is heavily driven by the requirement to achieve uniform spatial coverage in units of the galaxy's effective radius ($R_e$) while maximizing spatial resolution. The targeted galaxies were selected to have a median redshift of $z \sim 0.03$ [Yan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..197Y/abstract), [Blanton et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AJ....154...28B/abstract). To balance detailed spatial mapping with a large statistical sample size, the survey design splits the targets into two primary tiers based on radial coverage:
*   **Primary Sample:** Comprising roughly two-thirds of the targeted galaxies, this sample provides contiguous spectral mapping out to $1.5 R_e$ [Yan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..197Y/abstract).
*   **Secondary Sample:** Comprising the remaining one-third of the targets, these galaxies are deliberately selected to extend the spatial mapping further into the galaxy outskirts, out to $2.5 R_e$ [Yan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..197Y/abstract).

To broaden this demographic beyond traditional massive structures, the survey additionally compiled the MaNGA Dwarf galaxy (MaNDala) sample. This homogeneous catalog of 136 dwarf galaxies provides crucial insights into the early star formation bursts and complex assembly histories of low-mass systems [Cano-Díaz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AJ....164..127C/abstract).

## Instrumentation and Observational Strategy

Observations for MaNGA were conducted using the 2.5-meter Sloan Foundation Telescope at the Apache Point Observatory [Blanton et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AJ....154...28B/abstract). To efficiently perform [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] on a statistically powerful sample, the instrument employs 17 individually pluggable, hex-packed fiber bundles of varying sizes [Law et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152...83L/abstract), [Weijmans et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASPC..507..257W/abstract). By utilizing differently sized fiber bundles, the instrument closely matches the IFU footprint to the apparent size of each target galaxy on the sky, yielding an average footprint of $\sim 500$ arcsec$^{2}$ per bundle and allowing simultaneous multi-object mapping across the telescope's wide field of view [Law et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152...83L/abstract).

The dual-channel spectrographs deliver a broad continuous wavelength coverage from $3622$ to $10354$ \AA\ with a median spectral resolution of $\sigma = 72$ km s$^{-1}$, corresponding to a resolving power of $R \sim 2000$ [Law et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152...83L/abstract), [Yan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..197Y/abstract). The wavelength calibration is highly precise, accurate to $5$ km s$^{-1}$ rms [Law et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152...83L/abstract). To ensure high fidelity in extracting spatially resolved properties, the survey meets stringent depth requirements. For galaxies in the Primary sample, the median $r$-band signal-to-noise ratio is $\sim 70$ per $1.4$ \AA\ pixel when spectra are stacked in an annulus between $1 R_e$ and $1.5 R_e$ [Yan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..197Y/abstract). The data reaches a typical $10\sigma$ limiting continuum surface brightness of $\mu = 23.5$ AB mag arcsec$^{-2}$ in a 5-arcsec diameter aperture in the $g$-band, with nearly Poisson-limited sky subtraction shortward of $\sim 8500$ \AA\ [Law et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152...83L/abstract). The median spatial resolution of the survey is $2.54$ arcsec full width at half-maximum (FWHM), which corresponds to a physical scale of approximately $1.8$ kpc at the sample's median redshift of $z = 0.037$ [Law et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152...83L/abstract).

## Data Processing and Analysis Framework

Raw data from the multi-fiber instruments are processed through a dedicated Data Reduction Pipeline (DRP) that transforms individual dithered exposures into sky-subtracted, spectrophotometrically calibrated spectra, which are then combined into rectified 3D data cubes [Law et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152...83L/abstract). Following basic reduction, the data cubes are ingested by the Data Analysis Pipeline (DAP). The DAP is a comprehensive software framework designed to execute [[automated-spectral-feature-fitting|Automated spectral feature fitting]] to produce derived maps of physical properties across the spatial extent of each galaxy. By separating the stellar continuum from ionized gas emission, the DAP enables the extraction of 2D maps detailing stellar and gas kinematics, emission-line fluxes, and absorption-line spectral indices [Penny et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.3955P/abstract), [Feng et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..262....6F/abstract). 

To complement standard techniques, developers also introduced a Principal Component Analysis (PCA)-based software approach for estimating spatially resolved and integrated stellar mass-to-light ratios and surface densities. This method offers a robust, purely spectroscopic alternative to standard photometric mass estimates [Pace et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...883...83P/abstract). Similarly, for advanced structural studies, the BUDDI tool was developed to perform two-dimensional bulge-disk decompositions directly on MaNGA IFU data cubes, enabling the extraction of independent stellar population properties for distinct galactic sub-components [Johnston et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2317J/abstract). From these varied primary measurements, subsequent value-added analyses produce spatially resolved maps of star formation rates, stellar ages, and gas-phase metallicities [Barrera-Ballesteros et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023RMxAA..59..213B/abstract), [Wilkinson et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..328W/abstract).

A critical component supporting this modeling is the [[mastar|MaStar]] library, an empirical collection of over 10,000 highly calibrated stellar spectra obtained simultaneously with the galaxy observations via a piggyback program . This library shares the exact instrumental footprint and resolution as the galaxy data, providing an optimized basis for stellar population synthesis fitting. Although the MaNGA pipelines provide robust standard products, users must note that literature remains conflicted regarding the optimal algorithms for extracting physical properties; for example, significant discrepancies in derived absolute ages and metallicities arise when comparing properties derived from photometric multifilter data (using tools like MUFFIT) against those derived from IFU data (using codes like STARLIGHT) on predecessor surveys such as the [[califa-survey|CALIFA survey]] [San Roman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A.181S/abstract).

The sheer volume and high-dimensional nature of the MaNGA dataset present significant data-handling challenges. Because the pipelines output data in coarse units to preserve spatial correlations, traditional file-based curation is computationally expensive and time-consuming. To resolve this, the survey developed the Marvin toolkit [Cherinka et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AJ....158...74C/abstract). Marvin is an integrated suite consisting of a Python package, an Application Programming Interface (API), and a web application backed by a remote database. It abstracts the underlying file structure, allowing users to seamlessly interact with both local and remote data. Through Marvin, researchers can perform complex search queries, visually explore individual data cubes and their derived DAP maps, and execute high-level statistical analyses across the entire sample of $\sim 10,000$ galaxies without downloading the full survey footprint [Cherinka et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AJ....158...74C/abstract).

## Spatially Resolved Stellar Populations

To derive robust, spatially resolved stellar population properties, the MaNGA survey utilizes advanced stellar population synthesis techniques applied to [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]]. A cornerstone of this effort is the [[mastar|MaStar]] library, an empirical stellar spectral library specifically constructed by piggybacking on [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] APOGEE-2N observations. Comprising over $10,000$ stars—with $8,646$ high-quality spectra representing $3,321$ unique stars in its initial release—MaStar provides comprehensive coverage of stellar parameters across the $3622$-$10354$ Å wavelength range at a resolving power of $R \sim 1800$ . This library serves as the empirical basis for modeling the continuum and absorption features of MaNGA galaxies, allowing for the precise extraction of spatially resolved ages, metallicities, and mass-to-light ratios , [Nanni et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515..320N/abstract).

By mapping the chemical and evolutionary histories of galaxies across the Hubble sequence, MaNGA has revealed distinct radial gradients in age and metallicity. Late-type galaxies typically exhibit strong negative age gradients (averaging $\sim -0.39$ dex/$R_e$) and relatively flat metallicity gradients [Wilkinson et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..328W/abstract). Innovative methodologies like "time-slicing"—which decomposes MaNGA spectra into distinct temporal contributions—have further detailed the evolving star formation histories of specific structural features like bars and spiral arms [Peterken et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.1338P/abstract). By identifying Milky Way analogues based on mass and bulge-to-total ratios, MaNGA studies have revealed that these galaxies generally feature young stellar populations in their outskirts and flatter metallicity gradients in absolute physical units compared to our own Galaxy [Boardman et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.3672B/abstract). However, this apparent discrepancy in metallicity gradients is largely resolved when the gradients are scaled by the galaxies' disc scale lengths, indicating that the Milky Way is simply more compact rather than fundamentally different in its chemical profile [Boardman et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.3672B/abstract).

In contrast, early-type galaxies generally show flat age gradients but significant negative metallicity gradients of $\sim -0.15$ dex/$R_e$ [Wilkinson et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..328W/abstract). These structural differences heavily support a model of "inside-out" growth and quenching in massive galaxies: deep central gravitational potentials retain metals and undergo rapid, early star formation, leaving older, metal-rich bulges surrounded by younger, extended disks [Lah et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASA...40....2L/abstract), [Wylezalek et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.3119W/abstract). The survey has also enabled detailed studies of 'E+A' post-starburst galaxies; by leveraging MaNGA's spatially resolved spectrophotometry, researchers were able to stringently select a pure, uncontaminated sample of these galaxies by requiring them to be fully quenched out to at least three effective radii [Greene et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910..162G/abstract).

Statistical analyses of over $2.6$ million spatial bins from MaNGA have also confirmed a resolved mass density-metallicity relation, demonstrating that local stellar surface mass density ($\Sigma_\star$) is a primary driver of chemical enrichment [Neumann et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508.4844N/abstract). However, the literature remains ambiguous regarding the exact physical drivers governing the outer disks. At a fixed $\Sigma_\star$, metallicity is observed to unexpectedly increase with galactocentric radius in low- and intermediate-mass galaxies, sparking ongoing debate over whether this behavior is dominated by pristine gas accretion, radial migration of stars, or galactic fountains [Neumann et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508.4844N/abstract), [Belfiore et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..867B/abstract).

Furthermore, MaNGA data has profoundly challenged the historical textbook assumption of a universal stellar Initial Mass Function (IMF). By measuring gravity-sensitive optical and near-infrared absorption features in stacked spectra out to the half-light radius of $\sim 400$ early-type galaxies, researchers have found significant radial gradients in the IMF slope [Parikh et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.3954P/abstract). The centers of these massive galaxies are found to be "bottom-heavy," featuring a low-mass star excess factor of $\sim 1.5$ relative to a standard IMF, which then smoothly transitions to a Milky Way-type (Kroupa or Chabrier) IMF at $1 R_e$ [Parikh et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.3954P/abstract). While it remains an active area of debate whether these IMF variations are fundamentally dictated by local metallicity or are instead a byproduct of stellar mass surface density, the spatially resolved non-universality of the IMF represents a major paradigm shift in the study of galaxy evolution.

## Gas Kinematics and Chemical Abundances

The MaNGA survey utilizes spatially resolved H$\alpha$ emission and other strong collisionally excited lines acquired via [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] to map the ionization state of the interstellar medium across the optical extent of its target galaxies. By constructing spatially resolved BPT diagrams, MaNGA has demonstrated that global, single-fiber classifications are frequently incomplete and fail to capture complex internal structures [Belfiore et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..867B/abstract). For example, spatially resolved mapping reveals extended star formation in galaxies centrally dominated by Seyfert/LINER-like emission, and separately observes extended LINER-like emission reaching up to $1 R_e$ in certain central regions [Belfiore et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..867B/abstract). Furthermore, correlating a spaxel's position on the BPT diagram with its H$\alpha$ equivalent width, $\mathrm{EW}(\mathrm{H}\alpha)$, helps disentangle true active galactic nuclei from diffuse ionized gas that is primarily ionized by hot, evolved stellar populations [Belfiore et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..867B/abstract).

MaNGA's vast statistical power provides an authoritative local benchmark for gas-phase metallicity measurements. Utilizing a sample of over $1.1 \times 10^6$ star-forming spaxels, researchers have derived polynomial conversions between 11 strong-line metallicity calibrations with a typical $2\sigma$ scatter of just $0.1 \text{ dex}$, ensuring robust comparisons across different diagnostic methodologies [Scudder et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.2468S/abstract). In general, the survey confirms that most massive spiral galaxies exhibit negative radial metallicity gradients [Barrera-Ballesteros et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023RMxAA..59..213B/abstract). Recent studies confirm that while prominent spiral structure correlates with steeper gas-phase metallicity gradients, there is notably no significant difference in the averaged radial gradients between barred and unbarred spiral galaxies [Wisz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983...57W/abstract). Furthermore, investigations into central oxygen abundances in spiral galaxies indicate that they typically adhere to the general radial metallicity gradients, even in systems currently experiencing central starbursts [Pilyugin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240700739P/abstract).

Gas-phase abundances are sensitive to a region's localized star formation history. Starburst regions within MaNGA targets exhibit more pristine gas—characterized by lower $\mathrm{O/H}$ and $\mathrm{N/O}$ ratios—than normal star-forming regions, indicating that these bursts are likely fueled and diluted by the infall of metal-poor gas [Boardman et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.52710788B/abstract). These comprehensive chemical and age maps also provide vital environmental context for transients; spatially resolved analyses of supernova host galaxies in MaNGA reveal that supernova locations generally follow the global radial gradients of stellar population age and gas-phase metallicity [Zhang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021RAA....21..306Z/abstract). However, systematic uncertainties remain high depending on the chosen diagnostic; radial variations in the $\mathrm{N/O}$ abundance ratio at a fixed $\mathrm{O/H}$ can induce systematic errors of up to $\sim 40\%$ in oxygen abundance gradients when nitrogen-dependent calibrations are utilized [Schaefer et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...890L...3S/abstract). 

The 2D velocity fields extracted from H$\alpha$ emission lines provide critical insights into gas kinematics, non-circular motions, and morphological drivers. A census of barred galaxies within the sample found that bar length strongly correlates with passive galaxy states, and that H$\alpha$ emission along the bars is typically restricted to low-mass, star-forming systems [Fraser-McKelvie et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..353..226F/abstract). Additionally, a catalog of kinematic asymmetries for 5,353 MaNGA galaxies demonstrates that among "regular" (non-merging, unbarred) systems, kinematic asymmetry strongly anti-correlates with stellar mass for $M_* < 10^{9.7} M_\odot$, reflecting a higher degree of non-rotational or disordered motion in low-mass regimes [Feng et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..262....6F/abstract). 

The wide field of view of MaNGA's fiber bundles has also facilitated the serendipitous discovery of anomalous ionized gas complexes. Observations have identified populations of outlying H$\alpha$ blobs extending up to $\approx 6.3 \text{ kpc}$ beyond the effective radius of their hosts, completely lacking corresponding optical continuum emission [Cheung et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832..182C/abstract), [Bait et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019IAUS..344..265B/abstract). These blobs feature specific star formation rates substantially higher than local dwarf galaxies but maintain low metallicities. They are interpreted as representing either accretion of recycled wind material or a novel population of highly obscured, starbursting dwarfs, showcasing MaNGA's utility in uncovering the complex relationship between local environments, gas accretion, and star formation efficiency.

## Internal Dynamics and Galaxy Assembly

The mapping of [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] data across galactic disks and bulges allows the MaNGA survey to robustly characterize the internal dynamics and evolutionary history of the local galaxy population. By analyzing the spatially resolved physical properties of thousands of galaxies, researchers have utilized MaNGA to trace everything from chemical assembly to complex orbital kinematics.

MaNGA's combined spectral and spatial coverage provides robust evidence that massive galaxies predominantly assemble and quench via *inside-out growth* [Delgado et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..300D/abstract), [Wylezalek et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.3119W/abstract). In massive systems, bulge-dominated central regions host older, more metal-rich stellar populations compared to their actively star-forming disks [Lah et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASA...40....2L/abstract). Conversely, low-mass galaxies appear to quench more uniformly across their entire spatial extent. Investigations into faint, low-mass quenched galaxies within the sample further reveal that the majority of these systems maintain coherent stellar rotation but are preferentially found in high-density environments—typically within $\sim 1.5$ Mpc of a bright neighbor—suggesting that environmental interactions are the primary driver for their quenching [Penny et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.3955P/abstract). Radial gradient analyses confirm this paradigm: early-type galaxies generally exhibit flat age gradients and negative metallicity gradients (e.g., $\sim -0.15$ dex per effective radius), whereas late-type galaxies display distinct negative age gradients [Wilkinson et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..328W/abstract).

Beyond stellar population ages and metallicities, MaNGA delivers precision maps of *stellar kinematics* and ionized gas velocities. These measurements are crucial for evaluating the origins of galactic *angular momentum*. Standard models posit a kinematic morphology-density relation wherein dense local environments strip angular momentum through interactions, forming "slow rotators." However, MaNGA observations reveal no compelling evidence that local overdensity drives rotational support when stellar mass is strictly controlled. This suggests that stellar mass—rather than local environment—is the primary driver of the slow-rotator fraction [Greene et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...851L..33G/abstract). Furthermore, comparisons with higher-redshift samples, such as the [[magpi-survey|MAGPI survey]]—designed specifically to spatially resolve the kinematic and stellar population properties of galaxies at middle cosmic times [Foster et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASA...38...31F/abstract)—indicate that the massive slow rotator population was already largely in place by $z \sim 0.3$, implying that these structures have not accumulated significantly in the recent universe [Derkenne et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4602D/abstract).

Analysis of MaNGA's one-dimensional kinematic profiles demonstrates that approximately $68\%$ of elliptical galaxies exhibit flat *velocity dispersion* profiles, a feature that complicates simple dynamical mass modeling and highlights the need to better constrain central dark matter distributions [Duann et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023RASTI...2..649D/abstract). A notable achievement of MaNGA's widespread structural mapping was the serendipitous discovery of a new galaxy-scale gravitational lens system (SDSS J170124.01+372258.0), which provides a rare opportunity to directly compare precise lensing mass estimates against stellar population models [Smith et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464L..46S/abstract). Furthermore, MaNGA has enabled the first spatially resolved measurements of asymmetric drift for unresolved stellar populations separated by age (e.g., above and below $1.5$ Gyr), directly tracing the age-velocity relation in galaxy disks [Shetty et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...901..101S/abstract). Total-to-visible mass ratios derived from rotation curves show extreme values at both ends of the galaxy sequence, peaking in faint, low-metallicity blue galaxies and highly metallic, massive red sequence galaxies [Douglass et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...925..127D/abstract). Additionally, the release of dynamical property catalogs for 2,368 late-type galaxies—relying on rotation curves extracted from H$\alpha$ velocity maps—has provided essential empirical constraints for galaxy scaling relations and feedback models used in cosmological simulations [Arora et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.1208A/abstract).

Finally, kinematic maps frequently reveal dynamical disturbances not visible in broadband photometry. An analysis of over 5,000 MaNGA targets found that more than half of the galaxies exhibiting high kinematic asymmetry appear as photometrically "regular" systems [Feng et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..262....6F/abstract). At low stellar masses ($\log M_{\star} < 9.7$), this kinematic asymmetry strongly anticorrelates with mass. To untangle these complex dynamical histories, researchers have successfully deployed machine learning algorithms on MaNGA velocity fields and photometric images to classify distinct merger stages—ranging from first pericentric passage to final coalescence—achieving over $85\%$ precision [Chang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937...97C/abstract).

## Active Galactic Nuclei and Host Synergies

The MaNGA survey has provided a statistically robust framework for exploring the co-evolution of active galactic nuclei (AGNs) and their host galaxies. By mapping [[spatially-resolved-stellar-populations|Spatially resolved stellar populations]], studies find a direct connection between galaxy bulge growth and supermassive black hole accretion. Specifically, galaxies hosting more luminous AGNs exhibit an increased contribution from young ($\sim 40$ Myr) stellar populations compared to non-active control samples, whereas low-luminosity AGNs are typically found in galaxies dominated by older stars [Rembold et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.4382R/abstract). Galaxy mergers also play a role in AGN triggering; there is a measured excess of AGNs in merging systems, peaking in post-coalescence major mergers for galaxies with stellar masses of $\sim 10^{11} M_\odot$. However, this interaction-driven triggering does not appear to correlate with higher accretion rates or bolometric luminosities [Comerford et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963...53C/abstract).

MaNGA has been particularly valuable in the identification and characterization of "changing-look" AGNs (CL-AGNs), objects in which broad Balmer emission lines appear or disappear on timescales of years, challenging standard accretion disk models. Photometric searches for low-redshift CL-AGNs often fail because the host galaxy's optical continuum overwhelms the variable AGN signal [Hon et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497..192H/abstract). Spatially resolved kinematics reveal that while CL-AGN hosts generally lie on the star-forming main sequence and exhibit pseudo-bulges, they display a higher, albeit statistically broad, fraction ($20 \pm 16\%$) of counter-rotating kinematic features compared to the $1.8 \pm 0.6\%$ seen in the general star-forming population [Yu et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.3985Y/abstract). This suggests that asymmetric or counter-rotating gas inflows may be linked to the rapid state transitions characterizing the CL-AGN phenomenon.

By evaluating ionized gas kinematics—particularly the velocity dispersion of the $[\text{O III}] \lambda5007$ line—across hundreds of AGNs, researchers have traced the impact of AGN outflows on the surrounding interstellar medium. Optically identified AGNs (such as classical [[seyfert-galaxies|Seyfert galaxies]] selected via BPT diagnostics) exhibit different kinematic profiles than [[radio-selected-galaxies|Radio-selected galaxies]] hosting AGNs. Purely radio-selected AGNs demonstrate enhanced ionized gas line widths across all galactocentric radii, indicating long-duration or recurrent kinematic perturbations driven by radio-mode shocks [Albán et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A.124A/abstract). Moreover, MaNGA has identified populations of non-active galaxies exhibiting similar elevated radial velocity dispersion profiles, interpreted as "fossil" or remnant outflows from a past AGN phase [Albán et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A.124A/abstract).

Despite strong kinematic evidence for extended outflows, MaNGA results highlight significant tensions with textbook models of AGN feedback. Standard theoretical frameworks posit that radio-mode AGNs directly regulate and quench ongoing star formation. However, demographic studies of over 1,000 radio emitters in the MaNGA sample show that when controlling for stellar mass, the presence of a radio AGN is independent of the host galaxy's star formation rate, specific star formation rate, or central velocity dispersion [Suresh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240404780S/abstract). This lack of direct correlation indicates that radio AGN activity does not govern instantaneous star formation rates in a simple linear fashion, implying that the feedback cycle governing galaxy quenching is more complex than previously assumed.

## Ancillary Catalogs and Multi-wavelength Synergies

The scientific value of the MaNGA survey is significantly extended through synergistic multi-wavelength campaigns and the systematic release of Value-Added Catalogs (VACs). By combining spatially resolved optical spectra with external radio and millimeter data, astronomers can construct a complete census of the baryonic components—stars, ionized gas, neutral hydrogen, and molecular gas—within local galaxies. 

### Neutral and Molecular Gas Surveys
To complement MaNGA’s optical mapping of stellar populations and ionized gas kinematics via [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]], several ancillary programs provide resolved and integrated measurements of the cold interstellar medium. The HI-MaNGA project supplies 21-cm single-dish observations of neutral hydrogen ($\text{H\textsc{i}}$) for approximately 70% of the full MaNGA sample [Masters et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250209505M/abstract). These $\text{H\textsc{i}}$ measurements are critical for evaluating the physical mechanisms that govern cold gas accretion and the subsequent quenching of star formation [Masters et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250209505M/abstract).

Tracing the denser star-forming gas, the MaNGA-ARO Survey of CO Targets (MASCOT survey) utilizes the Arizona Radio Observatory to measure integrated CO(1-0) line emission for a subset of 187 MaNGA targets with stellar masses $M_{\star} > 10^{9.5} M_{\odot}$ [Wylezalek et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.3119W/abstract). The MASCOT survey reveals that galaxies with lower molecular gas mass fractions tend to exhibit older central stellar populations. This correlation between molecular gas depletion and central stellar age gradients provides strong supporting evidence for an "inside-out" quenching paradigm in massive galaxies [Wylezalek et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.3119W/abstract).

### Morphological and Photometric Value-Added Catalogs
To properly interpret the internal kinematics and stellar population gradients of MaNGA galaxies, accurate morphological classifications and structural decompositions are required. This need is fulfilled by dedicated VACs released alongside the final [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] data release. The MaNGA PyMorph photometric VAC provides parameters derived from two-dimensional surface brightness profile fits to the $g$-, $r$-, and $i$-band imaging of the MaNGA sample. It outputs robust structural metrics, including total fluxes, half-light radii, ellipticities, and bulge-to-disk fractions based on both single Sérsic and two-component Sérsic+Exponential models [Domínguez Sánchez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.4024D/abstract).

In parallel, the MaNGA Deep Learning Morphological VAC provides non-parametric classifications derived from convolutional neural networks. By analyzing optical imaging, this catalog assigns continuous Hubble T-types, distinguishes finely between elliptical and S0 morphologies, and reliably identifies barred and edge-on disk galaxies, complete with $k$-fold-based uncertainties [Domínguez Sánchez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.4024D/abstract). Additionally, a comprehensive Visually Classified Spectroscopic Object Catalog was compiled to identify over 11,000 galaxies, 1,300 stars, and 100 broad-line AGNs located serendipitously within MaNGA fields of view, dramatically expanding the usable demographic dataset [Steffen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RNAAS...6..192S/abstract). Together, these efforts represent a powerful baseline for correlating optical kinematics (such as the velocity dispersion profiles of massive slow rotators) with photometric structure.

### Software Infrastructure and Stellar Libraries
The creation of robust physical maps from the IFU data is further supported by the MaStar library, an empirical collection of over 10,000 highly calibrated stellar spectra obtained simultaneously with the galaxy observations . This library serves as the empirical backbone for the stellar population synthesis modeling used to derive ages and metallicities across the sample. Accessing, visualizing, and cross-matching this high-dimensional dataset—including the base data cubes and their associated VACs—is streamlined by Marvin, a specialized Python toolkit, API, and web application designed explicitly to handle the complex, multi-layered data structures generated by the survey [Cherinka et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AJ....158...74C/abstract).

## Recent Advances and Future Directions

The scale and complexity of the MaNGA dataset have increasingly necessitated the shift from traditional parametric fitting to sophisticated Machine Learning frameworks for high-dimensional data analysis. Deep learning architectures, particularly convolutional neural networks, have been deployed to generate automated, large-scale morphological classifications. This effort culminated in the MaNGA Deep Learning Morphological Value Added Catalog (MDLM-VAC), which provides robust T-types, bulge-to-disc fractions, and structural parameters across the entire survey [Domínguez Sánchez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.4024D/abstract). 

Beyond broadband photometry, machine learning is now being applied directly to integral-field kinematic data. Unsupervised and supervised algorithms have successfully classified one-dimensional velocity dispersion profiles in early-type galaxies, revealing that approximately $68\%$ of MaNGA elliptical galaxies exhibit flat dispersion profiles, a result that provides new constraints on central dark matter fractions [Duann et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023RASTI...2..649D/abstract). Machine learning has also proven highly effective in identifying and categorizing galaxy mergers. By combining $gri$ imaging with MaNGA's resolved H$\alpha$ velocity maps, classifiers can now distinguish specific merger stages—such as first pericentric passage, apocenter, or final coalescence—with greater than $85\%$ precision [Chang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937...97C/abstract). 

Recent evolutionary studies have also leveraged MaNGA's comprehensive low-redshift baseline to reconstruct galaxy evolutionary pathways across cosmic time. By comparing the local MaNGA sample with $z \sim 0.7$ objects from the [[lega-c-survey|LEGA-C survey]], researchers have identified local mass-metallicity "twins" of higher-redshift populations [Zhou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.1935Z/abstract). Detailed modeling of these high-mass, low-metallicity twins indicates that such galaxies continue to grow their discs through late-stage star formation. They then evolve rapidly toward higher metallicities following a cutoff in wind-driven mass loss, a transition that is likely linked to the belated cessation of active galactic nucleus activity [Zhou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.1935Z/abstract).

Looking forward, the hardware prototypes, observational strategies, and data reduction pipelines pioneered by MaNGA are establishing the foundation for the next generation of [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] programs. Most notably, MaNGA's legacy is paving the way for the Local Volume Mapper (LVM) program within SDSS-V, the latest generation of the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]]. While MaNGA achieved a typical spatial resolution of $1.5$ to $2.5$ kpc at a median redshift of $z \sim 0.03$, future endeavors aim to probe the interstellar medium at fundamentally smaller scales. Prototype data analysis pipelines developed for the upcoming Local Volume Mapper (LVM) are already being applied to existing MaNGA datacubes to extract the physical properties of H II regions at sub-kpc resolutions in nearby targets, such as those within the [[ic-342-group|IC 342 group]] [Barrera-Ballesteros et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250705414B/abstract). This local foundational work will also perfectly complement future multiplexed spectroscopic facilities like WEAVE, which is designed to survey stellar populations and kinematics in field galaxies at higher redshifts ($0.3 < z < 0.7$) [Jin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.2688J/abstract). This transition will ultimately bridge the gap between the global, galaxy-wide evolutionary trends mapped by MaNGA and the highly localized physics of star formation and feedback.
