**Legacy surveys** are large-scale, systematic observational programs designed to produce high-fidelity, non-proprietary public datasets that serve as foundational resources for multi-generational community research. They represent a departure from targeted, hypothesis-driven observations, prioritizing instead a panoramic, unbiased [[sky-mapping-and-surveys|mapping of the sky]]. This cartographic approach enables robust statistical analyses on samples of millions to billions of sources, freeing researchers from the selection biases inherent in targeted [[archival-catalogs|archival pointings]]. While the paradigm originated with early visual compilations and digitized photographic plates [Gal et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..1195G/abstract), it fully matured into the "big data" era with the advent of wide-field digital astronomy pioneered by the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] and the [[two-micron-all-sky-survey-2mass|Two Micron All-Sky Survey (2MASS)]]. Today, legacy surveys are the bedrock of modern [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], providing the comprehensive [[astronomical-catalogs|catalogs]] necessary for everything from rare [[stellar-transient-and-supernova-surveys|transient discovery]] to precision cosmology.

The architecture of modern legacy programs typically relies on a powerful synergy between deep multi-wavelength imaging and massive [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] campaigns. Deep broadband imaging—such as the optical data from the [[dark-energy-survey|Dark Energy Survey]] or the [[decals|DECaLS]] footprint—provides the high-precision [[observational-photometry|Observational Photometry]] required to characterize galaxy morphology, estimate photometric redshifts, and perform robust [[target-selection|Target selection]] [Blum et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AAS...22831701B/abstract). These photometric master catalogs are subsequently fed into high-multiplex spectroscopic facilities like the [[desi-survey|DESI survey]], yielding massive [[three-dimensional-galaxy-surveys|three-dimensional maps]] of the cosmic web. By federating data across the electromagnetic spectrum to create comprehensive [[panchromatic-surveys|Panchromatic surveys]]—integrating [[radio-surveys|radio continuum maps]], [[galex|ultraviolet fluxes]], and deep mid-infrared coadds from [[unwise|unWISE]]—astronomers can break physical degeneracies to accurately trace [[mass-assembly-survey|stellar mass assembly]], characterize local star formation histories, and utilize baryon acoustic oscillations and weak gravitational lensing as cosmological probes [Kirk et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451.4424K/abstract), [Square Kilometre Array Cosmology Science Working Group et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PASA...37....7S/abstract).

As the field transitions to Stage IV flagship missions—including the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]], the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, and the [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]]—legacy surveys are confronting unprecedented methodological challenges. At extreme optical depths, source confusion or "blending" affects a significant fraction of objects, systematically biasing structural profiles and number counts. To overcome these limitations, the community is advancing Joint Survey Processing (JSP) and simulation-based inference, utilizing synthetic source injection to rigorously model the observational [[selection-function|Selection function]] and mitigate [[sample-selection-biases|Sample selection biases]]. Resolving these systematics is critical for answering the field's most pressing open questions, which include characterizing the physical nature of extremely faint galaxies at the [[ultra-high-z-galaxy-search|epoch of reionization]], understanding discrepancies between theoretical models and JWST observations, and mapping the uncharted low surface brightness universe to define the physical boundaries of galaxy assembly.

## Definition and Philosophy of Legacy Surveys

The concept of a "legacy survey" is defined fundamentally by its intent: to produce highly uniform, non-proprietary [[analysed-science-data-sets|public datasets]] that serve as multi-generational foundational resources for the broad astronomical community. Unlike traditional, principal investigator-driven observations—which typically target specific objects to answer narrow hypotheses—legacy surveys prioritize panoramic, "cartographic" mapping of the sky [Majewski et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..262...99M/abstract). This unbiased approach enables statistical analyses on samples of millions to billions of sources, free from the selection biases inherent in targeted archival pointings. 

Historically, astronomy operated predominantly on proprietary data models. The paradigm shifted significantly with foundational projects like the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (SDSS), which demonstrated the immense scientific return of releasing calibrated data "early and often" [Jurić et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010EAS....45..281J/abstract). By treating survey data as continually evolving products, these programs actively encourage independent community research and serendipitous [[archival-data-in-astronomy|archival discovery]]. For instance, early infrared legacy programs such as the Spitzer Infrared Nearby Galaxies Survey (SINGS) were deliberately designed to be completely non-proprietary from their inception, maximizing their utility for the extragalactic community [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AAS...203.9010K/abstract).

Quantitatively, the scale of modern legacy surveys has pushed astronomy into the "Big Data" regime. Modern efforts require the processing and curation of immense data volumes; for example, the [[unwise|unWISE]] full-sky maps were constructed by coadding $>100$ terabytes of single-exposure mid-infrared images [Meisner et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AJ....154..161M/abstract). Geometrically, the [[survey-area|sky coverage]] ($\Omega$) has expanded from narrow [[pencil-beam-surveys|pencil-beam studies]] to vast cosmological footprints. Recent photometric data releases, such as the [[dark-energy-survey|Dark Energy Survey]] (DES) Year 6 catalog, reach depths of $i_{AB} \sim 23.4$ mag at a signal-to-noise ratio of $10$ over $\Omega \approx 5000$ deg$^2$ [Bechtol et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250105739B/abstract), while the DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys ([[decals|DECaLS]]) cover an unprecedented $\Omega \approx 20,000$ deg$^2$ [Zhou et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...11..097Z/abstract). 

A core philosophical pillar of the legacy framework is multi-wavelength synergy. The physical properties of galaxies and the large-scale structure of the Universe cannot be fully constrained within a single wavelength regime. Consequently, modern legacy catalogs often merge independent surveys to create comprehensive [[panchromatic-surveys|panchromatic]] databases [Bilicki et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016pas..conf..165B/abstract), [Chilingarian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ASPC..535..179C/abstract). This coordinated strategy ensures that future legacy surveys do not exist in isolation, but rather form an interconnected framework that provides the requisite precision for constraining the dark energy equation of state and the fundamental nature of dark matter.

## Historical Evolution: From Photographic Plates to Digital Cartography

The systematic mapping of the extragalactic sky began as an endeavor of manual observation and cataloging. By the late eighteenth century, visual compilations such as the list of [[messier-objects|Messier Objects]] established the practice of curating exhaustive databases of extended "nebulae" to distinguish them from transient phenomena. Throughout the twentieth century, the field became dominated by large-area photographic surveys. A milestone of this era was the Abell catalogue of [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|galaxy clusters]], which was constructed through the painstaking visual inspection of [[digitized-harvard-astronomical-plate-collection|photographic sky plates]] [Gal et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..1195G/abstract). Such early cartographic efforts provided the first statistical samples of large-scale structure. Ultimately, combining these historical photographic catalogues with modern large-scale digital [[galaxy-redshift-survey|redshift surveys]], such as the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS]] and [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|SDSS]], firmly established that the local number density of rich galaxy clusters is on the order of $10^{-5} h^3 \mathrm{Mpc}^{-3}$.

As computational technology advanced in the late twentieth century, observational astronomy transitioned into a hybrid photo-digital era. The subjective nature of visual plate inspection was superseded by the use of specialized, automated scanning machines, such as the Automated Plate Measuring (APM) machine at Cambridge and the COSMOS machine at Edinburgh. These instruments produced digitized sky plates, enabling the objective extraction of millions of sources and the automated detection of galaxy clusters [Gal et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..1195G/abstract). A prominent legacy of this transitional period is the [[apm-galaxy-catalogue|APM galaxy catalogue]], derived from systematic APM scans of photographic plates. This catalog provided the foundational targeting data for subsequent massive spectroscopic efforts, most notably the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]], which successfully measured redshifts for $\sim 220,000$ galaxies down to a limiting magnitude of $b_J \sim 19.45$.

The complete digital revolution in astronomical cartography was realized at the turn of the twenty-first century with the advent of wide-field CCD detectors and automated data processing pipelines. This transition marked a paradigm shift from targeted, object-by-object studies to panoramic sky mapping, allowing researchers to quantify large-scale structure and galaxy evolution without the severe selection biases of earlier eras [Majewski et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..262...99M/abstract). The [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey]] (SDSS) and the [[two-micron-all-sky-survey-2mass|Two Micron All-Sky Survey (2MASS)]] stand as the archetypal fully digital legacy surveys. SDSS, for instance, pioneered a photometrically and astrometrically calibrated imaging survey covering over a quarter of the sky in five bespoke [[ugriz-photometry|optical bands]] ($u, g, r, i, z$). Crucially, this homogeneous imaging data seamlessly fed into an integrated spectroscopic pipeline, yielding pioneering datasets of $\sim 10^6$ galaxies with $r < 17.77$ and $\sim 10^5$ quasars. By providing multi-color, highly uniform catalogs spanning unprecedented cosmological volumes, these automated digital surveys initiated the "big data" era of astronomy, laying the groundwork for all modern [[future-astronomical-facilities-and-surveys|precision cosmology]]. Parallel to these extragalactic efforts, the [[lamost-spectroscopic-survey|LAMOST Spectroscopic Survey]] emerged as a key spectroscopic legacy survey, collecting millions of stellar spectra to comprehensively map the kinematics and chemistry of the Milky Way [Liu et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015RAA....15.1089L/abstract). The SDSS legacy has continued to evolve; its current generation, SDSS-V, is pioneering panoptic spectroscopy by providing all-sky multi-epoch spectroscopic monitoring alongside wide-field integral field spectroscopy [Kollmeier et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv171103234K/abstract).

## Deep Photometric Imaging and Target Selection

Deep multi-wavelength imaging is the bedrock of modern [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], providing the high-precision [[observational-photometry|Observational Photometry]] necessary to estimate [[spectrophotometric-redshifts|photometric redshifts]], map large-scale structure, and measure galaxy morphology. Surveys spanning the optical to the mid-infrared increasingly utilize deep [[ugriz-photometry|ugriz photometry]] paired with wide-field space-based infrared coverage to construct these multi-color datasets. For example, the [[desi-survey|DESI survey]] Legacy Imaging Surveys—comprising the Dark Energy Camera Legacy Survey ([[decals|DECaLS]]), the Beijing-Arizona Sky Survey (BASS), and the Mayall z-band Legacy Survey (MzLS)—cover over 14,000 square degrees to depths of $g=24.7$, $r=23.9$, and $z=23.0$ [Blum et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AAS...22831701B/abstract). To maximize the utility of these optical data, pipelines such as the Tractor algorithm perform multi-wavelength forced photometry on matched infrared coadds, including the deep 3-5 $\mu$m [[unwise|unWISE]] maps. This approach mitigates blending and ensures consistent flux measurements across disparate angular resolutions [Blum et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AAS...22831701B/abstract), [Meisner et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AJ....154..161M/abstract). Similarly, modern software tools such as 'The Farmer' have been developed to perform robust profile-fitting photometry, specifically designed to handle deeply crowded fields across multi-wavelength legacy catalogs [Weaver et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ascl.soft12016W/abstract).

The primary functional role of these homogenous photometric catalogs is to facilitate [[target-selection|Target selection]] for massive spectroscopic programs. By leveraging broadband color selection and robust photometric redshifts, automated pipelines can isolate specific cosmological probes, including [[redmagic|luminous red galaxies]] (LRGs), emission-line galaxies (ELGs), and quasars [Myers et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....165...50M/abstract), [Zhou et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...11..097Z/abstract). The release of the DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys DR10 photometric redshift catalog, for instance, provides estimates for over 1.5 billion galaxies, utilizing [[eazy|EAZY]] and CatBoost algorithms [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..233L/abstract). Furthermore, the [[desi-bright-galaxy-survey|DESI Bright Galaxy Survey]] utilizes [[decals|DECaLS]] photometry combined with Gaia astrometry to construct highly complete input catalogs of [[bright-galaxies|Bright galaxies]]. However, the efficiency of these targeting algorithms remains sensitive to photometric uncertainties and survey systematics. Cross-matching with the [[gama-survey|GAMA survey]] reveals that high foreground stellar density can artificially suppress the retrieved target galaxy density by up to $7\%$ [Ruiz-Macias et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502.4328R/abstract).

Beyond redshift mapping and spectroscopic targeting, deep photometric imaging drives the precise characterization of galaxy morphology. Wide-field coadds, such as those from [[stripe-82|Stripe 82]], provide the necessary signal-to-noise ratio to perform robust bulge-to-disk decompositions and fit two-component Sérsic profiles, mitigating the systematic biases present in shallower imaging [Bottrell et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..390B/abstract). To consolidate morphological and structural data for the local Universe, the Siena Galaxy Atlas (SGA-2020) provides a multi-wavelength imaging atlas of over 380,000 nearby galaxies using DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys and [[unwise|unWISE]] data [Moustakas et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..269....3M/abstract).

Furthermore, the push into the [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|low surface brightness regime]] (where the $V$-band surface brightness is $\mu_V > 27 \text{ AB mag/arcsec}^2$) has opened a new frontier in structural studies. Precursor surveys for next-generation facilities like the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]] are reaching surface brightness limits of $30-31 \text{ mag/arcsec}^2$ [Montes et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230609414M/abstract), [Golini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...700A..91G/abstract). At these depths, it becomes possible to map the faint outskirts of galaxies, extended stellar halos, and tidal streams that were previously hidden by the sky background. At the highest resolutions, space-based archival legacy programs are essential for measuring these structures. For instance, the SKYSURF [[hst-observations|Hubble Space Telescope]] Legacy program processed over 82,000 archival images to provide integrated galaxy light measurements, effectively constraining the [[background-galaxies|extragalactic background light]] across 15 different wavelengths [Tompkins et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250703412T/abstract). In addition to extragalactic sources, these deep photometric datasets are revolutionizing stellar science. For instance, the 'PhotoD' pipeline applied to [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|LSST]]-scale data enables the Bayesian estimation of stellar photometric distances, metallicities, and extinction out to the edge of the Galaxy [Palaversa et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....169..119P/abstract).

## Large-Scale Spectroscopic and Integral Field Surveys

The measurement of large-scale structure and cosmic expansion relies heavily on the [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]]. The transition from targeted observations to massive multiplexed spectroscopy began with pioneering efforts like the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]] and the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]], which secured spectra for millions of local galaxies. As multiplexing technology advanced, surveys pushed to higher redshifts to trace the evolution of the universe over cosmic time. While early literature frequently cited the [[deep2-redshift-survey|DEEP2 Redshift Survey]] as the benchmark for intermediate-redshift clustering, it was subsequently superseded by the VIMOS Public Extragalactic Redshift Survey (VIPERS), which is a part of the [[deep-spectroscopic-surveys|Deep spectroscopic surveys]] that mapped the cosmic web across a volume of $\sim 5 \times 10^7 h^{-3} \text{ Mpc}^3$ with an unprecedented sample of $\sim 100,000$ galaxies at $0.5 < z < 1.5$ [Guzzo et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.108G/abstract). Today, "big data" spectroscopic cosmology is exemplified by the [[desi-survey|DESI survey]] (Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument), whose Early Data Release alone yielded high-quality spectra for over $1.7 \times 10^6$ unique targets, including emission-line galaxies, luminous red galaxies, and quasars [DESI Collaboration et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168...58D/abstract). At higher redshifts, specialized surveys like the Ly$\alpha$ Tomography IMACS Survey (LATIS) provide deep insights. LATIS has collected over 5,500 spectroscopic redshifts at $z \sim 2\text{--}3$, enabling researchers to create intergalactic medium tomography maps of transmission at cosmic noon [Newman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJS..281...32N/abstract).

Despite their immense statistical power, traditional single-fiber surveys suffer from aperture effects, capturing only the integrated light of a galaxy's central region. This limits their utility for tracing internal galaxy kinematics, radial metallicity gradients, and local star formation histories. To resolve the spatial distribution of these properties across statistically significant samples, the observational paradigm has shifted to include large-scale [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] (IFS) surveys [Sánchez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...538A...8S/abstract). 

A foundational effort in this regime is the [[califa-survey|CALIFA survey]] (Calar Alto Legacy Integral Field Area), which obtained spatially resolved spectra for $\sim 600$ diameter-selected galaxies in the local universe ($0.005 < z < 0.03$) [Sánchez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...538A...8S/abstract). Covering the optical window at spectral resolutions of $R \sim 850$ and $R \sim 1650$, CALIFA provides contiguous two-dimensional maps of stellar ages, metallicities, and ionized gas excitation. The survey reaches a $3\sigma$ limiting surface brightness depth of $\sim 23.0 \text{ mag arcsec}^{-2}$ in the $V500$ grating [Sánchez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...538A...8S/abstract). By spatially resolving the archaeological record of stellar populations, CALIFA has provided direct observational evidence that massive galaxies assemble their stellar mass via inside-out growth, and that radial age gradients strongly correlate with the local stellar mass density [Delgado et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..300D/abstract). 

The synergy between wide-area cosmological redshift surveys and targeted IFS campaigns bridges the gap between the macro-scale environment of galaxies and their internal baryonic physics. While surveys like [[desi-survey|DESI]] map the three-dimensional matter distribution to constrain cosmological parameters and measure baryon acoustic oscillations, resolved surveys like CALIFA dissect the localized chemistry and star formation histories that govern galaxy evolution within those dark matter halos.

## Panchromatic Federation and Multi-Instrument Synergies

Modern [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] relies heavily on multi-wavelength data federation to break physical degeneracies, such as the age-metallicity relation and [[dustpedia|dust obscuration]]. By integrating data across the electromagnetic spectrum, astronomers can construct comprehensive master catalogs that provide robust physical parameter estimations and mitigate the selection biases inherent to single-band observations.

A cornerstone of this effort is the integration of infrared imaging with wide-area optical surveys. The [[unwise|unWISE]] project, for example, systematically reprocessed archival Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer (WISE) and NEOWISE data to produce the deepest full-sky maps available at $3.4$ and $4.6\ \mu\mathrm{m}$. These image coadds reach depths $0.56$ and $0.46$ magnitudes deeper than the pre-hibernation WISE data, respectively [Meisner et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AJ....154..161M/abstract). This homogenized mid-infrared photometry provides critical [[target-selection|Target selection]] capabilities for large-scale spectroscopic campaigns like the [[desi-survey|DESI survey]] [Meisner et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021RNAAS...5..200M/abstract). In the deep-field regime, the Spitzer-CANDELS (S-CANDELS) program integrated 50 hours of Spitzer IRAC observations with the optical and near-infrared Hubble Space Telescope footprint of the [[candels-survey|CANDELS survey]]. Despite reaching an unprecedented depth of $26.5$ AB mag at $3.6$ and $4.5\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, this data federation did not resolve additional discrete contributions to the cosmic infrared background, leaving roughly half of the background unresolved [Ashby et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..218...33A/abstract).

Data federation extends to the ultraviolet (UV) and radio regimes, although the impact of specific wavebands is highly dependent on the targeted science. For instance, the GALEX-SDSS-WISE Legacy Catalog (GSWLC) combined [[galex|GALEX]] UV data with optical and mid-infrared photometry to measure precise star formation rates and dust attenuations for $\sim 700,000$ low-redshift galaxies [Salim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..227....2S/abstract). However, evaluating the necessity of UV photometry for next-generation cosmological constraints reveals a nuanced trade-off: recent analyses indicate that while UV photometry can improve redshift recovery for certain galaxy populations, its overall benefit is limited by the decreased likelihood of detecting high-redshift sources at shorter wavelengths [Scott et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985..227S/abstract). In the X-ray regime, synergies with the [[erosita|eROSITA]] All-Sky Survey (eRASS1) have enabled the identification of nearly 570,000 extragalactic counterparts, primarily active galactic nuclei (AGNs), using Bayesian matching with Legacy Survey and [[gaia-eso-survey|Gaia]] catalogs [Salvato et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250902842S/abstract). Moving to the sub-millimeter regime, the AS2UDS survey utilized ALMA follow-up of single-dish sub-millimeter sources in the SCUBA-2 Cosmology Legacy Survey to detect over 700 [[radio-surveys|sub-millimeter galaxies]], tracing strongly star-forming populations at $z \sim 2.6$ [Stach et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.4648S/abstract). In the radio regime, continuum and $21$-cm [[blind-hi-surveys|blind surveys]] add a critical dimension to panchromatic catalogs. A milestone in this area was the Arecibo Legacy Fast ALFA (ALFALFA) survey, a blind 21-cm legacy program that provided a census of the local H I universe, detecting nearly 730 sources in the Virgo region alone, most of which lacked previous radio identifications [Giovanelli et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AJ....133.2569G/abstract). Furthermore, 21-cm blind legacy surveys like ALFALFA hold significant discovery potential for uncovering optically dark, gas-rich galaxies that evade traditional optical detection [Davies et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.368.1479D/abstract). The MeerKAT Galaxy Cluster Legacy Survey (MGCLS) provides long-track L-band ($900\text{--}1670$ MHz) observations with typical sensitivities of $3\text{--}5\ \mu\mathrm{Jy\ beam}^{-1}$, yielding a full [[master-catalogue|catalog]] of $\sim 626,000$ sources, though cross-matching to optical and infrared counterparts was performed specifically for compact sources in the fields of Abell 209 and Abell S295 [Knowles et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...657A..56K/abstract).

The culmination of these multi-instrument efforts is the generation of homogenized, all-sky master catalogs that support multi-messenger astrophysics and large-scale structure studies. Projects such as the Reference Catalog of Spectral Energy Distributions (RCSEDv2) and REGALADE systematically reprocess archival imaging and spectroscopy into unified databases, with the latter uniting nearly 80 million galaxies out to $2000\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ [Chilingarian et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ASPC..535..179C/abstract), [Tranin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250813267T/abstract). 

Looking forward, traditional catalog-level matching is being superseded by Joint Survey Processing (JSP). JSP operates directly at the pixel level to federate data from upcoming flagships like the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]], the Roman Space Telescope, and [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]]. This rigorous pixel-level integration is required to accurately model mismatched isophotes and mitigate the severe source blending intrinsic to deep imaging, thereby limiting systematic biases in weak lensing profiles and photometric redshift estimations [Chary et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200810663C/abstract), [Ramel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241202587R/abstract).

## Legacy Samples as Cosmological Probes

Legacy astronomical surveys are foundational to modern cosmology, providing the massive, statistically robust datasets required to constrain dark energy, dark matter, and the growth of large-scale structure. By systematically mapping millions of galaxies across significant cosmological volumes, these programs enable precision tests of the standard cosmological model through baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO), weak gravitational lensing, and galaxy cluster abundances.

**Large-Scale Structure and BAO**
Massive spectroscopic programs, such as the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] and the [[desi-survey|DESI survey]], map the three-dimensional distribution of galaxies and quasars to measure the BAO scale and redshift-space distortions. The BAO feature acts as a standard cosmological ruler; for instance, cross-correlating the projected density of eBOSS quasars with dense photometric samples from the DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys yields measurements of the comoving angular distance, $D_M$, to 6% precision at $0.6 \le z \le 1.2$ [Zarrouk et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.2562Z/abstract). Additionally, 'Kinematic Lensing' (KL) is emerging as a novel technique for spectroscopic surveys like the [[desi-survey|DESI survey]] to probe structure formation at very low redshifts ($z < 0.15$) by measuring velocity fields [Xu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240720867X/abstract). 

Constructing such large-scale maps requires strict control over the survey [[selection-function|Selection function]] to mitigate Malmquist bias. To isolate intrinsic clustering from observational selection effects, analyses often utilize [[volume-limited-samples|Volume-limited samples]] complete down to a redshift-dependent absolute luminosity threshold, 
$$ L > L_{\rm lim}(z) $$
discarding fainter sources to maintain uniform density statistics.

**Weak Lensing and Photometric Tensions**
Weak lensing probes the underlying dark matter distribution by measuring the coherent distortion—or cosmic shear—of background galaxy shapes. However, the statistical power of Stage IV imaging surveys like the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]] and [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]] is critically bottlenecked by two main systematics: photometric redshift (photo-$z$) uncertainties and source blending. High-density, deep imaging inevitably leads to overlapping sources; if uncorrected, this source confusion biases the recovered weak lensing shear profiles and structure amplitude measurements [Ramel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241202587R/abstract).

To overcome these limitations, the community is moving toward Joint Survey Processing. Combining high-resolution near-infrared imaging from the Roman Space Telescope with the wide-field optical coverage of [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|LSST]] at the pixel level can break photo-$z$ degeneracies and resolve blended systems, which is forecast to substantially increase the dark energy Figure of Merit (FoM) compared to individual survey baselines [Eifler et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.1514E/abstract).

**Strong Lensing**
In addition to weak lensing, [[sloan-giant-arcs-survey|strong gravitational lensing]] is emerging as a competitive cosmic probe. Future Stage IV surveys like the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]] and [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]] are forecast to discover between $1.2 \times 10^5$ and $1.7 \times 10^5$ galaxy-galaxy strong lenses. This three orders of magnitude increase over current samples will significantly enhance the statistical power of lensing to constrain cosmological parameters and dark matter profiles [Collett et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...811...20C/abstract).

**Clusters and Systematics Mitigation**
Galaxy clusters provide an independent cosmological probe via their [[stellar-mass-function|mass function]] and redshift evolution. Cluster detection in wide-field data has evolved from visual identification to automated algorithms exploiting photometric redshifts and stellar mass overdensities. For example, the CluMPR algorithm applied to the DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys has successfully identified roughly 300,000 cluster candidates out to $z=1$ [Yantovski-Barth et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.2285Y/abstract).

Extracting unbiased cosmological parameters from these samples requires rigorous characterization of survey systematics, such as varying seeing and imaging depth. The state-of-the-art approach involves synthetic source injection (SSI) to forward-model observational biases. By injecting synthetic sources into pixel-level on-sky images, collaborations can achieve percent-level accuracy in calibrating object detection rates, star-galaxy classification, galaxy redshift estimation, and photometric performance [Anbajagane et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250105683A/abstract). Such systematic mitigation is essential across all cosmological probes, as uncorrected observational errors—such as those historically characterizing [[pantheon-type-ia-supernova-sample|Type Ia supernova]] distance measurements—can degrade the dark energy FoM by factors of 3 to 4 [Ruiz et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvD..86j3004R/abstract).

**Intensity Mapping**
Another emerging legacy technique is [[galaxy-mapping|intensity mapping]], which traces large-scale structure by measuring integrated atomic or molecular emission lines across wide fields. When cross-correlated with massive galaxy redshift surveys, intensity mapping allows researchers to evade the limitations of cosmic variance, yielding highly precise cosmological constraints without requiring individual galaxy detections [Oxholm et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104h3501O/abstract).

**Supernovae and Star Formation**
Large synoptic surveys like [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|LSST]] and [[pan-starrs-surveys|Pan-STARRS]] are also set to map the cosmic core-collapse supernova redshift distribution, providing independent constraints on the star-formation rate history of the Universe [Lien et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009JCAP...01..047L/abstract).

## Galaxy Evolution, Clustering, and Environmental Studies

Legacy surveys provide the homogeneous, multi-wavelength baselines necessary to trace galaxy assembly, stellar mass growth, and environmental effects across cosmic time. By combining wide-area photometry with massive spectroscopic campaigns, these datasets enable the statistical characterization of galaxies from the local Universe to the epoch of reionization.

**Stellar Mass Assembly and Galaxy Evolution**
At high redshifts, space-based infrared imaging is critical for measuring the growth of stellar mass. Surveys such as the Spitzer Extended Deep Survey (SEDS) and [[candels-survey|S-CANDELS]] utilized deep imaging to constrain stellar mass functions down to faint limits [Fazio et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..446..347F/abstract), [Ashby et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..218...33A/abstract). Recently, James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) legacy programs like [[jades|JADES]] have pushed these studies into the ultra-deep regime. Programs like CEERS and JADES have spectroscopically characterized hundreds of galaxies at $z > 5$, and utilized deep imaging to discover robust samples of candidates at $z > 10$ [D'Eugenio et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240406531D/abstract), [Finkelstein et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250104085F/abstract). However, tensions remain in the literature; for instance, JWST mid-infrared galaxy counts between 5 $\mu$m and 25 $\mu$m diverge from recent semi-analytic models, potentially due to inaccuracies in the theoretical treatment of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) emission features [Stone et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...972...62S/abstract).

At intermediate redshifts, the [[devils-survey|Deep Extragalactic VIsible Legacy Survey (DEVILS)]] provides high-completeness spectroscopic samples to study galaxy evolution and group environments over the last 6 Gyr, supported by matched-source multi-wavelength photometry from the FUV to $500\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ [Davies et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506..256D/abstract). For [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|Local Volume and Nearby Galaxy Studies]], the paradigm has shifted from integrated light measurements to spatially resolved histories. Spatially resolved surveys like [[califa-survey|CALIFA]] have been instrumental in providing 2D kinematic and chemical maps of nearby galaxies, confirming that massive galaxies predominantly exhibit inside-out stellar mass growth [Sánchez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...538A...8S/abstract), [Delgado et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..300D/abstract). To consolidate observations of nearby systems, tools like the Local Volume Database (LVDB) serve as community legacy resources for the structural and dynamical properties of [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|dwarf galaxies]] and star clusters in the Local Group [Pace et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025OJAp....8E.142P/abstract). 

**Morphology Classification**
Beyond stellar mass growth, wide-field legacy data facilitates the identification of rare evolutionary phases. High-quality imaging from the DECam Legacy Survey has facilitated the discovery of small-scale (1-2 arcsec) dual quasars, which serve as precursors to binary supermassive black holes [Chen et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210906881C/abstract). Additionally, the sheer volume of modern legacy datasets has necessitated automated approaches to morphology classification. While early efforts relied heavily on visual inspection through citizen science (e.g., Galaxy Zoo), current implementations leverage deep learning frameworks to replicate expert classifications at scales of tens of millions of objects. Advanced machine learning techniques—such as unsupervised domain adaptation and generative AI architectures—allow models to transfer seamlessly across surveys and extract latent structural features without relying on pre-defined parametric classes (see Methodological Advances).

**Cluster Finding and Environmental Studies**
Legacy imaging is foundational for cluster finding and mapping large-scale structure. The detection of galaxy clusters has evolved from visual inspections of photographic plates to fully automated algorithms that identify stellar mass overdensities utilizing [[redshift-catalogs|Redshift catalogs]] derived from multi-band photometry. For example, the [[madcows|MaDCoWS]]2 survey has cataloged over 22,000 infrared-selected clusters, including 1,300 systems at $z > 1.5$ [Thongkham et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967..123T/abstract). Extended halo-based group finders applied to these wide-area photometric sets have mapped 6.4 million galaxy groups, directly linking galaxy properties to the mass of their host dark matter halos [Yang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909..143Y/abstract). Finally, [[large-area-radio-surveys|Large area radio surveys]] complement optical studies by probing the non-thermal intra-cluster environment; the MeerKAT Galaxy Cluster Legacy Survey (MGCLS) has detected diffuse radio emission (halos and relics) across 62 clusters, exposing the dynamic and magnetic complexity of these massive structures [Knowles et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...657A..56K/abstract).

## Methodological Advances: Machine Learning and Synthetic Data

Modern [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|legacy astronomical surveys]] process millions to billions of sources, rendering traditional manual morphological classification and error characterization intractable. To meet the demands of this "big data" regime, survey pipelines have increasingly integrated machine learning and generative data techniques to extract robust astrophysical parameters and mitigate observational systematics.

To scale visual classification efforts, researchers have widely deployed convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on crowd-sourced labels. For instance, the Galaxy Zoo DESI project utilized deep learning to derive detailed morphological measurements for over 8.67 million galaxies across the [[decals|DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys]]. Beyond supervised classification, unsupervised approaches such as variational auto-encoders (VAEs) are now used to reduce the dimensionality of galaxy images, extracting latent morphological features without relying on pre-defined classes [Xu et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.6391X/abstract). 

A persistent challenge in applying deep learning to large survey composites is the discrepancy in image quality, such as variations in seeing, depth, and signal-to-noise ratio across different telescope footprints. To bridge this gap, unsupervised domain adaptation has been employed to transfer models trained on one data set (e.g., DECaLS) to others with different noise properties (e.g., BASS and MzLS) without introducing systematic biases [Ye et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241215533Y/abstract), [Ye et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.537..640Y/abstract). For rare objects where training data is intrinsically scarce, such as interacting systems, few-shot learning techniques have successfully identified large samples, yielding catalogues of over 648,000 galaxy merger candidates [Wei et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..274...23W/abstract). Machine learning also extends to parameter estimation; hybrid CNN and multilayer perceptron (MLP) architectures combining image and catalog data have significantly improved photometric redshift estimation for emission-line galaxies, achieving a normalized median absolute deviation ($\sigma_{\mathrm{NMAD}}$) of $0.0140$ [Wei et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250524175W/abstract). Further demonstrating this versatility, the SCONE deep learning framework was developed for photometric supernova classification, achieving $>99\%$ accuracy in distinguishing Type Ia supernovae for upcoming [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|LSST]] and Roman pipelines [Qu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240604529Q/abstract). Additionally, simulation-based inference (SBI) techniques, such as Neural Posterior Estimation, are being applied to automate mass model parameter estimation for thousands of galaxy-galaxy lenses in surveys like [[dark-energy-survey|DES]] and LSST [Poh et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221105836P/abstract).

As cosmological constraints become increasingly limited by systematic rather than statistical uncertainties, accurately characterizing a survey's [[selection-function|Selection function]] is paramount. For upcoming Stage IV surveys like [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid]] and Roman, it is critical to model complex systematics such as magnification and evolution biases, which can significantly alter the observed galaxy number density [Maartens et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...12..009M/abstract). The state-of-the-art approach to quantifying detection efficiency, photometric errors, and morphological biases is synthetic source injection (SSI). In this method, simulated galaxy profiles are inserted directly into pixel-level survey images and processed through the standard data reduction pipelines. A prime example is the Balrog pipeline utilized by the [[dark-energy-survey|Dark Energy Survey]] (DES). For the DES Year 6 analysis, Balrog was used to inject 146 million synthetic sources across the 5000 deg$^2$ survey footprint, allowing the collaboration to validate object detection rates and star-galaxy classification accuracy at the percent level [Anbajagane et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250105683A/abstract).

SSI and advanced ML modeling are particularly crucial for mitigating source confusion, or "blending," in deep imaging. In simulated data matched to the depth of the forthcoming [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]], blending affects approximately 27% of detected sources [Ramel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241202587R/abstract). If uncorrected, this blending introduces a number count bias that significantly alters clustering statistics, systematically deviating the two-point correlation function at $>3\sigma$ significance on angular scales $\theta < 10'$ [Levine et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241114564L/abstract). Furthermore, overlapping light profiles can severely bias the shear estimates required for weak gravitational lensing [Ramel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241202587R/abstract). By leveraging synthetic injection to map the response of detection algorithms to crowded fields, and by employing neural networks to model the spurious correlations between galaxy density and observational systematics (such as seeing and sky brightness), modern pipelines can effectively extract robust cosmological signals from highly contaminated regimes [Zarrouk et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.2562Z/abstract), [Eggert et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...30E/abstract).

## Future Directions: Stage IV Surveys and the Low Surface Brightness Frontier

The next era of observational cosmology is defined by the advent of Stage IV surveys, spearheaded by the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]], the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, and [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]]. A defining characteristic of this era is the necessary transition from isolated, single-facility datasets to joint survey processing (JSP) [Chary et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200810663C/abstract). Multi-wavelength synergy—specifically combining deep optical imaging with high-resolution near-infrared (NIR) data—is essential to break cosmological parameter degeneracies and mitigate systematic errors, such as intrinsic alignments and photometric redshift uncertainties [Yung et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.1578Y/abstract), [Bianco et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240202378B/abstract).

The potential overlap between the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope and [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|LSST]] offers unprecedented statistical power. Forecasts demonstrate that dedicating five months of Roman observing time to a wide, single-band survey covering the full $\sim 18,000$ deg$^2$ LSST footprint would yield high-resolution NIR imaging for over 95% of the LSST "gold" galaxy sample [Eifler et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.1514E/abstract). This combined Roman-Rubin strategy is projected to increase the dark energy Figure of Merit (FoM) by a factor of 5.5 compared to the baseline Roman high-latitude reference survey alone [Eifler et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.1514E/abstract). However, the optimal survey strategy for Roman—whether to prioritize a deep, narrow $\sim 2000$ deg$^2$ multi-band High Latitude Survey (HLS) or a wide, single-band overlap with LSST to maximize synergy—remains an active area of debate within the literature [Eifler et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.1514E/abstract), [Eifler et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241104088E/abstract). Extending this philosophy, there are proposals for NANCY (Next-generation All-sky Near-infrared Community surveY), an all-sky high-resolution infrared map from the Roman Space Telescope designed to drastically enhance the science reach of current spectroscopic and imaging legacy surveys [Han et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230611784H/abstract).

As these Stage IV surveys reach unprecedented depths, they confront severe source confusion. Simulation-based inference frameworks, such as the previously mentioned Balrog pipeline, will be critical for characterizing the [[selection-function|Selection function]] and quantifying [[sample-selection-biases|Sample selection biases]] in these crowded fields [Anbajagane et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250105683A/abstract). 

Beyond dark energy, Stage IV surveys will revolutionize the exploration of the low surface brightness universe. By reaching optical surface brightness limits of $\mu_V > 27$ to $30$ mag/arcsec$^2$, LSST and Roman will expose the faint structures that trace hierarchical galaxy assembly, including tidal tails, stellar streams, and intracluster light [Brough et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200111067B/abstract), [Montes et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230609414M/abstract). In preparation for this era, panoramic imaging from DECam has been used to visualize the "Field of Streams," mapping stellar substructures in the Milky Way halo across 18,700 square degrees [Ferguson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250605469F/abstract). At depths approaching $\mu \approx 31$ mag/arcsec$^2$, defining the physical "edge" of a galaxy becomes challenging and highly sensitive to imaging depth. New non-parametric methodologies, such as mapping the second derivative of the 2D surface mass density via Wiener-Hunt deconvolution, are being developed to robustly identify galaxy truncation radii at stellar mass densities of $\sim 1 M_\odot/\text{pc}^2$, which may trace the physical threshold for in-situ star formation [Golini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...700A..91G/abstract). In this regime, the high spatial resolution of the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will be critical to minimize source confusion, allowing astronomers to isolate globular clusters and [[miles-stellar-library|resolved stellar populations]] within these ultra-diffuse structures, effectively breaking the age-metallicity degeneracy in extragalactic halos [Dage et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230612620D/abstract), [Montes et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230609414M/abstract).
