The **Canadian Galactic Plane Survey (CGPS)** is a high-resolution, multi-wavelength imaging project designed to map the multi-phase interstellar medium (ISM) across the disk of the Milky Way. Utilizing the [[dominion-radio-astrophysical-observatory|Dominion Radio Astrophysical Observatory]] (DRAO) Synthesis Telescope, the survey provides simultaneous $1'$ resolution mapping of the 21-cm neutral hydrogen (H I) spectral line, dual-frequency radio continuum emission, and full Stokes polarimetry [English et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998PASA...15...56E/abstract). By integrating these core radio observations with infrared dust catalogs and molecular line data, the CGPS shifted the paradigm of Galactic astrophysics from targeted observations of isolated objects to panoramic studies of the ISM as a dynamic, interconnected system. It also serves as the foundational northern-hemisphere component of the International Galactic Plane Survey (IGPS), coordinating with southern and inner-galaxy [[radio-surveys|Radio surveys]] to construct a comprehensive view of the Galactic disk [Stil et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....132.1158S/abstract).

A primary scientific achievement of the CGPS is its rigorous characterization of the Galactic magnetic field and the magneto-ionic medium. By compiling a dense grid of Faraday Rotation Measures (RM) from background extragalactic sources, the survey allowed astronomers to trace the large-scale topology of the Milky Way's magnetic field [Brown et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563L..31B/abstract). This revealed that the magnetic field in the outer Galaxy is primarily azimuthal, disproving earlier models that postulated a global field reversal beyond the solar circle. Furthermore, the survey's polarimetry mapping introduced the concept of a "polarization horizon," demonstrating that differential Faraday dispersion limits the detection of polarized features to local kiloparsec scales [Landecker et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AAS...199.5807L/abstract). Alongside magnetic field structure, the survey precisely maps the thermodynamic phase transitions of the ISM, correlating H I self-absorption with dense gas tracers to observe the condensation of diffuse atomic clouds into star-forming molecular cores [Hill et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019clrp.2020...44H/abstract).

The comprehensive dataset provided by the CGPS has profoundly advanced our understanding of stellar feedback and Galactic structure. Its high sensitivity to extended, low-surface-brightness features has led to the discovery of faint supernova remnants (SNRs), massive H I shells, and wind-blown bubbles. By identifying some of the faintest shell-type remnants ever cataloged, the CGPS directly addresses the "missing SNR" problem—the historical discrepancy between theoretical supernova rates and observed remnant populations [Foster et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...549A.107F/abstract). In addition to discovering new structures, the CGPS pioneered refined methodologies for Galactic distance determination. By combining high-resolution atomic column densities with updated, non-flat Galactic rotation curve models—an approach echoed by complementary infrared mapping in the [[hi-gal-survey|Hi-GAL survey]]—researchers can now derive highly accurate physical distances and overcome the ambiguities of traditional kinematic models [Foster et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...598.1005F/abstract).

Despite these major advances, the CGPS highlights several critical open questions regarding the physics of the Galactic disk. Chief among these are the exact scale of the remaining "missing" SNR population that eludes current [[large-area-radio-surveys|Large area radio surveys]], and whether the CO-to-H$_2$ conversion factor (the X-factor) is highly non-linear, which would indicate that the Galaxy's total molecular mass has been significantly underestimated. Additionally, robustly isolating natural ISM turbulence from the sharp boundaries of local emission regions remains a significant challenge. Addressing these tensions, alongside clarifying the precise magnetic topology of the extreme outer Galaxy, drives ongoing research and motivates future upgrades to broadband polarimetry and recombination line mapping capabilities at DRAO and other major observatories.

## Survey Overview and Scientific Objectives

The CGPS represents a paradigm shift in Galactic astronomy, moving from targeted observations of individual objects to panoramic, high-resolution imaging of the Milky Way disk [Foster et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..438...16F/abstract). The primary scientific objective of the survey is to study the Interstellar Medium (ISM) not as a collection of isolated phenomena, but as a dynamic, multi-phase, and interconnected system. By mapping the Galactic plane at a common angular resolution of $1'$, the survey traces the physical states and transitions of the ISM—including neutral atomic gas, molecular gas, ionized gas, dust, and relativistic plasma [English et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998PASA...15...56E/abstract), [Hill et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019clrp.2020...44H/abstract). 

The principal instrument driving the survey is the [[dominion-radio-astrophysical-observatory|Dominion Radio Astrophysical Observatory]] (DRAO) Synthesis Telescope, which provides simultaneous 21 cm H I spectral line mapping, dual-frequency radio continuum imaging at 1420 MHz and 408 MHz, and full Stokes polarimetry [English et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998PASA...15...56E/abstract), [Kothes et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..438..415K/abstract). To fulfill its scientific mandate, the CGPS heavily relies on multi-wavelength imaging, integrating these radio observations with high-resolution $^{12}$CO ($J=1\rightarrow0$) molecular line data and infrared dust emission catalogs [Kerton et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJS..126...85K/abstract), [Foster et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...598.1005F/abstract). While the CGPS maps the northern Milky Way, comprehensive studies of the full Galactic disk rely on complementary datasets in other hemispheres and wavelengths. For instance, the completion and digital release of the [[anglo-australian-telescope|Anglo-Australian Telescope]] (AAO/UKST) H$\alpha$ survey serves as a primary optical line-emission resource for the southern Galactic Plane [Parker et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998PASA...15...28P/abstract), [Parker et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASPC..289..165P/abstract). Additionally, essential short-spacing and all-sky 21-cm H I data are provided by panoramic surveys such as [[gass-survey|GASS survey]] and EBHIS [Kalberla et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009pra..confE..26K/abstract). Complementary efforts also focus on the inner Galaxy; the GBT multiwavelength survey of the Galactic Center Region clarifies the relative contribution of thermal and nonthermal emission in the central degrees of the Galaxy [Law et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJS..177..255L/abstract), while the HST/[[nicmos|NICMOS]] Paschen-$\alpha$ survey provides a high-resolution near-infrared complement for studying ionized gas and massive stars in the nuclear environment [Wang et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.402..895W/abstract).

To map obscured stellar populations and interstellar dark clouds globally, researchers have relied on mid-infrared databases such as the ISOGAL survey [Omont et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003A&A...403..975O/abstract), and massive campaigns like the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (SEGUE) provide critical spectroscopic characterization of Galactic populations and kinematics [Yanny et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AJ....137.4377Y/abstract). In the Galactic anticenter, the [[lamost-spectroscopic-survey|LAMOST Spectroscopic Survey]] of the Galactic Anticentre (LSS-GAC) contributes a massive spectroscopic catalog of stellar parameters [Yuan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.448..855Y/abstract). Summaries of progress in mapping the stellar disk and spiral structure highlight the use of infrared standard candles, such as red clump giants, to trace these distributions [Benjamin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AAS...212.8601B/abstract). In the southern hemisphere, the Dark Energy Camera Plane Survey 2 (DECaPS2) provides a multi-band optical and near-infrared census of 3.32 billion objects [Saydjari et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..264...28S/abstract). Furthermore, wide-area optical surveys like the [[dark-energy-survey|Dark Energy Survey]] (DES) also sample billions of stars, providing constraints on the Galactic stellar halo, the low-mass end of the initial mass function, and halo flattening [Rossetto et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AJ....141..185R/abstract). The [[bolocam-galactic-plane-survey|Bolocam Galactic Plane Survey]] (BGPS) complements radio data as a systematic 1.1 mm continuum survey identifying thousands of cold dust clumps [Aguirre et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJS..192....4A/abstract). Finally, 21-cm observations not only map the Milky Way but also penetrate it; surveys like the WSRT Zone-of-Avoidance Perseus-Pisces filament survey are designed to map galaxies hidden by Galactic dust extinction [Ramatsoku et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460..923R/abstract).

A major objective of the CGPS is the detailed mapping of the large-scale Galactic magnetic field. Utilizing compact extragalactic background sources, the survey achieved an unprecedented RM source density of approximately $1 \text{ deg}^{-2}$ across the Galactic plane [Brown et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563L..31B/abstract), [Brown et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJS..145..213B/abstract). This RM grid was explicitly designed to address an active debate regarding the topology of the magnetic field. While earlier models postulated a global magnetic field reversal between the solar circle and the Perseus arm, the consensus derived from CGPS data strongly indicates that no such reversal exists in the outer Galaxy, where the field is likely purely azimuthal [Brown et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563L..31B/abstract), [Brown et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...592L..29B/abstract), [Van Eck et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...728...97V/abstract). Furthermore, CGPS polarimetry introduced the concept of the "polarization horizon"—demonstrating that due to depth and beam depolarization, the detection of extended polarized features is limited to a specific distance, constraining the majority of observed polarized structures to the relatively local ISM [Landecker et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AAS...199.5807L/abstract).

Additionally, the survey is designed to quantify the mechanical energy injected into the ISM by stellar feedback. The CGPS systematically identifies large-scale H I shells, wind-blown bubbles, and SNRs that act as the "fossils" of stellar evolution [Normandeau et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AJ....119.2982N/abstract). This directly targets a long-standing tension in astrophysics: the significant "missing population" of SNRs in the Galaxy compared to theoretical expectations. By achieving exceptional surface brightness sensitivities, the CGPS has discovered several of the lowest surface brightness SNRs ever cataloged, such as G152.4-2.1 ($\Sigma_{\text{1 GHz}} \lesssim 5 \times 10^{-23} \text{ W m}^{-2} \text{ Hz}^{-1} \text{ sr}^{-1}$), thereby helping to resolve the discrepancy between observed remnants and theoretical supernova rates [Foster et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...549A.107F/abstract). Complementary southern-hemisphere efforts, such as the Molonglo Galactic Plane Survey, also actively tackle this missing SNR problem [Green et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PASA...31...42G/abstract). Ultimately, the synthesis of these large-scale kinematic and continuum maps allows the CGPS to bridge the gap between parsec-scale ISM ecology and global Galactic structure [Foster et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..438...16F/abstract).

## Historical Development and the International Collaboration

The CGPS originated as an ambitious initiative at DRAO to image the northern Milky Way at an unprecedented $1'$ resolution across multiple constituents of the interstellar medium [English et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998PASA...15...56E/abstract). The scientific necessity of such high-resolution, panoramic mapping was demonstrated early on by a pilot project, which successfully uncovered complex, previously uncatalogued structures such as the large, outer-Galaxy H I shell G132.6-0.7-25.3 [Normandeau et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AJ....119.2982N/abstract).

Before the era of full synthesis mapping, early efforts like the MOST 843 MHz Galactic Centre survey provided an early panoramic view of regions such as Sgr A-E [Gray et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994MNRAS.270..822G/abstract), leading to the proposed scope of the broader MOST Galactic Plane Survey covering the southern fourth quadrant [Green et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PASA...14...73G/abstract). In the infrared regime, early insights were provided by the first ISOCAM survey results, which identified dusty young stars and optically thick dense filaments in the mid-infrared [Perault et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996A&A...315L.165P/abstract). In addition, multi-wavelength collaborations have leveraged the Zone of Avoidance; for instance, [[two-micron-all-sky-survey-2mass|Two Micron All-Sky Survey (2MASS)]] extended source catalogs proved essential in discovering new galaxies previously hidden by Milky Way extinction [Jarrett et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AJ....120..298J/abstract).

Realizing that a comprehensive understanding of Galactic structure required contiguous mapping beyond the northern sky limits of DRAO, the CGPS evolved into the foundational component of the International Galactic Plane Survey (IGPS). The IGPS was conceived as a coordinated, global endeavor to combine arcminute-resolution 21-cm line and continuum surveys, ultimately covering approximately 90% of the Galactic disk [Stil et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....132.1158S/abstract). 

To achieve this global coverage, the CGPS was joined by two major sister surveys. The Southern Galactic Plane Survey (SGPS) was executed to cover the fourth Galactic quadrant and southern hemisphere ($253^{\circ} \leq l \leq 358^{\circ}$), utilizing the Australia Telescope Compact Array combined with single-dish data to capture structures across a wide range of angular scales [McClure-Griffiths et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001PhDT.........9M/abstract), [Gaensler et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...549..959G/abstract). Meanwhile, the VLA Galactic Plane Survey (VGPS) filled the critical inner-Galaxy gap ($18^{\circ} \leq l \leq 67^{\circ}$) using the Very Large Array, supplemented by the Green Bank Telescope for essential short-spacing information [Lockman et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..317...20L/abstract), [Stil et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....132.1158S/abstract). Beyond these radio-specific initiatives, the broader IGPS framework synergizes with primary mid-infrared partner surveys like the Galactic Legacy Infrared Mid-Plane Survey Extraordinaire ([[glimpse-survey|GLIMPSE survey]]), which provided a critical census of star formation and stellar structure to complement the radio observations [Churchwell et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AAS...198.2504C/abstract).

Combining data from different interferometers and single-dish facilities presented significant technical challenges, primarily due to variations in instrumentation, beam characteristics, and image-processing techniques. Despite these hurdles, extensive cross-calibration yielded impressive consistency between the datasets. For instance, in overlapping or comparable regions, the differences between VGPS and CGPS brightness temperatures were found to be remarkably small—typically $<6$ K (root-mean-square) in channels where the mean H I brightness temperature exceeds 80 K [Stil et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....132.1158S/abstract).

The seamless integration of the CGPS, VGPS, and SGPS into the IGPS framework has had a profound impact on Galactic astrophysics. A premier example of this synergy is the study of the large-scale Galactic magnetic field. By compiling Faraday Rotation Measures from extragalactic background sources across all three surveys, researchers achieved a unified RM dataset that allowed astronomers to test competing models of magnetic field topologies. The global view provided by the IGPS demonstrated that the inner Galaxy field closely tracks the spiral arms, while the outer Galaxy field is consistent with a purely azimuthal geometry lacking the global reversals that had been previously hypothesized from more limited data [van Eck et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..438..236V/abstract), [Van Eck et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...728...97V/abstract). Through this international coordination, the CGPS transitioned from a regional mapping effort into a cornerstone of modern Galactic structure research using panoramic [[radio-surveys|Radio surveys]].

## Atomic and Molecular Gas Phase Transitions

The Interstellar Medium is a deeply interconnected, multi-phase environment. The CGPS established a new standard for investigating the thermodynamic and kinematic links between these phases. By mapping the H I 21-cm line alongside molecular and continuum tracers at a matched angular resolution of $\approx 1'$ [English et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998PASA...15...56E/abstract), the survey captures both diffuse atomic gas and dense molecular cores simultaneously. These matched-resolution observations demonstrated that the evolution of the ISM—particularly the condensation of gas and the onset of star formation—cannot be understood when treating interstellar phases in isolation [Hill et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019clrp.2020...44H/abstract).

A primary mechanism for studying the cooling of the atomic ISM is the observation of H I self-absorption (HISA). HISA occurs when a foreground population of cold, dense atomic hydrogen absorbs the 21-cm emission from a warmer, more diffuse H I background. The CGPS revealed a wealth of small-scale structure within these self-absorbing cold neutral clouds [Hill et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019clrp.2020...44H/abstract). Additionally, high-resolution H I 21-cm line absorption against background continuum sources has been utilized to probe the physical properties and distances of intermediate-velocity clouds within the Galactic disk [Kerton et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131.1501K/abstract). In addition to tracing disk clouds, these H I absorption profiles can be used to differentiate objects based on radial velocity; for example, CGPS H I absorption data was specifically applied to the radio source 18P87 to determine whether it was a local Galactic jet or an extragalactic radio galaxy [Behre et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AN....331..420B/abstract). These absorption features pinpoint the specific sites where the atomic gas has cooled sufficiently to initiate the transition into the molecular phase. Furthermore, recent inner-Galaxy initiatives like the THOR survey have derived high-resolution H I optical depth maps, significantly impacting and correcting atomic mass estimates in the inner Milky Way [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...634A..83W/abstract).

To trace the subsequent condensation into molecular clouds, the CGPS incorporates extensive observations of carbon monoxide isotopologues, specifically the $^{12}$CO ($J=1 \rightarrow 0$) and $^{13}$CO ($J=1 \rightarrow 0$) rotational transitions. High-latitude extensions of the survey mapped these molecular lines over roughly 200 square degrees in the outer Galaxy [Brunt et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..317...79B/abstract). While $^{12}$CO traces the bulk distribution and line-of-sight kinematics of molecular gas at $1'$ resolution [Foster et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...598.1005F/abstract), accurately deriving the true molecular mass from this tracer remains an active area of debate. The literature highlights that the standard conversion factor between $^{12}$CO integrated intensity and H$_2$ column density (the $X$-factor) may be highly non-linear, suggesting that the molecular mass in the Galactic disk has frequently been underestimated [Barnes et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812....6B/abstract). To mitigate these optical depth effects, the incorporation of $^{13}$CO emission is critical; because it remains predominantly optically thin, $^{13}$CO offers a more direct probe of the gas column density and enables the clean separation of complex, overlapping velocity components along the line of sight [Rathborne et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJS..182..131R/abstract). In the inner Galaxy, the CHIMPS2 survey contributes high-resolution $^{12}$CO mapping of the Central Molecular Zone and foreground spiral arms [Eden et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.5936E/abstract), while the Southern Parkes Large-Area Survey in Hydroxyl (SPLASH) maps extended OH emission and its anomalous satellite line excitation [Dawson et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.3345D/abstract). High-resolution, multi-scale studies, such as those targeting the MUSCLE W49 region, utilize these multi-phase tracers to reveal the hierarchical filamentary structure of massive giant molecular clouds [Galván-Madrid et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...779..121G/abstract).

Complementary dense gas surveys further constrain the earliest stages of star formation. The [[hops-survey|HOPS survey]] has provided vital NH$_3$ catalogs that serve as dense gas tracer comparisons to the CGPS atomic and molecular data [Purcell et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.1972P/abstract), actively tracing dense gas flows and identifying the progenitors of massive stellar clusters [Longmore et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.1462L/abstract). Submillimeter mapping acts as another crucial pillar; ATLASGAL serves as a comprehensive submillimeter database for identifying massive pre-stellar clumps in the southern sky [Schuller et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...504..415S/abstract), while the JCMT Plane Survey (JPS) offers 850-$\mu$m continuum data to trace dense molecular clumps and ongoing star formation [Eden et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.2163E/abstract). Detailed spectroscopic follow-up, such as the BGPS complete spectroscopic catalog of HCO$^+$ and N$_2$H$^+$ transitions, characterizes these dense clumps throughout the Galactic plane [Shirley et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJS..209....2S/abstract).

By correlating the distribution of HISA features with $^{13}$CO emission, astronomers can directly observe the spatial and kinematic overlap between the coldest atomic gas and the envelopes of molecular clouds. This multi-tracer approach is fundamental for constraining phase transitions in the ISM. Cold atomic gas traced by H I self-absorption frequently envelopes or spatially coincides with the molecular gas mapped by CO emission, representing the intermediary stage in cloud evolution where atomic hydrogen converts into molecular hydrogen.

## Galactic Magnetic Fields and Radio Polarization

The CGPS provides unprecedented arcminute-resolution mapping of 1420 MHz linear polarization and continuum emission, serving as a critical probe for the magneto-ionic medium of the Milky Way. By utilizing compact extragalactic sources as background probes, the survey achieved an RM grid with a solid-angle density of roughly $1 \text{ deg}^{-2}$—approximately ten times greater than previous surveys in the Galactic plane [Brown et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563L..31B/abstract), [Brown et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJS..145..213B/abstract). 

**Faraday Rotation and the Polarization Horizon**
A major paradigm shift introduced by CGPS observations is the concept of the "polarization horizon." Previously, it was often assumed that polarized emission integrated along the entire line of sight through the Galactic disk. However, CGPS data demonstrated that depth depolarization (differential Faraday dispersion) and beam depolarization severely limit the detection of polarized features to a finite distance [Landecker et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AAS...199.5807L/abstract). For example, toward the Cygnus region ($l \approx 90^\circ$), this horizon is located within a few kilparsecs, effectively restricting the observable polarized structures to the Local Arm. 

In addition to compact extragalactic sources, the CGPS provides widespread RM data for diffuse, extended emission. Analyses of these fields show that the RM of the extended emission closely tracks the RM of extragalactic sources, but at approximately half the magnitude. This factor of two is consistent with a classic Burn slab model of the ISM, indicating that the Faraday-rotating medium and the synchrotron-emitting medium share similar variations with Galactocentric distance, and that both sets of sources probe similar depths in the outer Galaxy [Ordog et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Galax...7...43O/abstract).

**Magnetic Field Topology and Reversals**
Historically, models of the Galactic magnetic field postulated a global magnetic field reversal between the Solar circle and the Perseus arm. The dense CGPS RM grid strongly refutes this. The predominance of negative RMs in the second quadrant indicates that no such global reversal exists in the outer Galaxy [Brown et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563L..31B/abstract), [Brown et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...592L..29B/abstract). Comprehensive models combining CGPS data with other [[radio-surveys|Radio surveys]] suggest that while the magnetic field in the inner Galaxy tightly follows the spiral arms, the large-scale field in the outer Galaxy is consistent with being purely azimuthal [Van Eck et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...728...97V/abstract). Reviews of these CGPS-led innovations highlight how the survey's high-density RM grids fundamentally shifted the broader understanding of both the Galactic magnetic field's overarching topology and its random components [Brown et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..438..216B/abstract). The precise identification of an RM null point in the outer Galaxy—where the average RM of extragalactic sources transitions through zero—remains a critical constraint for these global magnetic field models [Rae et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..438..229R/abstract).

**Turbulence and the Random Magnetic Field**
The high-resolution polarization data have also revised understandings of ISM turbulence. Prior to the CGPS, models commonly assumed that the random, turbulent component of the Galactic magnetic field was isotropic. CGPS RM distributions, however, suggest that the random component is not isotropic but is preferentially aligned with the uniform, large-scale field [Brown et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...563L..31B/abstract), [Brown et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhDT........25B/abstract). 

Attempts to quantify the Mach number of this magneto-ionic turbulence have leveraged spatial gradients of the complex polarization vector. However, CGPS mapping reveals that the skewness and kurtosis of these polarization gradients are heavily influenced by the boundaries of bright emission structures rather than the underlying turbulent cascade, making them suboptimal statistics for probing natural turbulence [Herron et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835..210H/abstract). Furthermore, structure function analyses of RM fluctuations yield non-Kolmogorov power-law indices that vary with Galactic longitude, underscoring that ISM turbulence is intrinsically inhomogeneous across the Galactic disk [Sun et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004mim..proc...25S/abstract).

## Stellar Feedback: Supernova Remnants and H II Regions

The interstellar medium is continuously shaped by the injection of energy and momentum from massive stars. The panoramic, $1'$ resolution radio maps produced by the CGPS provide an ideal dataset for studying the multi-phase "fossils" of stellar feedback, capturing the complex interactions between expanding ionized gas, atomic shells, and the ambient molecular medium [English et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998PASA...15...56E/abstract). To formalize the census of these objects, researchers released the CGPS catalogue of extended radio sources, which provides multi-frequency flux density measurements and cross-identifications for H II regions and SNRs throughout the second Galactic quadrant [Kerton et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.379..289K/abstract).

**Supernova Remnants**
A long-standing problem in Galactic astrophysics is the "missing" population of supernova remnants (SNRs); theoretical models of supernova rates predict significantly more remnants than have been observed. Like other [[large-area-radio-surveys|Large area radio surveys]], the CGPS has been instrumental in addressing this discrepancy due to its high sensitivity to extended, low-surface-brightness radio continuum emission. Notably, CGPS data facilitated the discovery of G152.4-2.1 and G190.9-2.2, two of the lowest surface brightness SNRs ever cataloged [Foster et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...549A.107F/abstract). Both are shell-type remnants located at distances of approximately 1 kpc, with radio continuum spectral indices of $\alpha = -0.65 \pm 0.05$ and $-0.66 \pm 0.05$, respectively. Their surface brightnesses are exceptionally faint, reaching:
$$ \Sigma_{\text{1 GHz}} \lesssim 5 \times 10^{-23} \text{ W m}^{-2} \text{ Hz}^{-1} \text{ sr}^{-1} $$
Independent, earlier efforts in the southern hemisphere, such as targeted searches for optical counterparts using the UKST/AAO survey, have similarly aimed to characterize the physical and chemical properties of the historically "missing" SNR population [Walker et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998PASA...15...79W/abstract). Multi-wavelength surveys also enhance the detection of remnants and compact objects; for instance, the [[h-e-s-s-galactic-plane-survey|H.E.S.S. Galactic Plane Survey]] mapped a diverse population of TeV emitters dominated by SNRs and pulsar wind nebulae [Carrigan et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ICRC...33..952C/abstract). Toward the inner Galaxy, the confirmation of new SNRs near the Galactic Centre was achieved using GMRT observations of candidates from the MGCS [Roy et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006JPhCS..54..152R/abstract). Furthermore, high-energy X-ray maps from the XMM-Newton XGPS survey have provided a deep catalog of hard X-ray sources in the Galactic Plane [Hands et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002astro.ph..2180H/abstract), while the NuSTAR Serendipitous Survey identified low-luminosity high-mass X-ray binary candidates [Tomsick et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJS..230...25T/abstract). Multi-wavelength campaigns also excel at finding accretion-powered systems; the ChaMPlane survey aims to identify low-luminosity accretion sources like cataclysmic variables [Grindlay et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AN....324...57G/abstract), supplemented by WIYN spectroscopy that has successfully classified dMe stars and quasars near the anticenter [Rogel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJS..163..160R/abstract). Such datasets are crucial for identifying distant magnetic cataclysmic variables, such as Swift J183920.1-045350 [Gorgone et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210314800G/abstract). Beyond direct discovery, CGPS H I and $^{12}$CO data have been used to constrain the kinematic distances to known SNRs, such as DA 530, by measuring atomic hydrogen column densities to the Galactic edge to bypass standard kinematic models and derive more accurate physical distances [Foster et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...598.1005F/abstract).

**H I Shells and Wind-Blown Bubbles**
In addition to SNRs, CGPS H I 21 cm line data reveal an abundance of large-scale H I shells driven by stellar winds and historical supernovae. Observations indicate that many of these expanding H I shells lack a corresponding radio continuum or infrared counterpart, suggesting they are either very old or driven purely by winds rather than explosive transients [Normandeau et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AJ....119.2982N/abstract), [Cichowolski et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...525A.121C/abstract). For instance, the shell G132.6-0.7-25.3 is hypothesized to be a wind-blown bubble created by a single late-type O star evolving in a gas layer with a remarkably small scale height of $<30 \text{ pc}$ [Normandeau et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AJ....119.2982N/abstract). Similarly, the massive outer-Galaxy shell GSH 91.5+2-114, located at a kinematic distance of roughly 15 kpc, lacks radio continuum emission and was likely excavated by the combined stellar winds and supernovae of an unseen OB association [Cichowolski et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...525A.121C/abstract). Expanding infrared bubbles also shape the ISM; studies of 13 Galactic infrared bubble regions have demonstrated their connectivity with massive molecular clouds and the frequent presence of triggered star formation at the bubble borders [Yan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..117Y/abstract).

**H II Regions and Cloud Disruption**
The CGPS also traces the destructive feedback of H II regions on their natal molecular clouds. A prime example is the rediscovery and characterization of DA 568 in the Perseus arm. Originally overlooked in several catalogs, CGPS 1420 MHz continuum and molecular line data revealed it to be the dominant H II region within a $45 \times 20 \text{ pc}$ star-forming complex containing over $10^4 M_\odot$ of ionized and molecular gas [Kerton et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127.1059K/abstract). The spatial correlation between the ionized gas and the surrounding molecular material demonstrates that the original giant molecular cloud has been severely disrupted by the expansion of DA 568 [Kerton et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127.1059K/abstract). Other radio surveys complement this picture; early inner-Galaxy surveys systematically classified compact radio sources into H II regions and planetary nebulae [Helfand et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJS...80..211H/abstract). At higher frequencies, the [[cornish-survey|CORNISH survey]] serves as a 5 GHz radio continuum complement capable of detecting ultra-compact H II regions right across the Galaxy [Purcell et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..387..389P/abstract). More recently, a Green Bank Telescope survey detected angularly large H II regions exhibiting extremely narrow radio recombination lines, which indicates the presence of low-temperature thermal plasma [Anderson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJS..234...33A/abstract). High-resolution mid-infrared data from the SOFIA/FORCAST Galactic Center Legacy Survey provide further evidence of embedded star formation feedback by imaging complex structures in Sgr A, B, and C [Hankins et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...894...55H/abstract). In the inner regions, an arcsecond-resolution 1-2 GHz view into the Galactic Bulge has identified new pulsar and planetary nebula candidates [Pattie et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.2191P/abstract). Additionally, the [[glimpse-survey|GLIMPSE survey]] has been instrumental in exploring stellar feedback, with early findings detailing infrared reddening laws and mapping triggered star formation in regions like RCW49 [Churchwell et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23...53C/abstract), alongside the discovery of low-latitude globular clusters such as GLIMPSE-C01 and their potential interactions with the molecular layer [Kobulnicky et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AJ....129..239K/abstract). The combination of CGPS H I data and $^{12}$CO velocity measurements has allowed for refined kinematic distance estimates to dozens of Sharpless H II regions, often yielding smaller distances than those predicted by standard flat Galactic rotation curves [Foster et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...598.1005F/abstract).

## Advancements in Galactic Distance Determination

Accurate distance determination to objects within the Galactic disk has historically been hindered by severe dust extinction and the intrinsic ambiguities of mapping radial velocities to physical distances. The CGPS has been instrumental in advancing these measurements by combining high-resolution 21 cm H I emission data with molecular gas tracers. 

A novel methodology developed using CGPS data bypasses the limitations of traditional velocity-to-distance conversions by utilizing the H I column density [Foster et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...598.1005F/abstract). This technique determines the line-of-sight distance by integrating the atomic hydrogen column density from the observer, past the object in question, out to the Galactic edge. Crucially, the background model incorporates the smooth, large-scale distribution of H I while explicitly accounting for structural complexities such as scale-height flaring with increasing Galactocentric radius and the Galactic warp. The Galactic warp is a major structural feature that is particularly prominent in the First and Second Quadrants of the Milky Way, making its inclusion essential for accurate mass and distance estimates in these regions [Foster et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...598.1005F/abstract), [Foster et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..438...16F/abstract).

This method was validated against a sample of 29 Sharpless H II regions in the Second Quadrant. By utilizing $1'$ resolution $^{12}$CO ($J=1 \rightarrow 0$) data from the CGPS to establish precise line-of-sight velocities, researchers derived H I column-based distances that closely matched independent photometric distances [Foster et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...598.1005F/abstract). Notably, these revised distances were systematically smaller than the standard kinematic distance estimates derived under the assumption of a flat Galactic rotation curve, highlighting the limitations of flat-curve assumptions in the outer Galaxy.

Improved constraints on the outer Milky Way's rotation curve, driven by the high-resolution multi-phase mapping of the CGPS, have also resolved long-standing astrophysical debates. A prominent example is the pulsar wind nebula 3C 58. Previous H I absorption measurements had suggested a distance of $\approx 3.2$ kpc, casting doubt on the nebula's suspected connection to the historical supernova SN 1181. However, by re-evaluating CGPS H I absorption data in the context of an updated rotation curve for the outer Galaxy, its kinematic distance was recalibrated to 2 kpc, making the association with the A.D. 1181 event highly viable [Kothes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...560A..18K/abstract).

More broadly across modern Galactic plane mapping, refining the rotation curve remains an active area of research for converting radial velocities into physical distances. Multi-wavelength efforts, including those by the [[hi-gal-survey|Hi-GAL survey]], have demonstrated that the Galactic rotation curve is effectively parametrized by a power-law expression for distance determination [Russeil et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...601L...5R/abstract). A derived and widely applicable analytical form is:

$$ \frac{\theta(R)}{\theta_0} = 1.022 \left( \frac{R}{R_0} \right)^{0.0803} $$

where the adopted solar parameters are $R_0 = 8.34$ kpc and $\theta_0 = 240$ km s$^{-1}$ [Russeil et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...601L...5R/abstract). Together, these advancements highlight a fundamental shift from idealized kinematic models to empirically constrained paradigms that explicitly incorporate non-uniform ISM structures, radially varying spiral pattern speeds, and the Galactic warp [Foster et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..438...16F/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the CGPS and subsequent [[radio-surveys|Radio surveys]] established foundational, high-resolution maps of the multiphase interstellar medium, several major theoretical and observational tensions remain unresolved. 

**The Missing SNR Population**
A significant discrepancy persists between the theoretical number of Galactic supernova remnants expected from stellar evolution models and the observed population. The CGPS demonstrated that at least part of this "missing SNR population" is simply hidden by background confusion and low surface brightness. The survey discovered extreme low-brightness remnants such as G152.4-2.1 and G190.9-2.2, which have surface brightness limits of $\Sigma_{\text{1 GHz}} \lesssim 5 \times 10^{-23} \text{ W m}^{-2} \text{ Hz}^{-1} \text{ sr}^{-1}$ [Foster et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...549A.107F/abstract). Finding and categorizing the rest of this missing population is a primary driver for the next generation of highly sensitive centimeter-wave surveys in the 2020s [Anderson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240916607A/abstract), [Anderson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.247A/abstract). High-energy monitoring also supports this effort; the Swift Deep Galactic Plane Survey (DGPS) provides an X-ray source catalog essential for identifying high-energy counterparts to radio transients and remnants [O'Connor et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..269...49O/abstract), while the INTEGRAL Galactic Plane scanning conducts hard X-ray monitoring for transient source detection [Fiocchi et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014cosp...40E.871F/abstract). Moreover, ongoing efforts toward the second H.E.S.S. Galactic plane catalogue leverage open scientific tools to refine our understanding of gamma-ray emission from such remnants [Remy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230808969R/abstract).

**Turbulence Statistics and Magnetic Topology**
The CGPS yielded unprecedented maps of the magneto-ionic medium, yet extracting accurate turbulence statistics from these data has proven challenging. Early theoretical work hypothesized that the skewness and kurtosis of the spatial polarization gradient correlated directly with the turbulent Mach number of the ISM. However, comprehensive analysis of the full CGPS dataset revealed that these statistics are highly sensitive to the angular resolution and the size of the calculation window. Furthermore, they are often dominated by the sharp boundaries of bright, localized emission regions rather than by intrinsic, widespread turbulence [Herron et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835..210H/abstract). Determining robust structure functions to isolate line-of-sight fluctuation spectra remains an open problem [Sun et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004mim..proc...25S/abstract). Additionally, it remains debated whether the large-scale magnetic field in the extreme outer Galaxy maintains a spiral topology or becomes purely azimuthal, as rotation measure samples in the outer disk still lack the spatial density to break modeling degeneracies [Brown et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...592L..29B/abstract), [Van Eck et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...728...97V/abstract), [Ordog et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Galax...7...43O/abstract).

**Molecular Gas Mass Estimations**
Another lingering tension involves the conversion of $^{12}$CO integrated intensity ($I_{\text{CO}}$) to molecular hydrogen column density. While the CGPS and similar surveys historically assumed a linear conversion factor (the X-factor), subsequent multi-isotopologue studies suggest a non-linear relationship scaling as $N_{\text{CO}} \propto I_{\text{CO}}^{1.38}$ [Barnes et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812....6B/abstract). If validated globally, this implies that the molecular mass in the Galactic disk has been substantially underestimated, requiring a recalibration of large-scale star formation laws.

**Stellar Populations and Galactic Demographics**
Beyond the ISM, mapping the Galactic plane presents open questions regarding the stellar mass function. For instance, the potential role of a large brown dwarf population remains a topic of debate in Galactic mass function studies [Peale et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...524L..67P/abstract). Future high-cadence plane surveys, such as those planned for the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]], hold immense potential for mapping the population of exoplanets and black holes across the disk [Street et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181203137S/abstract). Historically, specialized missions like the proposed Galactic Exoplanet Survey Telescope (GEST) were designed to detect Earth-mass planets via gravitational microlensing in the Galactic bulge [Bennett et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003SPIE.4854..141B/abstract).

**Future Directions and Facility Upgrades**
To address these outstanding questions, a major upgrade has been proposed for the [[dominion-radio-astrophysical-observatory|Dominion Radio Astrophysical Observatory]] Synthesis Telescope. Implementing broadband single-pixel feeds operating continuously between 400 and 1800 MHz will transform the array's capabilities for the 2020s and beyond [Hill et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019clrp.2020...44H/abstract). This wide frequency coverage is optimized for broadband polarimetry, providing the Faraday depth resolution necessary to cleanly separate multi-phase magnetic structures along complex sightlines. Furthermore, the upgraded bandwidth will enable wide-area, arcminute-resolution mapping of multiple radio recombination lines. Because these faint lines trace extended, low-density ionized gas that cannot be efficiently captured by other means, their measurement is critical for completing the census of the ISM phase balance and massive stellar feedback [Hill et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019clrp.2020...44H/abstract). Addressing these tensions drives ongoing research and motivates [[future-astronomical-facilities-and-surveys|Future Astronomical Facilities and Surveys]] and facility upgrades at DRAO and other major observatories.

At submillimeter wavelengths, proposed initiatives include massive imaging and spectroscopic surveys with the Chajnantor Sub/Millimeter Survey Telescope [Golwala et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018alas.confE..46G/abstract), as well as ultra-fast continuum surveys utilizing the next-generation 850 $\mu$m MKID camera at the EAO/JCMT 15-m telescope [Liu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190708702L/abstract). Future space-based observatories will also transform our view of the Galactic disk. The proposed CASTOR mission, a flagship Canadian space telescope, promises high-resolution UV imaging to deeply probe Galactic star formation physics [Cote et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019clrp.2020...18C/abstract). In the near-infrared, the Roman Galactic Roman Infrared Plane Survey (GRIPS) presents a future flagship opportunity to map highly extincted lines of sight with unprecedented clarity [Paladini et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230707642P/abstract). Finally, a white paper for the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) advocates for a dedicated SKA Galactic Centre Survey to tackle extreme ISM physics and star formation in the heart of the Milky Way [Schoedel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240604022S/abstract).

Finally, realizing the full scientific potential of these upgrades will require continued synergy with other wavebands. For instance, the [[bolocam-galactic-plane-survey|Bolocam Galactic Plane Survey]] (BGPS) has been a key millimeter continuum partner used to characterize dense molecular gas clumps and their associated star formation activity across the disk [Dunham et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741..110D/abstract). Looking forward, modern wideband radio projects such as the GLOSTAR survey continue the CGPS legacy by mapping the Galactic plane's multi-phase ISM at 4-8 GHz with arcsecond resolution, charting the next frontier of massive star formation and Galactic structure studies [Brunthaler et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...651A..85B/abstract).
