The **One-hundred-deg$^2$ DECam Imaging in Narrowbands (ODIN)** survey is a [[panoramic-imaging|Panoramic imaging]], [[deep-imaging-surveys|deep narrowband imaging]] campaign designed to map the large-scale structure of the Universe during "Cosmic Noon" [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962...36L/abstract). Utilizing the Dark Energy Camera ([[decam|DECam]]) mounted on the 4-meter [[blanco-telescope|Blanco Telescope]] at the [[cerro-tololo-inter-american-observatory|Cerro Tololo Inter-American Observatory]], ODIN specifically targets [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|Lyman-alpha ($\text{Ly}\alpha$) emitting galaxies]] (LAEs) at redshifts $z = 2.4, 3.1, \text{ and } 4.5$. By employing custom-built narrowband filters over a contiguous 100-square-degree footprint, the survey achieves highly complete selection functions down to a limiting magnitude of $\text{AB} \approx 25.7$. This marks a significant evolution in observational cosmology; while early searches for primeval $\text{Ly}\alpha$ galaxies were restricted to small-volume, [[pencil-beam-surveys|pencil-beam fields]] [Thompson et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995AJ....110..963T/abstract), and later advanced by foundational targeted searches like the [[large-area-lyman-alpha-survey|Large Area Lyman Alpha survey]] [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..240...97M/abstract), ODIN leverages modern wide-field mosaic cameras to comprehensively map the cosmic web on degree scales. 

A central objective of the ODIN survey is to understand the physical properties and [[mass-assembly-survey|stellar mass assembly]] of LAEs, which act as luminous tracers of the underlying dark matter distribution. To isolate these sources and reject lower-redshift interlopers, the survey utilizes an innovative hybrid-weighted double-broadband continuum estimation technique. Measurements from the survey challenge traditional textbook models, which have historically characterized LAEs as strictly primordial galaxies undergoing their first major burst of star formation. Instead, non-parametric spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting of ODIN samples reveals that while $67\%$ of these galaxies are experiencing their first major starburst, approximately $33\%$ harbor significant records of prior star formation [Firestone et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986L...8F/abstract). Furthermore, clustering analyses demonstrate that LAEs reside in dark matter halos of $\sim 10^{11} M_\odot$ but possess a remarkably low halo occupation fraction, indicating they are unusually luminous for their dark matter masses. 

On macroscopic scales, ODIN utilizes LAEs to pinpoint the formation sites of the most massive structures in the Universe. The survey has mapped hundreds of protoclusters—immature, highly biased overdensities expected to collapse into massive virialized galaxy clusters ($\sim 10^{14.5} M_\odot$) by the present day. Alongside compact LAEs, ODIN places special emphasis on detecting $\text{Ly}\alpha$ blobs (LABs), a rare class of spatially extended $\text{Ly}\alpha$ nebulae. By tracing these expansive halos of glowing gas, the survey has firmly established that LABs preferentially reside within massive proto-group environments and naturally illuminate the converging nodes of cosmic web filaments.

While ODIN is ultimately expected to catalog over 100,000 LAE candidates, early survey data—which have identified approximately 15,000 candidates in the extended COSMOS field—already highlight several open questions regarding the role of the large-scale environment in galaxy evolution. Key unresolved issues include a discrepancy in the faint-end slope of the $\text{Ly}\alpha$ luminosity function between protocluster and field environments, the exact onset of environmental quenching, and a complex redshift evolution in the relationship between $\text{Ly}\alpha$ line profiles and local environmental density. Addressing these tensions will require extensive synergies with the next generation of [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]], pairing ODIN’s wide-field narrowband maps with deep contextual multi-wavelength and broadband imaging from future and ongoing missions like the Rubin Observatory, Euclid, and specific parallel JWST programs.

## Overview and Survey Design

Building on the legacy of previous [[deep-field-campaigns|Deep field campaigns]], the ODIN survey is designed to map the large-scale structure of the Universe over an unprecedented 100 square degrees across seven well-studied extragalactic fields [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962...36L/abstract). The survey uses LAEs as luminous tracers of the underlying dark matter distribution to pinpoint protoclusters and filaments of the cosmic web. Reaching a deep limiting magnitude of $\text{AB} \approx 25.7$, the survey is expected to ultimately identify over 100,000 LAE candidates, making it one of the most extensive samples of [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|high-redshift star-forming galaxies]] to date [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962...36L/abstract), [Firestone et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..217F/abstract). The [[decam|DECam]] facility utilized by ODIN has a long-proven utility for massive cosmological campaigns, as highlighted by the [[dark-energy-survey|Dark Energy Survey]]'s 5-year data release—the largest single-instrument sample of supernovae used for cosmological constraints [Sánchez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975....5S/abstract). 

To accurately identify LAEs and measure their rest-frame Ly$\alpha$ equivalent widths (EW), the ODIN collaboration utilizes existing deep broadband imaging from facilities such as the [[subaru-telescope|Subaru Telescope]] (via [[hyper-suprime-cam-surveys|Hyper Suprime-Cam Surveys]]) alongside its custom DECam narrowband data. Furthermore, the MUSUBI (MegaCam Ultra-deep Survey: $u^*$-band Imaging) program provides the critical $u$-band imaging in the COSMOS and SXDS fields necessary for deriving high-quality photometric redshifts at $z < 3$ [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..260...54W/abstract). Morphological context for ODIN targets in the COSMOS field is additionally enhanced by the 3D-DASH survey, which provides the widest near-infrared imaging mosaic available from the [[hubble-space-telescope-deep-surveys|Hubble Space Telescope]] [Mowla et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933..129M/abstract). By covering a contiguous area of up to 91 square degrees with highly complete selection functions, ODIN overcomes the cosmic variance limitations that typically constrain narrower surveys. It is uniquely positioned to identify rare, spatially extended LABs and reliably map out hundreds of massive protoclusters [Ramakrishnan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951..119R/abstract), [Ramakrishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982...74R/abstract). 

The survey's multi-epoch design inherently enables the study of redshift evolution in the clustering properties, luminosity functions, and physical traits of high-redshift galaxies over a crucial 1.4 Gyr window of cosmic history [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962...36L/abstract). While ODIN relies on wide-field contiguous coverage to effectively capture these massive large-scale structures, complementary observational strategies remain necessary for probing the universe at its most extreme distances. For instance, finding galaxies at $z > 15$ is generally achieved more time-efficiently by utilizing multiple massive lensing clusters rather than relying on purely blank-field strategies [Vujeva et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974...23V/abstract).

## Historical Development of High-Redshift Tracers

The quest to identify star-forming galaxies at high redshift initially relied on deep, small-area observations. While continuum-selection techniques successfully identified high-redshift Lyman break galaxies (LBGs), both LBGs and narrowband-selected LAEs are strongly biased toward systems with relatively high star-formation rates. Capturing the full diversity of the early universe—including older, evolved galaxies that are dust-obscured or missed by rest-frame UV and Ly$\alpha$ selections—instead requires rest-frame optical and near-infrared selections, such as Distant Red Galaxies (DRGs). Notably, for the most massive galaxy populations (stellar masses $\gtrsim 10^{11} M_\odot$) at $2 < z < 3$, these DRGs actually dominate over UV-selected LBGs in both overall number density and stellar mass density.

Nevertheless, targeting redshifted Ly$\alpha$ emission through narrowband imaging became a fundamental technique for uncovering large populations of young, line-luminous galaxies. Early searches for primeval Ly$\alpha$-emitting galaxies using Fabry-Perot interferometers over very small fields (e.g., $\sim 5$ arcmin$^2$) yielded null results or very few candidates, heavily constrained by the limited comoving volumes probed ($\sim 1.1 \times 10^5$ Mpc$^3$) [Thompson et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995AJ....110..963T/abstract).

The historical development of high-redshift tracers fundamentally shifted with the introduction of larger-format CCDs. A pioneering effort was the [[large-area-lyman-alpha-survey|Large Area Lyman Alpha survey]] (LALA), which utilized a $36^{\prime} \times 36^{\prime}$ camera on the KPNO 4-meter telescope to search for LAEs at $z=4.5$ [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..240...97M/abstract). LALA demonstrated that the volume density of LAEs at this epoch was comparable to that of Lyman break galaxies. Shortly thereafter, the Subaru Deep Field project leveraged the 8.2-meter [[subaru-telescope|Subaru Telescope]] to image a $34^{\prime} \times 27^{\prime}$ blank field [Kashikawa et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASJ...56.1011K/abstract). By utilizing narrowbands to $3\sigma$ depths of AB $\sim 26.6$, the survey compiled large, robust samples of LAEs at $z \simeq 5.7$ and $6.6$, solidifying narrowband selection as a premier tool for [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|high-redshift galaxy discovery]].

Despite these successes, early narrowband surveys were largely restricted to areas of less than one square degree, making them susceptible to cosmic variance and limiting their ability to probe the largest scales of the cosmic web. ODIN follows a lineage of wide-field surveys on the [[blanco-telescope|Blanco 4-meter telescope]], including the Blanco Cosmology Survey (BCS), which mapped galaxy clusters at lower redshifts ($z < 1$) using the earlier Mosaic2 camera [Desai et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757...83D/abstract). The subsequent survey evolution was characterized by the deployment of massive wide-field mosaic cameras, such as the 570-megapixel [[decam|DECam]], which features a 3 deg$^2$ field of view [Diehl et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SPIE.9149E..0VD/abstract). Programs like the ODIN survey represent the culmination of this development, expanding the contiguous search area to an unprecedented 100 deg$^2$ [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962...36L/abstract). This massive scale allows modern surveys to move beyond simply cataloging high-redshift tracers, enabling detailed environmental analyses of cosmic filaments and their intersection nodes.

## Narrowband Methodology and Selection Techniques

The ODIN survey utilizes three custom-built narrowband filters mounted on the [[decam|DECam]] instrument to identify its targets. These filters—designated N419, N501, and N673—have central wavelengths of $419$, $501$, and $673$ nm, with full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) bandpasses of $7.5$, $7.6$, and $10.0$ nm, respectively [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962...36L/abstract). These specifications are explicitly tuned to isolate redshifted Ly$\alpha$ emission at $z = 2.4$, $3.1$, and $4.5$, corresponding to cosmic times of approximately 2.8, 2.1, and 1.4 Gyr. The ODIN survey strategy shares technical synergies with the Merian survey, which similarly utilizes custom N708 and N540 filters on the DECam instrument to identify dwarf galaxies at low redshift ($z \sim 0.06-0.10$) alongside [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|high-redshift LAEs]] at $z=3.3$ and $4.8$ [Danieli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..110D/abstract).

A central challenge in narrowband surveys is the precise subtraction of the underlying stellar continuum to accurately isolate line emission. ODIN implements an innovative hybrid-weighted double-broadband continuum estimation technique [Firestone et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..217F/abstract). Rather than relying on a single broadband filter, this approach synthesizes data from multiple adjacent broadband filters to establish a more accurate baseline continuum. This method significantly improves the precision of the derived Ly$\alpha$ equivalent width (EW) and minimizes photometric scatter. 

Narrowband LAE selection is historically susceptible to contamination from lower-redshift galaxies whose bright emission lines (such as [O II] $\lambda 3727$ or [O III] $\lambda 5007$) fall into the same filter bandpasses. ODIN mitigates this through a combination of narrowband excess criteria and broadband color cuts. Applying this selection methodology, studies have found that [O II] emitters (at $z \sim 0.34$) constitute only a minimal contaminant. However, highly star-forming, "Green Pea-like" [O III] emitters represent a more significant interloping population, particularly within the $z = 4.5$ (N673) sample [Firestone et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..217F/abstract). By isolating these sources via color-color diagrams, the survey actively filters out contaminants while simultaneously cataloging them for independent study.

The accuracy of the hybrid-weighted continuum estimation enables robust statistical analyses of LAE physical properties. Initial application of these selection techniques in the extended COSMOS field successfully identified over 15,000 LAE candidates across the three epochs [Firestone et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..217F/abstract). The rest-frame Ly$\alpha$ equivalent width distributions for the ODIN samples are well-described by an exponential function with scale lengths empirically measured to be $w_0 = 53 \pm 1$ Å at $z=2.4$, $65 \pm 1$ Å at $z=3.1$, and $59 \pm 1$ Å at $z=4.5$ [Firestone et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..217F/abstract). 

## Internal Physics: Star Formation Histories and Assembly

A central objective of the ODIN survey is characterizing the internal physics and [[mass-assembly-survey|stellar mass assembly]] of LAEs during Cosmic Noon. Traditionally, LAEs have been theorized to represent distinctly young systems undergoing their first major burst of star formation. However, recent analyses combining ODIN's narrowband LAE selection with deep, multi-wavelength broadband photometry from programs like the [[candels-survey|CANDELS survey]] have revealed a more complex picture of galaxy assembly [Firestone et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986L...8F/abstract).

To constrain the star formation histories (SFHs) of these sources, researchers employ non-parametric SED fitting techniques—specifically, Gaussian-process-based methods applied to rest-frame ultraviolet through near-infrared photometry. This approach is bolstered by foundational legacy campaigns like the Spitzer Extended Deep Survey (SEDS), which provides uniform depth across the ODIN fields and contributes essential constraints to the infrared background and SED fitting baselines [Ashby et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...769...80A/abstract). By utilizing these rich datasets, non-parametric modeling avoids the strong priors imposed by exponentially declining or constant star formation models, allowing for the unbiased detection of multiple, distinct burst episodes over a galaxy's lifetime.

Results from this non-parametric SED fitting demonstrate a tension with the classical "first-burst" archetype often cited in textbooks. While a strong majority—approximately $67\%$—of ODIN-selected LAEs do exhibit SFHs consistent with a first major star formation event, the remaining $33\%$ possess significant records of prior star formation [Firestone et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986L...8F/abstract). Specifically, $28\%$ of the LAE population shows evidence of earlier star formation episodes but are currently undergoing a "dominant" burst, defined as the highest star formation rate (SFR) the galaxy has yet experienced. An additional $5\%$ are undergoing "non-dominant" bursts, having already passed their peak historical SFR. 

By grouping the first-burst and dominant-burst populations, ODIN data establishes that $\sim 95\%$ of LAEs are currently experiencing a "formative starburst"—the most intense period of stellar mass assembly in their history [Firestone et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986L...8F/abstract). Consequently, while the assumption that LAEs are purely primordial, first-burst galaxies is an oversimplification, they do systematically represent systems undergoing their most significant phase of mass accumulation. The distinct nature of the LAE phase becomes apparent when compared to other high-redshift star-forming populations; ODIN SFH reconstructions show that the fraction of total stellar mass formed within the preceding $200 \text{ Myr}$ is a factor of $\sim 1.3$ higher in LAEs than in mass-matched Lyman break galaxy control samples.

## Clustering and the Galaxy-Halo Connection

Beyond their individual star formation histories, LAEs serve as powerful tracers of the underlying dark matter distribution. By measuring the angular correlation functions of over 14,000 LAEs identified across multiple epochs, the ODIN survey places tight constraints on the dark matter halos hosting [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|high-redshift star-forming galaxies]] [Herrera et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988L..57H/abstract). 

The clustering strength of LAEs is observed to evolve strongly with redshift. After accounting for redshift-space distortions, contamination rates, and the integral constraint, spatial clustering analyses yield linear galaxy bias factors that increase from $b = 1.72^{+0.26}_{-0.27}$ at $z=2.4$, to $b = 2.01^{+0.26}_{-0.29}$ at $z=3.1$, and up to $b = 2.95^{+0.40}_{-0.46}$ at $z=4.5$ [Herrera et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988L..57H/abstract). Complementing these contiguous wide-field observations, other independent techniques have successfully measured early environments; for instance, the [[borg-survey|BoRG survey]] utilized the cosmic variance seen across random [[hubble-space-telescope-deep-surveys|Hubble Space Telescope]] pointings to independently estimate the galaxy bias for $z \approx 2$ populations [Cameron et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.1922C/abstract). For the ODIN survey, the measured galaxy bias values correspond to median dark matter halo masses ranging from $\log(M_h/M_\odot) = 11.44^{+0.30}_{-0.28}$ at $z=2.4$ down to $10.85^{+0.24}_{-0.24}$ at $z=4.5$. 

Furthermore, ODIN data reveal that LAEs have a remarkably low halo occupation fraction, residing in just $3\%$ to $7\%$ of matched-clustering dark matter halos. This low occupation fraction, combined with the galaxies' strong Ly$\alpha$ emission and recent formative starbursts, suggests that LAEs are unusually luminous for their dark matter masses. This reflects a highly biased, star-formation-intensive phase that distinguishes them from broader continuum-selected populations [Herrera et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988L..57H/abstract).

## Lyα Blobs and Extended Nebulae

In addition to compact LAEs, ODIN has constrained the properties of much rarer, spatially extended Ly$\alpha$ nebulae known as Ly$\alpha$ blobs (LABs) [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962...36L/abstract). Because these objects are both uncommon and highly extended, previous small-area studies struggled to contextualize them within the broader cosmic web. ODIN's contiguous wide-field coverage has firmly established that LABs are strongly associated with the filaments of the cosmic web. An analysis of early ODIN data demonstrated that approximately $70\%$ of LABs at $z=3.1$ reside within a projected distance of $2.4$ pMpc from a cosmic filament [Ramakrishnan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951..119R/abstract). By illuminating the circumgalactic and intergalactic gas, LABs serve as visible signposts of merging filamentary flows. 

Clustering analyses of ODIN LABs provide quantitative constraints on their host dark matter halos. Auto-correlation measurements of over 100 LABs at $z=2.4$ and $z=3.1$ yield exceptionally high galaxy bias factors of $b = 4.0 \pm 0.8$ and $b = 3.8 \pm 0.7$, respectively [Moon et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260101991M/abstract). Consequently, LABs trace substantially more massive dark matter halos than typical LAEs, with inferred median halo masses of $4.2^{+3.8}_{-2.5} \times 10^{12} M_\odot$ at $z=2.4$ and $1.1^{+1.1}_{-0.7} \times 10^{12} M_\odot$ at $z=3.1$. 

The occupation fraction of LABs in these massive halos is low, estimated at roughly $3\%$--$11\%$ for dark matter halos above the minimum mass thresholds necessary to host them [Moon et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260101991M/abstract). This reinforces the conclusion that the conditions required to produce luminous, extended emission are transient. Ultimately, the high clustering strength confirms that LABs reliably trace dense proto-group environments expected to evolve into massive elliptical galaxies or group-sized halos ($\sim 10^{13} M_\odot$) by the present day [Moon et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260101991M/abstract).

## Protoclusters and the Cosmic Web

On the largest macroscopic scales, ODIN utilizes LAEs to pinpoint the formation sites of the most massive structures in the Universe. The survey has successfully identified 150 protocluster candidates at $z=2.4$ and $z=3.1$ across 13.9 square degrees of sky [Ramakrishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982...74R/abstract). Cross-correlation clustering measurements between protocluster candidates and the underlying LAE density field reveal that these protoclusters are highly biased tracers. Protoclusters exhibit linear bias factors of $b = 6.6^{+1.3}_{-1.1}$ at $z=2.4$ and $b = 6.1^{+1.3}_{-1.1}$ at $z=3.1$. These biases correspond to mean dark matter halo masses of $\log(M_h/M_\odot) = 13.53^{+0.21}_{-0.24}$ and $12.96^{+0.28}_{-0.33}$, respectively. By $z=0$, these structures are expected to evolve into massive, virialized galaxy clusters with typical descendant masses of $\sim 10^{14.5} M_\odot$ [Ramakrishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982...74R/abstract).

The influence of this large-scale environment on galaxy evolution remains an active area of investigation. ODIN observations indicate that the Ly$\alpha$ luminosity function is only mildly affected by the protocluster environment, with weak evidence suggesting a suppression of faint LAEs compared to the field [Nagaraj et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...995..126N/abstract). However, theoretical expectations from IllustrisTNG simulations predict a more pronounced environmental effect on the UV luminosity function, which exhibits a shallower faint-end slope and an excess at the bright end in protoclusters [Andrews et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...698A.280A/abstract). While simulated protocluster galaxies follow the same star formation rate-stellar mass ($\text{SFR}-M_*$) scaling relation as field galaxies, an enhanced major merger rate drives a $\sim 60\%$ increase in star formation. Despite this burst, quenching in protoclusters is predicted to begin significantly earlier ($z \sim 0.8-1.6$) than in the field ($z \sim 0.5-0.9$).

Further ambiguity exists regarding the relationship between Ly$\alpha$ emission profiles and local density. At $z \sim 3.1$, LAEs within protoclusters show a $\sim 15\%$ higher Ly$\alpha$ line luminosity, suggesting increased star formation; however, this luminosity enhancement is absent at $z \sim 2.4$, indicating potential redshift evolution [Uzsoy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251117498U/abstract). Despite variations in overall luminosity, the shape of the Ly$\alpha$ line profile is completely independent of large-scale environmental density, suggesting that line shape is dictated by internal or local physics rather than the cosmic web.

## Open Questions and Future Synergies

While the ODIN survey has successfully mapped large-scale structure at Cosmic Noon, the exact role of the environment in [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|high-redshift galaxy]] evolution remains an active area of debate. A primary open question is the onset and mechanism of environmental quenching. As previously noted, theoretical simulations predict earlier quenching inside protoclusters [Andrews et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...698A.280A/abstract), yet the relationship between local environmental density and Ly$\alpha$ emission properties continues to exhibit a complex redshift evolution that requires further study to fully detangle [Uzsoy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251117498U/abstract). Determining the precise cause of the luminosity function faint-end slope discrepancies and the redshift evolution of line luminosities remains a key objective for future analyses. To answer related open questions regarding the ionizing emissivity of high-redshift galaxies, complementary approaches such as the Parallel Ionizing Emissivity (PIE) survey use independent fields to measure average Lyman Continuum escape fractions [Beckett et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320878B/abstract).

Resolving these ambiguities will rely heavily on synergies between ODIN and the next generation of [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]]. Future deep optical imaging from the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) over the southern sky will provide unprecedented contextual broadband photometry. When combined with ODIN data, this is expected to yield a highly pure, interloper-free selection of over 100,000 LAEs [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962...36L/abstract). Joint analysis with the Rubin Observatory must account for the LSST Uniform Rolling cadence, which ensures depth uniformity essential for static-science tasks like the galaxy clustering studies central to ODIN [Leistedt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251216478L/abstract). Furthermore, the specific depth of the LSST u-band will be critical for high-redshift LBG detection and photometric redshift accuracy, with survey strategy variations potentially impacting dropout density by up to $500\%$ [Crenshaw et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJS..281...54C/abstract). These expansive optical programs are powerfully augmented by infrared coverage, such as the "DeepDrill" Spitzer survey, which provides comprehensive 3.6 and 4.5 $\mu$m imaging of the LSST Deep Drilling Fields to enable robust stellar mass measurements out to $z \approx 5$ [Lacy et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501..892L/abstract).

When interpreting future large-area observations, surveys like SPHEREx must also contend with the challenges of galaxy-galaxy blending, which can affect luminosity function measurements by up to $50\%$ in deep fields [Dachan et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260121627D/abstract). Beyond optical and near-infrared mapping, the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope survey designs are being optimized for cross-correlation science with CMB experiments like the Simons Observatory and CMB-S4 to significantly increase the dark energy figure of merit [Eifler et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241104088E/abstract).

Synergies with Euclid's Cosmic DAWN survey will provide consistently processed multi-wavelength catalogs and stellar mass measurements necessary to contextualize ODIN's Ly$\alpha$ emitters up to $z \sim 10$ [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A.259E/abstract). Additionally, the identification of massive protoclusters at $z > 2$ provides essential high-redshift counterparts to the X-ray-selected cluster catalogs generated by the [[erosita|eROSITA]] all-sky survey [Predehl et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...647A...1P/abstract).

Future spectroscopic follow-up must address systematic effects such as fiber assignment incompleteness and intrinsic alignment, which have been shown in [[desi-survey|DESI]] mock catalogs to impact lensing estimators [Lange et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..57L/abstract). Meanwhile, advanced wide-field space observations are actively complementing ODIN. The PANORAMIC survey's pure-parallel NIRCam imaging on JWST adds a deep 'wedding cake' tier of observations that complements ODIN's wide-field narrowband maps for studying galaxy evolution across cosmic time [Williams et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...979..140W/abstract). Similarly, the MegaScience survey utilizes JWST NIRCam medium-band imaging to reveal clumpy line emission and complex assembly in galaxies at $z > 6$ [Suess et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976..101S/abstract). Furthermore, in the [[extended-groth-strip|Extended Groth Strip]] field, ODIN observations are enhanced by the MEGA survey, which provides JWST MIRI mid-infrared imaging to trace obscured star formation and AGN activity [Backhaus et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024SCPMA..6719511Z/abstract). Ultimately, combining ODIN's wide-field narrowband maps with LSST, Euclid, eROSITA, dedicated JWST imaging, and vast local surveys like FASHI will reveal how the cosmic web drives galaxy assembly and environmental quenching at Cosmic Noon and beyond.
