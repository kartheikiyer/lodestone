## Article

**Spectroscopy surveys** are systematic observational programs designed to acquire the electromagnetic spectra of large populations of astronomical objects, such as stars, galaxies, and quasars. Unlike purely [[observational-photometry|photometric]] [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]], which capture broad-band spectral energy distributions, spectroscopy surveys measure the specific fluxes of localized emission and absorption features alongside the underlying continuum. By utilizing advanced [[ground-based-spectroscopic-instruments|multi-object spectrographs (MOS)]], space-based [[slitless-spectroscopy|Slitless spectroscopy]], and [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]], these programs enable precise measurements of a source's [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]], internal kinematics, and [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]]. Historically rooted in the painstaking single-object observations of early [[astrophysical-spectroscopy|Astrophysical spectroscopy]], the field was revolutionized by pioneering programs like the [[cfa-redshift-survey|CfA Redshift Survey]], which systematically mapped thousands of galaxies to unveil the three-dimensional "cosmic web" [Huchra et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ASPC....5...41H/abstract). Subsequent highly multiplexed campaigns, including the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] and the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS]], vastly expanded these efforts, transitioning [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] into an era of massive statistical samples and precision cosmology [Strauss et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996astro.ph.10033S/abstract).

Today, massive [[three-dimensional-galaxy-surveys|Three-dimensional galaxy surveys]] leverage these datasets to probe the large-scale structure of the universe, utilizing baryon acoustic oscillations and redshift-space distortions to test General Relativity and constrain the nature of dark energy. Beyond bulk cosmological metrics, spectroscopy surveys are fundamental to unraveling galaxy assembly and stellar population histories. Deep extragalactic programs trace the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and star formation rates back to the epoch of reionization [D'Eugenio et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240406531D/abstract), while in the local universe, spatially resolved data from [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] surveys map the internal dynamics of individual galaxies to investigate localized evolutionary mechanisms, such as "inside-out" quenching. Within the Milky Way and the Local Group, dedicated ground-based programs like the [[lamost-spectroscopic-survey|LAMOST Spectroscopic Survey]] chemically tag millions of stars to reconstruct the Galaxy's dynamical and assembly history. 

The design and interpretation of these massive observational programs require rigorous statistical frameworks to define the target [[selection-function|Selection function]] and mitigate [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]], such as Malmquist bias, which can otherwise severely distort intrinsic population metrics. As survey yields enter the "Big Data" regime, modern pipelines increasingly rely on [[automated-spectral-feature-fitting|Automated spectral feature fitting]] and transformer-based machine learning models to extract robust physical parameters. Furthermore, cross-correlating spectroscopic catalogs with [[panchromatic-surveys|panchromatic imaging]] and [[line-intensity-mapping-lim|Line Intensity Mapping (LIM)]] offers powerful new synergies for breaking parameter degeneracies. This article explores the historical evolution, multiplexing instrumentation, and primary scientific drivers of modern spectroscopy surveys. It also highlights ongoing open questions and tensions in the field, including stark discrepancies between hydrodynamical simulations and [[spatially-resolved-galaxy-analysis|Spatially Resolved Galaxy Analysis]] at intermediate redshifts, the algorithmic biases inherent to simulation-based cosmological inference, and the elusive sources of ionizing radiation in the early universe.

## Historical Evolution of Spectroscopic Redshift Surveys

The historical development of [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] and [[astrophysical-spectroscopy|Astrophysical spectroscopy]] as tools for mapping the universe has undergone a dramatic evolution over the past century. The first successful spectrum of a galaxy, M31, was obtained in 1898 [Rubin et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...451..419R/abstract), marking the dawn of extragalactic spectroscopy. However, for most of the 20th century, the acquisition of a [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]] remained a painstaking, single-object endeavor. Redshifts were primarily used to study the internal kinematics of individual galaxies or to confirm the distances of specific targets, rather than to map the large-scale distribution of matter in the universe. Early spectroscopy also played a crucial role in confirming gravitational lens candidates, such as the quintuple quasar image system Q1115+080 [Young et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981ApJ...244..723Y/abstract).

The paradigm shifted in the late 1970s and 1980s with the inception of systematic, wide-area [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] programs. The pioneering work of Marc Davis and John Huchra on the [[cfa-redshift-survey|CfA Redshift Survey]] represented a critical milestone in this historical development [Driver et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012IAUS..284..268D/abstract). By systematically measuring the redshifts of thousands of galaxies, the CfA survey provided the first true three-dimensional maps of the local universe. These observations revealed that the distribution of galaxies was highly structured, organizing into a "cosmic web" characterized by vast voids, elongated filaments, and spherical bubbles [Huchra et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ASPC....5...41H/abstract). This transitioned observational cosmology from analyzing two-dimensional projected sky maps to exploring the true [[three-dimensional-galaxy-surveys|3D spatial distribution]] of galaxies [da Costa et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph.12258D/abstract).

As telescope and detector technologies advanced, the scale of [[three-dimensional-galaxy-surveys|Three-dimensional galaxy surveys]] expanded exponentially. During this transitional era, the [[canada-france-redshift-survey|Canada-France Redshift Survey]] (CFRS) emerged as a major early program that successfully probed galaxies out to $z \sim 1.3$ [Crampton et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...455...96C/abstract). By the late 1990s and early 2000s, two monumental projects transformed the landscape by increasing sample sizes by orders of magnitude [Strauss et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996astro.ph.10033S/abstract). The [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]] (Two-degree Field Galaxy Redshift Survey) targeted the southern sky and measured $\sim 220,000$ galaxy redshifts over an area of $\sim 2000$ square degrees, reaching a limiting magnitude of $b_j \sim 19.45$ with a median redshift of $z \sim 0.1$. Simultaneously, the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (SDSS) mapped more than a quarter of the sky, compiling a spectroscopic catalog of $\sim 10^6$ galaxies down to a limiting magnitude of $r < 17.77$, along with $\sim 10^5$ quasars. Together, these programs provided unparalleled statistical power for characterizing the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and measuring large-scale structure.

The success of these massive optical surveys spurred further mapping efforts to achieve full-sky coverage and greater depth. In the southern hemisphere, the [[6df-galaxy-survey|6dF Galaxy Survey]] utilized the [[two-micron-all-sky-survey-2mass|Two Micron All-Sky Survey (2MASS)]] as a near-infrared base catalog to measure $\sim 150,000$ redshifts and $\sim 15,000$ peculiar velocities for galaxies at Galactic latitudes $|b| > 10^{\circ}$ [Jones et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005PASA...22..277J/abstract), [Wakamatsu et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASPC..289...97W/abstract). Expanding upon these near-infrared efforts, the completion of the 2MASS redshift survey provided a comprehensive census of baryonic mass concentrations out to a redshift of $z=0.04$ [Huchra et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IAUS..216..170H/abstract). Because near-infrared light is significantly less attenuated by foreground dust, this survey was particularly crucial for improving the mapping of large-scale structure at low galactic latitudes, effectively peering into the historical 'Zone of Avoidance' [Huchra et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..329..135H/abstract).

Ultimately, this historical progression from individual target follow-ups to dedicated, massive spectroscopic pipelines cemented redshift surveys as the fundamental tool for modern cosmology. The immense datasets generated by the CfA, 2dFGRS, and SDSS established the standard methods for quantifying galaxy clustering and redshift-space distortions, enabling precise constraints on cosmological parameters that continue to underpin the standard model of the universe [Strauss et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996astro.ph.10033S/abstract). As modern panoramic surveys dominate the field, reviews like the TONGS project note a broader historical transition, where traditional targeted galaxy surveys have increasingly evolved into specific projects designed to complement these massive all-sky mapping efforts [Christie et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PASP..136c3001C/abstract).

## Multiplexing Instrumentation: MOS and Slitless Spectroscopy

The advent of modern [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] programs has been fundamentally driven by advancements in multiplexing instrumentation. The "multiplex factor"—the number of individual spectra acquired simultaneously in a single exposure—has increased by orders of magnitude over recent decades, transforming [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] from an era of targeted, single-object observations to panoptic, data-intensive [[sky-mapping-and-surveys|Sky Mapping and Surveys]]. Two primary technological pathways have enabled this revolution: ground-based multi-object spectrographs (MOS) and space-based [[slitless-spectroscopy|Slitless spectroscopy]].

**Ground-Based Multi-Object Spectrographs**
Ground-based MOS systems typically employ robotic fiber positioners or micro-slit arrays at the telescope focal plane to channel light from discrete astronomical targets into bench-mounted spectrographs. Early massively multiplexed surveys, such as the [[6df-galaxy-survey|6dF Galaxy Survey]], measured ${\sim}150,000$ redshifts using a fiber-fed system [Jones et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005PASA...22..277J/abstract). Successive generations of [[ground-based-spectroscopic-instruments|Ground-Based Spectroscopic Instruments]] have pushed the multiplex factor substantially higher. For instance, the [[lamost-spectroscopic-survey|LAMOST Spectroscopic Survey]] has collected over $4.5 \times 10^6$ stellar spectra to map Galactic kinematics and chemistry [Liu et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015RAA....15.1089L/abstract). 

Currently, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument ([[desi-bright-galaxy-survey|DESI]]) represents the state of the art, utilizing a highly multiplexed instrument to rapidly map the three-dimensional distribution of matter; its Early Data Release alone provided high-quality spectra for nearly $2 \times 10^6$ objects [DESI Collaboration et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230606308D/abstract). Next-generation facilities aim to expand these capabilities even further. The proposed [[wst|WST]] (Wide-field Spectroscopic Telescope) is designed to operate a MOS with a multiplex factor of 20,000 over a $3$ square degree field of view, projecting a yield of $250$ million galaxy spectra over five years of operation [Bacon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240512518B/abstract).

**Space-Based Slitless Spectroscopy**
While fiber-fed MOS systems require pre-existing photometric catalogs for [[target-selection|Target selection]], space-based observatories increasingly employ wide-field [[slitless-spectroscopy|Slitless spectroscopy]] using grisms (grating prisms). By removing the entrance slit, grism instruments disperse the light of all sources within the field of view, enabling "blind" spectroscopic searches without photometric pre-selection biases. This technique is particularly sensitive to high-redshift, low-mass emission-line galaxies that might be otherwise missed by broadband [[photometry-and-redshift-estimation|Photometry and Redshift Estimation]].

Observations from the Hubble Space Telescope ([[hst-observations|HST]]) established the utility of grism spectroscopy for constraining the luminosity functions of $\text{H}\alpha$ and $[\text{O III}]~\lambda5007$ emitting galaxies in the range $1.16 < z < 1.90$ [Nagaraj et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...943....5N/abstract). The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has vastly extended this capability. Programs such as the Parallel Application of Slitless Spectroscopy to Analyze Galaxy Evolution (PASSAGE) utilize the NIRISS instrument to obtain continuous $1.0 - 2.3~\mu\text{m}$ spectra. PASSAGE has yielded over $10,000$ near-infrared grism spectrograms, measuring rest-frame optical lines at high spatial resolution while using orthogonal grism orientations to overcome the historical limitation of overlapping dispersed spectra [Malkan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250900596M/abstract). Because it captures the entire field, careful reduction of wide-field slitless spectroscopy (WFSS) data is required to mitigate spatially dependent background artifacts, such as dispersed zodiacal light [Hviding et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..220H/abstract).

Looking forward, upcoming missions like the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope and Euclid will rely heavily on grism spectroscopy. These facilities will measure redshifts for millions of galaxies to constrain cosmological parameters, although the exact yield predictions for intermediate-luminosity $[\text{O III}]$ galaxies remain a subject of active refinement in the literature [Nagaraj et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...943....5N/abstract). Together, massive ground-based MOS and space-based slitless techniques ensure that modern [[astrophysical-spectroscopy|Astrophysical spectroscopy]] can meet the immense statistical requirements of precision cosmology and galaxy assembly studies.

## Integral Field Spectroscopic (IFS) Surveys

For decades, massive spectroscopic surveys relied on single-fiber or slit observations that captured the integrated light of a target, effectively treating galaxies as point sources. While this traditional approach efficiently yields bulk properties and redshifts, it obscures internal dynamics and spatial variations. In recent years, the field has undergone a paradigm shift toward spatially resolved observations using [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] (IFS). By deploying IFU (Integral Field Unit) arrays, modern surveys can map internal kinematics, star formation rates, and [[chemical-abundances|chemical abundance]] gradients across the entire face of individual galaxy sub-structures, bridging the gap between global scaling relations and kiloparsec-scale astrophysical processes [Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..991C/abstract), [Barrera-Ballesteros et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020BAAA...61C...1B/abstract).

At low redshift, the observational landscape has been transformed by large-scale multiplexed IFS programs such as the SAMI Galaxy Survey, MaNGA (Mapping Nearby Galaxies at APO), and CALIFA. The SAMI survey, for instance, has released extensive 2D maps for $3,068$ unique galaxies (including $888$ cluster members) across blue ($370-570$ nm, $R=1808$) and red ($630-740$ nm, $R=4304$) optical wavebands [Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..991C/abstract). Furthermore, high-resolution IFS has proven crucial for dissecting galactic nuclei, uncovering decoupled kinematic components and asymmetric structures that shed light on central supermassive black hole assembly [de Zeeuw et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASPC..291..205D/abstract). Complementary 3D spectrophotometric observations utilizing Fabry-Perot interferometers have mapped the energetics and shock-driven kinematics of galactic winds, detailing the complex interplay between starbursts and AGN feedback [Veilleux et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..195..277V/abstract).

Building upon local benchmarks, observational efforts are now pushing spatially resolved spectroscopy to earlier cosmic epochs. Advancements in adaptive optics and instruments like the [[muse-instrument|MUSE (instrument)]] on the Very Large Telescope (VLT) have enabled surveys such as [[magpi-survey|MAGPI]] (Middle Ages Galaxy Properties with Integral Field Spectroscopy). Targeting massive central galaxies ($>7 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$) at intermediate redshifts of $0.25 < z < 0.35$, MAGPI traces the dynamical and morphological transformation of galaxies over a critical 3-4 Gyr lookback time [Foster et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASA...38...31F/abstract).

## Cosmological Constraints and Large-Scale Structure

Spectroscopic redshift surveys are the premier observational tools for mapping large-scale structure and probing the nature of dark energy [Strauss et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996astro.ph.10033S/abstract). By determining precise three-dimensional positions for millions of galaxies, these surveys map the architecture of the cosmic web. Historical benchmarks such as the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|Two-Degree Field Galaxy Redshift Survey (2dFGRS)]] measured $\sim 220,000$ [[spectroscopic-redshift|redshifts]] [Colless et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph.11326C/abstract), while the [[6df-galaxy-survey|6dF Galaxy Survey]] cataloged $\sim 150,000$ redshifts and $15,000$ peculiar velocities in the southern sky [Jones et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005PASA...22..277J/abstract). Beyond mapping voids and filaments, dense spectroscopic campaigns such as the WINGS-SPE survey tripled the known member galaxies across 48 clusters, allowing researchers to robustly investigate scaling relations like the X-ray luminosity-velocity dispersion relation [Cava et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...495..707C/abstract). Furthermore, multi-object spectrographs like Magellan/M2FS have been instrumental in surveying the internal kinematics and stellar population parameters, such as age and metallicity, of cluster galaxies [Tucker et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AJ....154..113T/abstract). Additionally, surveys utilizing the Sunyaev-Zeldovich (SZ) effect are highly effective in selecting massive galaxy cluster samples out to high redshifts ($z>1$), providing critical data for precise measurements of cosmic expansion [Bartlett et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.11211B/abstract).

Modern [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]] operate on vastly larger scales; for example, the Early Data Release of the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument ([[desi-bright-galaxy-survey|DESI]]) alone provides spectra for nearly 2 million objects, including luminous red galaxies, emission-line galaxies, and quasars [DESI Collaboration et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230606308D/abstract). 

The primary statistics extracted from these surveys are the two-point correlation function $\xi(r)$ and its Fourier transform, the power spectrum, $P(k)$ [Strauss et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996astro.ph.10033S/abstract). These statistics encode the signature of Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO), which provide a standard ruler to measure the cosmic expansion rate and the equation of state of dark energy out to $z \sim 3$ [Braun et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007astro.ph..3746B/abstract), [Square Kilometre Array Cosmology Science Working Group et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PASA...37....7S/abstract). Simultaneously, peculiar velocities induced by gravitational infall distort the measured redshifts. These redshift-space distortions yield direct constraints on the linear growth rate of structure, $f(z)$, enabling stringent tests of General Relativity against modified gravity models on cosmological scales [Feldman et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...494L.129F/abstract), [Square Kilometre Array Cosmology Science Working Group et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PASA...37....7S/abstract), [Jain et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011RSPTA.369.5081J/abstract). Historically, these efforts are complemented by projects like the Nearby Early-type Galaxies Survey (ENEAR), an all-sky survey designed to characterize nearby early-type galaxies and estimate distances using the $D_n-\sigma$ relation to map local peculiar velocity fields [Wegner et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..201...62W/abstract).

As surveys cover increasingly large solid angles and deeper volumes, classical analytical approximations are being re-evaluated. While the standard Kaiser formula's reliance on the distant-observer approximation introduces negligible errors in the redshift-space correlation function if properly averaged over the survey volume, substantial deviations can occur when applying traditional power spectrum estimators (such as the FKP method) due to the non-uniform distribution of galaxy pairs [Yoo et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.1789Y/abstract). To recover non-linear information in such discrete galaxy surveys, advanced analytical techniques increasingly utilize the log density power spectrum; specifically, the modified $A^\ast$ statistic serves as a sufficient statistic to effectively capture these non-linear clustering signatures [Repp et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475L...6R/abstract). Furthermore, modern analyses utilizing simulation-based inference (SBI) to model non-linear clustering are highly sensitive to internal algorithmic choices. For example, estimates for cosmological parameters like the amplitude of mass fluctuations, $\sigma_8$, can be significantly biased depending on whether a Friends-of-Friends (FoF) or Rockstar halo-finder is utilized in the forward model [Modi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230915071M/abstract). In addition, [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]], such as lensing magnification and evolution biases, must be carefully modeled, as they dynamically alter the observed galaxy number densities based on the underlying [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] [Maartens et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...12..009M/abstract).

To maximize cosmological constraints and mitigate sample variance, recent approaches cross-correlate 3D spectroscopic catalogs with 2D photometric data or [[line-intensity-mapping-lim|Line Intensity Mapping (LIM)]] observations. Jointly analyzing overlapping photometric and spectroscopic maps using spherical Fourier-Bessel (sFB) modes can improve cosmological parameter constraints by factors of $\sim 1.2$ to $1.8$ compared to disjoint spatial analyses [Passaglia et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PhRvD..95l3508P/abstract), [Kirk et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451.4424K/abstract), [Oxholm et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104h3501O/abstract). Furthermore, correlating intensity mapping signals (such as CO or [C II] rotational transitions) with Ly-$\alpha$ forest measurements from massive spectroscopic surveys can enhance the detection signal-to-noise ratio by up to $300\%$, offering a powerful, unbiased inference method free from shared foreground systematics [Qezlou et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.1933Q/abstract). To ensure the integrity of these statistical analyses, researchers often rely on [[volume-limited-samples|Volume-limited samples]] to maintain a uniform sample across a range of distances.

## Galactic Archeology and Stellar Population Surveys

Galactic archeology relies on the premise that the kinematics and [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] of resolved stellar populations serve as fossil records of a galaxy's formation and assembly history. By obtaining [[high-resolution-spectroscopy|High-resolution spectroscopy]] of millions of individual stars, researchers can reconstruct the evolutionary timeline of the Milky Way and other Local Group galaxies [Freeman et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..399..439F/abstract). A primary objective of these surveys is "chemical tagging"—the process of associating dispersed stars back to their original, disrupted star-forming clusters by identifying common signatures in their multi-dimensional abundance space.

The advent of wide-field, multi-object spectrographs on 4- to 8-meter class telescopes has transitioned the field from targeted stellar observations to massive, panoptic surveys. The [[lamost-spectroscopic-survey|LAMOST Spectroscopic Survey]], for instance, has successfully collected over $4.5 \times 10^6$ stellar spectra with a signal-to-noise ratio $\mathrm{SNR} > 10$, providing an exquisite mapping of the kinematics and chemistry for stellar populations within several kiloparsecs of the Sun [Liu et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015RAA....15.1089L/abstract). These massive datasets allow for robust measurements of local dark matter densities and place critical constraints on hierarchical merging scenarios within the Milky Way's halo and disk.

Operating in tandem with such optical efforts, near-infrared spectroscopy has become vital for penetrating the severe dust obscuration of the Galactic disk and bulge. Building upon the legacy of previous optical and near-infrared mapping, the upcoming SDSS-V project will pioneer a panoptic, all-sky, multi-epoch spectroscopic survey. Targeting over $6 \times 10^6$ objects, SDSS-V is designed to decode the history of the Milky Way, trace the emergence of chemical elements, and map the interstellar gas of the Local Group at a scale $10^3$ times larger than previous efforts [Kollmeier et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv171103234K/abstract). When synergized with high-precision astrometry from the *Gaia* satellite, these spectroscopic surveys yield full 6D phase-space coordinates alongside detailed chemical profiles, enabling dynamical tracing of the Galaxy's ancient mergers [Freeman et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..399..439F/abstract).

Despite the wealth of available data, rigorous statistical frameworks are required to interpret these surveys accurately. A critical methodological challenge lies in defining the observational [[selection-function|Selection function]]. Because spectroscopic targets are often drawn from photometric catalogs subject to complex spatial, color, and magnitude constraints, failure to properly model the selection function can introduce severe systematic biases into the derived distance moduli and metallicity distributions [Mints et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...621A..17M/abstract). The impact of these biases is particularly pronounced in surveys with highly inhomogeneous spatial coverage, requiring careful forward-modeling to recover the true underlying stellar population parameters.

Looking toward the next decade, the principles of Galactic archeology are being extended beyond the Local Group. Proposed next-generation instruments for Extremely Large Telescopes (ELTs) aim to utilize adaptive-optics-assisted integral field spectroscopy to resolve individual stellar populations out to distances of $\sim 16$ Mpc. This will allow researchers to unlock the detailed chemical and kinematic assembly histories of a highly diverse range of galaxy morphologies throughout the Local Volume [Evans et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010aoel.confE1004E/abstract).

## Galaxy Assembly, Quenching, and Evolution

Spectroscopic surveys are fundamental to unraveling the physical mechanisms governing galaxy assembly and evolution. By analyzing the continuum alongside localized emission and absorption features, observers can extract the [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] (e.g., gas-phase metallicity), kinematics, and star formation history of stellar populations across cosmic time.

Historically, the bulk of large-scale demographic studies relied on single-fiber or slit observations—such as those from the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]]—which measure global integrated properties but obscure internal physical gradients. These large-scale efforts were supplemented by dedicated sub-surveys, such as the SDSS-II SN program, which provided rapid-response spectroscopy to classify over 100 Type Ia supernovae, thereby improving distance calibrations essential for mapping cosmic expansion and evolution [Zheng et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....135.1766Z/abstract). Similarly, the ESSENCE project utilized rapid-response spectroscopy to classify high-redshift supernovae and characterize host-galaxy evolution, extending these critical cosmological measurements deeper into cosmic time [Foley et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AJ....137.3731F/abstract). 

At the low-mass extreme of the local universe, blind HI surveys have successfully identified extremely metal-poor, unevolved dwarf galaxies like UGCA 292, providing crucial spectroscopic targets for understanding pristine gas accretion [van Zee et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...543L..31V/abstract). The advent of large-scale [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] (IFS) surveys has further revolutionized [[spatially-resolved-galaxy-analysis|Spatially Resolved Galaxy Analysis]]. Surveys in the local universe, such as SAMI and MaNGA, have demonstrated that angular momentum and chemical distributions are inextricably linked to a galaxy's mass and morphology [Colless et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181101518C/abstract). 

A critical application of these spatially resolved data is investigating the physical drivers of galaxy transformation, specifically the mechanisms responsible for quenching star formation. Recent studies at intermediate redshifts have provided robust evidence for "inside-out" quenching. For example, the [[magpi-survey|MAGPI survey]] uses the [[muse-instrument|MUSE (instrument)]] with adaptive optics to resolve massive central galaxies ($M_* > 7 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$) at $0.25 < z < 0.35$ [Foster et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASA...38...31F/abstract). Observations from MAGPI reveal that while main-sequence galaxies exhibit relatively flat radial star-formation profiles, galaxies lying below the main sequence display positive gradients in their star formation rates, indicating that star formation is suppressed in the central regions first [Mun et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.5072M/abstract).

However, the theoretical interpretation of these resolved properties remains ambiguous and frequently conflicts with state-of-the-art models. Current cosmological hydrodynamical simulations, such as EAGLE and Illustris-TNG, produce predictions for the spatially resolved properties of galaxies at $z \approx 0.3$ that are significantly discrepant with MAGPI observations, highlighting critical open questions in galaxy formation theory [Foster et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASA...38...31F/abstract).

Environmental factors also play a measurable role in galaxy transformation. Overlapping volume-limited census surveys, such as the [[resolve-survey|Resolve survey]] and ECO, show that proximity to cosmic web filaments correlates with advanced galaxy evolution. However, when accounting for halo mass, studies from the RESOLVE and ECO surveys indicate that these trends in color and gas content are primarily dominated by the higher prevalence of galaxy group environments near filaments, though cosmic web stripping may still have an additional small effect on gas-poor galaxies in low-mass halos [Hoosain et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.4139H/abstract).

Finally, [[deep-field-campaigns|Deep field campaigns]] continue to push the boundaries of galaxy assembly toward the epoch of reionization. The foundational framework for this exploration was laid by early quests for primeval galaxies, which utilized long-slit spectroscopy and deep searches for Lyman $\alpha$ emission to establish historical constraints on the epoch of initial galaxy formation [Koo et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ASSL..122..419K/abstract). Programs such as JADES have since provided deep near-infrared spectra for thousands of galaxies, securing redshifts and emission-line measurements up to $z \sim 13$ [D'Eugenio et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240406531D/abstract), [D'Eugenio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJS..277....4D/abstract). Bridging the gap between the local universe and the epoch of reionization, the VANDELS survey stands as a key high-redshift spectroscopic program, providing high signal-to-noise ratio spectra for galaxies situated between $z=1$ and $z=6.5$ [Garilli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...647A.150G/abstract). Furthermore, analyses of grism spectroscopy from the Hubble Space Telescope demonstrate that the characteristic luminosity, ${\mathcal L}_*$, of both ${\rm H}\alpha$ and $[{\rm O III}]$ emission-line galaxies increases monotonically with redshift in the range $1.2 < z < 1.9$ [Nagaraj et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...943....5N/abstract). Combined with lower-redshift data, these surveys allow for the continuous mapping of [[galaxy-luminosity-function|galaxy scaling relations]] from the earliest mature systems to the complex structures of the local universe.

## Survey Methodology: Selection Functions and Statistical Biases

The design of any massive spectroscopic program begins with [[target-selection|Target selection]], which typically relies on pre-existing [[observational-photometry|Observational Photometry]] or [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]] to identify candidates. For example, the methodology for identifying galaxy cluster targets has evolved significantly over time, transitioning from early visual inspections of photographic surveys to the fully digital, algorithmic detection methods utilized in programs like the SDSS [Gal et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..1195G/abstract). Because spectroscopic observations are time-intensive and heavily multiplexed instruments often have strict constraints (such as fiber collisions), it is rarely feasible to obtain a [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]] for every source in a given field. Consequently, researchers must meticulously characterize the survey's [[selection-function|Selection function]]—the mathematical probability $S(\mathbf{x}, L, \dots)$ that a source with a given set of intrinsic properties (e.g., position $\mathbf{x}$, luminosity $L$, or color) is included in the final observed catalog. The selection function is essential for converting the observed catalog into an intrinsic population distribution; failing to model it accurately introduces severe [[sample-selection-biases|Sample selection biases]] and [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]] [Mints et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...621A..17M/abstract).

A fundamental challenge in survey methodology is the Malmquist bias. Because most [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]] are flux-limited (i.e., designed with a specific apparent magnitude threshold $m < m_{\mathrm{lim}}$), intrinsically luminous objects can be detected at much greater distances than intrinsically faint ones. As a result, the high-redshift tail of a flux-limited catalog is artificially dominated by the most luminous sources, distorting the apparent [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and related scaling relations. To correct for this, astronomers frequently construct [[volume-limited-samples|Volume-limited samples]] by discarding data to strictly include only galaxies brighter than a conservative absolute magnitude limit, $M_{\mathrm{lim}}$, across the entire target redshift range.

Beyond simple broadband flux limits, modern surveys suffer from complex, multi-dimensional selection effects. For example, in far-infrared and submillimeter surveys of dusty star-forming galaxies, the observed [[redshift-distribution|Redshift distribution]] is highly sensitive to the specific wavelength and depth of the survey. Studies demonstrate that the median redshift of sources selected at $1.4\text{ mm}$ is systematically higher than those selected at shorter wavelengths. The literature explicitly notes that, paradoxically, deeper surveys in these wavebands do not always probe higher redshifts, complicating the interpretation of cross-survey comparisons. This is not merely a depth effect, but rather a result of longer wavelengths and gravitational lensing selection biases [Béthermin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...576L...9B/abstract).

In cosmological [[three-dimensional-galaxy-surveys|Three-dimensional galaxy surveys]], spatial and flux selection functions must also account for secondary observational effects like magnification bias—where gravitational lensing alters the observed galaxy number density—and evolution bias, which describes the physical change in the comoving number density of galaxies relative to a conserved population. Because these biases depend sensitively on the logarithmic derivatives of the luminosity function, they must be rigorously modeled to avoid skewing constraints on dark energy and modified gravity [Maartens et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...12..009M/abstract). Moreover, meticulous galaxy-selection criteria and survey strategy design are paramount when accurately measuring the evolution of the field galaxy luminosity function across different epochs [Tresse et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph..2209T/abstract).

Similarly, in galactic archaeology and stellar surveys, the methodology of [[target-selection|Target selection]] heavily influences the derived distance modulus and metallicity distributions. For surveys characterized by sparse or inhomogeneous spatial coverage across the sky, precise spatial selection functions are mandatory. Without proper [[completeness-corrections|Completeness corrections]], the derived physical properties of the Milky Way will reflect the observational footprint and targeting algorithms rather than the true underlying Galactic populations [Mints et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...621A..17M/abstract).

## Advanced Data Analysis and Machine Learning in Spectroscopy

The exponential growth of data from modern [[sky-mapping-and-surveys|Sky Mapping and Surveys]], such as the millions of spectra released by the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument ([[desi-bright-galaxy-survey|DESI]]) [DESI Collaboration et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230606308D/abstract), has driven the development of advanced machine learning and [[automated-spectral-feature-fitting|Automated spectral feature fitting]] techniques. To manage the immense "Big Data" yields flowing from panchromatic all-sky surveys, modern computational astrophysics increasingly relies on Virtual Observatories, which provide the essential infrastructure to aggregate, query, and analyze these vast distributed datasets seamlessly [Mickaelian et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016BaltA..25...75M/abstract). Traditional analytical methods struggle to scale efficiently, prompting the implementation of highly automated pipelines to process such massive catalogs. For example, the eBOSS Data Analysis Pipeline provides uniform extraction of kinematics, continuum indices, and emission-line fluxes for over $1.89 \times 10^6$ galaxies [Matthews Acuña et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251218076M/abstract). For spatially resolved data, algorithms like the new Galaxy Integral-field Spectroscopy Tool (nGIST) optimize the analysis of [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] by robustly handling sky residuals at wavelengths $>7000$ \AA\ and improving stellar kinematic bias estimations [Fraser-McKelvie et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ascl.soft07015F/abstract).

In the realm of deep learning, transformer-based architectures are emerging as powerful tools for processing [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]]. The Spectroscopy Pre-trained Transformer (SpecPT) is a foundational model designed for automated spectrum reconstruction and [[spectroscopic-redshift|redshift measurement]] [Pattnaik et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250101070P/abstract), [Pattnaik et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988..139P/abstract). Applied to DESI data over a redshift range of $0 < z < 1.6$, SpecPT accurately captures emission lines, absorption features, and continuum shapes while simultaneously suppressing noise. It achieves highly competitive redshift precision, demonstrating a Normalized Median Absolute Deviation (NMAD) of $0.0006$ for [[desi-bright-galaxy-survey|DESI Bright Galaxy Survey]] targets and $0.0008$ for emission-line galaxies. Furthermore, the catastrophic outlier fractions are exceptionally low at $0.20\%$ and $0.80\%$, respectively [Pattnaik et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250101070P/abstract).

Generative artificial intelligence has also opened pathways for deriving spectroscopic properties from pure [[observational-photometry|Observational Photometry]]. Conditional diffusion models, paired with contrastive networks, can now predict optical spectra from broadband photometric images, successfully recovering the global star-forming and quiescent bimodality [Doorenbos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..131D/abstract). These models can infer standard properties normally requiring spectroscopy—such as metallicity, stellar velocity dispersion, and the 4000 \AA\ break—and can predict the presence of an Active Galactic Nucleus with $82\%$ accuracy [Doorenbos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..131D/abstract).

Finally, extracting cosmological constraints from the non-linear, non-Gaussian scales of galaxy clustering increasingly relies on Simulation-Based Inference (SBI). SBI leverages high-fidelity cosmological simulations to infer parameters like $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$ directly from the galaxy power spectrum and bispectrum [Modi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230915071M/abstract). However, the literature reveals significant unresolved tensions regarding the robustness of this method. SBI is highly sensitive to the internal choices made during forward modeling; for instance, swapping the underlying halo-finder algorithm from Friends-of-Friends (FoF) to Rockstar introduces systematic biases in the estimation of $\sigma_8$ derived from the bispectrum [Modi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230915071M/abstract). Additionally, models trained on simplistic halo-occupation distributions fail when applied to data exhibiting more complex galaxy-halo relationships, indicating that computational cost and theoretical accuracy must be carefully balanced to avoid biased cosmological inferences in future surveys [Modi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230915071M/abstract).

## Multi-Wavelength Synergies and Intensity Mapping

Modern extragalactic astronomy relies heavily on the integration of optical and near-infrared spectroscopy with broader multi-wavelength imaging. The transition toward [[panchromatic-surveys|Panchromatic surveys]] has allowed researchers to break long-standing degeneracies in stellar population ages, metallicity, and dust extinction by combining data from across the electromagnetic spectrum [Driver et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012IAUS..284..268D/abstract). 

In the infrared and X-ray regimes, cross-matching spectroscopic catalogs with targeted multi-wavelength data has proven vital for understanding galactic environments and active galactic nuclei (AGN). For instance, the SINGS survey successfully linked mid-infrared emission to the interstellar medium properties of nearby galaxies [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003PASP..115..928K/abstract), while combined Spitzer and Hubble Space Telescope observations have constrained the bolometric energy output of infrared-luminous galaxies at $z > 1$ [Papovich et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph..8454P/abstract). At closer distances, 2-micron near-infrared spectroscopic surveys of luminous infrared galaxies (LIRGs) have proven essential, revealing spectra dominated by strong Br$\gamma$ emission lines and prominent CO absorption bands that physically characterize their immense, dust-obscured starburst activity [Goldader et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJS..108..449G/abstract). Furthermore, submillimetre-selected galaxy surveys, such as the SCUBA Lens Survey, have crucially characterized faint infrared populations that are otherwise missed by optical programs [Smail et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.331..495S/abstract). The study of these heavily obscured objects is further advancing with the development of integrated superconducting spectrometers like Deshima 2.0, which enable rapid redshift surveys of [C II] and CO lines in dusty star-forming galaxies [Rybak et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JLTP..209..766R/abstract). However, interpreting the [[redshift-distribution|Redshift distribution]] of dusty, star-forming galaxies is complicated by strong selection effects; phenomenological models indicate that median redshifts vary significantly with the survey wavelength, and deeper flux limits do not universally probe higher redshifts [Béthermin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...576L...9B/abstract). In the high-energy regime, [[x-ray-surveys|X-ray surveys]] paired with spectroscopic follow-up—such as the SEXSI program—have revealed that roughly half of hard X-ray sources down to fluxes of $1 \times 10^{-14}$ erg cm$^{-2}$ s$^{-1}$ are significantly obscured AGN [Eckart et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJS..165...19E/abstract). Furthermore, multi-wavelength cluster identification via the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect, when combined with optical spectroscopy (e.g., the SPT-SZ survey), has yielded velocity dispersion-mass scaling relations consistent with dark matter simulations, exhibiting a $\sim 30\%$ log-normal scatter in dispersion at a fixed mass [Ruel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...792...45R/abstract).

Another powerful synergy is found in strong gravitational lensing. Large-scale spectroscopic pipelines like [[desi-bright-galaxy-survey|DESI]] have demonstrated remarkable efficiency in serendipitously confirming thousands of strong-lens candidates originally identified in deep imaging surveys, such as the Hyper Suprime-Cam (HSC) program [Shu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250516158S/abstract).

[[radio-surveys|Radio surveys]] mapping the 21 cm HI line represent another critical axis of synergy. Next-generation facilities like the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) will pair deep continuum imaging and HI mapping over $20,000$ square degrees ($0.35 < z < 3$) with massive optical/NIR datasets to constrain dark energy and the growth rate of structure [Square Kilometre Array Cosmology Science Working Group et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PASA...37....7S/abstract). In particular, extensive synergies are anticipated between ESO's optical/near-infrared spectroscopic surveys and the SKAO, combining high-resolution stellar kinematic data with radio continuum and HI studies to provide a comprehensive view of galaxy formation and evolution [Prandoni et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Msngr.193...14P/abstract). These highly sensitive radio datasets will be capable of tracing the local cosmic web down to neutral hydrogen column densities of $N_{\rm HI} = 10^{16}\ {\rm cm}^{-2}$ [Braun et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007astro.ph..3746B/abstract). Moreover, deep radio surveys have demonstrated strong evolution in the cosmic star-formation rate out to $z \sim 1$, a signal fundamentally dominated by star-forming galaxies at faint flux limits [Condon et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASPC..380..189C/abstract).

To probe the high-redshift universe, [[line-intensity-mapping-lim|Line Intensity Mapping (LIM)]] has emerged as a powerful complement to traditional surveys of [[discrete-astronomical-sources|Discrete astronomical sources]]. Rather than resolving individual galaxies, LIM measures the cumulative line emission (such as CO, [C II], or HI) on large angular scales. Furthermore, current developments in next-generation mm-wave spectrometer cameras are designed to perform volumetric line-intensity mapping surveys of star-forming galaxies across vast cosmic epochs, from $z\sim 1$ to $12$ [Kovács et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024SPIE13102E..07K/abstract). Because single-tracer intensity mapping is susceptible to severe foreground contamination, cross-correlation with established [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]] surveys is highly advantageous. Theoretical frameworks demonstrate that matching the survey depths of both probes can effectively evade the cosmic variance limit, isolating the clustering bias and intrinsic line intensity [Oxholm et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104h3501O/abstract). Quantitatively, cross-correlating CO rotational transition maps with Lyman-$\alpha$ forest measurements from surveys like the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (eBOSS) or DESI is forecast to enhance the CO detection signal-to-noise ratio by $50\%$ to $300\%$ [Qezlou et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.1933Q/abstract). This synergistic approach provides an unbiased inference of high-redshift large-scale structure that neither technique could easily achieve alone.

## Open Questions and Future Wide-Field Facilities

The next generation of [[three-dimensional-galaxy-surveys|Three-dimensional galaxy surveys]] is poised to map the spatial distribution of matter with unprecedented volume and density, aiming to constrain the equation of state of dark energy and the nature of dark matter. Ground-based facilities have already initiated this transition; the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument ([[desi-bright-galaxy-survey|DESI]]) has commenced operations, with its Early Data Release alone providing high-quality spectra for nearly 2 million [[discrete-astronomical-sources|Discrete astronomical sources]], including luminous red galaxies, emission-line galaxies, and quasars [DESI Collaboration et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230606308D/abstract). 

In the near future, space-based missions such as Euclid and the Roman Space Telescope will leverage wide-field [[slitless-spectroscopy|Slitless spectroscopy]] to measure the [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]] of millions of galaxies via H$\alpha$ and [O III] $\lambda 5007$ emission lines. However, the exact yield of these missions remains an active topic of debate. While standard baseline predictions are commonly used for survey design, recent analysis of intermediate-redshift galaxies ($1.16 < z < 1.90$) using [[hst-observations|Hubble Space Telescope]] grism data indicates a potentially higher space density of intermediate-luminosity [O III] emitters. This suggests that the Roman Space Telescope may detect significantly more galaxies than previously forecast, although predictions for the Euclid wide [[survey-area|Survey area]] remain largely unchanged [Nagaraj et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...943....5N/abstract).

Looking further ahead, the proposed Wide-field Spectroscopic Telescope ([[wst|WST]]) represents a massive leap in ground-based capabilities. Designed as a dedicated 12-meter facility, WST will feature a 3 square degree field of view and a highly multiplexed multi-object spectrograph capable of observing 20,000 targets simultaneously, alongside a giant $3 \times 3$ arcminute integral field spectrograph [Mainieri et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240305398M/abstract). Operating without the need for photometric [[target-selection|Target selection]] in its integral-field mode, WST aims to obtain spectra for 250 million galaxies and 25 million stars within its first five years, addressing fundamental questions in cosmology, galactic archaeology, and multi-messenger astrophysics [Bacon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240512518B/abstract). Additionally, space-based concepts like the ATLAS probe are being designed to provide deep $1-4\ \mu\text{m}$ slit spectroscopy for 200 million galaxies, aiming to trace the 3D structure of the universe with unprecedented fidelity [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PASA...36...15W/abstract).

Despite these technological advances, several persistent open questions and methodological tensions remain in the interpretation of spectroscopic data:

* **[[spatially-resolved-galaxy-analysis|Spatially Resolved Galaxy Analysis]]**: The physical mechanisms driving galaxy quenching at intermediate redshifts ($z \approx 0.3$) are not fully understood. While some integral field observations suggest an "inside-out" quenching mechanism for galaxies below the star-forming main sequence [Mun et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.5072M/abstract), there is an explicit discrepancy between these resolved kinematic properties and the predictions generated by leading cosmological hydrodynamical simulations, such as EAGLE and Illustris-TNG [Foster et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASA...38...31F/abstract). 
* **Cosmological Inference Biases**: As the volume of data grows, traditional analytical models of the power spectrum are being supplemented by machine learning and Simulation-Based Inference (SBI). However, SBI methods are highly sensitive to the underlying physical assumptions of the training simulations. For example, changing the halo-finding algorithm (e.g., from Friends-of-Friends to Rockstar) in the forward model can introduce severe biases in the estimation of the amplitude of mass fluctuations, $\sigma_8$, from the galaxy bispectrum [Modi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230915071M/abstract). 
* **The Ionizing Background**: At high redshifts, characterizing the sources of reionization requires understanding the escape fraction of ionizing photons. Direct quantification of the Lyman continuum radiation leaking from low-redshift star-forming galaxies remains elusive, leaving the exact contribution of stars versus active galactic nuclei to the metagalactic background radiation poorly constrained [McCandliss et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004SPIE.5488..709M/abstract).
