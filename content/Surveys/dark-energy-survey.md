## Article

The **Dark Energy Survey (DES)** is a wide-field optical and [[infrared-instrumentation-and-catalogs|near-infrared]] [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Stage III cosmological survey]] designed to characterize the nature of cosmic acceleration and the properties of dark energy. Operating over 525 observing nights between 2013 and 2019, the survey [[sky-mapping-and-surveys|mapped]] approximately 5,000 square degrees of the southern celestial hemisphere to unprecedented depths. The project was executed using the purpose-built, 570-megapixel Dark Energy Camera ([[decam|DECam]]), mounted at the prime focus of the [[blanco-telescope|Victor M. Blanco 4m telescope]] at the [[cerro-tololo-inter-american-observatory|Cerro Tololo Inter-American Observatory]] in Chile [Diehl et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhPro..37.1332T/abstract). By compiling precise [[astronomical-photometry-techniques|photometry]] in five broad bandpass filters ([[ugriz-photometry|$grizY$]]) for hundreds of millions of galaxies and stars, DES represents a major milestone in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], bridging the gap between early [[large-galaxy-catalogs|large-area mapping projects]] and upcoming Stage IV [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]] [The Dark Energy Survey Collaboration et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.10346T/abstract).

To constrain the dark energy equation of state ($w$) and the cosmic matter density ($\Omega_m$), DES utilizes a joint analysis of four independent astrophysical probes: [[large-area-weak-lensing-surveys|weak gravitational lensing]], large-scale galaxy clustering, baryon acoustic oscillations, and Type Ia supernovae [Flaugher et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IJMPA..20.3121F/abstract). A cornerstone of the survey's methodology is the "3x2pt" analysis, which simultaneously models cosmic shear, galaxy position auto-correlations, and galaxy-galaxy lensing to robustly break degeneracies between cosmological parameters and nonlinear galaxy bias. Using DES data alone, the collaboration definitively ruled out a universe without dark energy and provided precision constraints on the amplitude of structure growth ($S_8$) [Drlica-Wagner et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJS..235...33D/abstract), [Abbott et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvL.122q1301A/abstract). While the survey's core findings largely support a standard flat $\Lambda$CDM cosmological model, they also highlight a persistent, mild tension ($1.5-2\sigma$) wherein DES measures a universe that is slightly less clumped (lower $S_8$) than predicted by Planck cosmic microwave background observations.

Beyond core cosmology, the sheer volume and uniformity of DES data have generated a profound astrophysical [[legacy-surveys|legacy]]. The survey has facilitated major discoveries in near-field cosmology, including the identification of numerous ultra-faint Milky Way dwarf satellite galaxies that test cold dark matter paradigms [Dark Energy Survey Collaboration et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.1270D/abstract). Additionally, DES has provided critical optical follow-up for gravitational wave events in the era of multi-messenger astronomy [Cowperthwaite et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...826L..29C/abstract), [Doctor et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...873L..24D/abstract). Furthermore, DES pioneered advanced data calibration frameworks—such as pixel-level synthetic source injection (Balrog)—which are now foundational for mitigating selection biases in large datasets. However, critical open questions remain: researchers are actively debating whether the $S_8$ tension arises from unmodeled systematic effects or new physics, and whether emerging discrepancies between DES supernovae and overlapping [[desi-survey|DESI survey]] data hint at a time-evolving, dynamical dark energy. Ultimately, the software pipelines, observational strategies, and systematic error controls developed by DES establish the methodological baseline for [[future-astronomical-facilities-and-surveys|next-generation observatories]] like the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]] and the [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]].

## Scientific Objectives

The primary scientific motivation of DES is to constrain the dark energy equation of state, defined as the ratio of pressure to density ($w = p/\rho$), and its potential evolution over cosmic time. To achieve this with high precision and to mitigate the impact of systematic errors, DES was explicitly designed to combine four distinct and complementary astrophysical probes: the abundance and spatial distribution of galaxy clusters, [[large-area-weak-lensing-surveys|weak gravitational lensing]] (cosmic shear), large-scale galaxy angular clustering (including baryon acoustic oscillations), and luminosity distances derived from Type Ia supernovae [The Dark Energy Survey Collaboration et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.10346T/abstract), [Flaugher et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IJMPA..20.3121F/abstract). By jointly analyzing these probes within a unified framework, the survey breaks degeneracies between cosmological parameters that limit single-probe experiments, establishing a methodological baseline for the next generation of cosmological surveys. The survey's constraints on the dark energy equation of state are often compared with those from precursor [[deep-spectroscopic-surveys|spectroscopic efforts]] like the [[wigglez-survey|WiggleZ Dark Energy Survey]], which mapped BAO features over 1,000 deg$^2$ [Drinkwater et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.401.1429D/abstract).

## Historical Development and Survey Timeline

The Dark Energy Survey was conceptualized in the mid-2000s to measure the accelerating expansion of the Universe. Pre-survey Bayesian experimental design studies explored sparse sampling strategies for DES to optimize the trade-off between sky area and observational time for power spectrum constraints [Paykari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.433.3523P/abstract). To meet the stringent requirements of this program, the collaboration designed and constructed the Dark Energy Camera ([[decam|DECam]]), a 570-megapixel, 3-square-degree mosaic imager. Equipped with 62 science CCDs and 12 guiding and focus CCDs optimized for near-infrared sensitivity, DECam was installed at the prime focus of the 4-meter [[blanco-telescope|Blanco Telescope]] at the [[cerro-tololo-inter-american-observatory|Cerro Tololo Inter-American Observatory]] (CTIO) in Chile [Diehl et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhPro..37.1332T/abstract), [Soares-Santos et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhPro..37.1445S/abstract). 

Following DECam's first light in September 2012, the collaboration conducted a Science Verification (SV) phase. This initial period observed approximately $150$ to $250 \text{ deg}^2$ of the sky to full survey depth. The SV data successfully validated the camera's on-sky performance, refined the photometric reduction pipelines, and produced the survey's first early weak lensing mass maps , [Vikram et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..92b2006V/abstract). Initial cosmological constraints from the Science Verification data patch yielded a matter density of $\Omega_m = 0.31 \pm 0.09$ using a joint clustering and galaxy-galaxy lensing analysis [Kwan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4045K/abstract). 

Official DES observing operations began on August 31, 2013, initiating a multi-year campaign to map the southern sky. The survey's findings were subsequently released in major data increments. The Year 1 (Y1) Results were based on imaging covering $\sim 1800 \text{ deg}^2$. The resulting Y1A1 GOLD catalog contained roughly 137 million objects. The Y1 cosmological analysis firmly ruled out a Universe with no dark energy using DES data alone [Drlica-Wagner et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJS..235...33D/abstract), [Abbott et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvL.122q1301A/abstract). In 2021, the collaboration issued Data Release 2 (DR2), providing coadded catalogs for the full 5,000 deg$^2$ footprint containing 691 million objects with an internal astrometric precision of $\sim 27$ mas [Abbott et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..255...20A/abstract).

The Year 3 (Y3) Results marked a major milestone by expanding the analysis to the full $5000 \text{ deg}^2$ footprint. The Y3 GOLD dataset encompassed nearly 390 million objects and achieved top-of-the-atmosphere photometric uniformity of $< 3 \text{ mmag}$ [Sevilla-Noarbe et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..254...24S/abstract). Using this expanded footprint, the collaboration placed unprecedented photometric constraints on the matter density and clustering amplitude [Abbott et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.105b3520A/abstract).

After 525 observing nights, the DES successfully completed its data collection in early 2019. The culmination of this effort is the Year 6 (Y6) Final Dataset, embodied by the Y6 Gold catalog. This definitive release contains 669 million objects down to a $10\sigma$ limiting magnitude of $i \approx 23.4$ [Bechtol et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250105739B/abstract). A cornerstone of the final data processing was the deployment of synthetic source injection across the entire survey area. Using the Balrog pipeline, 146 million synthetic galaxies were embedded into pixel-level images to rigorously characterize detection biases, blending effects, and survey transfer functions at the percent level, providing an unparalleled calibration framework for the final cosmological analyses [Anbajagane et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250105683A/abstract).

## Instrumentation and Survey Strategy

The Dark Energy Survey was primarily executed using [[decam|DECam]], a massive [[panoramic-imaging|wide-field optical imager]] providing a 3 square-degree field of view, enabling highly efficient and rapid mapping of the southern sky [Diehl et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhPro..37.1332T/abstract). The DES observation strategy was divided into two distinct components. The primary component was a wide-area campaign covering a 5,000 square-degree Survey footprint across the southern Galactic cap [Dark Energy Survey Collaboration et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.1270D/abstract), [Abbott et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJS..239...18A/abstract). Complementing this was a deep, time-domain survey covering approximately 30 square degrees across ten selected fields. This time-domain component was optimized with a roughly weekly cadence to detect transient events, most notably Type Ia supernovae, to trace the cosmic expansion history [Diehl et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SPIE.9149E..0VD/abstract), [Kessler et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015AJ....150..172K/abstract).

Observations were conducted using five broad bandpass filters to obtain deep [[ugriz-photometry|$grizY$ photometry]] [Dark Energy Survey Collaboration et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.1270D/abstract). The survey achieved impressive depth and excellent image quality; in the first data release (DR1) co-added catalogs, the median $10\sigma$ limiting magnitudes for a 1.95-arcsecond diameter aperture were $g = 24.33$, $r = 24.08$, $i = 23.44$, $z = 22.69$, and $Y = 21.44$ [Abbott et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJS..239...18A/abstract). The median delivered point-spread function (PSF) full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) was $1.12''$, $0.96''$, $0.88''$, $0.84''$, and $0.90''$ in the $g, r, i, z$, and $Y$ bands, respectively [Abbott et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJS..239...18A/abstract). 

Achieving the stringent precision required for cosmological constraints necessitated advanced calibration and data processing techniques. DES reached a top-of-the-atmosphere photometric uniformity of $<2-3$ mmag and an absolute calibration accuracy of better than $2\%$ over the entire survey area [Drlica-Wagner et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJS..235...33D/abstract), [Sevilla-Noarbe et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..254...24S/abstract). 

## Primary Cosmological Probes

The cornerstone of the DES photometric cosmology program is the "3x2pt" analysis. This technique jointly models three two-point correlation functions: [[large-area-weak-lensing-surveys|cosmic shear]] (the correlation of galaxy shapes), galaxy clustering (the auto-correlation of galaxy positions), and galaxy-galaxy lensing (the cross-correlation of lens galaxy positions with source galaxy shear) [Abbott et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.105b3520A/abstract). By simultaneously fitting these observables using optimized, magnitude-limited (MAGLIM) lens catalogs, the 3x2pt approach successfully breaks degeneracies between linear galaxy bias and the underlying matter density field [Baxter et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvD..99b3508B/abstract), [Porredon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106j3530P/abstract). To achieve precision in the Y3 analysis, DES utilized effective perturbation theory models for galaxy bias, validated against mock catalogs to describe 3D correlation functions above 4 Mpc/h [Pandey et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.102l3522P/abstract). The DES Year 3 (Y3) 3x2pt analysis yielded tight constraints on the amplitude of matter clustering, $S_8 \equiv \sigma_8 (\Omega_m / 0.3)^{0.5} = 0.776 \pm 0.017$, and a matter density of $\Omega_m = 0.339 \pm 0.032$, assuming a flat $\Lambda$CDM cosmology [Abbott et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.105b3520A/abstract). However, these large-scale structure constraints exhibit a persistent, mild $1.5-2\sigma$ tension with the $S_8$ values predicted by cosmic microwave background measurements from the Planck satellite [Porredon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106j3530P/abstract), [Porredon et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215960P/abstract). Important validation work for such measurements began early; for example, the effect of weak lensing magnification on galaxy number counts was rigorously tested in the DES SV data using cross-correlation between redshift-separated galaxy samples [Garcia-Fernandez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.1071G/abstract).

Beyond the 3x2pt framework, DES utilizes galaxy cluster abundances as an independent probe of structure growth. Clusters are identified primarily using the redMaPPer algorithm, which uses optical richness ($\lambda$) as a proxy for halo mass [Rykoff et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..224....1R/abstract). Additionally, DES measures baryon acoustic oscillations to constrain the cosmic expansion history. Because DES relies on photometric rather than spectroscopic redshifts, BAO measurements require careful calibration of redshift distributions to accurately isolate the angular diameter distance scale using millions of galaxies over thousands of square degrees [Mena-Fernández et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240210697M/abstract). In related DECam-based analyses, surveys such as the [[decals|Dark Energy Camera Legacy Survey (DECaLS)]] have measured BAO by applying self-calibration techniques to redshift distributions to constrain $D_M/r_s$ [Song et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530..881S/abstract). DES BAO results are often interpreted alongside historical 2% distance measurements from reconstructed [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|SDSS]] BAO signals at $z=0.35$ [Mehta et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.427.2168M/abstract).

The time-domain component of the survey conducted a dedicated search for Type Ia supernovae using [[decam|DECam]] to measure luminosity distances. The DES-SN 5-Year data release produced a cosmology-grade sample of 1,635 photometrically classified Type Ia supernovae over a redshift range of $0.1 < z < 1.13$. This represents the largest sample of supernovae from a single instrument ever used for cosmological inference [Sánchez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975....5S/abstract), [Sánchez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240605046S/abstract). When early 3x2pt, BAO, and supernova data were combined, the survey independently ruled out a universe without dark energy, constraining $w = -0.80^{+0.09}_{-0.11}$ using only DES data [Abbott et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvL.122q1301A/abstract). Recent low-redshift supernova efforts include the Dark Energy Bedrock All-Sky Supernova (DEBASS) program, which uses DECam to create a uniformly calibrated sample of SNe Ia at $z < 0.08$ to replace historic external samples [Sherman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250810878S/abstract).

Despite the general success of the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm, combining DES probes with external datasets has highlighted emerging tensions. Most notably, a statistically significant $3.4\sigma$ discrepancy in $\Omega_m$ has been identified between low-redshift DES supernovae and overlapping galaxy clustering data from the [[desi-survey|DESI survey]] [Colgáin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241212905C/abstract). The literature remains actively divided on whether this tension provides genuine evidence for dynamical dark energy (e.g., a time-evolving $w_0$-$w_a$ model) or if it is driven by unexplored systematic errors in low-redshift supernova calibration [Colgáin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241212905C/abstract), [Zhong et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250926480Z/abstract).

## Data Processing and Calibration Frameworks

Transforming the petabytes of raw imaging data captured by [[decam|DECam]] into cosmology-ready catalogs requires a sophisticated, multi-stage data processing and calibration infrastructure. The culmination of this effort is the release of the "GOLD" catalogs (e.g., Y1, Y3, and Y6), which achieve top-of-the-atmosphere photometric uniformity of $<2-3$ mmag across the 5000 deg$^2$ survey footprint in the $grizY$ bands [Sevilla-Noarbe et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..254...24S/abstract), [Bechtol et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250105739B/abstract). 

A major paradigm shift introduced by the Dark Energy Survey is the transition from applying analytic selection functions to utilizing pixel-level synthetic source injection (SSI) for characterizing survey biases. This is primarily handled by the Balrog software pipeline, which embeds simulated galaxy models directly into real observational images. By processing these synthetic galaxies alongside actual data, Balrog accurately maps measurement biases, blending effects, and transfer functions that are otherwise too complex to model analytically [Suchyta et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457..786S/abstract). In the Year 6 analysis, Balrog injected 146 million synthetic sources, recovering detection rates and photometric properties to within $1\%$ accuracy for typical galaxy samples [Everett et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..258...15E/abstract), [Anbajagane et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250105683A/abstract).

Accurate photometric redshift calibration is another critical requirement, particularly for [[large-area-weak-lensing-surveys|weak lensing measurements]] where redshift uncertainties historically dominate the error budget. DES addresses this using the Self-Organizing Map Photo-Z (SOMPZ) framework, which derives redshift distributions by linking wide-field DES photometry to deep-field observations and Balrog SSI catalogs [Myles et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4249M/abstract), [Giannini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250907964G/abstract). To break degeneracies, SOMPZ constraints are combined with "clustering redshifts"—spatial cross-correlations between the photometric sample and overlapping spectroscopic reference samples. This joint approach successfully constraints the mean redshift of tomographic bins to an effective uncertainty of $\sigma_{\langle z \rangle} \sim 0.01$ [Myles et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4249M/abstract), [Ruggeri et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215963R/abstract).

For time-domain science and transient detection, DES initially utilized the Difference Imaging (DiffImg) pipeline. DiffImg aligns new search epochs to deep reference images and performs pixel-by-pixel subtraction to detect point sources like Type Ia supernovae [Kessler et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015AJ....150..172K/abstract). However, early analyses showed that DiffImg suffered from a "host-galaxy surface brightness anomaly," where flux measurements exhibited excess scatter dependent on the underlying galaxy's brightness. To resolve this, the final 5-year supernova sample was re-processed using Scene Modelling Photometry (SMP). By forward-modeling the transient and host galaxy simultaneously without resampling the images, SMP delivered more precise and accurate light curves for cosmological fits [Sánchez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240605046S/abstract), [Sánchez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975....5S/abstract).

Despite these advanced frameworks, characterizing residual systematics remains an active area of research. For example, mock catalog and image simulations for the upcoming [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]] have demonstrated that blending of overlapping sources induces a systematic bias in galaxy number counts that significantly alters clustering statistics on scales below 10 arcminutes [Levine et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025OJAp....8E..38L/abstract). Additionally, analyses of cosmic shear maps have occasionally detected non-lensing B-mode signals at the $2.8\sigma$ to $5.5\sigma$ level. These non-physical artifacts indicate the presence of residual systematics, such as point-spread function (PSF) leakage and repeating additive shear biases, which require ongoing modeling [Asgari et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...624A.134A/abstract). The simulation techniques developed for DES have transitioned into larger efforts like CosmoDC2, a synthetic sky catalog covering 440 deg$^2$ to $z=3$ designed to support LSST Dark Energy Science Collaboration analyses [Korytov et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..245...26K/abstract). Synthetic sky generation for DES and LSST has been further enhanced by the galsampler package, which recasts hydrodynamical simulations as galaxy libraries to populate large-volume halos [Hearin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.495.5040H/abstract). Together, the software solutions and rigorous systematic checks developed for DES establish a foundational precedent for next-generation [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]] such as those at the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]]. Additionally, the methodologies honed during DES are increasingly applied to other DECam datasets. For instance, the DECADE cosmic shear project processed all public DECam data in the northern Galactic cap to create a new weak lensing shape catalog of 107 million galaxies [Anbajagane et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025OJAp....846158A/abstract).

## Galaxy Clusters and Large-Scale Structure

By identifying thousands of galaxy clusters and mapping the cosmic web, the Dark Energy Survey provides powerful constraints on the late-time growth of structure. At the core of the DES cluster cosmology program is the redMaPPer algorithm, a photometric red-sequence cluster finder that produces high-purity, locally volume-limited catalogs. redMaPPer utilizes optical richness ($\lambda$) as a proxy for cluster halo mass, an observable that is extensively calibrated using [[large-area-weak-lensing-surveys|weak gravitational lensing]], [[x-ray-surveys|X-ray surveys]], and Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect measurements from instruments like the [[south-pole-telescope|South Pole Telescope]] [Rykoff et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..224....1R/abstract).

Using the coherent distortions of background galaxies caused by weak gravitational lensing, DES constructs wide-field mass maps that trace the projected mass density of both luminous and dark matter [Chang et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvL.115e1301C/abstract), [Vikram et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..92b2006V/abstract). These mass maps effectively trace the underlying dark matter scaffolding, allowing researchers to identify superclusters, voids, and large-scale filamentary networks. For example, DES weak lensing and galaxy distribution analyses have revealed filamentary structures stretching up to $\approx 20$ Mpc attached to massive systems like 1E 0657-56 [Melchior et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449.2219M/abstract).

On sub-cluster scales, DES images provide high-signal-to-noise measurements of intracluster light (ICL)—a faint, diffuse stellar component stripped from galaxies over cosmic time. In DES observations, ICL has been detected extending to at least 1 Mpc from cluster centers at $z \sim 0.25$, contributing, together with the central galaxy, $44\% \pm 17\%$ of the total cluster stellar luminosity within that radius [Zhang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874..165Z/abstract). The brightness of the ICL is a strong tracer of the radial mass distribution of the host cluster. Furthermore, independent DECam-based observations from the Local Volume Complete Cluster Survey (LoVoCCS) show a $19^\circ$ median misalignment between the brightest cluster galaxies and the broader red-sequence distributions within 2 Mpc, and a $32^\circ$ median difference between the red-sequence distribution and the overall cluster mass distribution within 1 Mpc [Fu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240210337F/abstract), indicating long-term dynamical evolution. Additionally, X-ray follow-up of DES-selected clusters indicates a cool core fraction of approximately $20\%$, with no significant trend in this fraction as a function of redshift or richness [Graham et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023RNAAS...7...86G/abstract).

While early DES Year 1 cluster abundance analyses reported a severe $5.6\sigma$ tension with Planck, subsequent studies utilizing forward-modeling frameworks argue that this specific cluster abundance tension is likely driven by unmodeled selection systematics in the optical cluster-finding process rather than a breakdown of the standard cosmological model [Salcedo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231003944S/abstract). Ongoing analysis of overlapping spectroscopic samples will be required to definitively determine whether the broader $S_8$ tension points to new physics or residual measurement biases.

## Astrophysical Legacy Beyond Cosmology

While the primary objective of the Dark Energy Survey is to constrain cosmological parameters, the survey's unprecedented combination of depth and area over 5,000 square degrees provides a rich legacy dataset for broader astrophysical research [Dark Energy Survey Collaboration et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.1270D/abstract). Observations taken with [[decam|DECam]] in the [[ugriz-photometry|$grizY$]] photometric bands have facilitated major discoveries in near-field cosmology, galactic structure, [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|high-redshift galaxy evolution]], and planetary science.

**Galactic Structure and Dwarf Galaxies**
Initial forecasts suggested DES would sample over 100 million stars, allowing for detailed constraints on the stellar initial mass function and the structure of the Galactic thick disk and halo [Rossetto et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AJ....141..185R/abstract). Indeed, DES data provide high-sensitivity maps of the Milky Way's stellar halo, cataloging approximately 120 million stars that trace the thick disk and halo out to large galactocentric distances [Bechtol et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250105739B/abstract). The wide-area photometry has been instrumental in the visualization of diverse substructures, including globular clusters and stellar streams [Ferguson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250605469F/abstract). 

Most notably, DES has significantly expanded the census of Local Group satellite galaxies, directly testing cold dark matter (CDM) predictions regarding the "missing satellites" problem. Early survey data led to the discovery of 17 new Milky Way dwarf satellites [Dark Energy Survey Collaboration et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.1270D/abstract). Subsequent analyses of Year 2 data identified an additional 8 [[low-surface-brightness-astronomy|ultra-faint dwarf galaxies]], characterized by extremely low central surface brightnesses ($\mu \gtrsim 27.5$ mag arcsec$^{-2}$) and physical half-light radii ranging from $17 \text{ pc} < r_{1/2} < 181 \text{ pc}$ [Drlica-Wagner et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...813..109D/abstract). Researchers found that the spatial distribution of these satellites is highly anisotropic and strongly associated with the Magellanic system rather than forming an isotropic halo [Drlica-Wagner et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...813..109D/abstract). DES has also structurally characterized the outskirts of the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC), revealing a warped and thickened stellar disk in its outer northern regions [Balbinot et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449.1129B/abstract), and confirmed other compact faint systems like [[horologium-ii|Horologium II]] and the low-mass halo cluster DES 1 at a heliocentric distance of $\sim 82$ kpc [Luque et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.458..603L/abstract). The discovery of Reticulum II in DES data also allowed for searches for dark matter annihilation signatures, identifying a gamma-ray excess with a $3.2\sigma$ local significance [Hooper et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...09..016H/abstract). Recent cross-correlation studies using DESI spectroscopy and DES lensing have provided the most precise halo mass measurements for dwarf galaxies to date, reaching stellar masses down to $10^{8} M_\odot$ [Treiber et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250920434T/abstract).

Furthermore, DES data have enabled the compilation of a massive catalog of 23,790 extended low-surface-brightness galaxies (LSBGs). Analyses of this sample revealed that reder LSBGs are more strongly clustered and associated with $z < 0.1$ galaxy clusters [Tanoglidis et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..252...18T/abstract). The Dark Energy Camera has also been utilized for targeted deep galaxy studies, such as the SCABS survey which mapped the baryonic structures around Centaurus A (NGC 5128) over 21 square degrees [Taylor et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160807285T/abstract). Furthermore, DES imaging has facilitated the study of star formation in unique environments, including HI-selected galaxies with little star formation, which were found to be dominated by LINER/LIER emission [Parkash et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.3169P/abstract).

The instrumentation and imaging techniques established by DES have also empowered subsequent surveys. For instance, the Dark Energy Camera Plane Survey 2 (DECaPS2) used DECam to map 6.5% of the southern Galactic plane in five bands, producing an unprecedented catalog of 3.32 billion objects [Saydjari et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..264...28S/abstract).

**High-Redshift Quasars and the Distant Universe**
Beyond the Local Group, the deep optical imaging from DES, when cross-matched with near-infrared and mid-infrared catalogs such as [[unwise|unWISE]], provides a powerful engine for selecting high-redshift quasars in the epoch of reionization. Spectroscopic follow-up of DES candidates has yielded the discovery of numerous luminous quasars at $z > 6.4$, including the identification of an extremely distant quasar at $z = 7.02$ [Yang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AJ....157..236Y/abstract). These sources act as vital probes of early supermassive black hole growth and the intergalactic medium. The survey depth also enables the detection of rare, massive galaxies ($>10^{12} M_\odot$) at redshifts $z \gtrsim 4$, providing insights into the early stages of galaxy assembly [Davies et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.434..296D/abstract).

**The Solar System**
The cadence of DES observations, originally optimized for supernova cosmology, is also highly sensitive to moving objects. Within the Solar System, early DES data alone enabled the discovery of 34 Trans-Neptunian objects [Dark Energy Survey Collaboration et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.1270D/abstract). 

## Survey Synergies and Multi-Messenger Astronomy

The Dark Energy Survey achieves its highest cosmological precision through a "joint probes analysis" that integrates its optical imaging with external spectroscopic, microwave, and transient datasets. By combining large-scale structure, weak lensing, and cosmic microwave background (CMB) measurements, DES effectively breaks degeneracies between astrophysical nuisance parameters and fundamental cosmology.

Spectroscopic follow-up is essential for calibrating photometric redshifts and classifying transients. The [[ozdes|OzDES]] program utilized the [[anglo-australian-telescope|Anglo-Australian Telescope]] to systematically observe DES deep fields, providing redshifts for thousands of Type Ia supernova host galaxies and confirming active transients [Childress et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472..273C/abstract). OzDES also enabled high-redshift multi-epoch science within the DES footprint, such as measuring supermassive black hole masses up to $M = 4.4 \times 10^9 M_\odot$ via C IV reverberation mapping [Hoormann et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.3650H/abstract). 

DES shares a deeply synergistic relationship with the [[desi-survey|DESI survey]]. Pre-DESI imaging campaigns, such as [[decals|DECaLS]], leveraged the [[decam|Dark Energy Camera]] to produce the targeting catalogs required for DESI's spectroscopic operations [Myers et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....165...50M/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....165..253H/abstract). In return, overlapping DESI spectroscopic catalogs provide crucial "clustering redshifts" used to calibrate DES weak lensing source distributions via cross-correlation, reducing mean redshift uncertainties to $\sigma_{\langle z \rangle} \sim 0.01$ [Myles et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4249M/abstract), [Ruggeri et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215963R/abstract). Joint fits combining DES cosmic shear with spectroscopic redshift-space distortions from DESI or BOSS (e.g., using the photometrically matched "DMASS" sample) robustly break degeneracies between galaxy bias and the matter density field, yielding tight constraints on the clustering amplitude $S_8$ [Lee et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.2887L/abstract), [Porredon et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215960P/abstract). Initial results from the 'Lensing Without Borders' project have successfully cross-correlated DESI DR1 spectroscopic lenses with DES source galaxies to measure galaxy-galaxy lensing and projected clustering signals, which serve as the foundation for forthcoming cosmological analyses [Heydenreich et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250621677H/abstract). 

DES also extensively cross-correlates its optical large-scale structure maps with CMB measurements, particularly from the [[south-pole-telescope|South Pole Telescope]] (SPT) and the Planck satellite. The cross-correlation between [[large-area-weak-lensing-surveys|weak lensing]] and CMB lensing maps isolates the growth of structure across cosmic time, detected at $5.8\sigma$ significance using DES Year 1 and SPT/Planck data [Giannantonio et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.3213G/abstract), [Omori et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvD.100d3517O/abstract). This multi-wavelength joint analysis allows for the percent-level self-calibration of shear multiplicative biases and linear galaxy bias, mitigating systematic uncertainties that plague optical-only surveys [Vallinotto et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...778..108V/abstract), [Baxter et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvD..99b3508B/abstract).

In the time domain, the wide field of view of the Dark Energy Camera positioned DES as a premier facility for multi-messenger astronomy, specifically for LIGO/Virgo follow-up. DES actively searched for optical counterparts to gravitational wave events using the difference-imaging pipelines developed for its supernova program [Kessler et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015AJ....150..172K/abstract). Following the binary black hole merger GW170814, DES imaged 87 square degrees of the localization region to a depth of $i \sim 23$ mag. The non-detection of a transient counterpart disfavored the existence of rapidly declining optical emission brighter than $L \sim 5 \times 10^{41} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$ [Doctor et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...873L..24D/abstract). Similar deep optical follow-up was performed for GW151226, demonstrating the survey's capacity to efficiently distinguish true multi-messenger sources from contaminating supernovae [Cowperthwaite et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...826L..29C/abstract). Beyond gravitational waves, cross-matches between DES optical imaging and radio surveys like RACS have led to the discovery of nearly 200 giant radio galaxies, significantly increasing the known population of Megaparsec-scale sources [Andernach et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Galax...9...99A/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The methodological advances pioneered by the Dark Energy Survey—particularly the joint "3x2pt" analysis of cosmic shear, galaxy clustering, and galaxy-galaxy lensing—have established the statistical and algorithmic foundation for upcoming Stage IV facilities, including the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]], the [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]], and the [[desi-survey|DESI survey]] [Abbott et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.105b3520A/abstract), [Linke et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240501037L/abstract). The LSST Dark Energy Science Collaboration (DESC) has established a Science Requirements Document that builds on DES methodologies to ensure Stage IV precision for galaxy clustering and [[large-area-weak-lensing-surveys|weak lensing]] [The LSST Dark Energy Science Collaboration et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180901669T/abstract). However, as statistical uncertainties shrink, several cosmological tensions and methodological challenges remain open.

A central open question in precision cosmology is the physical origin of the persistent $S_8$ tension. Measurements from low-redshift large-scale structure probes like DES consistently prefer lower $S_8$ values than those predicted by Planck CMB observations [Porredon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106j3530P/abstract), [Porredon et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215960P/abstract). It remains heavily debated whether this discrepancy points to new physics governing the growth of structure or arises from unresolved systematics, such as unmodeled optical cluster selection effects [Salcedo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231003944S/abstract). 

Furthermore, the possibility of a time-evolving dark energy equation of state ($w_0-w_a$ model) is an area of active investigation. As highlighted by recent joint analyses of DES supernovae and DESI clustering data, statistical preferences for dynamical dark energy may be heavily influenced by low-redshift parameter tensions rather than definitive new physics [Colgáin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241212905C/abstract). Resolving whether these discrepancies indicate true dynamical dark energy or unexplored systematics is a major priority for the field.

Realizing the sub-percent precision goals of Stage IV surveys will demand unprecedented systematic error control. Uncertainties in photometric redshift calibration and multiplicative shear bias currently dominate error budgets. Future efforts will heavily rely on combining clustering redshifts with self-organizing map techniques (SOMPZ), a calibration framework optimized by DES to control mean redshift uncertainties at the $\sigma_{\langle z \rangle} \sim 0.01$ level [Myles et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4249M/abstract), [Giannini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250907964G/abstract). Additionally, pixel-level synthetic source injection (e.g., Balrog) must be scaled to effectively characterize detection biases, transfer functions, and blending effects [Suchyta et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457..786S/abstract), [Anbajagane et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250105683A/abstract). Such simulations are critical, as mock catalogs for LSST forecast that blending can systematically bias galaxy number counts and alter clustering measurements on scales below 10 arcminutes [Levine et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025OJAp....8E..38L/abstract).

Beyond core cosmology, future surveys will massively expand the statistics of rare astrophysical objects. While DES successfully identified hundreds of strong gravitational lenses—such as the 247 candidate strong-lens systems found within the full 5,000 deg$^2$ footprint by the Dark Energy Survey Bright Arcs Survey [O'Donnell et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..259...27O/abstract)—the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]] is expected to catalog approximately $120,000$ galaxy-scale strong lenses, transforming them into a premier, independent cosmological probe [Collett et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...811...20C/abstract). Furthermore, DES has demonstrated the feasibility of using intracluster light (ICL) out to 1 Mpc to trace cluster mass distributions [Zhang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874..165Z/abstract). Future work must determine if ICL can serve as a primary mass proxy for Stage IV cluster cosmology without introducing surface mass density biases in weak lensing source selections [Gruen et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488.4389G/abstract). Finally, the discovery of ultra-faint Milky Way companions in the DES footprint raises questions about the threshold of galaxy formation; determining how many dark matter subhalos remain undetected due to surface brightness limits will be a key target for the next generation of [[deep-imaging-surveys|Deep imaging surveys]] [Drlica-Wagner et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...813..109D/abstract). Answering these questions will be greatly aided by synergistic programs like the Merian Survey, a DECam medium-band imaging program covering 750 deg$^2$ to study the dark matter halos of dwarf galaxies at $z \sim 0.06-0.10$ via H$\alpha$ and [OIII] emission [Danieli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241001884D/abstract).
