The **Massive and Distant Clusters of WISE Survey (MaDCoWS)** is a large-scale observational program designed to identify and characterize massive galaxy clusters during their peak epoch of assembly at redshifts $0.7 \lesssim z \lesssim 1.5$. Building upon the surface brightness overdensity techniques pioneered by earlier optical programs like the [[las-campanas-distant-cluster-survey|Las Campanas Distant Cluster Survey]] [Gonzalez et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000PhDT.......211G/abstract), MaDCoWS relies primarily on all-sky mid-infrared imaging from the Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer (WISE) [Gonzalez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..240...33G/abstract). By utilizing the $3.4\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and $4.6\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ WISE bands, the survey overcomes the limitations of traditional optical [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]], which suffer from severe bandpass shifting as the 4000 Å break moves into the near-infrared at $z > 1$. Because mid-infrared flux is relatively insensitive to recent star formation and dust extinction, it serves as a robust proxy for total stellar mass, enabling the uniform detection of thousands of high-redshift [[cluster-candidates|Cluster candidates]] across the extragalactic sky.

Subsequent multi-wavelength observations have validated the MaDCoWS selection methodology, confirming that these infrared-selected overdensities host hot, virialized intracluster gas halos. Mass calibrations using the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich (SZ) effect and cosmic microwave background (CMB) lensing demonstrate that the survey successfully detects the high-mass tail of the distant cluster population, identifying systems with typical masses exceeding $10^{14} M_\odot$ [Madhavacheril et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903L..13M/abstract). Consequently, the catalog has become a premier laboratory for studying [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] within extreme density peaks. Notably, MaDCoWS clusters exhibit a high prevalence of complex merger dynamics and [[cluster-substructure|Cluster substructure]], which correlate strongly with enhanced central radio and mid-infrared active galactic nuclei (AGN) activity. These findings challenge textbook models of the high-redshift red sequence as a uniformly quenched and passive population, suggesting instead that real-time cluster assembly directly regulates central black hole accretion and environmental quenching.

Despite its success in identifying massive distant clusters, the MaDCoWS sample presents notable tensions and open questions, particularly regarding its utility for precision cosmology. Follow-up SZ surveys indicate that the mid-infrared selection function is complex and incomplete relative to purely mass-selected samples, with a significant fraction of infrared candidates potentially representing lower-mass or unvirialized systems. Furthermore, observational discrepancies remain regarding the exact slope of the mass-[[optical-richness|Optical richness]] scaling relation, which appears flatter in some high-resolution SZ studies than previously assumed. To address these challenges and push the detection horizon further, the successor survey, MaDCoWS2, incorporates deeper optical and infrared data alongside advanced three-dimensional detection algorithms, successfully identifying clusters up to $z \sim 2$ [Thongkham et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967..123T/abstract). Future work will determine whether these next-generation methodologies can refine the selection function sufficiently to leverage these distant clusters for robust cosmological constraints.

## Survey Overview and Motivation

The Massive and Distant Clusters of WISE Survey (MaDCoWS) was motivated by the need to identify and characterize the most massive galaxy clusters during the peak epoch of cluster assembly. Tracing the evolution of large-scale structure at $0.7 \lesssim z \lesssim 1.5$ provides critical leverage for understanding the formation of massive halos and the environmental drivers of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. Historically, distant [[cluster-candidates|Cluster candidates]] were identified by searching for the optical red sequence of passive galaxies. However, as the 4000 $\mathrm{\AA}$ break shifts into the near-infrared at $z > 1$, optical-only surveys suffer from severe bandpass shifting and diminishing surface brightness, making all-sky cluster detection at high redshifts technically challenging. Concurrently, as astronomers anticipated huge catalogs of SZ-detected clusters from microwave missions like [[planck-sz-cluster-catalog|Planck SZ cluster catalog]], it was recognized that extensive follow-up would be necessary; early proposals strongly advocated for deep optical follow-up campaigns to provide host galaxy identifications and redshifts [Diego et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003RMxAC..16..233D/abstract). 

To overcome optical limitations and construct independent large-area catalogs, MaDCoWS utilizes mid-infrared imaging from the Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer (WISE) mission. The survey relies primarily on the $3.4\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and $4.6\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ (W1 and W2) bands, which effectively trace the total stellar mass of member galaxies and are largely insensitive to variations in star formation history [Gonzalez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..240...33G/abstract). This approach builds upon the foundational methodology for surface brightness overdensity searches established by earlier optical programs like the [[las-campanas-distant-cluster-survey|Las Campanas Distant Cluster Survey]] [Gonzalez et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000PhDT.......211G/abstract), but extends the search to a higher redshift range over the full extragalactic sky. 

The initial MaDCoWS search partitioned the sky into two primary regions based on the availability of supporting optical data: the Pan-STARRS footprint ($\delta > -30^\circ$) and the SuperCOSMOS region ($\delta < -30^\circ$). The survey's initial data release cataloged 2,433 high-amplitude cluster candidates in the Pan-STARRS region and 250 in the SuperCOSMOS region [Gonzalez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..240...33G/abstract). To secure the sample, extensive follow-up imaging was conducted using the Spitzer Space Telescope. These targeted observations provided richness estimates and photometric redshifts with an accuracy of $\sigma_z/(1+z) \simeq 0.036$ [Gonzalez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..240...33G/abstract), while early spectroscopic follow-up confirmed the viability of identifying massive, virialized systems out to $z \sim 1.3$ [Stanford et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..213...25S/abstract). 

Building upon the success of the first generation, the survey has been expanded as MaDCoWS2. This second iteration employs deeper optical data from the DECaLS survey and refined infrared photometry from the CatWISE2020 catalog. By deploying the three-dimensional PZWav overdensity algorithm, MaDCoWS2 has successfully pushed the detection horizon to $z \sim 2$ [Thongkham et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967..123T/abstract).

Despite its unprecedented success in locating massive clusters in the distant universe, follow-up observations utilizing the Sunyaev-Zeldovich (SZ) effect have revealed that the MaDCoWS infrared selection function is complex and the sample is not strictly complete relative to purely mass-selected catalogs [Orlowski-Scherer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A.135O/abstract). Consequently, researchers explicitly recommend utilizing the first-generation MaDCoWS sample primarily for investigations of cluster astrophysics, merger dynamics, and galaxy evolution, rather than for deriving tight constraints on cosmological parameters.

## Historical Development of Distant Cluster Searches

The systematic identification of galaxy clusters began with the visual inspection of photographic sky plates. This era culminated in the foundational [[abell-catalog-of-clusters|Abell Catalog of Clusters]], which identified massive systems based on local richness criteria. While these early optical catalogs were transformative—establishing a local cluster number density of $\sim 10^{-5} h^3 \text{ Mpc}^{-3}$—they were fundamentally limited to the relatively low-redshift Universe due to their reliance on manual identification and shallow optical depths.

To push cluster searches beyond $z \sim 0.3$, astronomers developed automated techniques that leveraged the characteristic photometric signatures and spatial distributions of cluster members. The [[las-campanas-distant-cluster-survey|Las Campanas Distant Cluster Survey]] (LCDCS) pioneered intermediate-to-high redshift cluster detection by searching for surface brightness overdensities of unresolved galaxies. Utilizing short-exposure drift-scan data, the LCDCS mapped an effective area of 69 square degrees and successfully identified over 1000 cluster candidates spanning $0.35 \lesssim z \lesssim 1.1$ [Gonzalez et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000PhDT.......211G/abstract), [Zaritsky et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..257..133Z/abstract). These early LCDCS results were particularly impactful, providing some of the first non-local constraints on the cluster-cluster spatial correlation function at $z \sim 0.5$ [Gonzalez et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10337G/abstract). 

The LCDCS was subsequently followed up by multi-band photometric programs such as the ESO Distant Cluster Survey (EDisCS), which confirmed and characterized these early candidate clusters out to $z \sim 1$ [White et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...444..365W/abstract). Concurrently, the [[red-sequence-cluster-survey|Red-sequence Cluster Survey]] (RCS) mapped 100 $\text{deg}^2$ by targeting the prominent 4000 Å break typical of the passive, early-type galaxies that dominate dense cluster environments [Gladders et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.11074G/abstract). Contemporaneously, other efforts such as the TexOx Cluster (TOC) survey utilized an entirely different multi-wavelength approach, finding [[radio-selected-galaxies|Radio-selected galaxies]] in the NVSS catalog to successfully identify massive clusters at $z \sim 1$ [Croft et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..268..353C/abstract). In parallel with these optical and radio searches, X-ray surveys became a vital tool. The [[massive-cluster-survey|MAssive Cluster Survey]] (MACS) emerged as a key predecessor, successfully identifying a complete sample of very X-ray luminous systems at $z > 0.5$ [Ebeling et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...661L..33E/abstract). This effort was later expanded by the extended Massive Cluster Survey ([[emacs|eMACS]]), which characterized extremely massive clusters in the challenging $z \sim 0.5 - 0.9$ regime [Ebeling et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240411659E/abstract). Additionally, near-infrared programs like the UltraVISTA survey provided deep imaging crucial for identifying remote galaxies and characterizing their environments at high redshifts [McCracken et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Msngr.154...29M/abstract).

Despite the success of these early automated surveys, identifying massive clusters at $z \gtrsim 1$ using optical data poses severe observational challenges. At these redshifts, the 4000 Å break shifts out of optical bandpasses and into the near-infrared. Furthermore, optical selections are highly sensitive to dust extinction and the evolving star formation histories of member galaxies. Because cluster populations evolve rapidly with redshift—exhibiting a significantly higher fraction of blue, star-forming galaxies at earlier cosmic times via the [[butcher-oemler-effect|Butcher-Oemler effect]]—a strict red-sequence color selection becomes increasingly incomplete at high redshift.

The advent of wide-field mid-infrared imaging provided a robust alternative. Early space-based campaigns using telescopes such as Spitzer demonstrated that mid-infrared observations could efficiently trace the total stellar mass of galaxies at $z > 1$. Because mid-infrared emission is dominated by the light of older stellar populations, it acts as a reliable proxy for stellar mass that is largely insensitive to both recent starburst activity and galactic extinction.

This methodological paradigm shift laid the groundwork for modern, all-sky mid-infrared searches. By utilizing the 3.4 $\mu$m and 4.6 $\mu$m bands of the Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer (WISE), MaDCoWS identifies overdensities of infrared-selected galaxies based on their total stellar mass, bypassing the limitations of optical selection [Gonzalez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..240...33G/abstract). This transition from localized optical surveys to all-sky mid-infrared mapping has enabled the uniform detection of thousands of massive galaxy clusters in the previously inaccessible $0.7 \lesssim z \lesssim 1.5$ regime [Stanford et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..213...25S/abstract), [Gonzalez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..240...33G/abstract).

## Selection Methodology and Catalog Construction

The Massive and Distant Clusters of WISE Survey (MaDCoWS) was designed to overcome the limitations of optical cluster searches, which struggle to trace the 4000 Å break in red-sequence galaxies at $z > 1$ due to bandpass shifting. Instead, MaDCoWS employs a mid-infrared color selection utilizing the 3.4 $\mu$m (W1) and 4.6 $\mu$m (W2) bands from the Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer (WISE). The transition from optical to infrared bands builds upon earlier foundational efforts, such as the Munich Near-Infrared Cluster Survey (MUNICS), an early $K'$-band selected survey designed specifically to identify clusters and early-type galaxies out to $z \sim 1.5$ [Drory et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.325..550D/abstract). Because mid-infrared flux in the WISE bands traces the total stellar mass of member galaxies and is relatively insensitive to recent star formation history, the $W1-W2$ color provides a robust discriminant for isolating overdensities of massive galaxies at $0.7 \lesssim z \lesssim 1.5$ over the full extragalactic sky [Gonzalez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..240...33G/abstract). The use of W1 and W2 as a proxy is underpinned by foundational empirical relations between mid-infrared WISE luminosities and optically determined stellar mass [Cluver et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782...90C/abstract).

In the first generation of the survey, the search area was partitioned into a northern and equatorial region cross-matched with Pan-STARRS optical data ($\delta > -30^\circ$) and a southern region utilizing shallower SuperCOSMOS data ($\delta < -30^\circ$) [Gonzalez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..240...33G/abstract). While early proof-of-concept efforts combined WISE with data from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey [Stanford et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..213...25S/abstract), the cornerstone of the final MaDCoWS catalog construction was its reliance on targeted follow-up imaging from the Spitzer Space Telescope. Spitzer IRAC photometry was obtained for thousands of the highest-amplitude [[cluster-candidates|Cluster candidates]] to provide high-precision photometric redshifts and [[optical-richness|Optical richness]] measurements (denoted $\lambda_{15}$). Spectroscopic calibration demonstrated that the Spitzer-derived photometric redshifts achieve a robust accuracy of $\sigma_z / (1+z) \simeq 0.036$ [Gonzalez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..240...33G/abstract).

Despite the success of this mid-infrared selection in identifying massive high-redshift clusters, independent follow-up observations using the SZ effect revealed that the MaDCoWS selection function is complex and incomplete with respect to a pure SZ mass selection [Orlowski-Scherer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A.135O/abstract). Because the selection function is not strictly well-defined for cosmological parameter constraints, the consensus in the literature explicitly recommends utilizing the first-generation MaDCoWS catalog primarily for studies of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] and intracluster astrophysics [Orlowski-Scherer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A.135O/abstract).

Theoretically, finding these high-redshift systems can be framed through statistical cosmology; modeling cluster overdensities as [[galaxy-overdensities|Galaxy overdensities]] helps predict the scale-dependent bias and traces the underlying process of structure formation [Desjacques et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvD..82j3529D/abstract). To push cluster detection to even higher redshifts, the survey methodology was substantially upgraded for the successor program, MaDCoWS2. This second-generation search replaces simple color-magnitude cuts with a systematic search for three-dimensional spatial overdensities, utilizing deeper imaging and full photometric redshift probability distribution functions to expand the survey's sensitivity to $z \sim 2$ [Thongkham et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967..123T/abstract).

## Mass Calibration and the Mass-Richness Relation

Determining the total mass of galaxy clusters at $z \gtrsim 1$ is observationaly challenging, requiring the cross-calibration of the survey's infrared-based [[optical-richness|Optical richness]] metric ($\lambda_{15}$) against secondary, multi-wavelength mass proxies. For MaDCoWS, mass calibration has primarily relied on the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich (SZ) effect and cosmic microwave background (CMB) lensing.

Initial validation of the MaDCoWS selection technique utilized targeted SZ observations. Follow-up with the CARMA 30 GHz array confirmed the presence of highly evolved, virialized systems with hot intracluster gas, yielding total masses in the range of $M_{200} \approx 2-6 \times 10^{14} M_\odot$ [Brodwin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806...26B/abstract). These measurements demonstrated that the survey was successfully detecting the high-mass tail of the $z \sim 1$ cluster population.

To establish a predictive mass-richness relation ($M-\lambda$), researchers have cross-matched the MaDCoWS catalog with wide-area SZ surveys. An analysis using Data Release 5 from the Atacama Cosmology Telescope (ACT) derived a power-law scaling relation between the SZ-derived mass and the MaDCoWS richness of $M \propto \lambda_{15}^{1.84_{-0.14}^{+0.15}}$ [Orlowski-Scherer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A.135O/abstract). However, the exact slope and scatter of this scaling relation remain a subject of active debate. High-resolution SZ follow-up using the MUSTANG2 receiver on the Green Bank Telescope suggests that the mass-richness relation may be significantly flatter than indicated by the ACT cross-calibration or earlier preliminary studies [Dicker et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..144D/abstract). These complexities in mass-richness scaling are echoed in other optical catalogs; for context, the HeCS-red survey of redMaPPer-selected clusters similarly revealed an intrinsic scatter of $\sim 24\%$ in the velocity dispersion-richness relation [Rines et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862..172R/abstract). Furthermore, massive SZ-selected clusters, such as those cataloged in the [[planck-sz-cluster-catalog|Planck SZ cluster catalog]] sample, are actively used to identify candidate [[brightest-group-galaxies|Brightest group galaxies]] (BCGs) for extensive multi-wavelength follow-up, providing a vital point of comparison for the central galaxies in infrared-selected MaDCoWS clusters [Smith et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023RNAAS...7...51S/abstract).

Furthermore, the ACT survey correlation revealed significant underlying complexities in the MaDCoWS selection function. The SZ signals for a substantial fraction ($\sim 57\%$) of the MaDCoWS [[cluster-candidates|Cluster candidates]] were statistically consistent with noise, implying that the infrared-selected catalog contains a large population of low-mass or unvirialized systems that fall below the ACT mass limit [Orlowski-Scherer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A.135O/abstract). Additionally, ACT-detected clusters in the same volume were frequently missed by MaDCoWS, demonstrating that the infrared selection is incomplete with respect to an SZ-selected sample [Orlowski-Scherer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A.135O/abstract). 

Because the high redshift of these clusters places them beyond the capabilities of current optical weak lensing measurements, CMB lensing has emerged as a critical alternative for mass calibration [Madhavacheril et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903L..13M/abstract). A stacked analysis of 677 MaDCoWS clusters at a mean redshift of $\langle z \rangle = 1.08$, using CMB maps from ACT and the Planck satellite, yielded a $4.2\sigma$ detection of the lensing convergence signal [Madhavacheril et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903L..13M/abstract). Assuming a Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) density profile, this lensing signature corresponds to a mean population mass of $\langle M_{500c} \rangle = (1.7 \pm 0.4) \times 10^{14} M_\odot$ [Madhavacheril et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903L..13M/abstract). In addition to SZ and lensing measurements, recent studies indicate that X-ray flux can also serve as a low-cost mass indicator for distant MaDCoWS clusters at $z \gtrsim 0.7$, provided a correction coefficient ($\eta \approx 0.8\text{--}1.1$) is applied to align with ACT-DR5 or weak-lensing masses [Lyskova et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250804400L/abstract).

Finally, deriving accurate SZ masses for high-redshift clusters requires careful modeling of point-source contamination. MaDCoWS clusters exhibit significant submillimeter emission, consistent with a gray-body spectrum originating from dusty star-forming member galaxies. If left uncorrected, this dust emission systematically infills the SZ decrement at millimeter wavelengths by 1% to 2%, potentially biasing the calibration of the mass-richness relation for next-generation experiments [Orlowski-Scherer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A.135O/abstract).

## Thermodynamics of the Intracluster Medium

High-resolution observations of the Intracluster Medium (ICM) in MaDCoWS clusters offer critical insights into the thermodynamic radial profiles and dynamical states of massive galaxy clusters at high redshift ($0.7 \lesssim z \lesssim 1.5$). Follow-up campaigns utilizing the Chandra X-ray Observatory and millimeter-wave facilities measuring the Sunyaev-Zeldovich (SZ) effect have successfully confirmed that these infrared-selected overdensities host hot, virialized gas halos [Brodwin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806...26B/abstract). Observations with arrays such as the Atacama Compact Array (ACA) further validate the presence of a collapsed ICM across the sample [Di Mascolo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...638A..70D/abstract).

Detailed spatial mapping of the ICM at $z > 1$ has been achieved by combining shallow X-ray data with high angular resolution SZ observations from instruments like MUSTANG-2 and NIKA2 [Ruppin et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022EPJWC.25700043R/abstract). For instance, MUSTANG-2 observations of the SZ decrement in a sample of high-redshift MaDCoWS clusters successfully resolved their radial pressure profiles. A notable fraction of these clusters exhibit significantly flatter pressure profiles than expected from local baseline models. Furthermore, while the clusters show widespread signs of disruption in their core regions, significant asymmetry is often restricted to the cluster centers rather than the outskirts [Dicker et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..144D/abstract). 

Thermodynamic maps reveal a high prevalence of complex merger dynamics and [[cluster-substructure|Cluster substructure]] among the MaDCoWS sample, pushing the peak epoch of cluster assembly to earlier times than suggested by models of the local universe. A striking example is MOO J1142+1527 at $z=1.2$. A joint pixel-level analysis of Chandra and NIKA2 SZ data enabled the extraction of temperature and entropy distributions up to $0.7 R_{500}$ with unprecedented precision at this redshift [Ruppin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...893...74R/abstract). The ICM of MOO J1142+1527 is characterized by a relatively low-entropy core at the X-ray emission peak, flanked by localized high-temperature regions to the south and west. When correlated with the infrared galaxy distribution, these thermodynamic features unequivocally identify the system as undergoing an active, major merger [Ruppin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...893...74R/abstract). Understanding the dynamics of these turbulent cores also requires considering fundamental plasma physics; for instance, the heat-flux-driven buoyancy instability (HBI) and magnetothermal instability (MTI) play crucial roles in regulating conductive heat flux and turbulence within the dilute plasma of the intracluster medium [McCourt et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.413.1295M/abstract).

To systematically quantify these dynamical states, researchers utilize X-ray morphological metrics such as photon asymmetry ($A_{\text{phot}}$). Analyses of MaDCoWS clusters demonstrate that the most dynamically disturbed systems, exhibiting high $A_{\text{phot}}$ values, are actively merging [Muhibullah et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250219535M/abstract). Furthermore, these disturbed ICM morphologies appear to influence the cluster galaxy populations directly; positive correlations have been observed between the dynamical disturbance of the ICM and an excess of central active galactic nuclei (AGN). This indicates that merger-driven shocks and gas flows may govern AGN triggering in high-redshift cluster environments [Muhibullah et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250219535M/abstract). 

## Galaxy Evolution and AGN Activity

The Massive and Distant Clusters of WISE Survey (MaDCoWS) provides a statistical sample of massive galaxy clusters at $0.7 \lesssim z \lesssim 1.5$, making it an ideal laboratory for studying [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] in the most extreme density peaks of the early universe. Studies of the MaDCoWS sample specifically trace the environmental impact on galaxy growth before the epoch of local cluster assembly is complete.

**Stellar Mass and Luminosity Functions**
Characterizing the galaxy populations of MaDCoWS clusters has yielded precise constraints on their $3.6\ \mu\text{m}$ luminosity functions. At a mean redshift of $\bar{z} = 1.18$, fits to a Schechter function yield a characteristic magnitude of $m^* = 19.83 \pm 0.12$ and a faint-end slope of $\alpha = -0.81 \pm 0.10$ [Decker et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...936...71D/abstract). Earlier measurements across similar redshift ranges found slightly brighter characteristic magnitudes of $m^* = 19.41 \pm 0.07$ [Decker et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...878...72D/abstract). Crucially, the redshift evolution of $m^*$ and $\alpha$ in these systems is consistent with the passive evolution of the red sequence, tracing the gradual buildup of faint cluster galaxies at $z \sim 1$ [Decker et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...936...71D/abstract).

Despite this passive stellar evolution, measuring the stellar mass fraction ($f_\star$) of MaDCoWS clusters reveals a negative correlation with total cluster mass [Decker et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...878...72D/abstract), [Decker et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...936...71D/abstract). This trend is further contextualized when comparing stellar mass fractions against massive SZ-selected systems, such as those from the South Pole Telescope (SPT)-SZ survey, which serves as a primary catalog source for these cross-population mass comparisons [Reichardt et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763..127R/abstract).

**AGN Activity and Environmental Quenching**
One of the most distinct signatures of environmental influence in the MaDCoWS sample is the spatial distribution and triggering of Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN). Stacked radial profiles demonstrate a significant overdensity of AGNs within $1'$ of the cluster center [Mo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869..131M/abstract). However, the literature reveals a stark dichotomy depending on the wavelength used for AGN selection, highlighting the complex nature of environmental quenching and triggering in dense environments.

While optically selected AGNs show a deficit in cluster cores compared to the field, mid-infrared (MIR) and radio-selected AGNs are strongly enhanced [Mo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869..131M/abstract). Most notably, radio-selected AGN activity in the centers of MaDCoWS clusters is more than $2.5$ times higher than field levels. This strong radio enhancement contradicts classical textbook models, which often treat the high-redshift cluster red sequence as a uniformly quenched and passive population devoid of significant nuclear activity.

Furthermore, this elevated AGN activity is directly linked to [[cluster-substructure|Cluster substructure]] and dynamical disturbance. X-ray morphological analyses of MaDCoWS clusters show a positive correlation (at $> 2\sigma$ significance) between cluster dynamical disturbance—quantified by photon asymmetry—and AGN surface density [Muhibullah et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250219535M/abstract). This suggests that ongoing mergers and the resulting shocks or gas flows in unrelaxed $z \sim 1$ clusters play a pivotal role in triggering central black hole accretion. Additionally, within the inner cluster regions ($r < r_{500}$), the space density of AGNs is found to scale inversely with total cluster mass, following $\sim M^{-0.5}$ [Muhibullah et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250219535M/abstract). Consequently, the MaDCoWS sample indicates that both the cluster mass and its real-time assembly state fundamentally regulate AGN activity during the peak epoch of structure formation.

## MaDCoWS2 and Next-Generation Extensions

The Massive and Distant Clusters of WISE Survey 2 (MaDCoWS2) is the direct successor to the original MaDCoWS program, designed to push the boundaries of infrared-selected galaxy cluster discovery to higher redshifts and lower mass limits. While the first generation of MaDCoWS successfully identified massive overdensities up to $z \sim 1.5$, its successor leverages significantly deeper multi-wavelength datasets and refined detection techniques to extend the search volume up to $z \sim 2$ [Thongkham et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967..123T/abstract). While MaDCoWS2 reaches $z \sim 2$, it is worth noting that earlier targeted searches for structures associated with [[radio-selected-galaxies|Radio-selected galaxies]] at $2 < z < 4$ paved the way for identifying the earliest [[galaxy-overdensities|Galaxy overdensities]], contextualizing the high-redshift reach of modern blind surveys [Kurk et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..268...23K/abstract).

To achieve this extended reach, MaDCoWS2 integrates deep $g$-, $r$-, and $z$-band optical imaging from the DECam Legacy Survey (DECaLS) with ultra-deep $W1$ and $W2$ mid-infrared photometry from the CatWISE2020 catalog. This combination allows for the derivation of highly accurate photometric redshifts—complete with full probability distribution functions (PDFs)—for galaxies initially selected in the WISE bands. 

The core of the MaDCoWS2 methodology relies on the PZWav detection algorithm. Unlike earlier cluster-finding methods that frequently relied on simple color-magnitude cuts or two-dimensional spatial overdensities, PZWav actively searches for three-dimensional galaxy overdensities by synthesizing both the angular sky positions and the full photometric redshift PDFs of the source galaxies [Thongkham et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967..123T/abstract). This approach minimizes line-of-sight projection effects and significantly improves the robustness of high-redshift cluster candidate identification. This advanced approach can be compared to contemporary techniques like the CluMPR (Clusters from Masses and Photometric Redshifts) algorithm, which similarly leverages WISE mid-infrared imaging and photometric stellar masses to identify clusters in the $0.75 < z < 1$ regime [Yantovski-Barth et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.2285Y/abstract), as well as the WaZP algorithm, which recently processed data from the Dark Energy Survey (DES-Y6) to successfully identify massive clusters up to $z \sim 1.3$ [Benoist et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250705360B/abstract).

In its Equatorial First Data Release, MaDCoWS2 mapped an effective unmasked area of $1461$ deg$^2$ centered on the [[hyper-suprime-cam-surveys|Hyper Suprime-Cam Surveys]] equatorial fields. This initial release yielded a catalog of 22,970 galaxy cluster candidates detected at a signal-to-noise ratio of $\text{S/N} > 5$. The identified clusters span a broad redshift range of $0.1 < z < 2.0$. Crucially, the deeper optical and infrared data allowed the survey to successfully identify 1,312 cluster candidates at $z > 1.5$, opening a critical observational window into the epoch of early cluster assembly [Thongkham et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967..123T/abstract).

Extensive cross-matching with six existing cluster surveys demonstrates the high reliability of the MaDCoWS2 selection. The survey successfully recovered $60\%$ to $92\%$ of previously known clusters in the target region at $\text{S/N} > 5$. The photometric redshifts derived for the MaDCoWS2 sample exhibit a median absolute offset of $< 0.02$ relative to these legacy surveys, with a standard deviation of $< 0.06$. Furthermore, the physical centering of the clusters is precise, with median spatial offsets of less than $0.25$ Mpc between the MaDCoWS2 coordinates and those of independent catalogs [Thongkham et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967..123T/abstract). 

Analyses of redshift-dependent power-law scaling relations in the MaDCoWS2 sample reveal that the algorithm's detection significance ($\text{S/N}$) correlates well with cluster gas mass, total mass, luminosity, and richness. Among these, the $\text{S/N}$-richness relation exhibits the lowest intrinsic scatter [Thongkham et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967..123T/abstract). 

Despite these methodological advancements, an open question remains regarding the utility of the catalog for precision cosmology. Earlier studies of the first-generation MaDCoWS sample highlighted that the infrared selection function was not sufficiently well-defined for cosmological parameter estimation due to incompleteness relative to Sunyaev-Zeldovich selections [Orlowski-Scherer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A.135O/abstract). While the enhanced depth and the PZWav algorithm of MaDCoWS2 drastically improve catalog purity and redshift precision, the community is actively investigating whether the $z > 1.5$ sample is sufficiently complete and unbiased to serve as a standalone probe for high-redshift cosmological constraints, or if it will primarily remain a premier laboratory for studying [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] and extreme cluster assembly.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the Massive and Distant Clusters of WISE Survey (MaDCoWS) has proven highly effective for identifying [[cluster-candidates|Cluster candidates]] at $z > 1$, transitioning its catalog from a tool for studying [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] to one suitable for precision cosmology presents significant challenges. Several open questions remain regarding the survey's completeness, mass calibration, and the astrophysical nature of its high-redshift detections.

A primary obstacle for deriving robust cosmological constraints is the poorly defined survey selection function. As noted previously, follow-up observations with the Atacama Cosmology Telescope (ACT) revealed that the MaDCoWS sample is incomplete relative to Sunyaev-Zeldovich (SZ) selected catalogs, with a significant fraction of candidates potentially representing low-mass or unvirialized systems that fall below the SZ detection threshold [Orlowski-Scherer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A.135O/abstract). Consequently, researchers currently recommend that the MaDCoWS catalog be restricted to structural and evolutionary studies rather than tests of cosmological models.

Related to these selection issues is an ongoing debate regarding mass-calibration bias. Establishing a precise mass-[[optical-richness|Optical richness]] ($M-\lambda$) scaling relation is critical, yet the literature exhibits notable tension. While initial survey calibrations and ACT SZ follow-up suggest a standard power-law slope of $M \propto \lambda_{15}^{1.84}$ [Gonzalez et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..240...33G/abstract), [Orlowski-Scherer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A.135O/abstract), high-resolution targeted observations using the MUSTANG2 receiver on the Green Bank Telescope indicate that the mass-richness relation may be significantly flatter than previously thought [Dicker et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..144D/abstract). Resolving this discrepancy requires deeper multi-wavelength follow-up to untangle intrinsic scatter from observational biases. Characterizing systematic uncertainties in mass measurements also remains a key area for future investigation; for example, dust emission from star-forming member galaxies introduces submillimeter contamination that can infill the SZ decrement by 1% to 2% [Orlowski-Scherer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A.135O/abstract).

Astrophysically, the environmental drivers of active galactic nuclei (AGN) within these $z \sim 1$ systems are not fully understood. There is a confirmed correlation between cluster dynamical disturbance—such as mergers and [[cluster-substructure|Cluster substructure]]—and enhanced AGN surface densities [Muhibullah et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250219535M/abstract). However, it remains an open question why radio- and mid-infrared-selected AGNs show strong central enhancements (up to 2.5 times the field density) while optically selected AGNs exhibit a deficit relative to the field [Mo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869..131M/abstract). Furthermore, the generation of diffuse radio emission in these high-redshift clusters pushes the limits of current detection capabilities. Initial results matching the MaDCoWS sample to the LOFAR Two-Meter Sky Survey (LoTSS) suggest that $z \gtrsim 1$ systems with diffuse radio emission may lie on or above local scaling relations for radio halos, potentially implying rapid magnetic field amplification during early cluster assembly, though deeper observations are required to confirm this trend [Di Gennaro et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A.215D/abstract).

Future directions rely heavily on expanded and deeper datasets. The next-generation MaDCoWS2 survey leverages deeper optical photometry from DECaLS and updated infrared catalogs (CatWISE2020) to push the detection boundary to $z \sim 2$ [Thongkham et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967..123T/abstract). A central question for MaDCoWS2 and subsequent [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]] will be whether refined optical-infrared selection algorithms can finally yield a sufficiently pure and complete sample to unlock the $z > 1.5$ regime for precision cosmology. In this context, the upcoming Euclid Catalogue of Galaxy Clusters (ECGC) will be transformative; the rigorous methodology developed for validating the ECGC using external multi-wavelength datasets will be essential for cross-calibrating and solidifying the cosmological utility of high-redshift samples like MaDCoWS2 [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250906805E/abstract).
