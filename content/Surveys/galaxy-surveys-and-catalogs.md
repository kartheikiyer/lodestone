## Article

**Galaxy surveys** are systematic observational campaigns that map expansive volumes of the sky to produce comprehensive catalogs of extragalactic sources. By collecting multi-band [[observational-photometry|Observational Photometry]], precise [[redshift-catalogs|Redshift catalogs]], and detailed [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]], these surveys transform two-dimensional sky projections into three-dimensional [[galaxy-mapping|Galaxy mapping]] of the universe. Historically evolving from early photographic compilations of "nebulae" to massively multiplexed [[spectroscopy-surveys|Spectroscopy surveys]], these datasets are the empirical foundation of modern [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]]. They are essential for visualizing the "Cosmic Web"—the vast network of high-density filaments, galaxy clusters, and underdense voids that characterizes the large-scale structure of the universe and traces the underlying [[galaxy-density-fields|Galaxy density fields]] [Lahav et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004LRR.....7....8L/abstract).

In cosmology, [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]] serve as the primary tool for testing the $\Lambda$CDM concordance model. By analyzing [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|Galaxy Bias and Clustering Statistics]] in large-volume samples, researchers can extract cosmological standard rulers like Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO) to measure the Hubble constant and constrain the dark energy equation of state [Lahav et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002astro.ph.12358L/abstract), [Ivanov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230507977I/abstract). Beyond fundamental physics, these surveys are critical for tracing the chemical, dynamical, and morphological evolution of [[galaxy-populations|Galaxy populations]] across cosmic time. Deep [[panchromatic-surveys|Panchromatic surveys]] spanning radio, infrared, and X-ray wavelengths allow astrophysicists to penetrate dust obscuration, trace [[mass-assembly-survey|Mass Assembly Survey]], and characterize the environments of massive [[cluster-candidates|Cluster candidates]] [Postman et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.11247P/abstract). Through these comprehensive datasets—such as those pioneered by the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]] and the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] [Colless et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RSPTA.357..105C/abstract)—researchers track how galaxies evolve, highlighting the complex interplay between the older bottom-up hierarchical merger paradigm and the observed "downsizing" of baryonic mass assembly [Sakr et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014sf2a.conf..367S/abstract). 

Constructing and analyzing modern surveys requires overcoming significant methodological challenges, particularly concerning [[photometry-and-redshift-estimation|Photometry and Redshift Estimation]] for billions of faint sources and the mitigation of [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]], such as gravitational lensing-induced size bias. To interpret complex spatial clustering signals and clean raw data, modern survey pipelines increasingly rely on sophisticated machine learning algorithms for [[star-galaxy-classification|Star-galaxy classification]] and advanced simulated mock catalogs to produce meticulously calibrated datasets. Historically, foundational work like the [[new-york-university-value-added-galaxy-catalogue|New York University Value-Added Galaxy Catalogue]] achieved this standard through rigorous cross-survey homogenization and manual quality checks [Blanton et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AJ....129.2562B/abstract). As the field transitions to Stage IV [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]]—driven by facilities like the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, the Euclid space mission, and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope—researchers aim to map the universe to unprecedented depths. These next-generation catalogs seek to resolve outstanding open questions, including the potential epoch-dependence of dark energy, the origin of large-scale bulk flows, and the precise timeline of the first galaxies forming during the epoch of reionization.

## Historical Evolution of Galaxy Mapping

The systematic mapping of the universe is a relatively modern scientific endeavor, evolving from rudimentary lists of "nebulae" into precise, multi-dimensional maps of cosmic structure. The earliest compilations of galaxies did not identify them as such; between 1771 and 1784, Charles Messier compiled a list of over 100 fuzzy, non-stellar sources—now known as the [[messier-objects|Messier Objects]]—primarily to prevent astronomers from confusing them with comets. It was not until the 1920s, following Edwin Hubble's measurement of Cepheid variable stars in the Andromeda galaxy, that the extragalactic nature of these nebulae was conclusively established.

Following this paradigm shift, the mid-20th century saw the initiation of systematic, wide-area photographic plate surveys. These efforts produced landmark two-dimensional maps of the projected galaxy distribution. Compilations such as the [[zwicky-catalogue|Zwicky Catalogue]] (formally the Catalog of Galaxies and of Clusters of Galaxies) and the [[shane-wirtanen-catalog|Shane-Wirtanen catalog]] provided the first deep, statistical samples of the nearby universe. Early spatial clustering analyses of these catalogs applied the angular two-point correlation function, $w(\theta)$, to infer the underlying 3D spatial correlation function, $\xi(r)$. Such analyses demonstrated that the surveyed volume acted as a "fair sample" of the universe and revealed that galaxies are non-randomly clustered, with correlation lengths extending to roughly $20h^{-1} \text{ Mpc}$ [Peebles et al. (1974)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1974ApJS...28...19P/abstract). Furthermore, cross-correlation of the [[shane-wirtanen-catalog|Shane-Wirtanen catalog]] with rich galaxy clusters—such as those identified via visual inspection in the [[abell-catalog-of-clusters|Abell Catalog of Clusters]]—established that galaxies exhibit a measurable long-range spatial correlation with massive cluster environments [Peebles et al. (1974)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1974ApJS...28...37P/abstract).

While these early 2D catalogs were foundational, they were fundamentally limited by line-of-sight projection effects. The critical transition to [[three-dimensional-galaxy-surveys|Three-dimensional galaxy surveys]] began in the late 1970s and 1980s with the advent of systematic spectroscopic campaigns, most notably the [[cfa-redshift-survey|CfA Redshift Survey]]. By measuring the redshift $z$ of thousands of galaxies, astronomers could estimate radial distances via the Hubble relation, $v = c z \approx H_0 d$, allowing for the construction of 3D spatial maps. 

The [[cfa-redshift-survey|CfA Redshift Survey]] and contemporary programs like the Southern Sky Redshift Survey (SSRS) fundamentally altered the cosmological paradigm. Instead of a smooth or simple Gaussian distribution of matter, these surveys revealed that the universe is organized into a highly structured "cosmic web" characterized by high-density filaments, sheets, and expansive underdense voids. Quantitative analyses of these early 3D catalogs also firmly established the principle of large-scale morphological segregation, demonstrating that elliptical and spiral galaxies cluster differently—with ellipticals preferentially residing in denser environments—on scales up to $15-20h^{-1} \text{ Mpc}$ in the CfA catalog, and up to $20-30h^{-1} \text{ Mpc}$ in the SSRS catalog [Dominguez-Tenreiro et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...424L..73D/abstract). Later efforts, such as the final redshift release of the [[6df-galaxy-survey|6dF Galaxy Survey]], mapped southern large-scale structures at $z < 0.1$ and provided over 110,000 new extragalactic redshifts, further cementing our detailed understanding of the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]]. Together, these historical milestones transitioned observational cosmology from an era of simple angular number counts to the precision mapping of large-scale structure, laying the essential groundwork for modern, massively multiplexed [[spectroscopy-surveys|Spectroscopy surveys]].

## Multi-Wavelength Observational Strategies

Single-band optical surveys are fundamentally limited by dust obscuration and morphological selection biases, such as size and magnification bias. To construct complete, unbiased representations of the universe, modern observational strategies increasingly rely on [[panchromatic-surveys|Panchromatic surveys]] that span the full electromagnetic spectrum, capturing a diverse range of physical mechanisms and hidden populations.

**Infrared and Optical Synergy**
Near- and mid-infrared observations are crucial for obtaining a dust-penetrated view of stellar mass assembly. For ultra-high-resolution studies of the immediate local universe, the [[angst-survey|ANGST survey]] (ACS Nearby Galaxy Survey Treasury) has provided a legacy archive of multi-color photometry for resolved stars in nearby galaxies within 4 Mpc [Dalcanton et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJS..183...67D/abstract). Moving to broader scales, the [[two-micron-all-sky-survey-2mass|Two Micron All-Sky Survey (2MASS)]] provided a foundational near-infrared census, achieving completeness down to a magnitude of $K=13.5$ for nearly a million galaxies [Huchra et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IAUS..216..170H/abstract). This allowed astronomers to map large-scale structures behind the Milky Way's dusty disk, traditionally known as the Zone of Avoidance [Kraan-Korteweg et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..170..103K/abstract). Additionally, the AKARI all-sky far-infrared survey provides crucial local benchmarks of star formation in thousands of resolved [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|Local Volume and Nearby Galaxy Studies]] [Serjeant et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PKAS...27..305S/abstract). For detailed investigations of nearby sources, the Siena Galaxy Atlas (SGA-2020) serves as a key multi-wavelength resource, covering 20,000 square degrees with highly precise coordinates and robust surface-brightness profiles for large galaxies [Moustakas et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..269....3M/abstract). However, photometric completeness at very low Galactic latitudes can still be heavily impacted by stellar confusion and structured infrared background emission. For example, UV-excited polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) emission produces bright, complex structures that significantly reduce point-source sensitivity in programs like the Spitzer Space Telescope GLIMPSE surveys [Kobulnicky et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJS..207....9K/abstract).

Deeper, targeted infrared campaigns, such as the Spitzer South Pole Telescope Deep Field (SSDF), have pushed detection thresholds significantly further, cataloging over $5.5 \times 10^6$ distinct sources across $94\ \mathrm{deg}^2$ down to $5\sigma$ limits of $7.0\ \mu\mathrm{Jy}$ at $3.6\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ [Ashby et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..212...16A/abstract). Fusing these deep infrared datasets with optical imagery substantially enhances [[photometry-and-redshift-estimation|Photometry and Redshift Estimation]]. For example, the 2MPZ catalog cross-matched 2MASS, WISE, and SuperCOSMOS data to derive highly accurate photometric redshifts for one million galaxies at a median redshift of $z \sim 0.1$, enabling robust all-sky clustering analyses [Bilicki et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016pas..conf..165B/abstract). Furthermore, cross-identifying ultraviolet sources from GALEX with [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] optical observations has proven essential to refine band-merging strategies and create robust statistical catalogs for luminosity function studies [Budavári et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...694.1281B/abstract). For broad transient host identification and multi-messenger astrophysics, modern compilations like the REGALADE catalog provide a high-completeness all-sky database of 80 million galaxies extending out to 2,000 Mpc [Tranin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250813267T/abstract). For even deeper probes into the early universe, programs like the UltraVISTA survey provide a premier near-infrared census, tracing stellar masses out to $z \sim 4$ and measuring the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] out to $z \sim 7$ [McCracken et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Msngr.154...29M/abstract). Complementing these efforts, the Spitzer Extended Deep Survey (SEDS) utilizes IRAC observations to provide a deep census of stellar mass assembly and black hole growth extending back to the era of reionization [Fazio et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..446..347F/abstract).

**Radio and Non-Thermal Probes**
[[radio-surveys|Radio surveys]] provide an entirely dust-unbiased census of star formation and active galactic nucleus (AGN) activity [Smith et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASPC..507..373S/abstract). Deep centimeter- and millimeter-wave observations isolate non-thermal synchrotron emission and thermal dust continuum. These regimes are indispensable for identifying high-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies and flat-spectrum sources that are frequently absent from or misrepresented in rest-frame ultraviolet and optical catalogs [De Zotti et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002astro.ph..4038D/abstract). Importantly, below flux densities of 0.1 mJy at 1.4 GHz, star-forming galaxies begin to dominate the radio source counts, allowing deep radio surveys to effectively trace the strong evolution of the cosmic star-formation rate out to $z \sim 1$ [Condon et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASPC..380..189C/abstract). To map these non-thermal indicators across significant cosmic volumes, the 10-square-degree deep L-band survey with the Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array (JVLA) serves as a key resource for creating a comprehensive census of star-formation and AGN activity out to $z \sim 6$ [Jarvis et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1401.4018J/abstract).

**X-ray and Sunyaev-Zel'dovich Catalogs**
At high energies, [[x-ray-surveys|X-ray surveys]] utilizing observatories like Chandra, XMM-Newton, and eROSITA excel at identifying obscured AGN populations, cataclysmic variables, and the hot intracluster medium (ICM) of galaxy groups and clusters [Xue et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..224...15X/abstract), [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250410794W/abstract). 

For large-scale structure and cosmological studies, multi-wavelength approaches are the gold standard for defining [[cluster-candidates|Cluster candidates]]. Clusters can be systematically detected via their X-ray bremsstrahlung emission or the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect (the inverse Compton scattering of cosmic microwave background photons by the hot ICM). Because the SZ surface brightness is remarkably independent of distance, SZ surveys are uniquely capable of cleanly selecting massive clusters out to $z > 1$ [Bartlett et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..6241B/abstract). Combining optical, near-infrared, and X-ray or SZ data is critical to accurately quantify and correct for the selection biases inherent in any single-band cluster survey [Postman et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.11247P/abstract). Notably, cross-correlating the [[planck-sz-cluster-catalog|Planck SZ cluster catalog]] with ROSAT X-ray catalogs has permitted large-scale measurements of the kinetic SZ effect. Current kinematic measurements of large-scale "bulk flows" utilizing these multi-wavelength cluster catalogs remain entirely consistent with the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological paradigm [Mody et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...758....4M/abstract).

## Redshift Determination and Estimation Techniques

The precise determination of cosmic distances is a cornerstone of [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], enabling the transition from two-dimensional sky maps to three-dimensional [[galaxy-mapping|Galaxy mapping]]. The methodology for measuring these distances ranges from high-resolution [[spectroscopy-surveys|Spectroscopy surveys]] to statistical estimation techniques designed for large-volume, flux-limited samples.

**Spectroscopic Redshifts**
The most precise distance measurements rely on dispersing a galaxy's light to identify specific bound-bound atomic transitions, such as $H\alpha$ and [O II] emission lines, or the $4000\ \mathring{A}$ break. Spectroscopic redshifts ($z_{\text{spec}}$) typically achieve relative errors of $\Delta z / (1+z) < 0.1\%$. This level of precision is essential for mapping linear-regime clustering, characterizing [[redshift-space-correlation-function|Redshift-space correlation function]], and measuring Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO) to constrain the Hubble constant, $H_0$ [Ivanov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230507977I/abstract). Major [[spectroscopy-surveys|Spectroscopy surveys]], such as the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (SDSS) and the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]], have accumulated millions of spectra [Colless et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RSPTA.357..105C/abstract). These extensive datasets also allow for advanced analyses; for example, the 2dFGRS employed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to robustly classify galaxy spectra into a continuous measure of spectral type [Madgwick et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10220M/abstract). Building upon this historical legacy, modern milestones like the eBOSS-DAP release provide uniform emission-line and stellar population measurements for 1.9 million galaxies out to $z < 1.12$, offering unparalleled spectroscopic precision for vast statistical samples [Matthews Acuña et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251218076M/abstract). More recent campaigns extend this reach by systematically targeting emission-line galaxies out to $z \sim 1.6$ [Raichoor et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....165..126R/abstract). Beyond single-fiber spectroscopy, modern integral-field spectroscopic (IFS) surveys like the SAMI Galaxy Survey have provided spatially resolved spectral cubes for over 3,000 galaxies, enabling astronomers to meticulously map their internal kinematics and stellar populations [Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..991C/abstract). However, spectroscopy is fundamentally limited by integration time and signal-to-noise requirements, restricting highly complete spectroscopic catalogs to relatively bright targets.

**Photometric Redshifts**
For galaxies too faint for spectroscopic follow-up, distances are estimated using photometric redshifts (photo-$z$). This approach leverages [[broadband-selection|Broadband selection]] and [[photometry-and-redshift-estimation|Photometry and Redshift Estimation]] across multiple filters to coarsely sample a galaxy's spectral energy distribution (SED). Through [[template-fitting|Template fitting]]—comparing observed fluxes against empirical or synthetic spectra—or machine learning algorithms, surveys can rapidly estimate redshifts for millions of sources. 

While efficient, photometric redshifts are inherently less precise than their spectroscopic counterparts, with typical uncertainties ranging from $3\%$ to $10\%$. Errors are strongly correlated with spectral type; star-forming galaxies, which lack a pronounced $4000\ \mathring{A}$ break, suffer from higher uncertainties than passive galaxies with older stellar populations. Utilizing narrower and more numerous filter bands can mitigate this; for instance, the [[combo-17-survey|COMBO-17 survey]] achieved photo-$z$ accuracies of $\sim 3\%$ using 17 optical bands. Looking forward, innovative projects like the J-PAS survey bridge the gap between photometry and spectroscopy by utilizing 56 narrow-band filters. This approach effectively provides low-resolution spectra, enabling highly accurate photometric redshifts across 8,000 square degrees [Sodré et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AIPC.1471...22S/abstract). Still, there is a recognized tension in the literature regarding the calibration of photo-$z$ distributions. Upcoming Stage IV surveys require an order-of-magnitude improvement in photometric redshift characterization to meet their dark energy precision targets [Newman et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ARA&A..60..363N/abstract). Furthermore, there is an ongoing debate regarding whether incorporating ultraviolet photometry meaningfully improves photo-$z$ accuracy for specific populations, or if such theoretical information gains are offset by stringent UV detection limits [Scott et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985..227S/abstract).

**Color Selection and Dropout Techniques**
At higher redshifts ($z > 3$), specific [[color-selection|Color selection]] techniques are employed to identify target candidates. The most prominent is the "dropout" technique used to identify [[break-selected-galaxies|Break-selected galaxies]]. Star-forming galaxies exhibit a relatively flat SED that drops precipitously at the Lyman limit ($\lambda \approx 912\ \mathring{A}$) due to the large ionization cross-section of neutral hydrogen in both stellar atmospheres and the intervening intergalactic medium. As this break is redshifted through successive photometric filters, the galaxy "drops out" (becomes undetectable) in bluer bands while remaining bright in redder bands. This technique provides a robust pre-selection for high-redshift populations and has been instrumental in pushing the observable frontier. Recent applications of this method using JWST/NIRCam imaging in the [[jades|JADES]] program have successfully identified galaxy candidates extending out to $z_{\text{phot}} \sim 18$ [Hainline et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964...71H/abstract).

## Galaxy Clusters and Groups as Cosmological Probes

Galaxy clusters and groups represent the most massive virialized objects in the Universe. With a typical number density of $\sim 10^{-5} h^{3} \text{ Mpc}^{-3}$ in the local Universe, [[abell-catalog-of-clusters|Abell Catalog of Clusters]] trace the peaks of the cosmic density field. Their abundance as a function of mass and redshift provides a sensitive probe of the growth of structure, yielding critical constraints on the matter density parameter $\Omega_M$ and the dark energy equation of state $w(z)$ [Mohr et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..257...49M/abstract). Furthermore, systematic surveys of these systems allow astronomers to test the volume-redshift relation and the power spectrum of density fluctuations on the largest observable scales.

Historically, systematic searches for [[cluster-candidates|Cluster candidates]] began with visual inspections of photographic plates, most notably culminating in the [[abell-catalog-of-clusters|Abell Catalog of Clusters]], which utilized strict criteria for local galaxy density and richness. The transition to three-dimensional cluster studies was accelerated by pioneering systematic spectroscopic campaigns—such as the [[cnoc-survey|CNOC survey]] (Canadian Network for Observational Cosmology)—which detailed the dynamics and mass distribution of rich clusters like Abell 2390 at intermediate redshifts [Yee et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJS..102..289Y/abstract). Detailed multi-wavelength investigations, such as the Space Telescope A901/2 Galaxy Evolution Survey (STAGES), further link [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] with [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] and stellar mass across complex cluster environments at intermediate epochs ($z \sim 0.165$) [Gray et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.393.1275G/abstract). On smaller, internal cluster scales, high-resolution imaging such as the ACS Virgo Cluster Survey utilizes King model fitting to systematically identify and characterize globular cluster candidates residing within [[early-type-fraction|Early-type fraction]], further linking cluster environments to dense star cluster formation [Jordán et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJS..180...54J/abstract). Modern optical and near-infrared cluster finders heavily rely on the presence of early-type galaxies to identify clusters. Methods based on the [[red-sequence-cluster-survey|Red-sequence Cluster Survey]] approach look for simultaneous enhancements in both spatial position and multi-band colors [Thanjavur et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...706..571T/abstract). For example, the K2 algorithm applied to Canada-France-Hawaii Telescope Legacy Survey data achieves a false detection rate of just $\sim 1\%$ and is $\sim 80\%$ complete up to $z = 0.6$ for Fornax-like and richer clusters [Thanjavur et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...706..571T/abstract). Increasingly, machine learning techniques such as the COSMIC algorithm are being deployed to efficiently identify the brightest cluster galaxies and estimate cluster richness from vast photometric datasets [Tian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241020083T/abstract).

Because optical searches can suffer from projection effects and dust obscuration, complementary methods utilizing X-ray emission and the Sunyaev-Zeldovich (SZ) effect are essential for constructing unbiased samples [Postman et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.11247P/abstract). The SZ effect is particularly powerful because its surface brightness is largely independent of distance, allowing surveys to cleanly select clusters out to $z > 1$ [Bartlett et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..6241B/abstract). Catalogs such as the [[planck-sz-cluster-catalog|Planck SZ cluster catalog]] provide robust mass proxies and help mitigate the selection biases inherent in single-wavelength studies. Statistically, because clusters sparsely sample the density field, researchers can construct nearly exact likelihood functions for cluster surveys, which are highly accurate for halo masses above several times $10^{14} h^{-1} M_\odot$ at $z=0$ [Holder et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..2251H/abstract).

Despite the successes of cluster cosmology in reinforcing the $\Lambda$CDM concordance model, the literature reflects several observational tensions and ambiguities. Notably, there is a well-documented difference in the correlation lengths between individual galaxies and rich clusters [Peebles et al. (1974)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1974ApJS...28...19P/abstract). The spatial clustering of these objects can be described by the two-point correlation function, typically parameterized as a power law on scales $\lesssim 20 \ h^{-1} \text{ Mpc}$:
$$ \xi(r) = \left( \frac{r}{r_0} \right)^{-\gamma} $$
where the correlation length $r_0$ for rich clusters is significantly larger than that of individual galaxies, reflecting the strong morphological and mass segregation inherent in hierarchical structure formation. Furthermore, measurements of large-scale "bulk flows" using clusters as tracers of peculiar velocities have occasionally conflicted. While some early predictions anticipated substantial bulk flows, recent analyses of the kinetic SZ effect using X-ray catalogs and the [[planck-sz-cluster-catalog|Planck SZ cluster catalog]] find no significant detection of a bulk flow, remaining entirely consistent with $\Lambda$CDM predictions [Mody et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...758....4M/abstract).

## Large-Scale Structure and Clustering Statistics

Large galaxy redshift surveys, such as the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]] and the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]], provide three-dimensional maps of the universe that reveal the underlying dark matter density field. The spatial distribution of galaxies—often referred to as the "Cosmic Web"—is quantified using robust statistical measures to test the $\Lambda$CDM concordance model and constrain the dark energy equation of state [Lahav et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004LRR.....7....8L/abstract).

The fundamental statistic for analyzing the spatial distribution of galaxies is the two-point correlation function, $\xi(r)$, which quantifies the excess probability over a random distribution of finding a pair of galaxies separated by a comoving distance $r$. It is often evaluated using data and random pair counts via variants of the [[peebles-estimator|Peebles estimator]]:
$$ \xi(r) = \frac{DD(r)}{RR(r)} - 1 $$
where $DD(r)$ is the number of observed galaxy pairs and $RR(r)$ is the expected number of pairs for a purely random distribution. Because peculiar velocities distort radial distances, astronomers frequently calculate the projected correlation function, $w(r_p)$, to integrate out the effects of the [[redshift-space-correlation-function|Redshift-space correlation function]] and retrieve real-space clustering. For optically selected $L^*$ galaxies on scales $r < 20\ h^{-1}$ Mpc, the real-space correlation function is well-approximated by a simple power law. 

Because galaxies form within the peaks of the dark matter density field, their distribution is a biased tracer of the underlying mass, a concept central to [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|Galaxy Bias and Clustering Statistics]]. The linear bias parameter $b$ relates the galaxy overdensity to the matter overdensity via:
$$ \delta_{\rm gal} = b \, \delta_{m} $$
Observations demonstrate that galaxy clustering is strongly dependent on intrinsic properties like luminosity, color, and morphology. Red, early-type galaxies exhibit higher clustering amplitudes and are preferentially found in dense environments compared to blue, star-forming galaxies. This morphological segregation is detectable up to scales of $20-30\ h^{-1}$ Mpc [Dominguez-Tenreiro et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...424L..73D/abstract). On large linear scales ($> 10\ h^{-1}$ Mpc), the bias for bright, optically selected galaxies approaches $b \approx 1$ [Lahav et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ntto.conf..145L/abstract). Recent precision analyses of the DESI Legacy Surveys Data Release 8 highlight how intrinsic clustering strongly depends on luminosity and color, further detailing the environmental biases inherent between red and blue galaxy subsamples on both small and large scales [Wang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021SCPMA..6489811W/abstract). In addition to high-density environments, tracing cosmic voids is critical for a complete picture of large-scale structure. The recent release of low-redshift void catalogs from the DESI Data Release 1 Bright Galaxy Survey (DESIVAST) provides a crucial tool for studying galaxy evolution within these massive underdense environments [Rincon et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982...38R/abstract).

In Fourier space, the power spectrum of density fluctuations provides complementary constraints. The detection of Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO) within the power spectrum serves as a critical cosmological standard ruler. By measuring the physical scale of the sound horizon at recombination, spectroscopic surveys extract precise values for the Hubble parameter ($H_0$) and the dark energy equation of state ($w$), which currently remain consistent with a cosmological constant ($w \approx -1$) [Lahav et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002astro.ph.12358L/abstract), [Ivanov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230507977I/abstract). On the largest cosmic scales, advanced statistical frameworks such as the pseudo Karhunen-Loève method are employed to properly account for wide-angle effects and anisotropic survey selection functions [Xavier et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...04..037X/abstract).

Interpreting these statistics requires careful mitigation of [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]]. Surveys lacking spectroscopic depth must instead measure angular clustering, relying on sophisticated [[photometry-and-redshift-estimation|Photometry and Redshift Estimation]] to jointly model the projected two-point correlation function and photometric redshift uncertainties [Wang et al. ## Galaxy Evolution and Stellar Mass Assembly

By analyzing deep multi-wavelength redshift catalogs across various cosmic epochs, researchers can trace the history of star formation, the growth of stellar mass, and the [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] transformation of galaxies over cosmic time. The statistical backbone of this evolutionary picture is the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and its mass-weighted counterpart, the [[stellar-mass-function|Stellar Mass Function]]. Observational estimates of the local stellar mass function—often derived from near-infrared data, such as [[k-selected-surveys|K-selected surveys]], where the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]] varies by less than a factor of two—are well-described by a [[schechter-function|Schechter function]] with a characteristic mass of $M^\ast \sim 5 \times 10^{10} h^{-2} M_{\odot}$ and a faint-end slope of $\alpha \sim -1.1$. Furthermore, modern surveys allow researchers to look beyond simple one-dimensional luminosity functions to analyze bivariate distributions, such as the luminosity-surface brightness plane, which is crucial for distinguishing between bulge and disk formation processes [Driver et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASA...21..344D/abstract). To properly extract these parameters, projects like MegaMorph have driven efforts to develop tools for the automated decomposition of spheroids and disks across large multi-band imaging surveys [Bamford et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..442..479B/abstract).

Tracking the luminosity function and [[stellar-mass-function|Stellar Mass Function]] out to $z \sim 1$ and beyond reveals divergent evolutionary paths for different [[galaxy-populations|Galaxy populations]]. Spectroscopic and photometric surveys indicate that the total stellar mass density within the quiescent, red galaxy population has roughly doubled since $z \sim 1$, while the mass density of the star-forming, blue population has remained largely constant. This implies a continuous morphological transformation wherein star-forming galaxies are quenched and migrate onto the red sequence. Interestingly, while the older hierarchical mass merger paradigm dictates a bottom-up assembly of dark matter halos, observations strongly support a "downsizing" scenario for the baryonic component: the most massive galaxies formed their stars rapidly and early in the Universe ($z > 2$), whereas star formation in lower-mass systems peaks at later epochs [Feulner et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ralc.conf..310F/abstract). Reconciling the hierarchical merger and downsizing paradigms into a single, self-consistent model remains an active area of theoretical and observational integration [Sakr et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014sf2a.conf..367S/abstract).

The cosmic star-formation history is typically quantified by the global star-formation rate density, $\dot{\rho}_{\star}(z)$. Extensive multi-wavelength surveys confirm that $\dot{\rho}_{\star}(z)$ peaked at $z \sim 2$ (often called "cosmic noon") and has since dropped by roughly an order of magnitude to the present day. Detailed studies using multi-object spectroscopy, such as the [[lega-c-survey|LEGA-C survey]], trace the internal kinematics and stellar population ages of massive galaxies during this decline ($0.6 < z < 1.0$), providing a high-precision benchmark for the transition from active starbursts to quiescent systems [van der Wel et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..256...44V/abstract). Furthermore, panchromatic imaging of intermediate-redshift early-type galaxies—such as those in the [[goods-survey|GOODS survey]] South field—reveals substantial diversity in rest-frame UV-optical colors. This diversity indicates low-level star formation episodes during the preceding gigayear, despite their seemingly quiescent morphology [Rutkowski et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJS..199....4R/abstract).

At higher redshifts ($z > 3$), [[galaxy-identification|Galaxy identification]] increasingly relies on [[color-selection|Color selection]] techniques, such as the Lyman-break "drop-out" method, to isolate star-forming systems from intervening foreground objects. Lyman-break galaxies at $z \sim 5$ display significant luminosity-dependent evolution when compared to lower-redshift samples, providing critical insights into the early buildup of stellar mass [Iwata et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..235..364I/abstract). Recently, the observational frontier has been extended dramatically into the epoch of reionization by space-based near-infrared observations. Programs such as [[jades|JADES]] have leveraged ultra-deep imaging to construct catalogs of galaxy candidates out to photometric redshifts of $z_{\mathrm{phot}} \sim 18$, uncovering the earliest stages of stellar mass assembly within the first few hundred million years of cosmic history [Hainline et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964...71H/abstract).

## Data Processing, Machine Learning, and Mock Catalogs

Modern survey science relies heavily on automated data processing and machine learning to construct robust catalogs for [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]]. As sample sizes grow to the millions, algorithmic [[star-galaxy-classification|Star-galaxy classification]] and decontamination become essential. Machine learning models, such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), are effective at separating stars from quasars, achieving classification accuracies of 91-100% using photometric color-color data [Makhija et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&C....2900313M/abstract). Furthermore, deep learning via Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) is highly effective at identifying image artifacts; for instance, CNNs can flag "galaxy shreds"—star-forming regions erroneously deblended as distinct sources—with $\sim 98\%$ accuracy across large datasets like the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] and the DESI Legacy Surveys [Di Teodoro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....165..123D/abstract). Historically, algorithmic interventions have also been required to identify active galaxies; for example, weak-lined AGN in the SDSS were frequently misclassified due to host galaxy light pollution, requiring algorithmic corrections to properly identify passive systems harboring radio or X-ray activity [Reviglio et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0902.1766R/abstract). CNNs are also increasingly utilized for complex morphological classification, having successfully assembled catalogues of over 62,000 ringed galaxies with 97% accuracy [Chen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250707552C/abstract). Complementing automated machine learning, crowdsourced visual classifications remain highly impactful; projects like Galaxy Zoo: Hubble have provided detailed, human-verified morphologies for 120,000 galaxies using HST legacy imaging [Willett et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.4176W/abstract).

To maximize scientific utility, raw pipeline outputs are often combined with multi-wavelength observations to produce value-added catalogs. Constructing reliable large-scale structure (LSS) catalogs from these vast datasets demands rigorous technical pipelines; for instance, the DESI survey's LSS pipeline meticulously accounts for varying observing history and hardware performance to model survey completeness at sub-arcsecond precision [Ross et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240516593R/abstract). A foundational example of value-added synergy is the [[new-york-university-value-added-galaxy-catalogue|New York University Value-Added Galaxy Catalogue]] (NYU-VAGC), which matched SDSS imaging and spectroscopy with data from the [[two-micron-all-sky-survey-2mass|Two Micron All-Sky Survey (2MASS)]] and various radio surveys. By strictly controlling cross-survey homogenization, the NYU-VAGC reduced systematic calibration errors across the sky from $\sim 2\%$ to $\sim 1\%$ and calculated derived [[rest-frame-galaxy-properties|Rest-frame galaxy properties]] critical for clustering statistics [Blanton et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AJ....129.2562B/abstract). More recently, one of the most extensive examples of this is the RCSEDv2 catalog, which operates as the largest homogeneously processed database uniting spectral and photometric analyses from ten major surveys [Chilingarian et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ASPC..535..179C/abstract).

Validating cosmological models and characterizing [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]] requires the generation of highly sophisticated mock catalogs. Algorithms like ADDGALS empirically populate galaxies into the lightcone outputs of moderate-resolution $N$-body simulations, successfully reproducing projected clustering statistics down to non-linear scales of $r \sim 100 \ h^{-1}\mathrm{kpc}$ [Wechsler et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931..145W/abstract). Other advanced mock frameworks, such as the Cardinal catalogs, utilize updated subhalo abundance matching to populate quarter-sky simulations to depths of $m_r = 27$ out to $z = 2.35$ [To et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961...59T/abstract). To avoid the computational expense of traditional subhalo finders on massive simulations, models like SMACC instead track halo cores and model mass-loss directly [Sultan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913..109S/abstract). To further simulate survey systematics, researchers utilize open-source Python tools like GLACiAR, which estimates source recovery and completeness to construct precise [[selection-function|Selection function]]—particularly crucial for high-redshift Lyman-break surveys [Carrasco et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...22C/abstract).

Despite these algorithmic advances, the literature highlights ongoing tensions in mock catalog generation. Standard population methods often suffer from artificial subhalo disruption within the simulations, leading to unresolved discrepancies in cluster-galaxy cross-correlations when compared directly to observational data [Wechsler et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931..145W/abstract). High-fidelity mock catalogs also reveal subtle observational biases that must be carefully modeled. For example, gravitational lensing induces a "size bias"—where lensed galaxies scatter across both magnitude and size selection cuts—that can impact deep survey statistics more severely than the well-known magnification bias [Schmidt et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvL.103e1301S/abstract). Finally, to control for varying observing conditions and on-sky modulations, researchers are transitioning away from traditional regressions against ad hoc foreground maps, favoring new methodologies that extract and mitigate spurious correlations directly from stacks of single-epoch exposures [Eggert et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...30E/abstract).

## Open Questions and the Future of Stage IV Surveys

The next generation of [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]], commonly referred to as Stage IV galaxy surveys, is poised to map the observable universe with unprecedented depth, volume, and precision. Facilities such as the Vera C. Rubin Observatory (conducting the [[legacy-surveys|Legacy surveys]] of Space and Time; LSST), the Euclid space mission, and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will collect data on billions of sources [Newman et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ARA&A..60..363N/abstract). By combining deep [[observational-photometry|Observational Photometry]] and extensive [[spectroscopy-surveys|Spectroscopy surveys]], these observatories aim to address foundational open questions in cosmology and galaxy evolution.

**Cosmology and Dark Energy**
A primary driver for Stage IV surveys is the precise measurement of the dark energy equation of state parameter, $w$. While prior [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] programs have found clustering patterns and expansion histories broadly consistent with a cosmological constant ($w = -1$), it remains an open question whether $w$ is epoch-dependent, as proposed in quintessence models [Lahav et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002astro.ph.12358L/abstract). Upcoming surveys will utilize standard rulers like baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO) and weak gravitational lensing to constrain the growth of structure. However, to meet the stringent precision requirements for dark energy constraints, the characterization of the [[redshift-distribution|Redshift distribution]] for photometric samples must be improved by roughly an order of magnitude compared to the current state of the art [Newman et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ARA&A..60..363N/abstract). Another outstanding cosmological problem is the exact quantitative nature of the "bulk flow" of galaxies relative to the cosmic microwave background dipole, and whether large-scale velocity fields perfectly align with standard $\Lambda$CDM predictions on all scales [Mody et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...758....4M/abstract). Another exciting new frontier is the emerging use of 3D galaxy clustering from spectroscopic surveys to model and infer the astrophysical host properties of gravitational wave sources, expanding the interdisciplinary reach of these immense datasets [Sadat Hosseini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250611201S/abstract).

**High-Redshift Galaxies and Reionization**
Beyond dark energy, upcoming deep surveys will push the frontier of [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] into the epoch of reionization. Recent discoveries have identified candidate galaxies extending out to $z \sim 18$ [Hainline et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964...71H/abstract), prompting new questions about the rapid assembly of the first massive structures. Stage IV missions will provide the large-area, statistically complete samples needed to definitively resolve the tension between the hierarchical merging paradigm (where mass assembly is driven by dark matter halo mergers) and the "downsizing" scenario (where the most massive galaxies formed their stars earliest and most rapidly), seeking to unify them into a single, self-consistent model of galaxy formation [Sakr et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014sf2a.conf..367S/abstract). To provide a fully holistic picture of the early universe, an emerging frontier highlighted for the coming decade involves the powerful synergy between these deep near-infrared galaxy surveys and upcoming 21-cm measurements, which together will map both the luminous galaxies and the neutral intergalactic medium during the epoch of reionization [Furlanetto et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190306197F/abstract).

**Systematics and Methodological Hurdles**
To harness the full statistical power of these [[panchromatic-surveys|Panchromatic surveys]], significant methodological challenges must be overcome. Single-survey photometric redshift estimates are inherently limited. While it is broadly accepted that optical data from LSST will benefit heavily from the addition of near-infrared coverage from Euclid and Roman, the degree to which ultraviolet photometry improves redshift determination for specific galaxy populations is still being quantified [Scott et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240920443S/abstract), [Scott et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985..227S/abstract). 

Furthermore, systematic biases can severely distort measurements of [[galaxy-density-fields|Galaxy density fields]]. For instance, size bias—where galaxies appear larger and fainter due to gravitational lensing—can have a dominant and often neglected impact on survey statistics, potentially exceeding the effects of magnification bias in deep datasets [Schmidt et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvL.103e1301S/abstract). To interpret clustering signals and mitigate these [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]], researchers are increasingly relying on advanced mock catalogs (such as the Cardinal and Buzzard simulations) [To et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961...59T/abstract). Additionally, machine learning techniques, such as convolutional neural networks, are being deployed to clean large datasets, achieving $>97\%$ accuracy in removing "galaxy shreds" and false detections that traditional automated pipelines frequently misclassify [Di Teodoro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....165..123D/abstract).
