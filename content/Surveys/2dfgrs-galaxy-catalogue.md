The **2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey (2dFGRS)** is a landmark [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] that utilized the two-degree field (2dF) multi-fibre spectrograph on the 3.9-meter [[anglo-australian-telescope|Anglo-Australian Telescope]] to map the [[three-dimensional-galaxy-surveys|three-dimensional spatial distribution]] of galaxies in the local Universe. Conducted between 1997 and 2002, the survey obtained high-quality [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]]s for approximately 221,000 galaxies down to an extinction-corrected limit of [[b-j-magnitude|b_J magnitude]] $b_J = 19.45$, reaching a median depth of $z \approx 0.11$ [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract), [Colless et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph..6581C/abstract). Operating contemporaneously with the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (SDSS), the 2dFGRS represented a paradigm shift in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]]. It transitioned the field away from sparsely sampled [[pencil-beam-surveys|Pencil-beam surveys]] toward the era of large-volume, high-density mapping necessary for precision cosmology. Its [[target-selection|Target selection]] was based on a revised version of the [[apm-galaxy-catalogue|APM galaxy catalogue]], allowing for highly uniform spatial sampling across 2,000 square degrees of the sky.

The unparalleled statistical power of the 2dFGRS catalogue provided foundational constraints on the $\Lambda$CDM cosmological model and the nature of the cosmic web. By precisely measuring the large-scale galaxy power spectrum, the survey yielded one of the first robust detections of baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO) and [[redshift-space-correlation-function|linear redshift-space distortions]] (the Kaiser effect) [Cole et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.362..505C/abstract), [Hawkins et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.346...78H/abstract). Furthermore, by analyzing [[high-order-galaxy-statistics|higher-order clustering statistics]] to break the historical degeneracy between the matter density parameter ($\Omega_m$) and the linear galaxy bias ($b$), the survey provided direct evidence that optically selected $L^*$ galaxies act as nearly unbiased tracers of the underlying dark matter distribution on linear scales, ultimately estimating $\Omega_m \approx 0.27$ [Verde et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.335..432V/abstract). The survey also rigorously detailed [[low-redshift-large-scale-structure|Low-redshift large-scale structure]], facilitating the construction of the Percolation-Inferred Galaxy Group (2PIGG) catalogue and characterizing the topology of cosmic voids and intercluster filaments.

In addition to cosmological parameters, the 2dFGRS critically advanced the understanding of how [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] and environments influence structure formation. The dataset revealed a strong dependence of clustering amplitude on both intrinsic luminosity and spectral type, demonstrating that early-type and highly luminous [[galaxy-populations|Galaxy populations]] are more strongly clustered than their faint, star-forming counterparts. Despite its successes, the survey's findings introduced several persistent theoretical tensions. Open questions remain regarding a surprising U-shaped dependence of pairwise velocity dispersion on luminosity—where faint galaxies exhibit relative velocities as high as the most massive systems—which challenges standard monotonic halo occupation models. Additionally, discrepancies between observed filament connectedness and mock catalogues, and the discovery of a massive $\sim 30\%$ local underdensity in the Southern Galactic Cap (the "Local Hole") continue to motivate ongoing research in modern [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]].

## Historical Development and Context

Before the late 1990s, the study of the large-scale structure of the Universe relied heavily on two-dimensional photometric [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]] or relatively small, sparsely sampled three-dimensional programs. Prior to modern digital surveys, the Third Reference Catalogue of Bright Galaxies ([[rc3-catalog|RC3 catalog]]) served as a standard compilation for over 23,000 systems [de Vaucouleurs et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991rc3..book.....D/abstract). Early observational efforts, including narrow [[pencil-beam-surveys|Pencil-beam surveys]] and the [[las-campanas-redshift-survey|Las Campanas Redshift Survey]], provided crucial initial insights into the cosmic web and galaxy clustering. The ESO Slice Project (ESP) served as a key precursor, mapping a 23 square degree region near the South Galactic Pole to $b_J = 19.4$ and providing a bridge between shallow wide-angle surveys and deep pencil-beams [Vettolani et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&AS..130..323V/abstract). The FLAIR Shapley-Hydra (FLASH) survey was another important precursor that mapped the Supergalactic plane and the Shapley Supercluster over 700 square degrees [Kaldare et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.339..652K/abstract). Early systematic searches for clusters were performed using the machine-based Edinburgh-Durham Cluster Catalogue (EDCC), which provided an objective sample of clusters over the South Galactic Pole [Lumsden et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992MNRAS.258....1L/abstract). Another significant precursor was the Durham/UKST Galaxy Redshift Survey, which utilized the FLAIR multi-object spectrograph to map the local distribution to a magnitude limit of $B=17$ over the South Galactic Pole [Ratcliffe et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.300..417R/abstract). However, these surveys lacked the volume and density required to fully constrain theoretical models; consequently, cosmological parameters were largely derived from 2D maps or limited spatial samples.

The initiation of the 2dFGRS, operating concurrently with the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (SDSS), marked a profound transition in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]]. Together, these projects inaugurated the era of large-volume spectroscopic mapping. By acquiring a high-resolution 3D map of the local Universe, the 2dFGRS became the first survey to probe a statistically representative volume on scales large enough to directly test theories of gravitational instability and cold dark matter structure formation [Colless et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004mmu..symp..196C/abstract). 

To achieve this unprecedented scale, the 2dFGRS leveraged pre-existing wide-field imaging for its target selection. The source catalogue for the survey was constructed from a revised and extended version of the [[apm-galaxy-survey|APM Galaxy Survey]], which was based on Automated Plate Measuring machine scans of photographic plates [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract). Target selection for 2dFGRS also drew from the [[edinburgh-durham-southern-galaxy-catalogue|Edinburgh Durham Southern Galaxy Catalogue]] (EDSGC), one of the largest digitized surveys of its time containing nearly 1.5 million galaxies [Nichol et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph..8184N/abstract). By selecting targets with an extinction-corrected magnitude limit of $b_J = 19.45$, the survey team achieved highly uniform sampling over a 2,000 square degree area, eventually yielding reliable [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]]s for approximately 221,000 galaxies [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract), [Colless et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph..6581C/abstract).

This dramatic leap—from samples of a few thousand to hundreds of thousands of galaxies—ushered in the era of precision cosmology. Prior to the 2dFGRS, galaxy bias was often treated as a simple, constant factor, and the limited scale of surveys restricted high-order statistical analyses. The dense, high-volume data of the 2dFGRS allowed researchers to measure the bispectrum and [[redshift-space-correlation-function|redshift-space distortions]] with sufficient statistical power to definitively break the historical degeneracy between the matter density parameter, $\Omega_m$, and the linear galaxy bias parameter, $b$ [Verde et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.335..432V/abstract). This provided some of the first direct, independent constraints on the matter density of the Universe and paved the way for modern precision cosmology [Colless et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004mmu..symp..196C/abstract).

## Survey Design and Instrumentation

The observational campaign for the 2dFGRS relied on the 2dF multi-fibre spectrograph mounted on the 3.9-meter [[anglo-australian-telescope|Anglo-Australian Telescope]] (AAT) [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract), [Colless et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RSPTA.357..105C/abstract). The 2dF instrument was specifically designed to enable high-multiplex [[spectroscopy-surveys|spectroscopy]] over a wide field of view, capable of observing up to 400 objects simultaneously across a $2^\circ$ diameter field [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract).

[[target-selection|Target selection]] for the primary sample consisted of galaxies with an extinction-corrected limiting [[b-j-magnitude|b_J magnitude]] of $b_J = 19.45$ [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract), [Colless et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph..6581C/abstract). At this photometric depth, the survey achieved a median redshift of $z \approx 0.11$ [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract). The total survey area encompassed approximately 2000 square degrees, organized into three distinct regions: an $80^\circ \times 15^\circ$ contiguous strip in the southern Galactic hemisphere around the South Galactic Pole (SGP), a $75^\circ \times 10^\circ$ strip in the northern Galactic hemisphere along the celestial equator, and 99 pseudo-random fields scattered around the SGP strip [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract).

The spectrograph acquired optical spectra spanning a wavelength range from $3600 \text{ \AA}$ to $8000 \text{ \AA}$ [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract). The spectrograph optics provided a two-pixel resolution of $9.0 \text{ \AA}$. Typical integrations yielded a median signal-to-noise ratio of $13 \text{ pixel}^{-1}$, well-suited for robust [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]] determination and automated spectral classification [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract). 

A defining feature of the survey's observational strategy was its use of adaptive tiling. Because galaxies are strongly clustered on the sky—residing in dense groups, clusters, and filamentary structures—a rigid, non-overlapping grid of circular $2^\circ$ pointings would lead to significant inefficiency. To overcome this, the adaptive tiling algorithm dynamically optimized the placement and overlap of the field pointings to closely match the local target density. This strategy enabled a highly uniform spatial sampling rate of 93% across the entire survey footprint [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract).

Following data acquisition, all redshift identifications were visually inspected and assigned a quality parameter, $Q$, ranging from 1 to 5. Spectra yielding $Q \ge 3$ were deemed highly reliable; they demonstrated a 98.4% reliability rate and a root-mean-square (rms) velocity uncertainty of $85 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract). Overall, the survey achieved a spectroscopic redshift completeness of 91.8% for $Q \ge 3$ sources. This completeness exhibited a slight dependence on the apparent magnitude of the source, ranging from 99% for the brightest galaxies down to 90% near the survey limit [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract). Subsequent comparisons with the deeper [[millennium-galaxy-catalogue|Millennium Galaxy Catalogue]] revealed that the 2dFGRS contains approximately 5.2% stellar contamination and may miss up to 14.3% of the galaxy population due to misclassification or surface brightness limits [Cross et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.349..576C/abstract). Further comparisons with the Las Campanas/AAT Rich Cluster Survey (LARCS) CCD imaging identified four distinct populations of galaxies, including low surface brightness systems, that were systematically omitted from the original 2dFGRS photometric parent catalogue [Pimbblet et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.327..588P/abstract). Systematic errors in 2dFGRS luminosity measurements were linked to these surface brightness selection effects, which were eventually addressed through deeper photometric surveys like the INT-WFS [Cross et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..232..181C/abstract). To confidently analyze [[galaxy-clustering-and-redshift-estimation|clustering statistics]] from this extensive dataset without shape distortions caused by the magnitude-limited selection, the standard FKP Fourier analysis method was extended to successfully incorporate luminosity-dependent bias weights [Percival et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.347..645P/abstract).

## Spectral Classification and Galaxy Properties

To leverage the unprecedented volume of spectroscopic data, researchers required an automated and robust method for spectral classification. Rather than relying on individual equivalent widths or broad-band colors, the survey employed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to parameterize the entirety of each optical spectrum. Early analyses used the two most significant principal components to divide the sample into discrete spectral classes [Folkes et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.308..459F/abstract). This approach was subsequently refined to define a continuous measure of spectral type, which proved highly robust against instrumental uncertainties and variations in signal-to-noise [Madgwick et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001misk.conf..331M/abstract). 

The continuous spectral parameter, denoted as $\eta$, is defined by a linear combination of the projections onto the first two principal components ($pc_1$ and $pc_2$):
$$ \eta = 0.44 pc_1 - pc_2 $$
This $\eta$ parameter effectively measures the relative strength of stellar absorption features versus nebular emission lines in a galaxy's spectrum, serving as a reliable proxy for the current star formation rate. The distribution of $\eta$ across the survey revealed a pronounced bimodality in local [[galaxy-populations|Galaxy populations]], cleanly separating quiescent "early-type" galaxies from star-forming "late-type" [[emission-line-galaxies|Emission-line galaxies]] [Madgwick et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10220M/abstract). 

Parameterizing spectra in this way was crucial for accurately measuring the fundamental statistical distributions of galaxies. Because the survey spans a significant redshift range, accurate k-corrections are essential for calculating absolute magnitudes. By dividing the survey according to rest-frame color and $\eta$, researchers deduced a self-consistent treatment of k-corrections and evolutionary effects for each sub-population, which was necessary to accurately define the survey's [[selection-function|Selection function]] [Cole et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.362..505C/abstract). 

Applying this classification to the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] revealed that early- and late-type galaxies populate distinct mass regimes. The overall, global $z=0$ sample is well-described by a [[schechter-function|Schechter function]] with a characteristic magnitude of $M^* = -19.66$ and a faint-end slope of $\alpha = -1.21$ [Norberg et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.336..907N/abstract). Later, deeper calibrations utilizing the Millennium Galaxy Catalogue played a key role in renormalizing the 2dFGRS luminosity function $\phi^*$ value by a factor of $1.05 \pm 0.05$ [Liske et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.344..307L/abstract). When divided by spectral class, quiescent (early-type) galaxies have a brighter characteristic magnitude ($M^* \approx -19.6$) and a relatively flat faint-end slope ($\alpha \approx -0.7$). In contrast, star-forming (late-type) galaxies have a fainter characteristic magnitude ($M^* \approx -19.0$) and a much steeper faint-end slope ($\alpha \approx -1.7$) [Folkes et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.308..459F/abstract). Furthermore, detailed explorations of these properties revealed that the luminosity functions of first-ranked (brightest) group galaxies depend strongly on the global environment, exhibiting a distinctive lower limit in supercluster cores [Tempel et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...495...37T/abstract).

The $\eta$ parameter enabled precise measurements of how galaxy clustering depends on intrinsic [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]]. Analysis of the projected two-point correlation function in real space demonstrated that early-type galaxies are significantly more clustered than late-types. At a given luminosity, the clustering amplitude for early-type galaxies is approximately 50% higher than that of late-type galaxies [Norberg et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.332..827N/abstract). Both spectral types exhibit a similar dependence on luminosity; for both populations, the clustering strength increases substantially at the bright end, with the correlation amplitude rising by a factor of $\sim 2.5$ between $L^*$ and $4L^*$ galaxies [Norberg et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.332..827N/abstract).

## Large-Scale Structure and Topology

The 2dFGRS provides a high-resolution three-dimensional map of the local universe, capturing the intricate topology and connectedness of the cosmic web. By applying a two-pass friends-of-friends algorithm to identify connected structures, researchers extracted 7,603 systems with a mean redshift of $z < 0.12$ [Murphy et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.413.2288M/abstract). Extending to even larger scales, a continuous density field smoothing method was utilized to construct the 2dFGRS supercluster catalogue, identifying 543 distinct supercluster systems within the survey volume [Einasto et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007A&A...462..811E/abstract). Analysis of intercluster filaments across 805 cluster-cluster pairs demonstrated that at least 40% of such pairs are connected by bona fide filaments. These structures exhibit diverse morphologies, categorized visually as straight (37%), warped or curved (33%), irregular (26%), and planar sheets (3%) [Pimbblet et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.354L..61P/abstract). However, there is active tension in the literature regarding whether standard $\Lambda$CDM models can fully replicate these features. Topological analyses utilizing object point processes indicate that numerical mock catalogs frequently fail to reproduce the extended, highly connected filamentary network observed in the real data, instead yielding model filaments that are statistically too short [Stoica et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...510A..38S/abstract).

To quantitatively measure the topology of this [[low-redshift-large-scale-structure|Low-redshift large-scale structure]], the three-dimensional genus statistic has been applied to volume-limited samples. On scales ranging from $8$ to $20~\mathrm{Mpc}$, the measured genus curve is consistent with a Gaussian random density field, satisfying a generic prediction of inflationary cosmology. Asymmetries in the genus number as a function of density are ruled out with 95% confidence between $8$ and $16~\mathrm{Mpc}$ [James et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375..128J/abstract).

The network of filaments inherently bounds large cosmic voids, whose spatial distribution is strictly constrained by the void probability function (VPF). Measurements of the redshift-space reduced VPF in the 2dFGRS data show excellent agreement with the paradigm of hierarchical scaling of galaxy clustering moments out to at least $20~h^{-1}~\mathrm{Mpc}$. This precision explicitly rules out the lognormal distribution model for galaxy clustering at high significance [Croton et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.352..828C/abstract). Furthermore, [[redshift-space-correlation-function|redshift-space distortions]] around these underdense regions provide direct dynamical evidence for their continuing growth, with maximum outflow velocities estimated between $110$ and $270~\mathrm{km~s}^{-1}$ for void radii of $7.5$ to $17.5~h^{-1}~\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Ceccarelli et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.373.1440C/abstract). 

A major topological anomaly observed in the survey is a massive underdensity in the Southern Galactic Cap, where galaxy counts exhibit a $\sim 30\%$ deficiency extending to $z \approx 0.1$ [Busswell et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.354..991B/abstract), [Frith et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..329...49F/abstract). The existence of this "Local Hole" remains an active debate, with interpretations split between a statistical sampling fluke, scale-dependent bias effects, or an unexpected excess of large-scale power that might challenge standard $\Lambda$CDM models.

Beyond two-point measurements, the detailed connectedness of the density field is evaluated through [[high-order-galaxy-statistics|High-order galaxy statistics]], most notably the [[high-order-galaxy-statistics|three-point correlation function (3PCF)]]. The 3PCF amplitude in the 2dFGRS decreases with triangle size and increases with the shape parameter $v$ [Jing et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...607..140J/abstract). Surprisingly, the measured 3PCF is consistently lower than the predictions of standard dark matter models. While $L^*$ galaxies act as nearly unbiased tracers on linear scales, the most luminous galaxies exhibit a linear bias factor of approximately $1.5$. The overall clustering requires refinements to simple dark matter biasing schemes to properly reconcile hierarchical structure formation theories with the lower observed 3PCF amplitudes [Jing et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...607..140J/abstract).

## Cosmological Parameters from the Power Spectrum

The 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey provided one of the first statistically robust measurements of the galaxy power spectrum over a large cosmological volume, firmly establishing the concordance cold dark matter (CDM) paradigm. By analyzing the clustering of optically selected galaxies, the survey yielded direct, independent constraints on the matter density parameter, $\Omega_m$. When large-scale measurements of the [[redshift-space-correlation-function|redshift-space distortion]] parameter ($\beta$) were combined with linear bias values derived directly from the survey's bispectrum, it yielded a direct measurement of $\Omega_m = 0.27 \pm 0.06$ at the survey's effective redshift of $z \approx 0.17$ [Verde et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.335..432V/abstract), [Verde et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002astro.ph.12311V/abstract). Complementing traditional Fourier techniques, a spherical harmonics analysis of the fluctuations in the final catalogue provided a robust linear model for redshift-space distortions, yielding the combined cosmological constraint $\Omega_m^{0.6}\sigma_8 = 0.46 \pm 0.06$ [Percival et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.353.1201P/abstract).

A landmark achievement of the 2dFGRS was the detection of baryon oscillations in the large-scale power spectrum. Analysis of the final dataset of 221,414 galaxies revealed characteristic modulations in the power spectrum that are a signature of acoustic waves in the early Universe's baryon-photon plasma [Cole et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.362..505C/abstract). Fitting the power spectrum to a CDM model—assuming a primordial scalar spectral index $n_s = 1$ and a Hubble parameter $h = 0.72$—the survey constrained the shape parameter to $\Omega_m h = 0.168 \pm 0.016$ and measured the cosmic baryon fraction to be $\Omega_b/\Omega_m = 0.185 \pm 0.046$ [Cole et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.362..505C/abstract). When combined with cosmic microwave background data from the WMAP satellite, this further refined the global matter density to $\Omega_m = 0.231 \pm 0.021$ [Cole et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.362..505C/abstract).

Initial comparisons between the power spectrum of the 2dFGRS and early results from the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (SDSS) revealed an apparent tension, with the 2dFGRS exhibiting greater large-scale power than the SDSS [Cole et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASPC..379...57C/abstract). Subsequent analyses demonstrated that this discrepancy arises primarily from differences in the photometric selection bands—the [[b-j-magnitude|b_J magnitude]] for 2dFGRS versus the $r$-band for SDSS—and the varying clustering properties of different [[galaxy-populations|Galaxy populations]]. The SDSS $r$-band sample is dominated by red galaxies, which exhibit a stronger, scale-dependent bias compared to the blue galaxies more prevalent in the 2dFGRS selection [Sánchez et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.385..830S/abstract). While selecting samples designed to have the same rest-frame colors yields excellent agreement between the two surveys, analytical corrections that attempted to model the distortion in the shape of the power spectrum due to non-linear evolution and scale-dependent bias on the full samples were found to be insufficient to completely reconcile the cosmological constraints [Cole et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASPC..379...57C/abstract), [Sánchez et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.385..830S/abstract).

## Redshift-Space Distortions and Peculiar Velocities

The use of redshift as a proxy for distance in the 2dFGRS introduces systematic shifts in the observed galaxy distribution, known as redshift-space distortions. These distortions arise from the peculiar velocities of galaxies, which superimpose Doppler shifts on the uniform cosmological expansion. By analyzing the anisotropy of the [[redshift-space-correlation-function|Redshift-space correlation function]], specifically the two-dimensional function $\xi(\sigma, \pi)$ (where $\sigma$ and $\pi$ are the transverse and line-of-sight pair separations, respectively), researchers can extract both the large-scale infall velocities and the small-scale random motions of galaxies.

On large scales, the coherent infall of galaxies into overdense regions flattens the clustering pattern along the line of sight, a phenomenon known as the Kaiser effect. This linear distortion is quantified by the redshift-space distortion parameter $\beta \simeq \Omega_m^{0.6}/b$. The 2dFGRS provided highly significant detections of this effect. Measurements of the quadrupole moment and the amplitude ratio of the real- and redshift-space correlation functions yielded robust constraints on this parameter, evaluated at $\beta = 0.49 \pm 0.09$ [Hawkins et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.346...78H/abstract) and $\beta = 0.43 \pm 0.07$ [Peacock et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..283...19P/abstract). 

On smaller scales, the random virialised motions of galaxies within dark matter halos create elongated features in redshift space, commonly referred to as the "fingers of God" effect. The high statistical power of the 2dFGRS allowed for detailed modeling of the distribution function of pairwise peculiar velocities, $f(v)$, while rigorously accounting for the aforementioned large-scale infall. This distribution was found to be well-described by an exponential form, with a global pairwise velocity dispersion of $a = 506 \pm 52 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Hawkins et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.346...78H/abstract).

Investigations into the pairwise velocity dispersion (PVD) as a function of galaxy luminosity revealed surprising trends that challenged contemporary models of galaxy formation. While simple halo models predicted a monotonic increase of the PVD with luminosity, 2dFGRS data demonstrated a distinctly U-shaped dependence [Jing et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...617..782J/abstract). The relative velocities of faint galaxies at small separations were found to be unexpectedly high—reaching approximately $700 \text{ km s}^{-1}$—which is comparable to the velocities of the brightest galaxies in the sample. Meanwhile, galaxies at intermediate luminosities (near $M^* - 1$, where $M^*$ is the characteristic luminosity of the Schechter function) exhibited a steep minimum in their PVD near $400 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Jing et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...617..782J/abstract). This implies that a substantial fraction of faint galaxies reside in massive, cluster-sized halos as satellite systems. This observational result created a persistent tension with standard halo occupation models, which struggled to reproduce this non-monotonic behavior without significant modifications to the assumed conditional luminosity functions in rich clusters.

## Galaxy Bias and Clustering Physics

To isolate the underlying mass distribution from the galaxies that trace it, the 2dFGRS enabled the direct measurement of galaxy bias. By analyzing the bispectrum of the galaxy distribution over scales of $0.1 < k < 0.5 \ h \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$ (approximately $5$ to $30 \ h^{-1} \text{Mpc}$), researchers demonstrated that optically selected $L^*$ galaxies are nearly perfect, unbiased tracers of the mass distribution on linear scales [Verde et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.335..432V/abstract). The measured linear bias parameter was $b_1 = 1.04 \pm 0.11$, with a non-linear (quadratic) bias consistent with zero ($b_2 = -0.054 \pm 0.08$). 

The unprecedented size of the survey also allowed for a precise quantification of luminosity-dependent clustering. Dividing the sample into volume-limited subsets revealed a strong dependence of clustering amplitude on intrinsic [[galaxy-properties|galaxy luminosity]] [Norberg et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328...64N/abstract), [Hawkins et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.11188H/abstract). The relative bias scales with luminosity $L$ approximately as:

$$ \frac{b}{b^*} = 0.85 + 0.15 \left( \frac{L}{L^*} \right) $$

where $b^*$ and $L^*$ are the bias and characteristic luminosity of the [[schechter-function|Schechter function]], respectively [Peacock et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..283...19P/abstract). In practice, the correlation function amplitude increases by a factor of $4.0$ between absolute magnitudes $M_{b_J} - 5\log_{10}h = -18$ and $-22.5$, with the most luminous galaxies clustering up to 3.0 times more strongly than $L^*$ galaxies [Norberg et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328...64N/abstract).

Galaxy clustering in the 2dFGRS was also found to depend heavily on spectral type. Using the continuous principal component analysis (PCA) parameter to robustly classify spectra [Madgwick et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001misk.conf..331M/abstract), galaxies were broadly divided into "early-type" (quiescent) and "late-type" (star-forming) populations. At all luminosities, the projected correlation function amplitude for early-type galaxies is approximately $50\%$ higher than that of late-type galaxies [Norberg et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.332..827N/abstract). However, both spectral classes exhibit nearly the same fractional increase in clustering strength with luminosity, supporting the view that luminosity, rather than spectral type, is the primary driver of the relative gradient in clustering strength. Further analysis of the clustering of different galaxy types provided evidence for relative stochastic biasing between populations [Lahav et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASA...21..404L/abstract).

Analysis of [[high-order-galaxy-statistics|High-order galaxy statistics]], such as the three-point correlation function (3PCF), confirmed that while $L^*$ galaxies are unbiased on large scales, luminous galaxies exhibit a linear bias factor of $b \approx 1.5$ [Jing et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...607..140J/abstract). Furthermore, 3PCF amplitudes measured in the 2dFGRS were consistently lower than those predicted by simple dark matter models, indicating a need for more sophisticated halo occupation modeling to extract precision cosmology from diverse [[redshift-catalogs|Redshift catalogs]].

## Group and Cluster Identification (2PIGG)

Early results from three-dimensional cluster searches within the 2dFGRS volume provided some of the first automated redshift and velocity dispersion measurements for large cluster samples, establishing a foundation for comprehensive group finding algorithms [de Propris et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001misk.conf..136D/abstract). To systematically map [[galaxy-overdensities|Galaxy overdensities]] and investigate environmental effects on [[galaxy-populations|Galaxy populations]], the 2dFGRS team subsequently constructed the 2dFGRS Percolation-Inferred Galaxy Group (2PIGG) catalogue [Eke et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.348..866E/abstract). As a milestone dataset for any large [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]], 2PIGG provided a homogeneous census of intermediate-scale structures in the local Universe. 

The catalogue was constructed using a friends-of-friends algorithm, which was rigorously calibrated and tested on mock redshift catalogues generated from $N$-body cosmological simulations [Eke et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.348..866E/abstract). The algorithm successfully grouped approximately 55% of the $\sim 190,000$ eligible survey galaxies into bound systems containing at least two members. The final 2PIGG sample comprises 28,877 galaxy groups, with an estimated interloper fraction of 40% [Eke et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.348..866E/abstract), [Padilla et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.352..211P/abstract). Of these, roughly 7,000 groups contain four or more galaxies, exhibiting a median redshift of $z = 0.11$ and a median velocity dispersion of $260\ \text{km}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ [Eke et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.348..866E/abstract). 

The 2PIGG catalogue has been instrumental in characterizing the clustering amplitude of galaxy groups and testing the halo model of structure formation. Group clustering is observed to be a strong function of total group luminosity; the most luminous groups in the catalogue are up to 10 times more strongly clustered than the 2PIGG average, aligning well with $\Lambda$CDM predictions [Padilla et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.352..211P/abstract). By empirically fitting the halo occupation distribution within 2PIGG, researchers demonstrated that the radial distribution of galaxies within halos follows a truncated Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile. Red galaxies were found to be significantly more centrally concentrated ($c=3.9$) than blue galaxies ($c=1.3$) [Collister et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.361..415C/abstract). This difference in the number of red and blue galaxies per halo directly explains the steeper slope observed in the red galaxy correlation function. However, the ability to fully constrain small-scale halo profiles is limited by close-pair incompleteness—arising from the overlap and merging of galaxy images in the 2dFGRS parent catalogue—which impacts the accurately measured radial distribution of satellite galaxies in dense environments [van den Bosch et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.356.1233V/abstract).

Furthermore, the catalogue enabled detailed studies of galaxy evolution within dense environments. Analysis of galaxy pairs within 2PIGG groups showed that tidal interactions efficiently trigger star formation at small projected separations ($r_p < 15\ h^{-1}\ \text{kpc}$). Crucially, this analysis of 2dFGRS galaxy pairs demonstrated that such interactions trigger significant star formation primarily in low- to moderate-density environments rather than in dense, massive clusters [Sol Alonso et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.367.1029S/abstract). However, on the whole, pairs located within groups are systematically redder and exhibit lower present-day star formation activity than isolated group members, underscoring the complex interplay between local interactions and the broader cluster environment [Alonso et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.352.1081A/abstract).

The kinematic properties of galaxies within these groups also helped explore unexpected tensions with contemporary models of galaxy formation. Observations of kinematic anomalies, such as the aforementioned U-shaped dependence of the pairwise velocity dispersion on galaxy luminosity, suggested that a significant fraction of faint galaxies act as satellites within massive, cluster-sized halos [Jing et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...617..782J/abstract). Resolving this tension required subsequent revisions to the faint-end slope of the conditional luminosity function in rich clusters.

## Multiwavelength Synergies

The cross-correlation of the 2dFGRS optical spectroscopic database with multiwavelength [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]] has significantly advanced the study of active galactic nuclei (AGN) and the physics of star formation in the local universe. By combining 2dFGRS spectra with radio, X-ray, and infrared data, researchers can break degeneracies inherent to purely optical [[target-selection|Target selection]] and isolate the diverse physical mechanisms driving galaxy evolution.

### Radio Synergies and the NVSS
The overlap between the 2dFGRS and wide-field [[radio-surveys|Radio surveys]] such as the NRAO VLA Sky Survey (NVSS) has been highly effective in characterizing the local [[radio-source-populations|radio source population]]. Approximately $1.5\%$ of 2dFGRS galaxies down to the optical survey limit of $b_J \le 19.45$ are detected in the NVSS at $1.4\text{ GHz}$ [Sadler et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999PASA...16..247S/abstract). By pairing NVSS continuum fluxes with 2dFGRS optical spectroscopy, researchers found that this radio-selected subset can be cleanly divided into two distinct physical classes: roughly $40\%$ are powered by intense star formation, while the remaining $60\%$ are AGN, primarily consisting of low-power radio galaxies and Seyferts [Sadler et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999PASA...16..247S/abstract). 

Cross-matching 2dFGRS-identified clusters with radio surveys like the NVSS has enabled the determination of the radio luminosity function for Brightest Cluster Galaxies (BCGs), revealing that powerful radio BCGs are typically located in relaxed clusters [Yuan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.3669Y/abstract). Spectroscopic diagnostic line ratios further revealed that many of these [[emission-line-galaxies|Emission-line galaxies]] are "composites" harboring both an accreting massive black hole and a nuclear starburst [Jackson et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000PASA...17..234J/abstract). The combined dataset of redshift determinations and spectral analysis provided a broad framework used to study the general properties of these local radio sources with fluxes $S \ge 1\text{ mJy}$ [Magliocchetti et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12286M/abstract). 

### X-ray Constraints on AGN and Normal Galaxies
In the X-ray regime, 2dFGRS data have been cross-matched with source catalogs from Chandra and XMM-Newton. A key objective in these multiwavelength studies has been the search for highly X-ray luminous "normal" galaxies—systems where the X-ray emission is dominated by stellar processes, such as high-mass X-ray binaries and supernova remnants, rather than an accreting supermassive black hole. 

However, cross-matching 2dFGRS galaxies with Chandra and XMM-Newton point sources revealed a stark upper limit on stellar X-ray production. There is a distinct lack of purely star-forming galaxies with X-ray luminosities exceeding $L_X = 10^{42}\text{ erg s}^{-1}$; essentially all sources surpassing this threshold in the local universe are AGN [Tzanavaris et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...454..447T/abstract). 

### Discovering Hidden Quasar Populations
Multiwavelength diagnostics also served as a tool for evaluating the completeness of the 2dFGRS galaxy sample. While the survey was designed specifically to target extended sources (galaxies) rather than point sources, automated spectral filtering of the 2dFGRS database uncovered 55 high-redshift ($z > 0.3$) quasars that had been inadvertently targeted [Madgwick et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.334..209M/abstract). These objects masqueraded as low-redshift galaxies in the input photometric catalog due to complex optical morphologies, often caused by line-of-sight alignments with diffuse foreground objects or highly compact host galaxies. The identification of these anomalous sources removed high-luminosity contaminants from the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and provided a valuable target list for subsequent absorption-line and gravitational lensing studies [Madgwick et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.334..209M/abstract). Additionally, the 2dF-SDSS LRG and QSO (2SLAQ) survey utilized the same spectrograph to produce a deep catalogue of over 12,000 QSOs, probing the faint end of the AGN luminosity distribution at $z < 2.6$ [Croom et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.392...19C/abstract).

### Infrared Synergies
Multiwavelength synergies additionally extended into the infrared regime. The creation of the Imperial IRAS-FSC Redshift Catalogue (IIFSCz) successfully utilized generally available spectroscopic redshifts to further study large-scale structure and galaxy bias within the local infrared-luminous population [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398..109W/abstract). Furthermore, analysis of the completeness of 2MASS selection using spectroscopic samples like the 2dFGRS and SDSS revealed that infrared surveys are highly effective for nearby galaxies but miss approximately 10% of objects, primarily faint, blue, low-surface-brightness systems [McIntosh et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.373.1321M/abstract).

## Legacy and Future Directions

The 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey (2dFGRS) marked a fundamental paradigm shift in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], transitioning the field from two-dimensional maps and pencil-beam observations into the era of precision cosmology via statistically representative three-dimensional volumes [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract). Its success with highly multiplexed fiber-optic [[spectroscopy-surveys|spectroscopy]] paved the way for a subsequent generation of [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]]. The [[6df-galaxy-survey|6dF Galaxy Survey]], a direct successor in the Southern Hemisphere, began releasing data incrementally starting with 45,000 redshifts in its First Data Release (DR1) [Jones et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..329...11J/abstract). This project extended the spectroscopic mapping of the southern sky, obtaining redshifts for over 130,000 galaxies and measuring peculiar velocities for a sub-sample of approximately 15,000 of the brightest early-type galaxies [Jones et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.399..683J/abstract). The 6dFGS further refined measurements of the redshift-space distortion parameter $\beta$ and the galaxy-mass correlation parameter $r_g$ by combining redshift and peculiar velocity data [Colless et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IAUS..216..180C/abstract). Expanding on 2dFGRS findings, the 6dFGS provided integrated luminosity densities in both near-infrared and optical bands, showing that simple [[schechter-function|Schechter functions]] may not fully capture the true [[galaxy-luminosity-function|luminosity distribution]]'s bright-end decline [Jones et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.369...25J/abstract). Modern efforts to provide verified broad emission-line AGN catalogues in follow-up surveys like the 6dFGS have built upon this foundation, identifying thousands of objects for future study with next-generation facilities like the Vera C. Rubin Observatory [Hon et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.536.3611H/abstract). Similarly, the [[gama-survey|GAMA survey]] and the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (SDSS) utilized these methodologies to probe galaxy evolution and the cosmic web with unprecedented multi-wavelength depth and volume.

Data from the 2dFGRS has been incorporated into value-added full-sky catalogues like [[glade-catalog|GLADE]], which are used to identify host candidates for [[gravitational-wave-galaxy-catalogue|gravitational-wave events]] and other poorly localized transients [Dálya et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.2374D/abstract). Additionally, the Reference Catalog of Spectral Energy Distributions (RCSEDv2) has homogenized 2dFGRS spectra alongside other major surveys, providing a massive multi-wavelength database of galaxy physical properties [Chilingarian et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ASPC..535..179C/abstract).

A major focus of subsequent literature has been reconciling cosmological constraints derived from the 2dFGRS with those from other major datasets, notably the SDSS. Because the surveys utilized different photometric selection bands (favoring different morphological mixes with distinct, scale-dependent bias profiles), early comparisons of their power spectra showed discrepancies [Sánchez et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.385..830S/abstract). This realization highlighted the inadequacy of treating galaxy bias as a simple constant factor ($b$) and drove the widespread adoption of sophisticated Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD) and conditional luminosity function (CLF) models that rigorously account for luminosity, color, and scale-dependent bias. Notably, CLF modeling utilizing 2dFGRS data helped reconcile halo occupation statistics with WMAP3 cosmology, significantly reducing predicted cluster mass-to-light ratios by approximately 35% and advancing toward a concordant model of galaxy clustering [van den Bosch et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.376..841V/abstract).

Despite the successes of the 2dFGRS in establishing the $\Lambda$CDM concordance model, several theoretical challenges regarding non-linear evolution and scale-dependent bias remain open. Measurements of the pairwise velocity dispersion as a function of luminosity revealed non-monotonic behavior that contradicted the velocity-luminosity predictions of standard halo models [Jing et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...617..782J/abstract). Furthermore, accurately characterizing the non-linear evolution of the cosmic web has posed ongoing challenges for standard mock catalogues. While $N$-body simulations broadly replicate the clustering statistics of the 2dFGRS, advanced topological analyses utilizing object point processes indicate that simulated structures are consistently shorter and less connected than the highly extended filamentary network observed in the real 2dFGRS data [Stoica et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...510A..38S/abstract). 

Finally, the 2dFGRS data raised enduring questions about homogeneity in the local Universe. Observations of the Southern Galactic Cap revealed a "Local Hole"—a $\sim 30\%$ deficiency in galaxy counts extending out to $z \approx 0.1$ [Busswell et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.354..991B/abstract), [Frith et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..329...49F/abstract). Whether this large-scale underdensity represents a severe statistical fluctuation, an effect of complex observational biases, or an excess of large-scale power that challenges standard $\Lambda$CDM models on $300 \text{ Mpc}$ scales remains an active subject of debate in cosmology.
