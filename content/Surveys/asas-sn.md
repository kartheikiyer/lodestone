## Article

The **All-Sky Automated Survey for Supernovae (ASAS-SN)** is a global network of robotic telescopes designed for the continuous, high-cadence optical monitoring of the entire sky. Unlike historical [[stellar-transient-and-supernova-surveys|Stellar Transient and Supernova Surveys]] that relied heavily on host-galaxy [[target-selection|Target selection]], ASAS-SN employs a strictly untargeted [[sky-mapping-and-surveys|Sky Mapping and Surveys]] strategy. By scanning both the northern and southern hemispheres uniformly to a depth of approximately the 18th magnitude in the $g$-band, the survey effectively eliminates host-galaxy bias. This architecture allows ASAS-SN to achieve over 90% completeness for bright optical transients peaking at $m \le 17.0$, compiling an unprecedented volume-limited census of the local universe [Neumann et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.4356N/abstract). As a result, the survey has been instrumental in the rapid discovery and characterization of a diverse array of astrophysical phenomena, ranging from local stellar outbursts to the most luminous supernova ever recorded, ASASSN-15lh [Dong et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ATel.7774....1D/abstract).

The unbiased nature of ASAS-SN has fundamentally reshaped our understanding of transient demographics and extreme accretion events. Most notably, the survey's supernova catalogues revealed that the specific rate of Type Ia supernovae (SNe Ia) is 10 to 20 times higher in low-mass dwarf galaxies than in massive hosts. This extreme environmental dependence raises critical open questions regarding underlying progenitor physics, binary evolution channels, and star formation efficiencies in low-mass regimes. Beyond stellar explosions, ASAS-SN has emerged as a premier engine for detecting nuclear transients, including changing-look active galactic nuclei and tidal disruption events (TDEs). The survey established some of the first systematic local TDE rates—identifying benchmark events like ASASSN-14li—though its derived rate of roughly $4.1 \times 10^{-5}$ per year per galaxy remains surprisingly higher than estimates from other [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]], leaving the true demographics of TDEs an active area of debate [Holoien et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2918H/abstract).

Within the Milky Way, ASAS-SN's long-baseline photometry drives parallel advances in Galactic archaeology and stellar variability. Its high-cadence data enables precise ground-based asteroseismology of red giant stars and robust separation of classical and dwarf novae. The survey has also highlighted a persistent discrepancy between theoretical models and observations of the Galactic classical nova rate, providing strong evidence that interstellar dust obscures nearly half of these events from standard optical [[detection-limits|Detection limits]]. Moving forward, ASAS-SN serves as a foundational bright-transient trigger within a broader multi-wavelength ecosystem. By capturing the rapid, early-time evolution of bright events, it provides critical synergies with [[deep-imaging-surveys|Deep imaging surveys]] like the upcoming [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]], space-based astrometry from the [[gaia-mission-and-data-products|Gaia Mission and Data Products]], and next-generation, high-multiplex spectroscopic facilities such as the [[wst|WST]] and the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]].

## Introduction to ASAS-SN

The All-Sky Automated Survey for Supernovae (ASAS-SN) is a global network of robotic telescopes designed for untargeted, high-cadence optical monitoring of the entire sky. Unlike earlier [[stellar-transient-and-supernova-surveys|Stellar Transient and Supernova Surveys]] that relied heavily on host-galaxy [[target-selection|Target selection]], ASAS-SN scans the sky uniformly. This unbiased approach allows for the discovery of a wide variety of optical transients without an a priori preference for massive or high-luminosity galaxies [Chen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..259...53C/abstract). Originally operating in the $V$-band, the survey later upgraded to $g$-band observations, which deepened its limiting magnitude and achieved unprecedented completeness for bright, nearby optical transients [Neumann et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.4356N/abstract). 

This complete volume-limited sampling has significantly refined studies of supernova demographics. Notably, ASAS-SN data revealed that the specific rate of Type Ia supernovae (SNe Ia) continues to rise dramatically in low-mass galaxies. The rate scales as $(M_\star/10^{10} M_\odot)^{-0.5}$, appearing 10 to 20 times higher in dwarf galaxies ($M_\star \lesssim 10^7 M_\odot$) than in massive hosts ($M_\star \sim 10^{10} M_\odot$) [Brown et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3785B/abstract). While spectroscopic analysis confirms that these low-luminosity host galaxies have typical metallicities and star formation rates for their mass [Holoien et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...950..108H/abstract), the extreme rate enhancement raises important astrophysical questions. Given the overabundance of SNe Ia in low-mass environments, these recent findings have raised important questions regarding whether systematic environmental influences fundamentally alter progenitor physics or formation efficiencies in these extreme mass regimes [Brown et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3785B/abstract). ASAS-SN has also captured extreme individual events, including ASASSN-15lh, which remains one of the most luminous supernovae ever recorded [Dong et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ATel.7774....1D/abstract).

Beyond stellar explosions, ASAS-SN is a leading engine for discovering nuclear transients, including changing-look active galactic nuclei (e.g., NGC 2617) [Shappee et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ATel.5010....1S/abstract) and TDEs. The survey's architecture has enabled the identification of benchmark TDEs in the local universe, such as ASASSN-14li [Holoien et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2918H/abstract), and ASASSN-23bd, the lowest-redshift and least-luminous optically selected TDE found to date, representing a novel "Low Luminosity and Fast" class of TDEs [Hoogendam et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.4501H/abstract). Interestingly, there is an unresolved tension regarding event rates: ASAS-SN derives a TDE rate of $r \simeq 4.1 \times 10^{-5}$ per year per galaxy, which is noticeably higher than estimates derived from other [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]] [Holoien et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2918H/abstract).

In the realm of Galactic astronomy, ASAS-SN's continuous monitoring provides high-precision asteroseismic distance measurements (precise to $\sim 15\%$) for M giant stars, complementing space-based astrometry from platforms such as the [[gaia-mission-and-data-products|Gaia Mission and Data Products]] [Auge et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....160...18A/abstract). The high-cadence data also permits robust separation of classical novae and dwarf novae based on outburst amplitude, with minimal population overlap [Kawash et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910..120K/abstract). However, ASAS-SN has highlighted a persistent tension in the Galactic classical nova rate: while theoretical models predict $\sim 30-50$ novae per year, only $\sim 10$ per year are observed. Recent 3D dust mapping suggests that interstellar extinction obscures approximately half of these events in the optical, strongly impacting the [[detection-efficiency|Detection efficiency]] of all-sky surveys and likely explaining the discrepancy [Kawash et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922...25K/abstract).

## System Architecture and Survey Strategy

The All-Sky Automated Survey for SuperNovae (ASAS-SN) operates a global array of 14-cm diameter telescopes to achieve high-cadence, continuous monitoring of the entire night sky [Shappee et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...826..144S/abstract). The system architecture relies on modular clusters of these small-aperture lenses hosted on independent robotic mounts, strategically distributed across premier astronomical observatories to ensure redundant coverage of both the northern and [[southern-hemisphere-surveys|Southern Hemisphere Surveys]] to include both hemispheres. Notable operational units include "Brutus", a quadruple-telescope mount deployed at Haleakala Observatory in Hawaii [Jose et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ATel.6777....1J/abstract), and "Cassius", which is hosted at the [[cerro-tololo-inter-american-observatory|Cerro Tololo Inter-American Observatory]] in Chile [Brimacombe et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ATel11976....1B/abstract). 

Unlike historical transient searches that relied on targeted monitoring of massive or highly luminous galaxies, ASAS-SN employs a strictly untargeted strategy. By scanning the entire sky without regard to underlying host galaxy properties, the survey effectively eliminates host-galaxy bias [Chen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..259...53C/abstract). This unbiased [[sky-mapping-and-surveys|Sky Mapping and Surveys]] approach is crucial for recovering the true luminosity functions of transient phenomena and allows for the construction of statistically complete, volume-limited samples in the local universe. Because of this strategy, ASAS-SN has been instrumental in characterizing populations that inhabit low-luminosity or dwarf galaxies, which were historically under-sampled. Furthermore, the high observing cadence enables rapid discovery and [[spectroscopic-follow-up|Spectroscopic follow-up]] of fast-evolving transients; for example, the bright Type Ia supernova ASASSN-14lp was discovered a mere $\sim 2$ days after first light [Shappee et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...826..144S/abstract).

A major operational milestone in the ASAS-SN survey strategy was the systematic transition from continuous V-band to g-band monitoring. This shift fundamentally improved both the limiting magnitude and the overall transient recovery rate. During the survey's initial V-band phase, observations achieved strict completeness up to a peak magnitude of $m_{\rm peak} \approx 16.2$ mag, but the detection efficiency dropped to roughly $70\%$ for transients at $m_{\rm peak} \leq 17.0$ mag [Neumann et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.4356N/abstract). By upgrading the network and shifting the primary observing filter to the g-band, the survey extended its nominal [[detection-limits|Detection limits]] to $g \leq 18$ mag. Consequently, the g-band transient sample is nearly complete up to $m_{\rm peak} = 16.7$ mag and achieves $90\%$ completeness for events peaking at $m_{\rm peak} \leq 17.0$ mag [Neumann et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.4356N/abstract).

## Supernova Demographics and Completeness

The ASAS-SN survey has profoundly impacted the demographic study of local transient phenomena by compiling a highly complete bright supernova catalogue. Historically, many supernova searches relied on targeted observations of massive, high-luminosity galaxies to maximize discovery rates. In contrast, ASAS-SN employs an untargeted, all-sky monitoring strategy, allowing for the discovery of transients largely unbiased by host galaxy properties [Chen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..259...53C/abstract). This shift from biased [[target-selection|Target selection]] to untargeted, full-sky scanning is critical for constructing an accurate volumetric luminosity function of local supernovae and isolating intrinsic population demographics from selection effects.

**Statistical Completeness**
The survey's [[detection-efficiency|Detection efficiency]] has been rigorously quantified through its cataloguing efforts. Benefiting from the network's transition to a $g \leq 18$ mag detection limit, the compiled $g$-band sample provides a nearly 100% complete record of transients up to $m_{\rm peak} = 16.7$ mag, maintaining a 90% completeness threshold for events with $m_{\rm peak} \leq 17.0$ mag [Neumann et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.4356N/abstract). By the release of the 2018-2020 catalog, the dataset comprised over 2,400 ASAS-SN discovered or recovered supernovae, establishing it as a foundational benchmark for local transient demographics.

**Host Galaxy Demographics and Type Ia Rates**
Because ASAS-SN provides an unbiased selection of host environments, it has revealed significant demographic trends in the specific rates of Type Ia supernovae as a function of stellar mass. Analysis of the catalogue indicates that the specific SN Ia rate rises sharply toward lower-mass galaxies, scaling approximately as $(M_\star / 10^{10} M_\odot)^{-0.5}$ [Brown et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3785B/abstract). In dwarf systems with $\log(M_\star/M_\odot) \lesssim 7.0$, the specific SN Ia rate is enhanced by a factor of 10 to 20 relative to massive galaxies of $10^{10} M_\odot$ [Brown et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3785B/abstract). 

The survey routinely flags bright events in such low-mass environments and other local galaxies, as exemplified by early discoveries of probable supernovae in dwarf galaxies like UGC 11860 [Stanek et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ATel.6301....1S/abstract), as well as under-studied systems like UGC 11868 with the discovery of ASASSN-16fp [Holoien et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ATel.9086....1H/abstract). The untargeted discovery strategy continuously yields events in UGC galaxies, such as the probable supernova in UGC 04251 [Brown et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ATel.8496....1B/abstract) and ASASSN-16at in UGC 08041, further highlighting the survey's sensitivity to the local volume [Bock et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ATel.8566....1B/abstract). Similarly, ASAS-SN provides a continuous stream of bright transient discoveries across various other host galaxy catalogs. Early observations from the Southern hemisphere Cassius unit captured events like the probable supernova in 2MASX J00453260-1415328 [Brimacombe et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ATel.6525....1B/abstract). This catalog-agnostic depth is further demonstrated by discoveries like ASASSN-16jt in ESO 344-G 021 [Brimacombe et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ATel.9439....1B/abstract), the probable supernova in 2MASX J20390918-3045201 [Brimacombe et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ATel.7910....1B/abstract), and the bright supernova ASASSN-17at in 2MASX J11383367+2523532 [Masi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ATel.9989....1M/abstract). Conversely, ASAS-SN also ensures the recovery of significant bright transients in well-known, massive systems, such as the discovery of ASASSN-16fq (AT 2016cok) within the prominent spiral galaxy M66 [Bock et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ATel.9091....1B/abstract).

Despite this extreme enhancement in specific rates, [[spectroscopic-follow-up|Spectroscopic follow-up]] of the low-luminosity host galaxies themselves reveals properties broadly consistent with the general galaxy population. Their measured star formation rates and metallicities are typical for general field galaxies of similar masses [Holoien et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...950..108H/abstract). This presents a notable tension in the literature: while the low-mass host environments appear statistically "normal," the strongly enhanced specific supernova rates raise open questions regarding whether progenitor physics, formation efficiencies, or underlying environmental influences diverge in these extreme mass regimes. The survey's unbiased strategy also enables deep constraints on individual SN Ia progenitors; for example, high-cadence early monitoring of ASASSN-14lp confidently ruled out a large non-degenerate secondary companion [Shappee et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...826..144S/abstract).

**Extreme Transients**
In addition to defining population-level demographics, the ASAS-SN unbiased selection has facilitated the discovery of rare, extreme events residing in faint hosts that might have otherwise been overlooked by surveys prioritizing massive galaxies. A prominent example is the discovery of ASASSN-15lh. Originally identified as a probable supernova in the host APMUKS(BJ) B215839.70-615403.9 [Nicholls et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ATel.7642....1N/abstract), the event peaked in June 2015 and remains one of the most luminous supernovae recorded to date [Dong et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ATel.7774....1D/abstract). These results are often compiled in comprehensive [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]].

## Tidal Disruption Events and Nuclear Transients

The high-cadence, untargeted observing strategy of ASAS-SN has made it a premier facility for the discovery of rare, dynamic phenomena occurring in galactic nuclei. Because ASAS-SN monitors the entire extragalactic sky without pre-selecting specific galaxies (avoiding biases associated with traditional [[target-selection|Target selection]]), it is highly sensitive to tidal disruption events (TDEs) and extreme variability in active galactic nuclei (AGN).

**Tidal Disruption Events**
ASAS-SN has been instrumental in discovering nearby TDEs and characterizing their multi-wavelength evolution. A landmark discovery was ASASSN-14li, located at a distance of $d \simeq 90$ Mpc, which served as a crucial nearby laboratory for TDE physics [Holoien et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2918H/abstract). ASASSN-14li reached a peak bolometric luminosity of $L \simeq 10^{44}$ erg s$^{-1}$ and radiated a total energy of $E \simeq 7 \times 10^{50}$ erg over the six months following its discovery. Its UV and optical emission was well-described by a blackbody with a roughly constant temperature of $T \sim 35,000$ K, decaying exponentially as $L \propto e^{-t/t_0}$ with an $e$-folding timescale of $t_0 \simeq 60$ days. The event was also accompanied by luminous soft X-ray emission, highlighting the complex emission geometry of the forming accretion disk [Holoien et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2918H/abstract).

Based on early discoveries such as ASASSN-14ae—which was initially announced as a possible supernova in the galaxy ESO 253-G003 [Holoien et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ATel.6732....1H/abstract) before being subsequently recognized in later literature as the survey's first tidal disruption event—and the subsequent benchmark ASASSN-14li, ASAS-SN provided one of the first systematic estimates of the local TDE rate. The survey implied an average rate of $r \simeq 4.1 \times 10^{-5}$ yr$^{-1}$ per galaxy, with a 90% confidence interval of $(2.2 - 17.0) \times 10^{-5}$ yr$^{-1}$ per galaxy [Holoien et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2918H/abstract). Notably, this observed rate—corresponding to roughly 1 TDE for every 70 Type Ia supernovae detected by ASAS-SN in 2014—is significantly higher than the TDE rates derived from other [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]]. The exact cause of this discrepancy remains an active area of debate, potentially reflecting differing survey selection effects, detection efficiencies, or host-galaxy biases.

More recently, ASAS-SN has expanded the known parameter space of TDEs to include a newly identified "Low Luminosity and Fast" class. This was exemplified by the discovery of ASASSN-23bd, the lowest-redshift ($z = 0.01107$) and least-luminous optically selected TDE yet found [Hoogendam et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.4501H/abstract). Peaking at a UV/optical luminosity of only $(5.4 \pm 0.4) \times 10^{42}$ erg s$^{-1}$, the event exhibited a rapid rise of $t < 15$ days and a steep decline. Light curve modeling and scaling relations imply a central black hole mass of $\sim 10^6 M_\odot$, pushing observational limits toward the lower end of the supermassive black hole mass function [Hoogendam et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.4501H/abstract).

**Changing-Look AGN and Other Nuclear Transients**
Beyond TDEs, ASAS-SN's high cadence has proved highly effective at identifying extreme states of AGN variability, including "changing-look" AGN. A prominent early example was the discovery of a dramatic Seyfert type change in NGC 2617, which underwent a strong optical outburst tracked by ASAS-SN [Shappee et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ATel.5010....1S/abstract). More recently, the survey identified ASASSN-22cy as a likely optical changing-look AGN located in the nucleus of NGC 3822 [Hinkle et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ATel15280....1H/abstract). Together, these discoveries demonstrate ASAS-SN's critical role in constructing an unbiased census of transient accretion phenomena.

## Stellar Variability and Galactic Archaeology

While primarily recognized for its contributions to extragalactic transient detection, ASAS-SN provides a continuous, high-cadence photometric record of the Milky Way. This makes it an invaluable resource for Galactic archaeology and the study of stellar variability. 

**Classical and Dwarf Novae**
A major application of ASAS-SN data within the Galaxy is the detection and characterization of accretion-powered dwarf nova outbursts and thermonuclear-powered classical novae. Distinguishing between these two populations in [[all-sky-surveys|All-sky surveys]] is a foundational challenge. Analysis of over 1,600 dwarf nova outbursts observed by ASAS-SN demonstrates that population confusion is minimal. On average, classical novae brighten by ${\sim}11$ magnitudes during an outburst, compared to ${\sim}5$ magnitudes for a dwarf nova, with their respective amplitude distributions overlapping by only about $15\%$ [Kawash et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910..120K/abstract). Furthermore, ASAS-SN light curves reveal that the outburst amplitude and the time required to decline by two magnitudes from peak are positively correlated for dwarf novae, whereas these parameters exhibit a weak negative correlation for classical novae [Kawash et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910..120K/abstract).

The ASAS-SN classical nova sample has also been leveraged to address a long-standing tension in astrophysics: the discrepancy between the observed Galactic classical nova rate of roughly $10\text{ yr}^{-1}$ and the theoretical rate of $30\text{--}50\text{ yr}^{-1}$ predicted by Galactic population models. Research incorporating three-dimensional dust maps indicates that interstellar extinction severely biases optical detections. For a detection threshold of $g \lesssim 15$, which is typical for ASAS-SN, only $53\%$ of Galactic novae are expected to be easily detectable [Kawash et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922...25K/abstract). By modeling this obscuration effect alongside data from complementary infrared and optical surveys, astronomers estimate a corrected Galactic nova rate of ${\sim}30\text{--}40\text{ yr}^{-1}$. This strongly suggests that severe dust extinction—rather than over-predictive models—is the primary cause of the historical discrepancy [Kawash et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922...25K/abstract).

**Asteroseismology and Variable Stars**
Beyond eruptive transients, ASAS-SN's long-baseline photometry has proven highly effective for ground-based asteroseismology, particularly for evolved M giants. These stars exhibit solar-like oscillations with periods spanning hours to months and photometric amplitudes between ${\sim}1$ and $100\text{ mmag}$. By extracting oscillation frequencies from ASAS-SN light curves, researchers have demonstrated that it is possible to derive asteroseismic distance measurements to oscillating M giants with a precision of ${\sim}15\%$ [Auge et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....160...18A/abstract). This capability allows for the potential mapping of an estimated $2 \times 10^6$ Galactic M giants out to distances of $20\text{--}50\text{ kpc}$. Such ground-based asteroseismic constraints significantly extend the accessible distance limit for precise stellar distance measurements, serving as a critical complement to space-based parallax measurements provided by the [[gaia-mission-and-data-products|Gaia Mission and Data Products]] [Auge et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....160...18A/abstract).

## Multi-wavelength Synergy and Space-based Coordination

The high-cadence, untargeted optical monitoring provided by ASAS-SN serves as a critical trigger for [[spectroscopic-follow-up|Spectroscopic follow-up]] and multi-wavelength observations across both ground- and [[space-based-observatories-and-surveys|Space-Based Observatories and Surveys]]. Rapid identification of bright transients enables comprehensive multi-wavelength follow-up, which is essential for characterizing the broadband spectral energy distributions (SEDs) of these events. 

Coordination with the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory has been particularly fruitful. For example, joint ASAS-SN and Swift UVOT observations of the Type IIn supernova SN 2017hcc (ATLAS17lsn) constrained its near-UV to optical SED to a cooling blackbody dropping from $T_{\rm bb} \simeq 16,500$ K to $11,700$ K. This multi-wavelength data yielded a peak bolometric luminosity of $L_{\rm bol, peak} \simeq 1.3 \times 10^{44}$ erg s$^{-1}$, making it one of the most luminous Type IIn supernovae studied to date [Prieto et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017RNAAS...1...28P/abstract). 

In the domain of nuclear transients, the synergy between ASAS-SN and space-based X-ray/UV facilities has been instrumental in characterizing tidal disruption events (TDEs). The discovery of ASASSN-14li triggered extensive Swift coverage, revealing an optical/UV SED well-described by a constant temperature of $T \sim 35,000$ K and a soft X-ray component that decayed more slowly than the UV/optical emission, dominating the late-time energy output [Holoien et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2918H/abstract). Similarly, the classification of the exceptionally low-luminosity TDE ASASSN-23bd relied heavily on stacked Swift X-ray upper limits ($L_{0.3-10\,\mathrm{keV}} < 1.0\times 10^{40}$ erg s$^{-1}$) combined with subsequent XMM-Newton soft thermal detections to distinguish the transient's emission from typical active galactic nucleus activity [Hoogendam et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.4501H/abstract).

ASAS-SN's all-sky footprint also provides vital baseline and complementary photometry for space missions dedicated to ultra-high-cadence, continuous monitoring. The survey has actively monitored regions corresponding to the Kepler and Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) footprints. Discoveries such as the bright supernova ASASSN-18tb in the TESS field [Brimacombe et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ATel11976....1B/abstract) and ASASSN-18bt in a Kepler supernova monitoring field [Brown et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ATel11253....1B/abstract) demonstrate how ASAS-SN identifies transients that fall within the purview of these missions. 

The [[gaia-mission-and-data-products|Gaia Mission and Data Products]] are built on the foundational goal of creating a stereoscopic census of roughly one billion stars [Gilmore et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..274..581G/abstract). While Gaia is anticipated to provide a vast complementary baseline for ASAS-SN's extragalactic transient mission [Vaccari et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002EAS.....2..313V/abstract), ASAS-SN's all-sky photometric measurements also drive significant synergy for Galactic stellar astrophysics. By combining ASAS-SN light curves with constraints from Gaia and the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (specifically utilizing SDSS Data Release 10 and the [[apogee-survey|APOGEE survey]] to provide vital spectroscopic stellar parameters [Ahn et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..211...17A/abstract), [Majewski et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AJ....154...94M/abstract)), researchers have demonstrated that solar-like oscillations in M giants can yield distance measurements with precisions of $\sim 15\%$ [Auge et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....160...18A/abstract).

Looking forward, ASAS-SN is positioned as a foundational element within a broader multi-survey ecosystem. Its continuous bright-transient feed will guide the [[target-selection|Target selection]] and rapid response for upcoming high-resolution, all-sky panchromatic programs. Proposed synergies with the Roman Space Telescope imaging arrays and [[spectroscopy-surveys|Spectroscopy surveys]] will further integrate ASAS-SN's optical baselines to map the multi-epoch kinematics and transient properties of the local universe [Han et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230611784H/abstract).

## Historical Development and Survey Evolution

The development of ASAS-SN must be contextualized relative to earlier transient searches. For instance, early programs like the ROTSE-IIIb survey aimed to establish unbiased nearby supernova samples, while the Fall 2004 SDSS Supernova Survey successfully targeted intermediate-redshift events [Sako et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005tsra.conf..415S/abstract). While such large-scale searches, including the broader [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]], had demonstrated the power of untargeted, rolling searches in designated survey footprints [Frieman et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....135..338F/abstract), the development of ASAS-SN marked a paradigm shift in [[stellar-transient-and-supernova-surveys|Stellar Transient and Supernova Surveys]] by expanding untargeted monitoring to the entire sky. This approach effectively eliminated host-galaxy bias, allowing for the construction of complete, volume-limited samples in the local universe [Chen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..259...53C/abstract).

In its early operational phase, the ASAS-SN network relied on a $V$-band sample, discovering landmark events such as the early-detected Type Ia SN ASASSN-14lp [Shappee et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...826..144S/abstract) and the hyper-luminous ASASSN-15lh [Dong et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ATel.7774....1D/abstract). The detection of possible extreme luminosity transients early in the survey helped establish ASAS-SN's foundational sensitivity to uncovering high-luminosity phenomena across a wide range of host environments [Kiyota et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ATel.6853....1K/abstract). To improve detection limits and survey depth, the network subsequently underwent a major expansion, transitioning to a global $g$-band observing strategy. This upgrade pushed the effective limiting magnitude to $g \leq 18$ mag, significantly expanding the survey's transient recovery rate and volume completeness [Neumann et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.4356N/abstract).

A cornerstone of ASAS-SN's historical development has been the publication of its yearly bright supernova catalogues. These [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]] systematically compile multi-wavelength photometry and [[spectroscopic-follow-up|Spectroscopic follow-up]] data for all discovered and recovered transients. For example, the fourth iteration covering the year 2017 documented 308 bright ($m_{\rm peak} \leq 17$) spectroscopically confirmed supernovae [Holoien et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.1899H/abstract). By the release of the 2018-2020 catalogue, the complete ASAS-SN sample had grown to encompass 2,427 supernovae [Neumann et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.4356N/abstract). 

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite providing high-completeness catalogs for the brightest optical transients, ASAS-SN observations have highlighted several unresolved tensions in time-domain astrophysics. First, the survey derives a tidal disruption event (TDE) rate of approximately $4.1 \times 10^{-5} \text{ yr}^{-1}$ per galaxy, effectively discovering roughly one TDE for every 70 Type Ia supernovae [Holoien et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2918H/abstract). This rate is notably higher than those inferred from other surveys, leaving the true demographics of TDEs an open question [Hoogendam et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.4501H/abstract). Second, ASAS-SN data reveal a dramatic 10 to 20-fold enhancement in the specific Type Ia supernova rate within low-mass host galaxies [Brown et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3785B/abstract). This extreme environmental dependence raises unresolved questions regarding potential shifts in progenitor physics or binary fraction demographics in dwarf galaxies [Brown et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3785B/abstract). Finally, the Galactic classical nova rate remains heavily debated; while theoretical models predict 30-50 novae per year, the observed rate is only $\sim 10 \text{ yr}^{-1}$.

As time-domain astrophysics enters a new era, the role of shallow, ultra-high-cadence surveys like ASAS-SN will evolve alongside next-generation [[deep-imaging-surveys|Deep imaging surveys]] facilities, most notably the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]]. While LSST will probe faint transients down to $r \sim 24.5$ with less frequent revisits, ASAS-SN and contemporary high-cadence networks will serve as crucial complements. As highlighted by future science designs, high-cadence monitors can fully resolve the initial rapid rise and peak of local transients, while LSST characterizes the deeper pre-explosion history and late-time fading phases [Miller et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PASP..137i4204M/abstract). These future deep-imaging efforts also present severe classification challenges, building upon lessons learned from previous large-scale surveys like the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]].

The primary bottleneck for future transient science will be rapid classification. To this end, ASAS-SN discoveries will rely heavily on upcoming [[spectroscopy-surveys|Spectroscopy surveys]]. Facilities such as the [[wst|WST]] (Wide-field Spectroscopic Telescope) are designed with unprecedented high-multiplex capabilities to provide the massive [[spectroscopic-follow-up|Spectroscopic follow-up]] required to classify the deluge of transients identified by all-sky photometric monitors. Concurrently, projects like SDSS-V are pioneering "panoptic spectroscopy," offering continuous multi-epoch spectral monitoring of millions of objects, which will be highly synergistic for tracking variable sources identified by ASAS-SN [Kollmeier et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv171103234K/abstract).
