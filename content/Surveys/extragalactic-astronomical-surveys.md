**Extragalactic astronomical surveys** are systematic observational programs designed to map the spatial distribution, physical properties, and evolutionary history of galaxies beyond the Milky Way. By cataloging millions of sources across vast cosmological volumes, these programs serve as the empirical foundation for modern [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] and cosmology. They provide the statistical power necessary to trace the [[low-redshift-large-scale-structure|Low-redshift large-scale structure]] of the universe, constrain the parameters of the $\Lambda$CDM model, and investigate the complex interplay between dark matter halos and baryonic physics. From early visual compilations like the [[messier-catalog|Messier Catalog]] to the massively multiplexed [[spectroscopy-surveys|Spectroscopy surveys]] and [[panchromatic-surveys|Panchromatic surveys]] of the 21st century, these campaigns have transformed our understanding of the cosmos from isolated "island universes" into a dynamic, interconnected cosmic web [da Costa et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph.12258D/abstract), [Colless et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASSL..212..227C/abstract).

The methodology of a modern survey is defined by its balance of depth, [[survey-area|Survey area]], and spectral resolution. Wide-field [[deep-imaging-surveys|Deep imaging surveys]] capture the integrated light of galaxies across broad photometric bands, providing foundational catalogs and photometric redshift ($z_{\rm phot}$) estimates for billions of faint sources. Conversely, multi-object spectrographs (MOS) and [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] (IFS) disperse incoming light to yield highly precise spectroscopic redshifts ($z_{\rm spec}$) alongside spatially resolved internal kinematics. Because galaxies emit radiation across the entire electromagnetic spectrum, surveys increasingly rely on multi-wavelength data—combining optical observations with [[radio-surveys|Radio surveys]], X-ray mapping, and infrared data—to construct a complete picture of stellar mass assembly, active galactic nucleus (AGN) activity, and heavily obscured star formation [Lonsdale et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003PASP..115..897L/abstract), [Huchra et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJS..199...26H/abstract). Managing the Petascale data streams generated by these multi-probe approaches now mandates the use of Bayesian inference and unsupervised machine learning for automated [[galaxy-identification|Galaxy identification]] and robust parameter estimation [Mazzarella et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001SPIE.4477...20M/abstract).

Data from extragalactic surveys have established the core timeline of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]], revealing that the cosmic star formation rate peaked during "cosmic noon" ($z \sim 1-2.5$) and that the bimodal distribution of quiescent and star-forming galaxies was firmly in place by $z \sim 1$. Current deep-field campaigns utilizing the James Webb Space Telescope are pushing the observational frontier into the Epoch of Reionization ($z > 6$), identifying candidate galaxies out to $z \sim 18$ to determine their contribution to the intergalactic ionizing photon budget. Despite these successes, significant open questions remain. Recent mid-infrared number counts exhibit stark tensions with established semi-analytical models of dusty galaxies, while the precise role of Population III stars in the early universe has yet to be definitively observed. As the field enters the era of "Stage IV" [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]] and envisions massive 10-meter-class spectroscopic facilities, future observational efforts aim to resolve these astrophysical discrepancies while constraining fundamental physics, including the absolute neutrino mass scale and the signatures of primordial non-Gaussianity.

## Historical Evolution: From Nebulae to Billion-Galaxy Maps

[[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] is a relatively young scientific discipline, rooted in the early cataloging of "nebulous" objects during the 18th and 19th centuries. Early compilations, such as the [[messier-catalog|Messier Catalog]] and John Dreyer's 1888 *New General Catalogue*, documented hundreds of extended optical sources whose true physical nature remained highly controversial. This ambiguity culminated in the 1920 "Great Debate" between Harlow Shapley and Heber Curtis, which was definitively settled in 1925 when Edwin Hubble used Cepheid variable stars in the Andromeda Nebula to prove that these objects were extragalactic "island universes." Shortly thereafter, Hubble demonstrated that the recession velocities of these galaxies scale linearly with their distances, establishing the expansion of the Universe and laying the observational groundwork for all subsequent studies.

For much of the 20th century, extragalactic observations were limited to optical wavebands using photographic plates. The transition to the modern era of [[three-dimensional-galaxy-surveys|Three-dimensional galaxy surveys]] began with the advent of digital CCD detectors and 4-meter class telescopes in the 1990s. Early multi-object spectroscopic campaigns, such as the [[canada-france-redshift-survey|Canada-France Redshift Survey]] and the [[autofib-redshift-survey|AUTOFIB redshift survey]], collected on the order of $1,000$ spectra. Despite their modest sample sizes, these surveys successfully probed the universe out to $z \sim 1$ and provided the first statistical confirmation that the galaxy population undergoes significant luminosity and density evolution. Concurrently, the deployment of space-based observatories yielded the [[hubble-space-telescope-deep-surveys|Hubble Space Telescope Deep Surveys]]. As part of the HST Key Project on the Extragalactic Distance Scale, multiwavelength imaging of galaxies such as M100 was famously utilized to resolve Cepheid variables and accurately quantify recent cosmic evolution [Freedman et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...435L..31F/abstract). These ultra-deep imaging campaigns eventually resolved the morphologies of high-redshift galaxies, identifying elliptical and spiral galaxies out to $z \sim 1$ and highlighting a vast excess of faint blue galaxies at earlier epochs.

At the turn of the 21st century, technological advancements in both instrumentation and computing ushered in the era of massively multiplexed, digital mega-surveys. In the near-infrared, the [[two-micron-all-sky-survey-2mass|Two Micron All-Sky Survey (2MASS)]] produced an unprecedented, dust-penetrated census of the local universe. Notably, 2MASS made highly specific contributions to mapping galaxies in the "Zone of Avoidance," discovering thousands of nearby sources that were previously hidden behind the dust and gas of the Galactic plane [Jarrett et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AJ....120..298J/abstract). Building upon this photometry, the 2MASS Redshift Survey mapped the three-dimensional distribution of baryonic mass within a $300\text{ Mpc}$ volume, achieving $97.6\%$ spectroscopic completeness for a sample of $44,599$ galaxies down to a limiting magnitude of $K_s \leq 11.75$ [Huchra et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJS..199...26H/abstract). 

In the optical regime, wide-field programs scaled up their data acquisition by orders of magnitude. The [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]] utilized the [[anglo-australian-telescope|Anglo-Australian Telescope]] to measure redshifts for $\sim 220,000$ galaxies down to $b_J \sim 19.45$. In parallel, the SDSS (Sloan Digital Sky Survey) revolutionized the field by combining a rigorously calibrated five-band ($u, g, r, i, z$) photometric map with a spectroscopic follow-up of over $10^6$ galaxies brighter than $r < 17.77$. 

Together, these foundational surveys expanded extragalactic datasets from a few thousand objects to millions, directly enabling precision cosmology and large-scale structure mapping. The statistical power generated by projects like SDSS and 2MRS successfully bridged the gap between early photographic classification and the contemporary landscape of Petascale data streams, where automated algorithms now map billion-galaxy distributions across cosmic time. Furthermore, as cataloged in reviews like A Treasury of Nearby Galaxy Surveys (TONGS), the "modern era" of galaxy surveys has matured to not only include large statistical mapping programs but also smaller, ultra-specific targeted projects that provide the local physical benchmarks necessary to complement wide-area panoramic maps [Christie et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PASP..136c3001C/abstract).

## Methodologies in Extragalactic Observation

An overview of the systematic approaches used to map the universe requires an understanding of the complementary techniques that define modern [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]]. The design of any galaxy survey must balance depth, [[survey-area|Survey area]], and spectral resolution, typically employing a combination of wide-field imaging, multi-object spectroscopy, and integral field unit (IFU) technologies.

**Wide-Field Imaging and Photometry**
Deep imaging surveys capture the integrated light of millions of sources across broad wavelength bands, providing the foundational catalogs necessary for morphological classification and [[target-selection|Target selection]]. While highly efficient for mapping vast areas of the sky, broad-band imaging yields only low-resolution spectral energy distributions (SEDs), which must be used to estimate photometric redshifts ($z_{\mathrm{phot}}$). To manage the inherent uncertainties in photometric mapping and source blending, modern surveys increasingly utilize Bayesian neural networks and simulation-based inference to compute full posterior probability distributions rather than relying on deterministic point estimates [Jones et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220207121J/abstract), [Mendoza et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260103422M/abstract). Additionally, automated morphological measurements applied to these catalogs have become crucial for disentangling complex variables, such as environment and luminosity, across huge digital sky survey samples [Wadadekar et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1201.2252W/abstract).

**Multi-Object Spectroscopy**
To overcome the limitations of broad-band imaging, [[spectroscopy-surveys|Spectroscopy surveys]] employ multi-object spectrographs (MOS) to obtain simultaneous spectra for thousands of targeted sources. By dispersing light with a specific resolving power $R = \lambda/\Delta\lambda$, MOS provides precise spectroscopic redshifts ($z_{\mathrm{spec}}$), allowing surveys to map the three-dimensional large-scale structure of the Universe and trace the evolution of galaxy properties. For example, the VIPERS survey mapped the distribution of galaxies at $0.5 < z < 1.2$ with exceptional precision. The survey's first public data release (PDR-1) provided $57,204$ spectroscopic measurements, demonstrating the high sampling rate and completeness achievable in the CFHTLS fields [Garilli et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...562A..23G/abstract), enabling highly detailed measurements of galaxy clustering [Guzzo et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.108G/abstract). Similarly, the [[lega-c-survey|LEGA-C survey]] serves as a benchmark for high-quality spectroscopy at $z > 0.6$, delivering high-precision measurements of stellar velocity dispersions and comprehensive stellar population properties [van der Wel et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..256...44V/abstract). Looking to the future, next-generation facilities are prioritizing extreme multiplexing to acquire hundreds of millions of spectra, providing the statistical power necessary to test fundamental cosmology [Bacon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240512518B/abstract).

**Integral Field Spectroscopy**
While traditional MOS isolates the integrated light of a galaxy using a single fiber or slit, [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] (IFS) resolves spectral data across a galaxy's two-dimensional spatial extent. Surveys utilizing IFUs, such as the SAMI Galaxy Survey, generate 3D data cubes (two spatial dimensions and one spectral dimension) that map internal gas ionization, stellar kinematics, and spatially resolved stellar populations [Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..991C/abstract). This spatially resolved approach allows astronomers to dissect the internal physical processes driving galaxy evolution, moving beyond the limitation of single integrated measurements. Future facility designs are scaling up this technology significantly; for instance, WST plans to incorporate a massive $3 \times 3$ arcmin$^2$ IFU to perform deep, blind searches for serendipitous sources and detailed structural analyses without the prerequisite of high-resolution pre-imaging [Bacon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240512518B/abstract).

## Panchromatic Surveying: Probing Galaxies Across the Spectrum

Galaxies emit radiation across the entire electromagnetic spectrum, with distinct wavelength regimes tracing different physical components. [[panchromatic-surveys|Panchromatic surveys]] are therefore essential for a holistic understanding of galaxy evolution, as observations in individual bands capture only a fraction of the underlying astrophysical processes [Mauduit et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PASP..124..714M/abstract). 

Unobscured star formation is robustly traced in the ultraviolet by missions like [[galex|GALEX]], which mapped the distribution of young, massive stars across the sky [Seibert et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AAS...21934001S/abstract). However, much of the star formation in the Universe—particularly during "cosmic noon" ($z \sim 1-2.5$)—is heavily obscured by dust. Large-area programs such as the [[herschel-multi-tiered-extragalactic-survey|Herschel Multi-tiered Extragalactic Survey]] (HerMES) and the Spitzer Wide-area Infrared Extragalactic Survey (SWIRE) utilize mid-to-far infrared and submillimeter wavelengths to penetrate this obscuration and capture reprocessed light from dusty starbursts [Oliver et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.1614O/abstract), [Lonsdale et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003PASP..115..897L/abstract). Additionally, the AKARI far-infrared all-sky survey played a pivotal role in this domain, delivering a highly comprehensive map of the local dusty universe to provide crucial local benchmarks for understanding the evolution of high-redshift dusty galaxy populations [Serjeant et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PKAS...27..305S/abstract). Indeed, theoretical models based on earlier 15 $\mu$m ISOCAM data successfully predicted that Spitzer's 24 $\mu$m source counts would be overwhelmingly dominated by exactly these starburst galaxies at $z \sim 1-2$ [Gruppioni et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...618L...9G/abstract). To accurately measure the statistical background of these populations, unique observing strategies were employed; for instance, the DEBRIS survey successfully secured 100 $\mu$m extragalactic number counts using a sparse sampling technique, providing a measurement completely free from cosmic variance [Sibthorpe et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428L...6S/abstract). 

At slightly shorter wavelengths, near-infrared surveys are strongly favoured for measuring integrated stellar mass, as they probe the older, less-obscured stellar populations that dominate a galaxy's total baryonic mass [Huchra et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJS..199...26H/abstract). Furthermore, campaigns like the Spitzer Infrared Nearby Galaxies Survey (SINGS) established a comprehensive infrared imaging and spectroscopic baseline for 75 nearby galaxies, designed specifically to link global star formation properties to the localized physics of the interstellar medium [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003PASP..115..928K/abstract).

Despite the success of infrared observational campaigns, tensions remain between the data and current theoretical frameworks. Deep mid-infrared number counts from the JWST Mid-Infrared Instrument (MIRI) have recently revealed significant discrepancies with predictions from semi-analytical models. These models struggle to reproduce the observed abundance of extreme, high-mass dusty galaxies, likely due to incomplete theoretical treatments of mid-infrared polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) emission features and dust heating mechanisms at high redshift [Stone et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...972...62S/abstract). To further probe these obscured processes, large programs using the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) are increasingly pushing studies of $z > 4$ galaxies into far-infrared wavelengths. The ALPINE survey, for example, focuses specifically on tracing the gas and dust content in normal star-forming galaxies to construct a complete timeline of early mass assembly [Faisst et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..341...12F/abstract).

At the extremes of the spectrum, non-thermal and high-energy processes require dedicated radio, millimeter, and X-ray mapping. Low-frequency radio surveys provide an orientation-independent method for selecting active galactic nuclei (AGN). Catalogs of [[radio-selected-galaxies|Radio-selected galaxies]], such as the MWA GLEAM 4-Jy (G4Jy) sample, identify thousands of the brightest radio sources without the severe dust extinction biases inherent to optical selection [White et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181001226W/abstract), [White et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250513619W/abstract). Additionally, blind HI (neutral hydrogen) surveys, specifically the HIPASS Bright Galaxy Catalog, have identified dozens of gas-rich galaxies that were previously hidden from optical surveys due to dust extinction directly in the Galactic plane [Ryan-Weber et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002AJ....124.1954R/abstract). 

In the millimeter band, surveys are adept at detecting rare populations like Gigahertz Peaked Spectrum (GPS) sources and high-redshift dusty galaxies, providing vital links between AGN feedback and broader galaxy evolution [De Zotti et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002astro.ph..4038D/abstract). The South Pole Telescope (SPT) survey exemplifies observational synergies in this band by utilizing the thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (tSZ) effect to detect massive galaxy clusters, relying heavily on subsequent optical and near-infrared follow-up to ascertain precise distances and characteristics [Desai et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JPhCS.375c2011D/abstract). Conversely, high-energy X-ray surveys, substantially augmented by the [[erosita|eROSITA]] mission, probe the hot intra-cluster medium (ICM) and accreting supermassive black holes. Furthermore, high-energy survey efforts now extend into the very-high-energy gamma-ray regime; observatories such as the High Altitude Water Cherenkov (HAWC) facility are actively conducting updated surveys of active galaxies, successfully detecting energetic emissions from nearby AGNs like M87 and Mkn 421 [Ureña-Mena et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231002484U/abstract). Multi-probe analyses combining X-ray emission, gamma-ray surveys, the tSZ effect in the cosmic microwave background, and weak gravitational lensing are now effectively breaking degeneracies between cosmological parameters and ICM astrophysics in distant clusters [Shirasaki et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491..235S/abstract).

Finally, the synthesis of multi-wavelength data is critical for estimating redshifts and physical properties for the millions of faint sources that lack [[spectroscopic-follow-up|Spectroscopic follow-up]]. For example, the [[help-survey|Herschel Extragalactic Legacy Project (HELP)]] utilizes a multi-wavelength dataset encompassing millions of sources—from the ultraviolet to the far-infrared—specifically to test complex spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting models and to rigorously calibrate stellar mass estimation modules across different cosmic epochs [Małek et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..341...39M/abstract). The optimal combination of wavelength coverage to minimize photometric redshift uncertainties remains a key design consideration for future surveys.

## Redshift Determination and Distance Estimation

Establishing the third dimension is critical for mapping the large-scale structure of the Universe and understanding galaxy evolution. While angular positions are easily measured, radial distances require dedicated observational strategies, which are broadly divided into spectroscopic redshifts, photometric estimates, and redshift-independent distance indicators.

**Spectroscopic Redshifts**
Spectroscopy provides the most accurate redshifts by dispersing a galaxy's light to identify discrete atomic transitions, such as the $4000 \mathrm{\AA}$ break, the Balmer series, or the Lyman-$\alpha$ line. Because it isolates specific spectral features, this method typically achieves relative errors of less than $0.1\%$. For example, the VIPERS survey secured highly precise measurements with an rms error of:
$$ \frac{\sigma_z}{1+z} = 4.7 \times 10^{-4} $$
for over 50,000 galaxies out to $z \sim 1.2$ [Guzzo et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.108G/abstract). However, obtaining high signal-to-noise spectra is extremely time-consuming and limited by target flux, restricting spectroscopic completeness to relatively bright samples.

**Photometric and Spectrophotometric Redshifts**
For faint galaxies beyond the reach of multi-object spectrographs, surveys must rely on photo-$z$ estimators. These methods use broad-band photometry to coarsely sample the spectral energy distribution (SED). The precision of these estimates is highly dependent on the number of filters and the galaxy's spectral type; errors typically range from $3\%$ to $10\%$. Star-forming galaxies lacking a pronounced $4000 \mathrm{\AA}$ break generally yield much larger uncertainties than quiescent populations. When surveys incorporate a large number of intermediate bands, the resulting [[spectrophotometric-redshifts|Spectrophotometric redshifts]] can achieve higher precision of $\sim 3\%$.

Modern analyses increasingly apply machine learning, such as Bayesian Neural Networks, to derive full probability density functions for photometric redshifts rather than single point estimates, a requirement for precise cosmological parameter inference [Jones et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220207121J/abstract). Within the literature, there remains an active debate over the optimal wavelength coverage for next-generation photo-$z$ catalogs. While common wisdom dictates that optical data is best augmented by infrared coverage, recent information-theoretic studies demonstrate that the inclusion of ultraviolet photometry significantly benefits redshift recovery for specific populations. However, this advantage is frequently tempered by the lower detection rates of UV-faint targets at high redshift [Scott et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240920443S/abstract), [Scott et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985..227S/abstract).

**Clustering-Based Estimation**
An alternative technique for constraining distances in faint samples is clustering-based redshift estimation. This data-driven approach infers the spatial distribution of a target photometric sample by cross-correlating its sky positions with those of a reference spectroscopic sample, such as quasars. By measuring clustering amplitudes across different physical scales, researchers can statistically map the photometric observables to a redshift probability distribution without requiring spectra of the faint targets themselves [Ménard et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1303.4722M/abstract).

**Redshift-Independent Distances**
In the local Universe (typically within $\sim 300 \mathrm{\, Mpc}$), the cosmic expansion is heavily perturbed by peculiar velocities induced by local gravitational potentials. Consequently, separating the Hubble flow from structural kinematics requires redshift-independent distance indicators. These rely on established scaling relations, such as the Tully-Fisher relation for spirals ($L \propto V_{\rm max}^\alpha$, where $\alpha \sim 3$) and the $D_n-\sigma$ relation for ellipticals ($D_n \propto \sigma_0^\beta$, where $\beta \sim 1.2$). Foundational efforts to calibrate these scaling relations include the Nearby Early-type Galaxies Survey (ENEAR), an all-sky survey of early-type galaxies specifically designed to derive an internally consistent $D_n-\sigma$ relation for robust distance estimation and peculiar velocity mapping [Wegner et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..201...62W/abstract). For nearby galaxies ($\lesssim 10 \mathrm{\, Mpc}$), resolving individual stellar populations allows the use of the Tip of the Red Giant Branch (TRGB) as a highly reliable distance measure [Anand et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....162...80A/abstract).

To synthesize the vast array of available measurements, foundational repositories such as the Lyon-Meudon Extragalactic Database (LEDA) have historically played a vital role in standardizing and homogenizing the astrophysical parameters of galaxies [Paturel et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997A&AS..124..109P/abstract). Concurrently, the [[nasa-ipac-extragalactic-database|NASA/IPAC Extragalactic Database]] (NED) curates NED-D, an extensive catalog containing over $100,000$ redshift-independent distances for more than $28,000$ galaxies. This repository standardizes estimates derived from 75 different indicators, providing the community with the empirical foundation necessary to disentangle the Hubble flow from local cosmic structure [Steer et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AJ....153...37S/abstract). Expanding heavily on these efforts, the Siena Galaxy Atlas (SGA-2020) provides a modern multiwavelength imaging atlas containing precise geometric and photometric data for over 380,000 nearby galaxies, acting as a crucial reference sample for both star formation studies and local velocity field measurements [Moustakas et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..269....3M/abstract). Modern value-added local galaxy catalogs, such as the Heraklion Extragalactic Catalogue (HECATE), further facilitate synthesis by providing comprehensively matched data specifically tailored for multimessenger astrophysics and the initial distance characterization of transient events [Kovlakas et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.1896K/abstract).

## Galaxy Evolution and the Morphology-Environment Relation

By mapping the distribution of galaxies across cosmic time, large-scale surveys provide critical constraints on the star formation history of the Universe and the physical drivers of galaxy assembly. Observations across multiple wavelengths demonstrate that the global star formation rate density, $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$, peaked during "cosmic noon" at $z \sim 1-2.5$ and has subsequently declined by roughly an order of magnitude to the present day. Because much of the star formation at this epoch was driven by heavily dust-obscured starbursts, tracking this history requires deep infrared and sub-millimeter data. However, current semi-analytical models of galaxy formation still struggle to reproduce the observed number counts of high-mass dusty galaxies, particularly diverging from recent James Webb Space Telescope mid-infrared measurements [Cirasuolo et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..399..153C/abstract), [Stone et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...972...62S/abstract). 

Deep spectroscopic and photometric surveys have shown that the bimodal distribution of galaxies—a star-forming "blue cloud" and a quiescent "red sequence"—is firmly established by $z \sim 1$ [Weiner et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...620..595W/abstract). Crucial insights from the DEEP2 Galaxy Redshift Survey illuminated this relationship between galaxy properties and their local environment at $z \sim 1$, demonstrating how dense environments accelerate the quenching of star formation and directly drive the assembly of the red sequence at these early epochs [Cooper et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..235..167C/abstract). Over the last 8 Gyr, the characteristic number density, $\phi^*$, of the red sequence has nearly quadrupled, while the density of the blue population has remained remarkably constant. This evolution highlights a dramatic collapse in the number density of massive star-forming galaxies ($\log(M_*/M_\odot) > 11$) after $z \sim 0.8$ as they undergo quenching and migrate to the red sequence [Haines et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021essp.confE..51H/abstract). The local baseline for these evolutionary tracks is anchored by the [[gama-survey|Galaxy And Mass Assembly (GAMA) survey]], while the higher-redshift structural evolution is captured by programs like the [[devils-survey|DEVILS survey]], which maps environments over the past $\sim 6$ Gyr with $90\%$ spectroscopic completeness down to $Y < 21.2$ mag [Liske et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.2087L/abstract), [Bravo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250913530B/abstract).

A major focus of these surveys is the morphology-density relation, which quantifies the impact of local environmental density on a galaxy's evolutionary trajectory. Observations confirm that the specific star formation rate (sSFR) of galaxies declines with increasing environmental density at $z \sim 1.5-2$ [Duivenvoorden et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..277D/abstract). However, the literature remains ambiguous and conflicting regarding whether the star-forming "main sequence" strictly depends on the environment at earlier ($z > 2$) or later ($z < 1.5$) epochs [Duivenvoorden et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..277D/abstract). Dense environments also subtly modify active galactic nucleus (AGN) demographics; for instance, satellite galaxies in clusters show increased AGN fractions but decreased overall AGN luminosities compared to their field counterparts [Bravo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250913530B/abstract).

As modern surveys push to Petascale data volumes, characterizing [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] and morphology relies increasingly on unsupervised machine learning. These algorithms can autonomously separate early- and late-type galaxies directly from pixel data without human-labeled training sets [Hocking et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.1108H/abstract). These automated morphological studies are deeply indebted to established local multi-wavelength databases, such as the EFIGI catalogue, which provides highly detailed visual classifications for 4,458 nearby galaxies densely sampling all Hubble types. This catalogue continues to serve as an essential local benchmark for morphological calibration [Baillard et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...532A..74B/abstract).

## Probing Large-Scale Structure and Cosmological Parameters

Extragalactic astronomical surveys provide the statistical power necessary to map the distribution of dark matter and constrain the fundamental parameters of the $\Lambda$CDM cosmological model. By treating galaxies as luminous tracers of the underlying cosmic web, researchers extract cosmological information primarily through two avenues: the spatial clustering of galaxies and the weak gravitational lensing of [[background-galaxies|Background galaxies]].

**Galaxy Clustering and Baryon Acoustic Oscillations**
The primary statistic used to quantify [[low-redshift-large-scale-structure|Low-redshift large-scale structure]] and high-redshift clustering is the two-point correlation function, $\xi(r)$. In the local universe, the observed real-space correlation function of optically selected $L^*$ galaxies at scales $r < 20 h^{-1}$ Mpc can be approximated by a power law. Because line-of-sight distances are inferred from redshift, peculiar velocities systematically distort the observed clustering. Survey analyses separate these redshift-space distortions using the projected correlation function $w(r_p)$, enabling simultaneous measurements of real-space clustering and the structure growth rate, parameterized by $f\sigma_8(z)$ [Guidi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215294G/abstract).

To precisely measure baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO) and clustering amplitudes, observational uncertainties must be managed through a careful balance of shot noise and cosmic variance, which drives the requirement for massive [[survey-area|Survey area]]s in next-generation projects [Pearl et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...925..180P/abstract). Furthermore, for surveys lacking complete spectroscopy, spatial cross-correlation between sparse spectroscopic reference samples and dense photometric datasets provides a method to robustly infer the [[redshift-distribution|Redshift distribution]] of photometric galaxies without individual spectra [Ménard et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1303.4722M/abstract).

Future massively multiplexed facilities—aiming for $\mathcal{O}(1000)$ simultaneous spectra over wide fields—seek to push galaxy clustering measurements to unprecedented precision. These post-Stage IV surveys target stringent cosmological constraints on the absolute neutrino mass scale of $\sum m_\nu \max \lesssim 0.015$ eV (sufficient to resolve the neutrino mass hierarchy) as well as bounds on primordial non-Gaussianity at the level of $f_{\mathrm{NL}} \sim 1$ [Guidi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215294G/abstract).

**Weak Gravitational Lensing and Multi-Probe Synergies**
While galaxy clustering relies on the complex mapping between luminous baryons and dark matter halos, cosmic shear—the coherent distortion of galaxy shapes due to weak gravitational lensing—probes the intervening mass distribution directly. [[large-area-weak-lensing-surveys|Large area weak lensing surveys]] serve as a primary tool for mapping dark matter, but the literature explicitly notes that their accuracy remains constrained by severe systematic bottlenecks. Specifically, precise shear measurement requires the accurate deconvolution of noise and spatially variable Point Spread Function (PSF) kernels from the intrinsic shapes of galaxies, an ongoing algorithmic challenge [Kitching et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1009.0779K/abstract), [Liaudat et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023FrASS..1058213L/abstract).

To break degeneracies between cosmological parameters and baryonic astrophysics, modern surveys increasingly rely on joint multi-probe analyses. Combining weak lensing, galaxy clustering, and cluster abundance measurements from [[x-ray-surveys|X-ray surveys]] and thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (tSZ) observations significantly improves constraints. This multiwavelength synergy isolates the underlying cosmological signal from complex intra-cluster medium (ICM) physics, enabling robust constraints on dark energy and structure formation even when incorporating distant, faint galaxy clusters [Shirasaki et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491..235S/abstract).

## The High-Redshift Frontier and the Epoch of Reionization

Extragalactic astronomical surveys have progressively pushed the observational horizon to the first billion years of cosmic time ($z > 6$), a critical period encompassing the Epoch of Reionization. During this era, the neutral intergalactic medium was ionized by the first generations of stars and galaxies. Identifying these primordial sources primarily relies on two targeted photometric techniques. The first is [[lyman-break-selection-and-galaxies|Lyman-Break Selection and Galaxies]], which isolates objects exhibiting a pronounced flux deficit blueward of the rest-frame Lyman limit at $\lambda \approx 912$ \AA. This "dropout" feature arises from the intrinsic opacity of stellar atmospheres combined with the large ionization cross-section of intervening neutral hydrogen gas. The second technique utilizes narrow-band imaging to detect strong Ly$\alpha$ emission at rest-frame $\lambda = 1216$ \AA\ from Ly$\alpha$ emitters (LAEs). Together, these methods have generated robust catalogs of star-forming galaxies at $z > 3$, providing crucial constraints on mass assembly in the early universe [Finkelstein et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PASA...33...37F/abstract).

Historically, the [[ultra-high-z-galaxy-search|Ultra-high-z galaxy search]] relied on deep-field campaigns such as the [[goods-survey|GOODS survey]], the [[candels-survey|CANDELS survey]], and the UltraVISTA survey—a critical deep near-infrared program providing essential information on the origin of cosmic structures and high-redshift galaxy mass functions [McCracken et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Msngr.154...29M/abstract). These efforts utilized space-based and premier ground-based telescopes to measure the rest-frame ultraviolet luminosity function and stellar mass functions at $z \sim 6-8$ [Finkelstein et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PASA...33...37F/abstract). Complementing these photometric campaigns, prominent high-redshift spectroscopic efforts like the VANDELS survey have provided crucial high-signal-to-noise spectra for galaxies at redshifts between $z \sim 1$ and $6.5$, offering robust constraints on stellar populations and metallicities across cosmic time [Garilli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...647A.150G/abstract).

The advent of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has catalyzed a paradigm shift in our understanding of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. The JWST Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey ([[jades|JADES]]) has conducted exceptionally deep near-infrared imaging and spectroscopy over the legacy GOODS-North and South fields. Utilizing NIRCam photometry across 15 filters, JADES has identified 717 galaxy candidates at $z > 8$ within a 125 square arcminute area, pushing the current extragalactic frontier out to photometric redshifts of $z_{\mathrm{phot}} \sim 18$ [Hainline et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964...71H/abstract). Follow-up NIRSpec and FRESCO observations have successfully confirmed spectroscopic redshifts out to $z \sim 9$, demonstrating excellent agreement with photometric estimates ($\langle \Delta z \rangle = 0.26$) and effectively ruling out widespread contamination by low-mass stellar interlopers [Hainline et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964...71H/abstract).

These deep multi-wavelength surveys are critical for determining whether the observed galaxy populations can fully account for cosmic reionization. While there is consensus that vigorous galaxy assembly and chemical enrichment occurred early in the universe, the precise contribution of faint star-forming galaxies to the total ionizing photon budget remains an active subject of tension and investigation in the literature. Specifically, the complex interplay between the escape fraction of ionizing photons and the faint-end slope of the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] requires further constraint to build a complete model of reionization [Bunker et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..352..342B/abstract). Furthermore, future deep-field observations aim to exploit the high sensitivity and spectral resolution of JWST to search for the first signatures of Population III stars, such as pristine He II emission lines, which have yet to be conclusively detected in these early galaxies [Bunker et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..352..342B/abstract).

## Big Data, Machine Learning, and Simulation-Based Inference

The transition to Petascale data streams in modern [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] has rendered the manual inspection of [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]] obsolete. With next-generation facilities such as the [[dark-energy-survey|Dark Energy Survey]] and other [[legacy-surveys|Legacy surveys]] poised to observe billions of galaxies across cosmic time, the universal adoption of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning has become a practical necessity. While the universal adoption of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning has recently become a practical necessity for these massive streams, algorithmic approaches have historical roots; artificial neural networks were successfully applied as early as the mid-1990s for foundational tasks like star/galaxy separation in near-infrared surveys such as DENIS and 2MASS [Bertin et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Ap&SS.217...49B/abstract). Prior to the full adoption of modern deep learning frameworks, pioneering initiatives like the Galaxy Zoo project effectively utilized crowdsourced visual classification to address the initial challenge of labeling massive survey datasets [Masters et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015HiA....16....1M/abstract). 

Today, deep learning algorithms are standard tools for automating foundational tasks, including [[galaxy-identification|Galaxy identification]] (such as star-galaxy separation) and the automated detection of strong gravitational lenses [Djorgovski et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221201493D/abstract), [Madireddy et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191103867M/abstract). Specialized automated pipelines also leverage sophisticated image analysis to handle real-time transient events; for example, the ELEPHANT pipeline automatically filters and identifies hostless transients within vast data streams from surveys like the Zwicky Transient Facility [Pessi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A.181P/abstract). Expanding on transient detection paradigms, modern deep learning architectures such as the "ConvEntion" approach seamlessly integrate attention-based transformers and convolutions to highly accurately classify astronomical image time series [Bairouk et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...673A.141B/abstract).

Unsupervised machine learning has emerged as a particularly powerful paradigm, allowing researchers to group galaxies by morphology and photometry without relying on human-directed labels. For instance, techniques like Self-Organizing Maps (SOMs) successfully map high-dimensional spectral energy distribution (SED) spaces, providing a visual framework to identify complex [[selection-function|Selection function]] biases [Sanjaripour et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..202S/abstract). Similarly, unsupervised clustering algorithms have demonstrated the ability to cleanly segment early and late-type galaxies based solely on pixel data [Hocking et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.1108H/abstract). However, the literature reveals a significant tension regarding these techniques: different unsupervised methods—or even small variations in the chosen data sub-spaces—can produce markedly different classification outcomes. This ambiguity suggests that no single optimal categorization exists for highly dimensional data, necessitating the use of ensemble approaches and interactive visual analytics to interpret the results [Reis et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&C....3400437R/abstract).

The sheer volume of survey data also introduces severe computational bottlenecks for parameter estimation. Traditional analysis tools for fitting the light profiles of galaxies can require computing times of $\gtrsim 10$ hours per object, which is prohibitively expensive for large samples. To circumvent this, researchers are increasingly adopting Simulation-based Inference (SBI). By leveraging Monte Carlo techniques to properly marginalize over out-of-distribution measurement errors and missing data, SBI can reduce parameter inference times from hours to approximately $1.5$ minutes per object, drastically expanding the feasibility of analyzing massive datasets [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221103747W/abstract).

Concurrently, there is a major paradigm shift in cataloging driven by Bayesian modeling, moving the field away from deterministic point estimates toward fully probabilistic inference. To properly infer overarching demographic properties from these large survey datasets, researchers are increasingly moving toward multilevel and hierarchical Bayesian modeling to rigorously account for pervasive selection effects, such as Malmquist and Eddington bias [Loredo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191112337L/abstract). Deep learning frameworks, such as Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs), are now extensively deployed to estimate photometric redshifts. Unlike standard neural networks, BNNs provide the critical uncertainty estimates required to confidently constrain cosmological parameters [Jones et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220207121J/abstract). Additionally, Neural Posterior Estimation (NPE) is utilized to automate the deblending of overlapping sources and to model spatially varying backgrounds and point spread functions (PSFs). By approximating the full posterior distribution over the number of light sources, their centroid locations, and their morphological types, NPE generates robust probabilistic catalogs that accurately account for blending-induced systematic errors [Patel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250300156P/abstract), [Mendoza et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260103422M/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions: The Era of Stage IV Surveys

Following the foundational successes of Stage III programs like the [[dark-energy-survey|Dark Energy Survey]], observational cosmology is entering the era of Stage IV [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]]. These facilities—most notably the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's LSST, the ESA Euclid mission, and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope—are designed to map the universe with unprecedented volume and precision. A proposed initiative for the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, known as NANCY, aims to provide uniform near-infrared imaging over the entire sky at high spatial resolution ($\sim 0.1$ arcsec), probing stellar and galactic populations $100$ times fainter than Gaia out to the edge of the Milky Way [Han et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230611784H/abstract). 

However, the overarching scientific goals of the 2030s and 2040s extend beyond characterizing dark energy to targeting fundamental physics limits. Key open questions include determining the absolute scale and hierarchy of neutrino masses and detecting primordial non-Gaussianity, which would provide direct signatures of inflationary physics in the early universe [Guidi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215294G/abstract). Stage IV photometric surveys will face fundamental bottlenecks in these regimes due to redshift uncertainties and cosmic variance.

To surpass these limitations, the community is developing concepts for massively multiplexed, wide-field [[ground-based-spectroscopic-instruments|Ground-Based Spectroscopic Instruments]] in the 10-meter class. Complementing these future survey structures are key ongoing observational efforts such as the MOONRISE survey, a major GTO spectroscopic program specifically targeting cosmic noon ($z \sim 1-2.5$) by fully utilizing the high multiplexing and expansive near-infrared coverage of the MOONS instrument [Maiolino et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Msngr.180...24M/abstract). Looking even further ahead, the proposed Wide-field Spectroscopic Telescope (WST), a 12-meter facility with a 3 square degree field of view, aims to operate with a multiplexing capability of $20,000$ fibers. Over a five-year nominal mission, WST would acquire 250 million galaxy spectra [Bacon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240512518B/abstract). Such massive spectroscopic datasets are strictly required to push cosmological constraints on the sum of neutrino masses to $\Sigma m_\nu \lesssim 0.015\,\text{eV}$ and to detect multi-field inflationary signatures at the level of $f_{\text{NL}} \sim 1$ [Guidi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215294G/abstract). Similarly, the Maunakea Spectroscopic Explorer (MSE) aims to provide critical high-resolution spectroscopic follow-up for overlapping [[all-sky-surveys|All-sky surveys]] [McConnachie et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160600060M/abstract), [The MSE Science Team et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190404907T/abstract).

Unlocking the scientific potential of billions of targets requires a paradigm shift in data processing and [[target-selection|Target selection]]. As deep, dense fields inevitably suffer from severe source blending, traditional deterministic deblending is being rendered obsolete [Mendoza et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260103422M/abstract). Consequently, advanced machine learning frameworks and probabilistic inference techniques have become structural requirements for [[future-astronomical-facilities-and-surveys|Future Astronomical Facilities and Surveys]]. By providing rigorous uncertainty quantification and marginalizing over complex instrumental systematics, these computational methods ensure that next-generation pipelines can efficiently extract robust cosmological constraints from Petascale datasets.
