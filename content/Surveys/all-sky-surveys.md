**All-sky surveys** are systematic astronomical observational campaigns designed to map the entire celestial sphere—or a statistically significant fraction of it—down to a specified uniform detection limit. Unlike targeted observations of [[discrete-astronomical-sources|Discrete astronomical sources]], these wide-area programs provide the continuous, high-completeness datasets necessary to evaluate the statistical properties of astrophysical populations and the large-scale distribution of matter. Historically transitioning from early visual [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]] of nebulae to digital mapping with large-format CCD mosaics, modern surveys now operate comprehensively across the entire electromagnetic spectrum. Foundational optical and infrared efforts, such as the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] and the digitized USNO-B catalog, revolutionized observational astrophysics by enabling the automated extraction and characterization of hundreds of millions of objects, permanently altering the scale of structural [[galaxy-mapping|Galaxy mapping]] [Loveday et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ConPh..43..437L/abstract), [Monet et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....125..984M/abstract).

By federating panchromatic data—from low-frequency radio continuum to high-energy X-rays—all-sky surveys mitigate the severe selection biases inherent to single-wavelength observations [Brunner et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..225..135B/abstract). In cosmology, deep [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] programs trace the three-dimensional cosmic web, utilizing baryonic acoustic oscillations and peculiar velocities to constrain dark energy parameters and the growth of structure. On a local scale, Galactic archaeology leverages wide-field astrometry and massively multiplexed [[spectroscopy-surveys|Spectroscopy surveys]] to reconstruct the Milky Way's formation history by mapping stellar kinematics and chemical abundances. Furthermore, the advent of high-cadence [[synoptic-optical-surveys|Synoptic optical surveys]] and [[x-ray-surveys|X-ray surveys]] has opened the time domain, transforming static maps into dynamic datasets capable of identifying variable sources, finding rogue microlensing planets, and tracking transient phenomena through dedicated [[stellar-transient-and-supernova-surveys|Stellar Transient and Supernova Surveys]].

The petabyte-scale data volumes generated by modern facilities have necessitated profound shifts in [[scientific-data-analysis|Scientific data analysis]], relying increasingly on machine learning algorithms for automated source classification and techniques like [[intensity-mapping|Intensity mapping]] to capture the integrated emission of ultra-faint, unresolved populations. Looking forward, the next generation of Stage-IV [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]]—driven by future facilities such as the Square Kilometre Array and the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]]—aims to address critical open questions in fundamental physics, including the precise measurement of primordial non-Gaussianity. However, resolving more extreme challenges like the neutrino mass hierarchy will remain beyond their reach, requiring future "Beyond Stage IV" next-generation facilities in the coming decades [Guidi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215294G/abstract). Furthermore, significant observational barriers and methodological tensions remain. Chief among these are the active debate over whether photometric redshift estimation can meet the stringent precision requirements of Stage-IV cosmology without massive spectroscopic calibration samples, the persistent "Galactic extinction horizon" obscuring the far side of the Milky Way disk, and the theoretically unexplained high frequency of "hard-only" accretion states observed in Galactic black hole binary outbursts.

## Historical Evolution: From Messier to Digital Mapping

The systematic mapping of the night sky began long before the advent of modern digital detectors. By the late eighteenth century, the proliferation of telescopic observations led to the discovery of numerous "fuzzy," extended sources, collectively referred to as nebulae. Between 1771 and 1784, the French astronomer Charles Messier compiled the [[messier-catalog|Messier Catalog]], an inventory of over 100 of these bright, non-stellar objects, primarily to prevent them from being mistaken for comets during visual searches. Today, these historical designations remain in standard use for prominent [[messier-objects|Messier Objects]], such as the Andromeda Galaxy (M31).

For most of the twentieth century, [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]] and surveys relied entirely on photographic emulsions. Wide-field telescopes utilizing Schmidt plates enabled the systematic mapping of the celestial sphere in [[visible-light|Visible light]]. However, these photographic surveys were inherently analog, and source extraction was initially a manual, labor-intensive process. Early systematic mapping efforts like the CfA Redshift Survey pioneered the discovery of large-scale "bubbles," filaments, and voids in the three-dimensional distribution of galaxies [Huchra et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ASPC....5...41H/abstract). The transition from purely photographic to hybrid "photo-digital" surveys marked a major paradigm shift in observational astronomy. Large-scale digitization efforts utilized high-speed microdensitometers and automated measuring machines to scan archival photographic plates. A notable milestone from this era is the [[apm-galaxy-survey|APM Galaxy Survey]], which constructed its source catalog from Automated Plate Measuring (APM) machine scans of photographic plates. The APM catalog reached a limiting magnitude of $b_j \sim 19.45$ and served as the foundational target list for the massive [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]].

The culmination of the digitized photographic era is exemplified by the USNO-B Catalog [Monet et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....125..984M/abstract). By scanning 7,435 Schmidt plates acquired from various sky surveys over a 50-year period, this effort generated a comprehensive all-sky catalog of over $1.04 \times 10^9$ objects derived from more than $3.6 \times 10^9$ separate observations. The USNO-B Catalog achieved completeness down to $V=21$ and provided an astrometric accuracy of $0.2''$ at J2000, along with a $0.3$ mag photometric accuracy across up to five optical bands. Furthermore, the automated classification algorithms achieved an 85% accuracy rate in distinguishing stars from non-stellar objects [Monet et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....125..984M/abstract). 

Before the full transition to optical CCD surveying, near-infrared efforts also produced landmark digital catalogs. The 2MASS Extended Source Catalog (XSC) provided a foundational census of baryonic mass concentrations over the whole sky out to $z=0.04$, maintaining completeness even at low Galactic latitudes [Huchra et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IAUS..216..170H/abstract). At the turn of the twenty-first century, astronomical mapping fully transitioned into the digital era with the deployment of large-format CCD mosaics. The [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (SDSS) pioneered this shift, conducting a highly calibrated, continuous imaging survey over more than a quarter of the sky [Loveday et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ConPh..43..437L/abstract). By utilizing five custom broad optical bands ([[ugriz-photometry|ugriz photometry]]), SDSS dramatically outpaced its photographic predecessors in both photometric precision and depth, detecting roughly 50 million galaxies down to a limiting magnitude of $g \sim 23$ [Loveday et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ConPh..43..437L/abstract). This technological transition from static photographic plates to digital CCD imaging fundamentally altered the scale of cosmology, enabling the automated extraction, characterization, and classification of billions of discrete sources.

## Panchromatic and Multi-Wavelength Mapping

To achieve a comprehensive census of the universe’s energy content, large-scale structure, and physical processes, contemporary astrophysics relies heavily on the federation of data across the electromagnetic spectrum. Observations confined to a single wavelength regime are subject to severe selection biases; thus, cross-matching multi-wavelength data is now mandatory to build robust 3D maps of the cosmic web [Brunner et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..225..135B/abstract), [Bilicki et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016pas..conf..165B/abstract). 

In the ultraviolet regime, GALEX provided an unprecedented all-sky census of recent star formation. Cross-matching GALEX detections with the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] enables robust estimations of star-formation histories that optical data alone cannot provide. For instance, galaxies hosting recent starbursts ($<20$ Myr) can be unambiguously identified when their far-ultraviolet (FUV) flux exceeds their near-ultraviolet (NUV) flux [Agüeros et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AJ....130.1022A/abstract). Furthermore, optically unresolved GALEX targets are heavily dominated by hot white dwarfs, blue turnoff stars, and low-redshift quasars ($z < 2.2$) [Agüeros et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AJ....130.1022A/abstract). Additionally, at optical and near-infrared wavelengths, projects like SKYSURF have repurposed over 200,000 archival Hubble Space Telescope (HST) exposures to serve as an absolute photometer, successfully constraining [[diffuse-astrophysical-emission|Diffuse Astrophysical Emission]] and zodiacal emission across the sky [Windhorst et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AJ....164..141W/abstract).

In the infrared, surveys trace older stellar mass distributions, obscured active galactic nuclei (AGN), and interstellar dust. [[akari|AKARI]] resolved the far-infrared emission in thousands of nearby galaxies, providing critical $z \sim 0$ benchmarks for evaluating the evolution of high-redshift dusty starbursts [Serjeant et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PKAS...27..305S/abstract). The [[wide-field-infrared-survey-explorer|Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer]] mapped the sky in four mid-infrared bands (3.4, 4.6, 12, and 22 $\mu$m); when combined with optical redshift surveys and cut to high-completeness limits (e.g., fluxes $>80\ \mu\text{Jy}$ at 3.4 $\mu$m), it yields highly reliable extragalactic catalogs [Lake et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...866...44L/abstract). In the submillimeter regime, large-area surveys (for example, utilizing SCUBA-2) are critical for characterizing obscured star-forming galaxy populations that are frequently missed in traditional optical catalogs [Wang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200107447W/abstract). Indeed, submillimeter galaxies (SMGs) constitute a cosmologically significant population of luminous high-redshift dusty galaxies that account for a major fraction of the energy output in the early universe [Blain et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhR...369..111B/abstract). However, interpretations of panchromatic dust emission are not without tension; measurements of gas-to-dust ratios exhibit discrepancies between targeted Herschel observations and all-sky far-infrared surveys (such as IRAS and Planck) due to fundamental limitations in the absolute zero-point calibration of diffuse versus dense interstellar media [Roman-Duval et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...841...72R/abstract).

Radio continuum surveys reveal non-thermal synchrotron emission, avoiding the dust obscuration that plagues optical observations. The [[gleam-survey|GLEAM survey]] (GaLactic and Extragalactic All-sky MWA) maps the southern sky at low frequencies. Its 4-Jy (G4Jy) sample isolates the brightest extragalactic radio sources ($S_{151\text{ MHz}} > 4.0\text{ Jy}$), demonstrating that radio-continuum selection is significantly less biased than optical selection for identifying AGN, as not all radio-bright host galaxies exhibit optical emission lines [White et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181001226W/abstract), [White et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PASA...42...85W/abstract). In deep radio continuum surveys, however, star-forming galaxies begin to dominate the source population at flux densities fainter than approximately 0.1 mJy at 1.4 GHz [Condon et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASPC..380..189C/abstract). To interpret the millions of sources detected by such high-sensitivity radio surveys, massive multi-object spectroscopic follow-up campaigns, such as WEAVE-LOFAR, are fundamentally critical [Smith et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASPC..507..373S/abstract). High-resolution MeerKAT follow-up of these G4Jy sources has revealed complex radio morphologies (such as head-tail and X-shaped structures), yet a significant fraction of their host galaxies remains ambiguous despite the improved imaging [Sejake et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.4290S/abstract). Low-frequency polarimetry from GLEAM at 216 MHz has also detected linearly polarized sources, revealing substantial depolarization compared to higher frequencies and enabling high-precision Faraday rotation measurements [Riseley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...43R/abstract).

Finally, [[blind-hi-surveys|Blind HI surveys]] operating at 21-cm wavelengths map the neutral atomic gas content of the local universe. Surveys such as WALLABY and FASHI preferentially detect gas-rich dwarf galaxies and late-type spirals. Because these objects often reside in lower-density environments, HI all-sky maps provide a systematically less-clustered view of the universe than optical catalogs, tracing the diffuse filaments and filling the spatial gaps of the cosmic web [Koribalski et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..329..217K/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024SCPMA..6719511Z/abstract).

By synthesizing these panchromatic catalogs, astronomers can overcome the limitations of spectroscopic incompleteness. Feeding federated multi-wavelength photometry—such as combined [[two-micron-all-sky-survey-2mass|Two Micron All-Sky Survey (2MASS)]], WISE, and optical data—into machine-learning classifiers has enabled the derivation of accurate photometric redshifts for over 20 million sources, pushing structural mapping of the universe to gigaparsec depths [Bilicki et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016pas..conf..165B/abstract), [Bilicki et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..308..143B/abstract).

## Cosmology and Large-Scale Structure

Cosmological models rely fundamentally on mapping the three-dimensional distribution of galaxies across cosmic time to constrain dark energy, the nature of dark matter, and the growth of structure. Because the galaxy distribution is strongly clustered on scales up to $\sim 10 \text{ Mpc}$, extracting representative statistical properties of the cosmic web requires deep, wide-area observational volumes. 

The modern era of large-scale structure mapping was pioneered by extensive optical [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Redshift surveys]]. Prior to the gigaparsec volumes mapped today, the Las Campanas Redshift Survey (LCRS), with an average redshift of $z=0.1$, provided early high-precision measurements of the power spectrum $P(k)$ on scales up to $400 \text{ Mpc}/h$, revealing that brighter galaxies cluster approximately 50% more strongly than fainter ones [Lin et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...471..617L/abstract). Notably, the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (SDSS) and the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]] revolutionized the field by measuring precise spectra for roughly $10^6$ and $230,000$ galaxies, respectively [Loveday et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ConPh..43..437L/abstract), [Lahav et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..329....3L/abstract). Building on this legacy, the eBOSS-DAP pipeline has recently delivered uniform measurements of stellar and gas kinematics, as well as emission-line fluxes, for nearly 1.9 million SDSS-IV galaxy spectra below $z < 1.12$ [Matthews Acuña et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251218076M/abstract). These foundational datasets enabled precise measurements of the three-dimensional galaxy power spectrum, $P(k)$, and the detection of baryonic acoustic oscillations (BAO), which serve as standard rulers for tracing the expansion history of the Universe. Furthermore, these surveys robustly quantified the [[angular-correlation-and-clustering|Angular Correlation and Clustering]] of galaxies. Using early SDSS data, the angular two-point correlation function on scales $1^\prime < \theta < 1^\circ$ was shown to be well-described by a power law:
$$ w(\theta) \propto \theta^{-0.7} $$
which decreases in amplitude at fainter magnitudes, in good agreement with results from existing galaxy surveys [Connolly et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...579...42C/abstract).

While spectroscopic surveys provide definitive 3D maps, obtaining high-resolution spectra for millions of faint galaxies is observationally expensive. Consequently, deep photometric surveys are increasingly employed to reach larger volumes. Although tensions remain in the literature regarding whether photometric redshifts alone can meet the stringent precision requirements for Stage IV cosmology, recent methodological advances offer potential pathways to mitigate these concerns. Specifically, clustering-based redshift estimation—which infers redshift distributions via cross-correlation with a reference spectroscopic sample—can significantly elevate the constraining power of photometric data. When applied, this technique yields dark energy and non-Gaussianity constraints that are highly competitive with those derived from purely spectroscopic surveys [Kovetz et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3650K/abstract).

To further refine measurements of the growth rate of structure, contemporary cosmological surveys are combining galaxy density mapping with peculiar velocity data. The physical relationship between galaxy density and peculiar velocities systematically reduces the impact of cosmic variance. Fisher matrix forecasts demonstrate that incorporating peculiar velocities can improve constraints on the growth rate by a factor of 2 to 5 on large scales, specifically at wavenumbers:
$$ k \le 0.2 \, h \, \text{Mpc}^{-1} $$
compared to analyses relying on galaxy density alone [Koda et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.445.4267K/abstract).

Looking ahead, the next generation of cosmological constraints will heavily leverage radio astronomy. All-sky 21-cm [[blind-hi-surveys|Blind HI surveys]] provide an essential complement to optical mapping. Neutral hydrogen (HI) surveys are uniquely valuable because they preferentially detect gas-rich, late-type spirals and dwarf galaxies, offering a less-clustered tracer of the underlying dark matter that fills in the gaps between the filaments of the optically-selected cosmic web [Koribalski et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..329..217K/abstract). However, accurately modeling how these HI-detected galaxies trace the underlying matter distribution requires the development of new Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD) models calibrated directly to cosmological simulations [Qin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..113Q/abstract). Early results from pathfinders have already validated this observational regime; the first data release of the LOFAR Two-meter Sky Survey (LoTSS DR1) successfully measured angular clustering and galaxy bias, confirming the power of low-frequency radio surveys for large-scale structure studies [Tiwari et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928...38T/abstract). Future facilities, most notably the Square Kilometre Array (SKA), are projected to detect the 21-cm emission of roughly one billion galaxies out to $z \sim 2$ [Yahya et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.2251Y/abstract). By combining unprecedented measurements of BAO and redshift-space distortions, the SKA is expected to yield the largest dark energy Figure of Merit of any planned large-scale structure survey, providing transformational constraints on the equation of state of dark energy and potential modifications to General Relativity [Abdalla et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015aska.confE..17A/abstract), [Yahya et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.2251Y/abstract). Furthermore, future radio continuum surveys like EMU and WODAN, when cross-matched with optical datasets to obtain low-redshift information, can significantly improve Dark Energy Figure of Merit (FoM) constraints [Camera et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.427.2079C/abstract). Upcoming large-area surveys like the SKA are projected to discover tens of thousands of strong gravitational lens systems, enabling detailed studies of mass distribution and evolution out to $z \sim 1$ [Koopmans et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004NewAR..48.1085K/abstract).

## Galactic Archaeology and Stellar Populations

Galactic archaeology seeks to reconstruct the formation and evolutionary history of the Milky Way by mapping the spatial distribution, kinematics, and chemical abundances of its constituent stellar populations. The advent of the [[gaia-mission-and-data-products|Gaia Mission and Data Products]] has revolutionized this field, transitioning it from localized [[star-counts|Star counts]] to comprehensive all-sky phase-space catalogs. While Gaia provides exquisite astrometry, identifying accreted substructures and distinct Galactic components requires multi-dimensional clustering in both kinematic and chemical space. Because spectroscopic sampling often lags behind photometric and astrometric data volumes, researchers have successfully combined Gaia proper motions with photometric metallicities to map the Galaxy in 5D space. For example, all-sky samples of RR Lyrae stars have been used to trace extended substructures in the Galactic halo—such as the Hercules-Aquila Cloud, the Virgo Overdensity, and the Gaia-Enceladus-Sausage—circumventing the traditional limits of spectroscopic completeness [An et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...897...39A/abstract), [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...979..213S/abstract).

To complement astrometric data with precise radial velocities and detailed chemical abundance patterns, massively multiplexed [[spectroscopy-surveys|Spectroscopy surveys]] are required. The optical [[lamost-spectroscopic-survey|LAMOST Spectroscopic Survey]] has collected approximately $4.5 \times 10^6$ stellar spectra with a signal-to-noise ratio $S/N > 10$, yielding robust constraints on the kinematics and metallicity distribution of the local Galactic disk and halo within a few kiloparsecs of the Sun [Liu et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015RAA....15.1089L/abstract). However, optical surveys are fundamentally limited at low Galactic latitudes by dust obscuration. To probe the heavily obscured bulge, bar, and mid-plane regions, [[near-infrared-astronomy|Near-Infrared Astronomy]] is essential. The [[apogee-survey|APOGEE survey]], operating under the umbrella of the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]], leverages high-resolution ($R = \lambda / \Delta\lambda \sim 22,500$) near-infrared spectroscopy in the $1.51 \leq \lambda \leq 1.70 \ \mu\mathrm{m}$ band. By achieving $S/N > 100$ for over $146,000$ stars, APOGEE provides homogeneous chemical tagging across all major Galactic populations, tracing elemental abundance ratios through regions that are entirely inaccessible to optical instruments [Majewski et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AJ....154...94M/abstract).

Despite these tremendous advances, two major obstacles remain in the complete characterization of the Milky Way. First, the "Galactic extinction horizon" continues to prevent a complete census of the far side of the Galactic disk. Overcoming this barrier will require significantly deeper near-infrared and mid-infrared surveys to penetrate the densest dust clouds [Minniti et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024IAUS..377..107M/abstract). Ancillary efforts mapping dust and star-forming structures, such as the [[milky-way-project|Milky Way Project]], provide necessary contextual maps of the interstellar medium to aid in this complex extinction modeling. Second, stellar age estimation represents the primary "weak link" in current Galactic evolution models. Because stellar ages cannot be measured directly and rely heavily on mass proxies, current literature suggests that future long-duration, all-sky asteroseismology surveys will be required to complement existing astrometric and chemical datasets. Only by resolving this age-dating bottleneck will researchers achieve the precision necessary to fully unravel the assembly history of the Milky Way [Michel et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ExA....51..945M/abstract).

## The Interstellar Medium and Galactic Magnetic Fields

All-sky surveys are critical for characterizing the multiphase interstellar medium (ISM) and the large-scale structure of Galactic and extragalactic magnetic fields. By mapping the sky across a wide range of frequencies, these surveys constrain the distribution of neutral, ionized, and molecular gas, as well as the dust content and magnetic topologies of the Milky Way and the local Universe.

Investigations of the neutral ISM heavily rely on 21-cm radio observations. Surveys such as [[hipass|HIPASS]] (the HI Parkes All-Sky Survey) have provided an unprecedented view of local large-scale structures, detecting gas-rich dwarf galaxies and late-type spirals that are frequently missed by optical surveys [Koribalski et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..329..217K/abstract). This less-clustered view of the local Universe effectively maps the gaseous cosmic web, filling gaps in cosmic filaments [Koribalski et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PASA...29..359K/abstract). Modern extensions like the WALLABY pilot survey have further demonstrated that marginally resolved kinematic models from their data can successfully derive fundamental HI scaling relations, such as the size-mass and baryonic Tully-Fisher relations, for gas-rich galaxies [Deg et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976..159D/abstract). Furthermore, the FAST All Sky HI survey (FASHI) has already cataloged over 40,000 extragalactic HI sources [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024SCPMA..6719511Z/abstract). Extending 21-cm observations to extreme distances, high-redshift all-sky spectrum experiments like SCI-HI play a vital role in measuring the global 21-cm brightness temperature during the cosmic Dark Ages ($14 < z < 23$) [Voytek et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782L...9V/abstract). Complementing the neutral gas maps, the ionized component of the Milky Way has been globally mapped by the Wisconsin H-Alpha Mapper (WHAM) Northern Sky Survey. By achieving a one-degree resolution and an unprecedented sensitivity down to 0.1 Rayleighs, WHAM traced Galactic $H\alpha$ features corresponding to emission measures as low as $\sim 0.25 \text{ cm}^{-6} \text{ pc}$ [Madsen et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.12232M/abstract).

To quantify the molecular and dust components of the ISM, multi-wavelength data federation is essential. The distribution of molecular gas in the Galactic halo—specifically the rare molecular intermediate-velocity clouds (MIVCs)—has been constrained by cross-correlating far-infrared (FIR) data from [[planck-spacecraft|Planck (spacecraft)]] with HI data from the [[parkes-galactic-all-sky-survey|Parkes Galactic All-Sky Survey]] and the Effelsberg-Bonn HI Survey [Röhser et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...596A..94R/abstract). Furthermore, all-sky FIR surveys provide the absolute zero-point calibration necessary to reliably measure variations in the gas-to-dust ratio, revealing that dust abundance decreases by factors of 3 to 7 between the dense and diffuse ISM in the Magellanic Clouds [Roman-Duval et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...841...72R/abstract).

Finally, [[continuum-radio-surveys|Continuum radio surveys]] and wide-field polarimetry are the primary tools for probing cosmic magnetism. The strength and structure of Galactic and intergalactic magnetic fields are optimally modeled using a dense grid of Faraday rotation measures (RM) derived from polarized background sources and diffuse polarized emission [Beck et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010amjs.conf...57B/abstract), [Beck et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ASSL..407....3B/abstract). A fundamental observational challenge in these studies is the absolute zero-level calibration of the Stokes $Q$ and $U$ parameters, which is notoriously difficult in all-sky mapping [Reich et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IAUS..259..603R/abstract). Recent efforts like the Southern Twenty-centimetre All-sky Polarization Survey (STAPS) overcome this using long azimuth scans to yield absolutely calibrated Stokes $I$, $Q$, and $U$ maps at a 20 arcminute resolution [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250114203S/abstract). At extremely low frequencies (74 MHz), the VLA Low-frequency Sky Survey (VLSS) provides a high-resolution map of the southern sky, serving as a critical calibration grid for next-generation arrays like LOFAR [Lane et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..395..370L/abstract). Additionally, at 216 MHz, the POlarised GLEAM Survey (POGS) mapped 6,400 square degrees, revealing that while Faraday rotation is consistent with higher-frequency measurements, the diffuse emission suffers from significant depolarization [Riseley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...43R/abstract).

## High-Energy Surveys and Galaxy Clusters

Historically, the detection of galaxy clusters progressed from pre-digital visual inspection of photographic plates to hybrid photo-digital catalogs, and eventually to fully automated digital pipelines like those employed by the Sloan Digital Sky Survey [Gal et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..1195G/abstract). High-energy and microwave background surveys have since become crucial for mapping the hot, ionized components of the Universe. By capturing emission from the intracluster medium (ICM) and the accretion disks of active galactic nuclei (AGN), these surveys trace the most massive bound structures and the most energetic discrete phenomena across cosmic time.

While [[rosat|ROSAT]] established the foundation for wide-area X-ray cluster catalogs, [[x-ray-surveys|X-ray surveys]] can suffer from source confusion when a bright central AGN outshines the extended thermal emission of the host cluster. The Clusters Hiding in Plain Sight (CHiPS) survey demonstrated that some massive, nearby clusters were incorrectly identified as isolated point sources in the ROSAT All-Sky Survey. A prime example is PKS 1353-341 at $z = 0.223$, a highly massive ($M_{500} = 6.9_{-2.6}^{+4.3} \times 10^{14} M_\odot$) and luminous ($L_X = 7 \times 10^{44}$ erg s$^{-1}$) cool-core cluster initially missed by X-ray pipelines despite being massive enough to generate a detectable Sunyaev-Zel'dovich signal [Somboonpanyakul et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...863..122S/abstract). 

At microwave frequencies, surveys utilize the thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect to detect the ICM. Because the SZ surface brightness is largely independent of cosmological distance, these surveys provide a remarkably clean selection function for massive clusters out to redshifts $z > 1$ [Bartlett et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..6241B/abstract). Ground-based facilities such as the [[atacama-cosmology-telescope|Atacama Cosmology Telescope]] (ACT) and the [[south-pole-telescope-survey|South Pole Telescope survey]] have surveyed thousands of square degrees with arcminute resolution. Next-generation upgrades, such as the Advanced ACTPol (AdvACT) survey, expand this footprint to roughly half the low-foreground sky, intentionally prioritizing overlap with massive spectroscopic programs to facilitate robust [[cross-correlation-analysis|Cross-correlation analysis]] [De Bernardis et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016SPIE.9910E..14D/abstract). Space-based SZ measurements from [[planck-spacecraft|Planck (spacecraft)]] have generated expansive all-sky cluster catalogs that are highly complementary to existing X-ray and optical samples. Complementing microwave observations, wide-field infrared programs provide another robust method for detecting distant structures. The Massive and Distant Clusters of WISE Survey 2 (MaDCoWS2) successfully utilized CatWISE and Legacy Survey data to detect over a thousand massive galaxy clusters out to redshifts of $z > 1.5$ [Thongkham et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967..123T/abstract). Complementing high-energy and microwave observations, extremely deep optical campaigns have also been essential for tracing structure across cosmic time; for instance, the VIMOS VLT Deep Survey (VVDS) provided a comprehensive census of 35,000 galaxies and AGN, mapping galaxy evolution across a broad redshift range up to $z \sim 6.7$ [Le Fèvre et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Msngr.155...33L/abstract).

Beyond the thermal SZ effect, the kinematic SZ (kSZ) effect offers a direct probe of large-scale structure velocities. By combining small-scale cosmic microwave background data with large galaxy surveys, researchers can constrain the large-scale galaxy-velocity power spectrum, $P_{gv}(k)$. This is optimally extracted using a bispectrum estimator, $\langle ggT \rangle$, which yields cosmological constraints on large length scales where the effective noise is substantially lower than that of traditional galaxy surveys [Smith et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181013423S/abstract).

## Time-Domain Surveys and Transient Phenomena

The advent of [[synoptic-optical-surveys|Synoptic optical surveys]] marks a fundamental paradigm shift in observational astronomy, transitioning from the production of static catalogs to the dynamic, time-domain monitoring of the celestial sphere [Ivezić et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AIPC.1082..359I/abstract). By repeatedly scanning the sky at high cadences, these surveys produce dense photometric light curves that enable the identification of variable and transient phenomena. 

Space-based astrometric and photometric missions have revolutionized the census of variable sources. The Gaia Data Release 3 (DR3), for example, leveraged 34 months of multi-epoch data to detect over 10.5 million variable sources, including approximately 9.5 million variable stars and 1 million active galactic nuclei [Eyer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ees..conf...27E/abstract). The staggering data volumes generated by such high-cadence monitoring necessitate the use of advanced [[detection-algorithms|Detection algorithms]] and autonomous classifiers, which are increasingly adopted to manage the influx of time-domain data. Furthermore, high-cadence synoptic monitoring uniquely facilitates the discovery of dark or exceedingly faint unbound objects via microlensing. Theoretical estimates suggest the Galaxy may host up to $\sim 10^5$ "nomad" compact objects—spanning masses from $10^{-8}$ to $10^{-2} M_\odot$—per main-sequence star. Future ground-based facilities, notably the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]], are projected to identify vast numbers of these rogue planets and free-floating bodies through their transient microlensing signatures [Strigari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.1856S/abstract).

In the extragalactic regime, dedicated [[stellar-transient-and-supernova-surveys|Stellar Transient and Supernova Surveys]] are critical for mapping the cosmic expansion and star-formation history. High-etendue facilities like the [[pan-starrs-surveys|Pan-STARRS Surveys]] and the upcoming LSST are poised to detect core-collapse supernovae in numbers orders of magnitude greater than historical yields. The statistical power of these datasets will allow precision measurements of the cosmic core-collapse supernova rate out to redshifts of $z \sim 0.5$ for general populations, and up to $z \sim 1$ for highly luminous Type IIn events [Lien et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009JCAP...01..047L/abstract). 

Time-domain astronomy is equally transformative in the X-ray regime, where all-sky monitors (e.g., MAXI, Swift, and RXTE) continually track accretion outbursts from compact objects. The WATCHDOG database, compiling 20 years of all-sky observations, identified 132 transient outbursts from Galactic black hole X-ray binaries (BHXBs) [Tetarenko et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..222...15T/abstract). Furthermore, all-sky monitors tracking specific transient systems, such as the MAXI observations of GX 339-4, enable the detailed study of black hole X-ray binary evolution using hardness-intensity (q) diagrams [Agarwal et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220907166A/abstract). Notably, these synoptic data have revealed a significant tension in standard accretion models. While traditional theoretical frameworks assume that BHXB outbursts reliably evolve into a thermally dominant state, observational statistics demonstrate that approximately $40\%$ of Galactic transient outbursts remain in "hard-only" states. This significant fraction contradicts standard evolutionary assumptions and indicates that the physical mechanisms governing BHXB outbursts frequently restrict the mass-transfer rate, preventing the system from reaching the theoretical threshold for thermal dominance [Tetarenko et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..222...15T/abstract).

Time-domain mapping extends beyond electromagnetic waves into the multi-messenger regime. All-sky searches for unknown isolated neutron stars via continuous gravitational wave radiation (such as those conducted by LIGO and Virgo) represent a significant computational and methodological challenge, which is currently being addressed by advanced algorithms like PowerFlux and distributed computing platforms like Einstein@Home [Walsh et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..94l4010W/abstract).

## Methodological Advances: Data Science and Intensity Mapping

The transition of observational astronomy into a petabyte-scale discipline has fundamentally altered the methodological landscape of [[scientific-data-analysis|Scientific data analysis]]. Modern astronomical surveys routinely detect billions of discrete sources, ushering in a "Big Data" era where federating multi-wavelength datasets relies heavily on the architecture of Astrophysical Virtual Observatories [Brunner et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..225..135B/abstract), [Mickaelian et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016BaltA..25...75M/abstract). To process these deeply crowded, multi-wavelength fields, modern astronomical pipelines deploy advanced profile-fitting photometry routines, an approach exemplified by software tools such as 'The Farmer' [Weaver et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ascl.soft12016W/abstract). Homogeneous processing efforts such as RCSEDv2 have compiled datasets for over 4 million galaxies, integrating multi-wavelength photometry and spectral analysis from various major surveys [Chilingarian et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ASPC..535..179C/abstract). Because manual cataloging is impossible at this scale, the field has increasingly adopted sophisticated computational architectures, notably machine learning and intensity mapping, to optimize cosmological and galactic inferences.

As data volumes continue to grow—with the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]] projected to catalog over 20 billion objects [Ivezić et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AIPC.1082..359I/abstract)—supervised and unsupervised machine learning algorithms have become mandatory for source classification. For instance, the [[gaia-mission-and-data-products|Gaia Mission and Data Products]] Data Release 3 successfully employed supervised algorithms, such as eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and Random Forest classifiers, to categorize its massive catalog of variable sources into 24 variability classes or class groups [Rimoldini et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..14R/abstract). Unsupervised neural networks, such as Rotation/Flipping Invariant Kohonen Maps, are also being deployed to autonomously identify morphological prototypes in massive imaging surveys [Polsterer et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ASPC..495...81P/abstract). Beyond direct source classification, machine learning is increasingly utilized to emulate and calibrate complex subgrid models in cosmological hydrodynamical simulations using Gaussian processes, directly linking subgrid parameter variations to survey observables [Kugel et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.6103K/abstract).

To push beyond the boundaries of discrete source detection, [[line-intensity-mapping-lim|Line Intensity Mapping (LIM)]] (or simply [[intensity-mapping|Intensity mapping]]) has emerged as a transformative observational technique. Rather than resolving individual galaxies, this approach measures the integrated spectral line emission over broad frequency and spatial bins, capturing the cumulative signal of both bright and ultra-faint sources that fall below conventional [[detection-limits|Detection limits]] [Padmanabhan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191014059P/abstract), [Oxholm et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104h3501O/abstract). A significant methodological breakthrough in this subfield is the evasion of cosmic variance via [[cross-correlation-analysis|Cross-correlation analysis]]. By cross-correlating line intensity maps with overlapping discrete [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] catalogs, observers can isolate the line intensity and clustering bias. Theoretical sensitivity is optimized when the survey depths—defined by the ratio of the clustering power spectrum to the noise, $P(k)/N$—are matched between the cross-correlated surveys [Oxholm et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PhRvD.104h3501O/abstract).

[[cross-correlation-analysis|Cross-correlation analysis]] is equally vital for deriving clustering-based photometric redshifts, which rely on the spatial cross-correlation between a poorly constrained photometric sample and a well-characterized spectroscopic reference catalog to reconstruct the redshift distribution, $N(z)$ [Kovetz et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3650K/abstract). Additionally, to measure galaxy clustering on the largest physical scales while properly accounting for light-cone effects, researchers have introduced specialized statistical approaches like the pseudo Karhunen-Loève method [Xavier et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JCAP...04..037X/abstract). Furthermore, next-generation surveys must rigorously account for magnification and evolution biases, which depend on the underlying luminosity function and will distort large-scale clustering measurements if left unmodeled [Maartens et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...12..009M/abstract). However, the literature reveals a significant tension regarding whether photometric redshifts alone can meet the stringent precision requirements for "Stage IV" dark energy cosmology. Some researchers demonstrate that clustering-based redshift inference can elevate photometric data to yield cosmological constraints competitive with those of spectroscopic surveys [Kovetz et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3650K/abstract). In contrast, others argue that without an order-of-magnitude improvement in characterization and massive spectroscopic calibration samples, the [[systematic-errors-and-statistical-biases|Systematic Errors and Statistical Biases]] inherent to photometric redshifts will dominate the cosmological error budget [Newman et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ARA&A..60..363N/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The upcoming decade of astrophysics will be dominated by Stage-IV [[cosmic-surveys|Cosmic surveys]] aiming to map the observable Universe with unprecedented volume and precision. Facilities such as the Square Kilometre Array (SKA), [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]], the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]], and the [[china-space-station-telescope-csst|China Space Station Telescope (CSST)]] will cross-correlate billion-object catalogs across the electromagnetic spectrum [Abdalla et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015aska.confE..17A/abstract), [Square Kilometre Array Cosmology Science Working Group et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PASA...37....7S/abstract). A primary objective of these missions is constraining the dark energy equation of state and the growth rate of structure, $f\sigma_8(z)$, out to $z \sim 3$ [Square Kilometre Array Cosmology Science Working Group et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PASA...37....7S/abstract). However, an active debate remains regarding whether photometric redshifts alone can achieve the requisite precision for these cosmological constraints. Reaching the necessary order-of-magnitude improvement will likely require either massive spectroscopic calibration samples or advanced clustering-based redshift estimation techniques that cross-correlate photometric data with spectroscopic reference sets [Kovetz et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3650K/abstract), [Newman et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ARA&A..60..363N/abstract).

In the realm of large-scale structure, controlling systematic errors is paramount for Stage-IV weak-lensing surveys. A key objective in this area is measuring spin-filament alignments, which will help unravel how galaxies acquire their angular momentum and effectively model intrinsic alignment systematics [Barsanti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215276B/abstract). Looking beyond Stage-IV, surveys planned for the 2030s and 2040s aim to address fundamental physics inaccessible to current experiments. A major open question is the resolution of the neutrino mass hierarchy. Next-generation wide-field spectroscopic surveys targeting $1 < z < 6$ project constraints on the sum of absolute neutrino masses to $\Sigma m_\nu \lesssim 0.015 \text{ eV}$, a factor of three tighter than Stage-IV limits [Guidi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215294G/abstract). Additionally, these surveys will probe inflationary physics by measuring primordial non-Gaussianity at the level of $f_{\mathrm{NL}} \sim 1$, providing critical tests of multi-field inflation models [Guidi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215294G/abstract). Achieving these metrics will require revolutionary instruments, such as the proposed [[wst|WST]] (Wide-field Spectroscopic Telescope), capable of obtaining $\mathcal{O}(1000)$ simultaneous spectra with sub-$2\times 10^{-4}(1+z)$ redshift precision [Guidi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251215294G/abstract).

To overcome the cosmic variance limits inherent in traditional density-based [[redshift-catalogs|Redshift catalogs]], future surveys will increasingly integrate peculiar velocity measurements. By capturing the direct physical relationship between galaxy density and velocity, these data can improve low-redshift growth rate constraints by a factor of $2$ to $5$ [Koda et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.445.4267K/abstract). Furthermore, radio $21\text{-cm}$ (HI) mapping with the SKA presents its own set of open questions, particularly whether deep, blind surveys at $z > 3$ will successfully image the early cosmic web and individual proto-galaxies prior to the epoch of peak star formation [Braun et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007astro.ph..3746B/abstract). 

In mapping the nearby universe, the TONGS survey has revealed that while optical and radio coverage is currently extensive, space-based wavelength regimes—specifically the ultraviolet and X-ray bands—represent the largest opportunity for completing the local cosmic census [Christie et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PASP..136c3001C/abstract). Expanding into this domain, the proposed Ultraviolet Explorer (UVEX) mission aims to perform a synoptic all-sky survey 50 to 100 times deeper than GALEX, driving new explorations into low-mass galaxy evolution and the dynamic UV universe [Kulkarni et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv211115608K/abstract).

On smaller scales, a persistent barrier in Galactic archaeology is the "Galactic extinction horizon," which limits our ability to map the stellar populations, chemical abundances, and kinematic substructures on the far side of the Milky Way [Minniti et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024IAUS..377..107M/abstract). Resolving this will require highly complete, multi-epoch near-infrared surveys to penetrate the dust of the Galactic plane. To this end, the proposed NANCY project aims to leverage the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope to deliver precisely such a high-resolution, multi-epoch all-sky near-infrared map [Han et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230611784H/abstract). The shift toward high-cadence [[stellar-transient-and-supernova-surveys|Stellar Transient and Supernova Surveys]] also introduces new phenomenological mysteries, such as the physical mechanisms driving the unexpectedly high fraction ($\sim 40\%$) of "hard-only" outbursts in Galactic black hole X-ray binaries, a behavior that contradicts standard assumptions of thermal state evolution [Tetarenko et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..222...15T/abstract). Finally, expanding the search for extragalactic phenomena, all-sky SETI surveys—such as the Breakthrough Listen initiative—are not only providing a comprehensive census of extragalactic objects but also placing robust limits on the presence of very powerful extraterrestrial transmitters in the distant universe [Garrett et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220908147G/abstract). Furthermore, despite the reliance on advanced machine learning algorithms and fully automated digital pipelines, the role of amateur astronomers remains vital in identifying unique, large-angular-scale galaxy candidates that these automated systems may overlook [Shaheer Niazi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230914743S/abstract).
