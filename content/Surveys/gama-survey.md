The **Galaxy And Mass Assembly (GAMA) survey** is a comprehensive multi-wavelength photometric and spectroscopic campaign designed to study galaxy evolution, fundamental scaling relations, and [[low-redshift-large-scale-structure|Low-redshift large-scale structure]] across the low-to-intermediate redshift Universe ($0.01 < z < 0.5$). Building on the legacy of wide-field [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] campaigns such as the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (SDSS) and the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]], GAMA was explicitly constructed to bridge the observational gap between wide-shallow sky maps and deep-narrow [[pencil-beam-surveys|Pencil-beam surveys]]. Utilizing the AAOmega spectrograph on the [[anglo-australian-telescope|Anglo-Australian Telescope]], the survey employed a highly optimized, multi-pass adaptive tiling strategy. This approach deliberately circumvented the "fiber collision" limits that artificially suppressed small-scale clustering signals in previous surveys. By revisiting the same fields multiple times, GAMA achieved an unprecedented $>98\%$ spectroscopic completeness down to a uniform optical depth of $r < 19.8$ mag [Driver et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IAUS..254..469D/abstract), [Driver et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.413..971D/abstract). 

A defining hallmark of the GAMA survey is its extensive panchromatic data integration. The project synthesized observations across more than 20 photometric bands—spanning from the far-ultraviolet to the far-infrared—by combining data from facilities such as [[galex|GALEX]], VST, VISTA, WISE, and the Herschel Space Observatory. This panchromatic synthesis produced a rigorous empirical measurement of the low-redshift Cosmic Spectral Energy Distribution (CSED), revealing that the Universe's total energy output is declining and that 30-50% of stellar optical/UV emission is reprocessed by interstellar dust into the far-infrared [Driver et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.427.3244D/abstract), [Driver et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.3911D/abstract). Paired with automated structural decomposition pipelines and the robust GAMA Galaxy Group Catalogue (G3C) [Robotham et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2640R/abstract), these multi-wavelength data have yielded definitive constraints on [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]]. The survey successfully traced the local galaxy stellar mass function (GSMF) down into the dwarf regime ($M_* \sim 10^{6.75} M_\odot$), establishing that a double Schechter function is required to accurately model the distribution of low-mass systems [Baldry et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421..621B/abstract), [Driver et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513..439D/abstract). 

By accurately mapping local densities and bound structures without spatial bias, GAMA has provided vital insights into environmental quenching and the cosmic web. The survey discovered that star formation is predominantly suppressed from the "outside-in" as galaxies enter dense environments, and identified a massive local population of passive "red spirals" that challenge traditional views coupling disk morphology strictly to active star formation. Furthermore, spatial analyses of the GAMA footprint have refined the topology of the Universe by discovering "tendrils"—thin, coherent chains of galaxies that link major filaments and penetrate into otherwise empty voids. While GAMA serves as a foundational benchmark for modern [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]], its findings highlight several open questions, including the true faint-end slope of the GSMF, the causality versus correlation of quenching mechanisms, and the impact of a potential 300 Mpc local under-density on cosmological measurements. Resolving these questions continues to drive the design of next-generation, massively multiplexed campaigns like the 4MOST hemisphere survey and cold gas studies utilizing Square Kilometre Array pathfinders.

## Historical Context and Evolution from 2dFGRS and SDSS

The late 1990s and early 2000s marked a fundamental shift in extragalactic astronomy, driven by the advent of massively multiplexed spectrographs. The resulting generation of wide-field campaigns, most notably the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey (2dFGRS)]] and the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS)]], successfully mapped the large-scale structure of the local Universe at a median redshift of $z \sim 0.1$. The 2dFGRS obtained spectra for approximately $250,000$ galaxies down to $b_J = 19.45$ [Colless et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.328.1039C/abstract), while the SDSS provided spectroscopy for nearly a million galaxies down to $r < 17.77$ over a quarter of the sky. The [[6df-galaxy-survey|6dF Galaxy Survey]] (6dFGS) also served as a contemporary counterpart, mapping the southern sky at $z \sim 0.05$ and providing peculiar velocity measurements for 15,000 galaxies [Jones et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..329...11J/abstract).

Despite their transformative impact, these wide-shallow surveys left a critical parameter space unexplored. At the other extreme of observational cosmology were deep-narrow [[pencil-beam-surveys|Pencil-beam surveys]] and deep fields, which probed highly localized volumes at high redshift but suffered strongly from cosmic variance. The [[millennium-galaxy-catalogue|Millennium Galaxy Catalogue]] (MGC), which mapped a $37.5 \text{ deg}^2$ equatorial strip to an isophotal detection limit of $26 \text{ mag arcsec}^{-2}$ and a magnitude depth of $B < 24$, served as an important precursor. The MGC demonstrated the scientific necessity of connecting bright local surveys with faint deep-field counts to construct a continuous picture of the galaxy population [Liske et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.344..307L/abstract).

The GAMA survey was explicitly designed to represent the next necessary stage in redshift survey evolution. Building upon the legacy of both the 2dFGRS and SDSS, GAMA was optimized to bridge the divide between wide-shallow and deep-narrow observing regimes. By targeting the intermediate $0.01 < z < 0.5$ redshift range (with a median redshift of $z \approx 0.2$) down to a faint limiting magnitude of $r < 19.8$, GAMA achieved an unprecedented target density of over $1000 \text{ deg}^{-2}$ [Baldry et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.404...86B/abstract), [Hopkins et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.430.2047H/abstract). Crucially, it was designed to overcome a significant mechanical limitation of its predecessors: "fiber collisions," which occurred when the physical size of spectroscopic fiber ferrules prevented the simultaneous observation of closely separated targets, artificially suppressing measured clustering signals at small angular scales [Gordon et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018RNAAS...2..132G/abstract).

## Survey Design and High-Completeness Tiling Strategy

To overcome the spatial limitations that biased previous surveys against dense environments, GAMA implemented a highly specialized targeting approach. Utilizing the AAOmega fiber-fed dual-beam spectrograph on the 3.9-meter [[anglo-australian-telescope|Anglo-Australian Telescope]], the survey deployed a unique multi-pass observation strategy [Driver et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IAUS..254..469D/abstract). Central to this approach was the development of a heuristic greedy tiling algorithm that optimized the placement of pointings across the survey footprint [Robotham et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PASA...27...76R/abstract). By adaptively re-observing the same patches of sky with different fiber configurations over multiple visits, the tiling algorithm naturally corrected for the physical constraints of the instrument, allowing the survey to efficiently target dense fields without rejecting closely separated sources.

This intensive tiling strategy allowed GAMA to probe regions with extremely high target densities, reaching up to $1080 \text{ deg}^{-2}$ in the equatorial G12 field [Baldry et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.404...86B/abstract). Over its observational campaigns, the survey gathered 248,682 galaxy spectra across three equatorial and two southern sky regions, covering approximately $250 \text{ deg}^{2}$ [Driver et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513..439D/abstract). Crucially, GAMA achieved an unprecedented spectroscopic completeness of $98.5\%$ in its main equatorial survey regions down to a uniform optical depth of $r < 19.8$ [Baldry et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.3875B/abstract). 

This near-total completeness demonstrates the success of the collision-free strategy, ensuring that the survey maintained high fidelity even in dense group and cluster cores. Ultimately, this approach successfully recovered the small-scale pair fractions that earlier surveys missed, providing definitive local constraints on close-pair interactions and enabling the construction of robust, unbiased group catalogs [Robotham et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2640R/abstract), [Gordon et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018RNAAS...2..132G/abstract). Beyond its primary low-redshift targets, the survey also expanded its footprint to higher redshifts ($z \sim 1$) by constructing the G10/COSMOS region. This deep 1 square degree field was built by re-reducing archival [[zcosmos|zCOSMOS]] data using the survey's robust [[autoz-cross-correlation-algorithm|AUTOZ cross-correlation algorithm]] pipeline, demonstrating the adaptability of GAMA's tools to deeper cosmological datasets [Davies et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.1014D/abstract).

## Panchromatic Data Integration from UV to Far-IR

The GAMA survey distinguishes itself through its extensive multi-wavelength coordination, culminating in the GAMA Panchromatic Data Release (PDR) [Driver et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.3911D/abstract). The PDR integrates data across more than 20 photometric bands from the far-ultraviolet (FUV) to the far-infrared (FIR), spanning wavelengths from $0.1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ to $500\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. This vast spectral coverage is achieved by synthesizing observations from several leading extragalactic facilities, including [[galex|GALEX]] (UV), VST/KiDS and the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey and Sub-surveys]] (optical), VISTA VIKING and UKIDSS (NIR), WISE (MIR), and the Herschel Space Observatory's [[hatlas|HATLAS]] campaign (FIR) [Driver et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.427.3244D/abstract), [Driver et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.3911D/abstract), [Bellstedt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496.3235B/abstract). Furthermore, cross-correlation of H-ATLAS galaxies with the GAMA sample indicates that typical far-infrared selected sources in the local Universe are hosted by dark matter halos with masses comparable to the Milky Way ($\sim 10^{12} M_\odot$) [Guo et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412.2277G/abstract).

A major challenge in panchromatic surveys is the integration of imaging data with vastly different point spread functions and pixel scales. GAMA addressed this by processing all imaging to a common astrometric solution. The resultant 21-band photometry permits robust Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) fitting using energy balance codes, such as MAGPHYS and PROSPECT. These models explicitly conserve energy by requiring that the stellar UV and optical luminosity attenuated by interstellar dust rigorously matches the thermal emission re-radiated by that dust in the FIR [Driver et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.3911D/abstract), [D'Silva et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.1448D/abstract). To further leverage this multi-wavelength coverage, researchers developed empirical scaling relations between stellar mass and WISE 3.4 and 4.6 $\mu\mathrm{m}$ luminosities, enabling the robust tracing of [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] out to $z \sim 0.5$ [Cluver et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782...90C/abstract). By aggregating these individual SEDs, the GAMA survey provides a precise, empirical measurement of the Cosmic Spectral Energy Distribution (CSED) in the low-redshift Universe [Driver et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.427.3244D/abstract). 

A key result of this panchromatic synthesis is the quantification of the cosmic energy budget. GAMA data reveal that the Universe currently generates $(1.5 \pm 0.3) \times 10^{35} \, h_{70} \mathrm{\, W \, Mpc}^{-3}$ of energy, a significant decline from the $(2.5 \pm 0.2) \times 10^{35} \, h_{70} \mathrm{\, W \, Mpc}^{-3}$ generated at a lookback time of 2.3 Gyr [Driver et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.3911D/abstract). Furthermore, the energy balance models show that approximately 30% to 50% of the total energy produced by stellar emission is absorbed and reprocessed by dust into the FIR [Driver et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.427.3244D/abstract). The data also indicate an evolution in the photon escape fraction; for instance, the UV escape fraction is observed to increase as the Universe ages, rising from roughly 27% at $z = 0.18$ to 34% at $z = 0.06$ in the NUV [Driver et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.3911D/abstract).

Despite the comprehensive nature of the panchromatic data, the literature reveals tensions when deriving fundamental [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] such as star formation rates (SFRs). Meta-analyses within the GAMA framework demonstrate that relying on different SFR indicators across the electromagnetic spectrum can yield inconsistent normalizations and slopes for the SFR-Stellar Mass ($M_*$) relation [Davies et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461..458D/abstract). These discrepancies are predominantly driven by variations in the assumptions underlying dust attenuation corrections. Consequently, careful recalibrations using radiative transfer models are required to yield consistent SFRs across different epochs and varying galaxy populations [Davies et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461..458D/abstract). In the radio regime, stacking analyses using FIRST data provided a robust calibration for the 1.4 GHz radio continuum star formation rate indicator. This calibration successfully produced SFR-$M_*$ relations that are highly consistent with those derived from optical indicators, providing a dust-unbiased measure of star formation [Davies et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.2312D/abstract). Such recalibrated metrics have been crucial in tracing the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]], confirming that both cosmic star formation and active galactic nucleus (AGN) accretion have declined in a strongly coeval manner since $z \sim 2$ [D'Silva et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.1448D/abstract).

## Automated Redshifting and Structural Analysis Pipelines

To process the massive volume of data generated by its multi-wavelength campaign and over 248,000 spectra observed with the [[anglo-australian-telescope|Anglo-Australian Telescope]], the GAMA survey developed highly automated, robust software pipelines for spectral and photometric extraction. 

**Automated Redshifting**
To homogenize redshift measurements and minimize human error across the survey, the GAMA team developed the [[autoz-cross-correlation-algorithm|AUTOZ cross-correlation algorithm]] [Baldry et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441.2440B/abstract). AUTOZ measures redshifts by cross-correlating both absorption and emission-line spectra against a suite of templates. To ensure robustness against uncorrected artifacts, high-pass-filtered spectra are partially clipped, reducing the statistical weight of spurious single-line matches. The pipeline generates a single Figure of Merit (FOM) that places all template matches on a consistent confidence scale, fitting the redshift confidence as a $\tanh$ function of the FOM. This automated approach significantly improved the reliability of assigned redshifts across the survey, achieving an exceptional median velocity uncertainty of $33\text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Baldry et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441.2440B/abstract). Furthermore, AUTOZ's cross-correlation methodology proved highly effective at untangling composite signals, leading to the identification of 280 blended spectra that serve as strong candidates for discovering galaxy-galaxy lenses and occulting galaxy pairs [Holwerda et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449.4277H/abstract). To expand upon this, the survey's spectroscopic catalogs have been combined with high-resolution [[hyper-suprime-cam-surveys|Hyper Suprime-Cam Surveys]] (HSC) imaging to identify and model robust galaxy-scale gravitational lens candidates [Chan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832..135C/abstract).

**Multi-Wavelength Photometry and Photometric Redshifts**
Combining heterogeneous panchromatic data requires consistent aperture matching to produce reliable spectral energy distributions. For deep subset regions, such as the 1 square degree G10/COSMOS field, the survey explicitly utilized the LAMBDAR code to produce aperture-matched photometry across 38 bands. This approach ensured robust total flux measurements and upper limits despite widely varying instrumental resolutions [Andrews et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.1569A/abstract). For the broader survey and in later data releases, the team incorporated the PROFOUND image analysis package to provide matched-segment photometry, which is particularly effective at extracting fluxes in the unresolved mid- and far-infrared regimes [Bellstedt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496.3235B/abstract). 

Using the highly complete GAMA spectroscopic sample as a training set, researchers have also driven advancements in machine-learning-derived photometric redshifts. Using frameworks such as ANNz trained on GAMA spectra, photometric redshifts were derived for 20 million sources in the all-sky WISE × SuperCOSMOS catalog, achieving a normalized scatter of $\sigma_z = 0.033$ and a mean bias of $|\delta z| \sim 10^{-3}$ [Bilicki et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..225....5B/abstract). Within GAMA's own footprint, photometric redshifts have been similarly estimated for all 18 million detected objects down to a depth of $r \sim 25$ using KiDS imaging [Driver et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513..439D/abstract). More recently, state-of-the-art advancements in estimating GAMA photometric redshifts have been achieved by implementing the TOPz (Tartu Observatory Photo-z) Bayesian template-fitting code [Tempel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250324039T/abstract).

**Structural Decomposition**
Standard aperture photometry techniques, such as Kron or Petrosian magnitudes, have been shown to underestimate the cosmic total luminosity density by at least 15% [Hill et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412..765H/abstract). To mitigate this missing flux and extract detailed [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]], GAMA developed SIGMA (Structural Investigation of Galaxies via Model Analysis), an automated structural decomposition wrapper for GALFIT3 [Kelvin et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AIPC.1240..247K/abstract), [Hill et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412..765H/abstract). SIGMA models the two-dimensional surface brightness distributions of galaxies to calculate total Sérsic magnitudes and extract parameters like the Sérsic profile index and the semi-major half-light radius across multiple passbands. Establishing robust measurement limits is critical for this structural analysis; for instance, studies evaluating multi-wavelength depth have demonstrated that structural measurements for GAMA fields utilizing VISTA VIKING data are robust down to 18.8 AB mag, significantly surpassing the shallower limits of older surveys like 2MASS and UKIDSS-LAS [Andrews et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PASA...31....4A/abstract).

This automated decomposition revealed that galaxy size measurements are intrinsically wavelength-dependent. For example, local galaxies appear approximately 13% more compact in the $K$-band compared to the $g$-band [Lange et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.2603L/abstract). Furthermore, while the Sérsic index exhibits a strong bimodality that effectively separates early- and late-type galaxies at high masses, this structural bimodality becomes indistinct below stellar masses of $M_* \sim 10^{10} M_\odot$. This indicates that the Sérsic profile index ceases to be a reliable morphological separator for low-mass systems [Lange et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.2603L/abstract). Furthermore, GAMA data serves as a primary input for forward-modeling tools like GALSBI-SPS, which generates realistic galaxy populations to characterize selection effects in next-generation surveys [Tortorelli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...703A.255T/abstract).

## The GAMA Galaxy Group Catalogue (G3C)

A central data product of the survey is the GAMA Galaxy Group Catalogue (G3C), which identifies galaxy overdensities and bound structures across the survey volume [Robotham et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2640R/abstract). Because GAMA's multi-pass adaptive tiling strategy ensures exceedingly high spatial completeness without the fiber collision limitations of earlier surveys, it provides a nearly complete sampling of close galaxy pairs. This is critical for the robust identification of interacting systems and compact groups in diverse environments.

The G3C is constructed using a friends-of-friends algorithm calibrated against mock catalogues populated with semi-analytic galaxies [Robotham et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2640R/abstract). The first release of the catalogue (G3Cv1) identified 14,388 galaxy groups with a multiplicity of $\geq 2$, incorporating 44,186 galaxies—capturing approximately 40% of the total galaxy sample available at the time. Comparisons between the G3C and mock catalogues generally show good agreement in global characteristics. However, observations reveal a noticeable deficit of high-multiplicity and compact groups (those with five or more members) compared to predictions from early semi-analytic simulations, indicating potential limitations in the simulated physics of halo occupancy [Robotham et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2640R/abstract).

The G3C enables detailed observational constraints on the dark matter halo mass function, primarily through the measurement of group velocity dispersion [Driver et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IAUS..254..469D/abstract). By combining the G3C with data from [[large-area-weak-lensing-surveys|Large area weak lensing surveys]] such as the Kilo-Degree Survey (KiDS), researchers have measured the density profiles of $\sim 1400$ spectroscopically identified groups. These weak lensing studies find that halo mass, probing the regime of $M \sim 10^{13} - 10^{14.5} h^{-1} M_\odot$, scales with the group velocity dispersion as a power law with a slope of $1.89 \pm 0.27$ [Viola et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.3529V/abstract). Furthermore, lensing analyses of G3C groups with typical masses of $10^{13} h^{-1} M_\odot$ have shown no significant evidence for halo assembly bias, yielding results consistent with standard $\Lambda$CDM predictions [Dvornik et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3251D/abstract). Comparison between GAMA galaxy-galaxy lensing profiles and EAGLE hydrodynamical simulations shows broad agreement, although simulations slightly underestimate the signal at intermediate scales for massive galaxies [Velliscig et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471.2856V/abstract). Multi-wavelength cross-matching has further enriched group characterizations; for example, comparisons between [[xxl-survey|XXL survey]] X-ray data and GAMA optical selections revealed 13 young galaxy groups that are undetected in X-rays, indicating they are still in the early stages of virialization [Giles et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.1227G/abstract).

The G3C has also facilitated studies of intrinsic galaxy alignments within group halos. Analyses combining GAMA and KiDS data demonstrate that the shapes of satellite galaxies are preferentially radially aligned towards the brightest group galaxy (BGG). Conversely, the orientations of the BGGs are found to align with the spatial distribution of their satellite galaxies, highlighting the strong dynamical coupling within these bound structures [Georgiou et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...628A..31G/abstract). Furthermore, by integrating the xSAGA machine learning catalog into the GAMA framework, researchers have been able to rigorously probe the faint end of group memberships, demonstrating that GAMA groups exhibit satellite population densities consistent with those observed in the Local Group [Holwerda et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.4575H/abstract).

By establishing a reliable mapping of environmental densities, the G3C serves as the foundational framework for studying how environment influences galaxy evolution, gas content, and star formation—questions that form a core pillar of GAMA's science objectives.

## The Galaxy Stellar Mass Function and Bimodality

The GAMA survey provides definitive, highly complete measurements of [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]], tracing the local galaxy stellar mass function (GSMF) down to the dwarf regime of $M_* \sim 10^{6.75} M_\odot$ [Driver et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513..439D/abstract). GAMA data consistently demonstrate that the local GSMF cannot be adequately described by a single Schechter function. Instead, a double Schechter function is required to capture a prominent dip at intermediate masses and a steep upturn at the faint end, characterized by faint-end slopes of $\alpha_1 = -0.35$ and $\alpha_2 = -1.47$ [Baldry et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421..621B/abstract). Integrating this function yields a total stellar mass density of $\rho_* = (2.97 \pm 0.04) \times 10^8 h_{70} M_\odot \, \text{Mpc}^{-3}$ at $z < 0.1$. This indicates that the Universe has converted $4.9 \pm 0.1\%$ of its primordial baryonic mass into stars [Driver et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513..439D/abstract). Crucially, obtaining these measurements requires careful photometric modeling; total-magnitude systems like Sérsic profile fitting are necessary to accurately recover the steep faint-end slope missed by standard aperture photometry [Hill et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412..765H/abstract). Conversely, at the massive end, comparisons with deep data from the Dragonfly Wide Field Survey indicate that GAMA stellar-mass estimates for massive galaxies ($\log M_* > 10.75$) may be overestimated by approximately 7% due to systematic photometric errors in measuring highly extended light profiles [Miller et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...74M/abstract).

GAMA has driven a paradigm shift in how the bimodality of the galaxy population is interpreted. Rather than employing simple color-magnitude cuts, GAMA analyses model the red and blue galaxy populations as two naturally overlapping distributions, each with its own GSMF and color-mass relation [Taylor et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.2144T/abstract). This approach reveals that the tight "dead sequence" of passive galaxies does not extend much below $M_* \sim 10^{10.5} M_\odot$. At lower masses, the stellar populations of red galaxies become progressively bluer, and the distinct red sequence effectively dissolves below $M_* \sim 10^{9.3} M_\odot$. Furthermore, bivariate functions reveal that approximately 20-30% of red galaxies across all stellar masses are actually star-forming, likely representing a population of dusty star-forming systems rather than truly passive ellipticals [Gunawardhana et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447..875G/abstract). Consequently, the exact shape of the red galaxy mass function at the faint end remains difficult to constrain objectively, highlighting a limitation in classifying low-mass populations [Taylor et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.2144T/abstract). 

For the active star-forming population, GAMA data confirmed the existence of a Fundamental Plane linking stellar mass, gas-phase metallicity, and star formation rate, establishing a clear multidimensional scaling relationship governing galaxy growth [Lara-López et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.434..451L/abstract). Additionally, when combining GAMA data with deeper surveys like the Deep Extragalactic VIsible Legacy Survey ([[devils-survey|DEVILS survey]]), researchers identified a significant bend in the SFR-$M_*$ relation (the 'main sequence' of star-forming galaxies) at low redshifts ($z < 0.45$). This bend becomes notably less evident at higher redshifts, indicating an evolving efficiency in stellar mass assembly [Thorne et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..540T/abstract).

The survey also provides key insights into how galaxies evolve and quench as they transition from the blue cloud to the red sequence through the 'green valley'. High-resolution structural decomposition reveals a significant surplus of rings and morphological lenses in disk-type galaxies traversing the green valley. The prevalence of these structures suggests that the transition is often driven by passive quenching mechanisms rather than violent morphological transformations like major mergers [Kelvin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.4116K/abstract). 

## Environmental Drivers of Galaxy Transformation

By correlating G3C group membership and local density with internal galaxy properties, GAMA has provided precise constraints on environmental quenching—the processes by which a galaxy's [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] is suppressed as it enters a dense structure. Spatially resolved H$\alpha$ emission profiles from the SAMI Galaxy Survey that overlap with GAMA fields show that star formation rate gradients are significantly steeper in dense environments. For galaxies with stellar masses $10^{10} < M_*/M_\odot < 10^{11}$, the gradient steepens by $0.58 \pm 0.29$ dex $r_e^{-1}$ in regions where the fifth-nearest-neighbor density exceeds $\log_{10}(\Sigma_5/\text{Mpc}^2) > 0.5$ [Schaefer et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464..121S/abstract). Furthermore, the fraction of galaxies exhibiting centrally concentrated star formation rises from $\sim 5 \pm 4\%$ in low-density field regions to $30 \pm 15\%$ in the highest-density environments [Schaefer et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464..121S/abstract). These metrics strongly support an "outside-in" quenching mechanism, where processes such as ram-pressure stripping or tidal interactions preferentially remove gas from the galaxy outskirts while centralized star formation temporarily persists. In terms of overall population statistics, the galaxy luminosity function is observed to vary smoothly with local overdensity; however, comparisons with theoretical frameworks reveal that semi-analytic models like GALFORM tend to over-predict the abundance of faint red galaxies in these environments due to the implementation of excessive satellite quenching [McNaught-Roberts et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.445.2125M/abstract).

This environmentally driven gas removal provides a nuanced view of the classical morphology-density relation. GAMA imaging reveals a substantial population of "red spiral" galaxies, which account for approximately 47% of the total spiral galaxy population in the survey [Mahajan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491..398M/abstract). These red spirals challenge the traditional assumption that spiral morphology strictly implies active star formation, a hallmark of [[late-type-galaxies-and-stellar-populations|Late-type Galaxies and Stellar Populations]]. Instead, they exhibit statistically lower specific star formation rates and reside in local environments 2.6 times denser than their blue spiral counterparts. Because these systems have quenched their star formation without undergoing immediate morphological transformation to early-type spheroids, the data suggest that environmental quenching often operates on long timescales (several Gyr) through gradual gas starvation rather than violent merger events [Kelvin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.4116K/abstract), [Mahajan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491..398M/abstract). Conversely, in the lowest density environments, GAMA has identified a distinct population of 'Little Blue Spheroids' (LBS). These are star-forming, rotating dwarf galaxies typically found in isolation, underscoring how the absence of dense environmental interactions permits the preservation of gas-rich, active dwarfs with spheroidal morphologies [Moffett et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.2830M/abstract).

Cold gas inventories further corroborate the impact of the group environment. While group central galaxies tend to have a higher absolute HI mass than satellite galaxies, their overall HI gas fractions are lower [Rhee et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.4646R/abstract). The total group HI mass scales sub-linearly with halo mass, decreasing from $1.3\%$ of the total halo mass at $10^{11.6} M_\odot$ to $0.4\%$ at $10^{13.7} M_\odot$ [Dev et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.2693D/abstract). GAMA also identifies a measurable metallicity decrement in gas-rich group galaxies compared to the field [Lara-López et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AN....334..466L/abstract). This environmental stripping directly contributes to the build-up of intragroup light (IGL). Deep optical imaging of GAMA groups shows that IGL stellar populations are notably younger ($\sim 2$--$2.5$ Gyr) and more metal-poor ([Fe/H] $\sim -0.4$) than the host group central galaxies, consistent with the progressive tidal stripping of the outer disks of infalling satellite galaxies [Martínez-Lombilla et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.1195M/abstract). Because extracting this diffuse intra-halo light is notoriously challenging, recent efforts have focused on developing advanced PSF-scattering correction techniques; applying these methods to deep Hyper Suprime-Cam observations of GAMA groups has improved the measurement accuracy of intra-halo light fractions by an order of magnitude [Garate-Nuñez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250524395G/abstract).

## Mapping the Cosmic Web: Filaments, Tendrils, and Voids

The highly complete spectroscopic targeting of the GAMA survey makes it an ideal dataset for mapping the [[low-redshift-large-scale-structure|Low-redshift large-scale structure]] of the Universe. Using a volume-limited sample of 45,542 galaxies and 6,000 galaxy groups extending to $z \leq 0.213$, researchers applied an adapted minimal spanning tree algorithm to rigorously trace the hierarchical skeleton of the cosmic web [Alpaslan et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438..177A/abstract). 

The minimal spanning tree analysis successfully identified 643 major filaments across GAMA's three equatorial fields. These massive structures span up to $200\, h^{-1}\text{ Mpc}$ in length and contain an average of eight galaxy groups each [Alpaslan et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438..177A/abstract). However, GAMA's exceptional depth and spatial uniformity allowed for the discovery of a secondary, finer population of coherent structures dubbed "tendrils." Tendrils are thin chains of galaxies that branch off to link major filaments together or penetrate deeply into underdense voids. On average, a tendril measures $\sim 10\, h^{-1}\text{ Mpc}$ in length and consists of roughly six galaxies. Statistical verification using the line correlation function has confirmed that tendrils represent genuine, physically coherent structures rather than accidental line-of-sight alignments [Alpaslan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440L.106A/abstract).

The discovery of tendrils fundamentally alters the observational definition of cosmic voids. When GAMA data are overlaid on the footprint of shallower surveys (such as SDSS DR7), regions previously classified as empty voids are found to contain a significant number of galaxies. These so-called void galaxies are predominantly members of tendril structures that the shallower surveys lacked the sensitivity to detect [Alpaslan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440L.106A/abstract). Consequently, the perceived size and emptiness of voids are highly sensitive to the number density and flux limits of a given survey, suggesting that isolated void galaxies actually inhabit local environments with higher densities than previously assumed.

Within this mapped cosmic web, GAMA data demonstrate that a galaxy's stellar mass is the most direct tracer of its overarching environment, with $K$-band luminosity serving as an effective observational proxy [Sureshkumar et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...653A..35S/abstract). Marked correlation function analyses have also demonstrated that mid-infrared luminosities serve as effective tracers of the local environment, though selections based on W1 or W2 bands do not perfectly duplicate the clustering behavior of pure stellar mass-selected samples [Sureshkumar et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...669A..27S/abstract). However, characterizing the kinematics of galaxies within these structures has revealed notable tensions with contemporary galaxy formation models. Measurements of the pairwise velocity dispersion (PVD) at small projected separations ($r_{\perp} \lesssim 1\, h^{-1}\text{ Mpc}$) show that the velocity dispersion, $\sigma_{12}$, increases nearly monotonically with luminosity, ranging from approximately $200\text{ km s}^{-1}$ at $M_r = -17$ to $600\text{ km s}^{-1}$ at $M_r \approx -22$ [Loveday et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.3435L/abstract). Semi-analytic models fail to reproduce this luminosity trend, instead overpredicting the PVD for faint galaxies. This discrepancy indicates an error in the models' halo occupancy physics, suggesting that current simulations incorrectly populate massive dark matter halos with too many low-luminosity galaxies [Loveday et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.3435L/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions with ASKAP and 4MOST

While the GAMA survey has provided a definitive benchmark for low-redshift [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]], its findings have illuminated several outstanding open questions in galaxy evolution. Key unresolved issues have historically included whether the galaxy stellar mass function maintains a constant slope down to extreme dwarf regimes ($M_* \sim 10^{5.5} M_\odot$). Recent synergistic studies utilizing the MUSE instrument alongside GAMA data suggest that the star formation rate density (SFRD) does indeed maintain a constant slope down to $10^{5.5} M_\odot$, implying there is no turnover in the GSMF at extreme low masses [Murrell et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008036M/abstract). Furthermore, debates continue over whether environmental quenching is driven by causal physical mechanisms or is merely correlative with other [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]]. Indications of a $\sim 300$ Mpc local under-density in the K-band galaxy distribution present an ongoing debate; verifying the existence of such a void is critical, as it has the potential to bias local measurements of the Hubble constant [Keenan et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...775...62K/abstract). Building directly on the foundational science of GAMA, the [[magpi-survey|MAGPI survey]] acts as a direct successor using the MUSE instrument to perform detailed integral field spectroscopy; MAGPI aims to map the kinematic and chemical properties of massive galaxies at $z \sim 0.3$ within the established GAMA fields [Foster et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASA...38...31F/abstract).

To address these questions and expand the multi-wavelength legacy of GAMA, the survey's infrastructure and meticulously defined fields serve as direct precursors to massively multiplexed next-generation campaigns. In the radio regime, the Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder (ASKAP) is systematically surveying GAMA fields to probe cold gas astrophysics. A central pillar of this effort is the ASKAP [[dingo-survey|DINGO survey]] (Deep Investigation of Neutral Gas Origins). Because the direct detection of 21-cm emission is challenging at cosmological distances, DINGO leverages the highly complete GAMA redshift catalogs to perform neutral hydrogen stacking. Early science results in the GAMA 23h field have successfully used neutral hydrogen stacking with ASKAP to measure the baseline HI gas content of galaxies at $0.04 < z < 0.09$ [Rhee et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.4646R/abstract). Independently, separate stacking analyses utilizing data from the Arecibo Legacy Fast ALFA (ALFALFA) survey quantified the depletion of HI as a function of group halo mass [Dev et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.2693D/abstract). Additionally, ASKAP's EMU (Evolutionary Map of the Universe) survey has utilized these well-characterized regions to great effect, including the recent discovery of 63 giant radio galaxies within the GAMA-23h field [Gürkan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.6104G/abstract). These ASKAP campaigns act as a vital testing ground for the future SKA (Square Kilometre Array), demonstrating the power of combining deep optical priors with next-generation radio interferometry.

In the optical and near-infrared, GAMA's methodology and panchromatic source catalogs—recently reprocessed using the automated PROFOUND image analysis package—are heavily utilized for [[target-selection|Target selection]] in upcoming massively multiplexed spectrographs [Bellstedt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496.3235B/abstract). Chief among these is the 4MOST facility, which will execute the 4HS (4MOST Hemisphere Survey). Expanding the GAMA strategy to a hemispheric scale, 4HS aims to obtain uniform spectroscopy for approximately 6 million galaxies over $\sim 2\pi$ steradians, achieving highly unbiased completeness at $z < 0.15$ [Taylor et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Msngr.190...46T/abstract). This unprecedented volume will complete the kinematic map of the [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|Local Volume and Nearby Galaxy Studies]] and provide the overwhelming statistical power required to firmly disentangle the physical drivers of environmental quenching. The methodological legacy of GAMA also directly influences other major spectroscopic campaigns; for instance, the next-generation [[desi-bright-galaxy-survey|DESI Bright Galaxy Survey]] builds upon the legacy of GAMA by producing new k-correction fits that supersede those from GAMA Data Release 4 [Moore et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251101803M/abstract).

Furthermore, GAMA spectroscopy has been utilized to calibrate the photometric redshifts of massive cluster catalogs identified in wide-field photometric surveys like KiDS using algorithms such as [[amico-galaxy-cluster-catalogue|AMICO galaxy cluster catalogue]] [Maturi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.201M/abstract). The GAMA footprint also provides a unique testing ground for multi-tracer techniques, which aim to mitigate cosmic variance by observing multiple distinct types of galaxy tracers within the same volume [Wang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020RAA....20..158W/abstract). Looking ahead to future spectroscopic advancements, the Hector survey plans to expand GAMA's reach by obtaining integral field spectroscopy for 100,000 galaxies, providing a massive statistical increase over the current SAMI sample [Bland-Hawthorn et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..309...21B/abstract).

Additionally, the GAMA survey's strategy of dense, multi-pass fiber allocation directly informs the design of deeper tier campaigns like WAVES (Wide Area VISTA Extragalactic Survey). WAVES will push the GAMA paradigm to higher redshifts, balancing survey area and targeting completeness to minimize the cosmic variance that often plagues deep extragalactic observations [Pearl et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...925..180P/abstract). Together, these synergistic programs ensure that GAMA's foundational datasets will continue to anchor studies of galaxy assembly, from the local mapping of HI gas to the overarching structure of the cosmic web.
