The **Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1)** is the first major public data release from the European Space Agency's [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]] mission, comprising high-resolution imaging and spectroscopy from the Early Release Observations (ERO) and the mission's initial Science Demonstration Phase. Designed to map the geometry of the "dark universe," Euclid pairs a wide field of view with space-based resolution using two primary instruments: a visible-wavelength imager (VIS) and a near-infrared spectrometer and photometer (NISP) [Laureijs et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012SPIE.8442E..0TL/abstract), [Troja et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023chep.confE..94T/abstract). Covering approximately 63 square degrees, the Q1 release represents a massive leap forward in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]]. It reaches median point-source depths of 27.1 AB mag in the optical and successfully validates the automated extraction of millions of spectra via wide-field [[slitless-spectroscopy|Slitless spectroscopy]] [Cuillandre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513496C/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315307E/abstract). By providing deep, high-resolution multi-band imaging that far exceeds the capabilities of classical ground-based campaigns like the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS)]], Euclid Q1 establishes a new observational baseline for tracking cosmic structure from the local Universe to the Epoch of Reionization.

The Q1 data highlight a diverse array of astrophysical findings that showcase the mission's transformational capabilities across multiple scales. Because the extended point spread function of the VIS instrument closely approximates a pure diffraction halo, Euclid is uniquely optimized for [[low-surface-brightness-astronomy|Low Surface Brightness Astronomy]]. This sensitivity has enabled unprecedented mapping of diffuse intracluster light and the discovery of hundreds of new dwarf galaxies in nearby structures like the Perseus cluster. At cosmological distances, Euclid's high-resolution imaging and machine-learning discovery engines have already doubled the known sample of space-based strong gravitational lenses and facilitated a vast census of active galactic nuclei. Furthermore, Q1 severely challenges textbook limits in [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|High-Redshift Galaxy Observations]] by identifying tens of thousands of High-redshift Infrared Extremely Red Objects (HIEROs). While classical extremely red objects (EROs) were typically constrained to $z \sim 0.7-1.5$, these newly identified HIEROs trace a massive, dust-obscured population at much earlier epochs ($z \sim 3-6$) that was largely missed by historical rest-frame ultraviolet surveys.

Despite its early successes, the Q1 release also surfaces critical methodological challenges and theoretical tensions that will define future cosmological analyses. For instance, while the mission's photometric redshift (photo-$z$) pipelines demonstrate excellent precision, they currently exhibit biases that require strict calibration corrections to meet the accuracy thresholds necessary for dark energy probes [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315306E/abstract). Additionally, deriving physical properties for the newly discovered HIEROs is plagued by spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting degeneracies; without further short-wavelength constraints, it remains ambiguous whether some of these sources are truly massive early galaxies or lower-redshift dust-obscured interlopers. On cluster scales, the faint-end slopes of the Perseus cluster's luminosity and stellar mass functions are notably flatter than the predictions of standard dark matter halo simulations, raising fundamental questions about the efficiency of central stellar mass build-up in current theoretical models. Resolving these open questions will ultimately rely on the immense statistical power of upcoming Euclid data releases and sophisticated joint survey processing with future facilities like the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]].

## Mission Context and the Q1 Release

The European Space Agency (ESA) [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]] mission was designed to map the geometry of the dark universe, using weak gravitational lensing and galaxy clustering to constrain the properties of dark energy, dark matter, and gravity [Laureijs et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012SPIE.8442E..0TL/abstract), [Racca et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016SPIE.9904E..0OR/abstract). Over its nominal six-year primary mission, Euclid will survey approximately $15,000 \text{ deg}^2$ of the sky. The observatory's payload features a 1.2-meter Korsch telescope that feeds two primary instruments: the visible imager (VIS) and the near-infrared spectrometer and photometer (NISP) [Racca et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016SPIE.9904E..0OR/abstract), [Troja et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023chep.confE..94T/abstract). 

Prior to the main survey, the Euclid Consortium executed the Early Release Observations (ERO) program. Allocating 24 hours of observation time in the mission's early months, the ERO targeted 17 distinct astronomical objects—ranging from nearby star-forming regions to distant galaxy clusters—to demonstrate the telescope's capabilities and engage the community through an early public data release [Cuillandre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513496C/abstract), [Cuillandre et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A...6C/abstract). The subsequent Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1) builds upon this foundation, delivering science-grade data products over a $63.1 \text{ deg}^2$ footprint [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315309E/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315311E/abstract). The observing strategy relies on a single Reference Observing Sequence, which efficiently maps a large field of view while preserving exquisite spatial resolution on parsec scales for nearby targets [Hunt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513499H/abstract), [Hunt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A...9H/abstract).

Data from the Q1 release confirm that both VIS and NISP meet or exceed pre-launch specifications. The VIS instrument achieves a point spread function (PSF) full width at half maximum (FWHM) of $0.16''$ in the broad optical $I_{\rm E}$-band, while NISP delivers a FWHM of $0.49''$ across the near-infrared $Y_{\rm E}$, $J_{\rm E}$, and $H_{\rm E}$ bands [Cuillandre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513496C/abstract), [Cuillandre et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A...6C/abstract). The photometric depth is unprecedented for a wide-field space survey: for point sources, the median $5\sigma$ depth reaches $27.1$ AB mag in VIS and $24.5$ AB mag in NISP [Cuillandre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513496C/abstract), [Cuillandre et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A...6C/abstract). VIS absolute flux calibration is accurate to roughly $1\%$. However, NISP calibration is currently limited to approximately $10\%$ accuracy due to the restricted calibration set available during the early mission phase, and both instruments exhibit notable colour terms that must be accounted for in precision measurements [Cuillandre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513496C/abstract), [Cuillandre et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A...6C/abstract).

In addition to deep imaging, Q1 provides a massive spectroscopic dataset. Data processing pipelines successfully extracted approximately 3 million validated [[slitless-spectroscopy|Slitless spectroscopy]] measurements using the NISP red-grism mode, demonstrating that both wavelength accuracy and resolving power fall well within mission requirements [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315307E/abstract). 

Furthermore, the ERO and Q1 data showcase Euclid's exceptional sensitivity to diffuse emission. Because the extended PSF closely approximates a pure diffraction halo, the telescope is highly optimized for low surface brightness astronomy. For an extended $10'' \times 10''$ feature, $1\sigma$ surface-brightness limits reach between $29.5$ and $30.1$ AB mag/arcsec$^2$ for VIS, and $27.9$ to $29.4$ AB mag/arcsec$^2$ for NISP [Cuillandre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513496C/abstract), [Cuillandre et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A...6C/abstract), [Hunt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513499H/abstract). This resolving power and sensitivity to ultra-faint structures make Euclid a transformative facility for [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], enabling robust, wide-field studies of dwarf galaxies, tidal streams, and intracluster light. Beyond its primary cosmological goals, Euclid is expected to produce vast legacy catalogues with significant impacts on general galaxy science, active galactic nuclei evolution, and detailed studies of the Milky Way and Local Universe [Sorce et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023chep.confE..96S/abstract).

## Historical Development of Extragalactic Surveys

Systematic searches for [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|extragalactic sources]] have undergone a profound evolution over the past two centuries. Formal catalogs of extended astronomical sources trace their origins to the late eighteenth century with the compilation of the [[messier-catalog|Messier Catalog]], which was originally designed to prevent comet-hunters from misidentifying static, fuzzy "nebulae." Today, many of these prominent [[messier-objects|Messier Objects]] are recognized as some of the brightest neighboring galaxies in the sky.

Until the mid-20th century, extragalactic astronomy was almost entirely restricted to optical wavebands. The widespread implementation of deep CCD imaging and the construction of multiplexed ground-based spectrographs transformed the field into a quantitative, statistical discipline. Foremost among these optical efforts was the [[sloan-digital-sky-survey-and-sub-surveys|Sloan Digital Sky Survey]] (SDSS), which utilized a dedicated 2.5-meter telescope to obtain high-quality photometry and spectroscopy for over a million galaxies, effectively mapping the large-scale structure of the local universe [Adelman-McCarthy et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJS..172..634A/abstract). This foundation was expanded by initiatives like the MaNGA IFU survey within the SDSS-IV program, which produced detailed three-dimensional data cubes to explore internal galaxy structures for approximately 10,000 low-redshift systems [Law et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152...83L/abstract), and the SAMI Galaxy Survey, whose final data release delivered [[integral-field-spectroscopy|integral-field spectroscopy]] for over 3,000 galaxies, providing 2D maps of gas ionization and stellar kinematics across large-scale structures [Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..991C/abstract). Furthermore, wide-field optical mapping was enriched by the [[gama-survey|Galaxy And Mass Assembly (GAMA)]] Panchromatic Data Release, which provided 21-band photometry for over 221,000 galaxies, enabling empirical measurements of the Universe's total energy output from 0.1 to 500 microns [Driver et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.3911D/abstract). In parallel, deep spectroscopic efforts like the VANDELS survey targeted star-forming galaxies at $z=2.4-5.5$ with ultra-long exposures to study high-redshift galaxy evolution via absorption lines [Pentericci et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...616A.174P/abstract). Additionally, modern ground-based efforts such as the S-PLUS survey have provided a crucial southern hemisphere multi-band baseline, mapping over 3000 square degrees with 12 filters to enable precise star-galaxy-quasar classification for fields that overlap with Euclid [Herpich et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.249H/abstract).

While optical surveys successfully mapped rest-frame ultraviolet and blue light, such selections are strongly biased toward systems with high star-formation rates and low dust obscuration. To conduct a true mass-selected census of the universe—tracing the evolved, old stellar populations that dominate galactic baryonic mass—astronomers required large-format near-infrared (NIR) detectors. The transition to [[near-infrared-astronomy|Near-Infrared Astronomy]] was spearheaded by [[all-sky-surveys|all-sky initiatives]] such as the [[two-micron-all-sky-survey-2mass|Two Micron All-Sky Survey (2MASS)]], which mapped $44,599$ galaxies down to $K_s \le 11.75$ mag to provide a comprehensive 3D distribution of baryonic mass in the nearby universe [Huchra et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJS..199...26H/abstract).

As large-format NIR arrays improved, so did the capacity for deep-space missions. The [[spitzer-space-telescope|Spitzer Space Telescope]] extended deep photometric surveys into the mid-infrared. Legacy programs like the Spitzer Extended Deep Survey (SEDS) imaged $1.46$ deg$^2$ to a depth of $26$ AB mag at $3.6\ \mu\text{m}$ and $4.5\ \mu\text{m}$ [Ashby et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...769...80A/abstract), while the Spitzer South Pole Telescope Deep Field (SSDF) covered an unprecedented $94$ deg$^2$ of extragalactic sky [Ashby et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJS..209...22A/abstract). In parallel, initiatives like the Spitzer Data Fusion merged IRAC and MIPS data for 4.4 million sources, providing the essential multi-wavelength baseline that later enabled Euclid's identification of high-redshift extremely red objects [Vaccari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015fers.confE..27V/abstract).

These NIR and mid-IR capabilities led to the discovery of Extremely Red Objects (EROs)—faint galaxies defined by unusually red optical-to-NIR colors, such as $R - K > 5$. Canonical textbook models historically placed the majority of EROs at intermediate redshifts of $0.7 \lesssim z \lesssim 1.5$, driven either by strong $4000$ Å breaks in old stellar populations or severe dust extinction. However, early data from the [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid]] Quick Data Release (Q1) severely challenges this established limit. By cross-matching Euclid's deep NIR photometry with archival Spitzer data, researchers have identified a population of "High-redshift Infrared Extremely Red Objects" (HIEROs) exhibiting extreme colors (e.g., $H_E - \text{IRAC}2 > 2.25$). Contrary to historical text models, these sources reside at much higher redshifts ($z \sim 3-6$) and represent a massive ($M_* > 10^{11} M_\odot$), dust-obscured population largely missed by traditional rest-frame UV surveys [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315322E/abstract). While some initial estimates place them at much higher redshifts ($z \sim 3-6$) with extreme masses, researchers caution that SED fitting at these epochs is currently highly degenerate and unreliable, meaning many of these objects may actually be lower-redshift interlopers [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315322E/abstract).

Furthermore, where historical spectroscopic surveys required decades of targeted fiber-plugging or slit alignment to yield a million spectra, Euclid represents a methodological paradigm shift. Preceding Euclid's space-based spectroscopic ramp-up, ground-based programs like the [[lamost-spectroscopic-survey|LAMOST survey]] demonstrated the power of massive multiplexing by releasing over 10 million spectra in its first decade [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Innov...300224Y/abstract). Building on this legacy, Euclid utilizes its wide-field slitless red-grism capabilities to process millions of galaxies simultaneously at substantially higher spatial densities. In Q1 alone, the mission successfully processed and validated approximately 3 million spectra, demonstrating a massive leap into highly automated, space-based cosmological mapping [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315307E/abstract).

## Instrumentation and Data Processing Pipelines

The [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]] Quick Data Release (Q1) demonstrates the exceptional performance of its two primary instruments: the visible imager (VIS) and the Near Infrared Spectrometer and Photometer (NISP). VIS achieves a point spread function (PSF) full width at half maximum (FWHM) of $0.16^{\prime\prime}$ in the optical $I_{\rm E}$ band, reaching a $5\sigma$ point-source depth of 27.1 AB mag [Cuillandre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513496C/abstract). The VIS mean absolute flux calibration is accurate to approximately $1\%$, although considerable colour terms remain for individual sources [Cuillandre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513496C/abstract), [Cuillandre et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A...6C/abstract). The NISP instrument, equipped with a large focal plane array of 16 near-infrared H2RG detectors, provides imaging in the $Y_{\rm E}$, $J_{\rm E}$, and $H_{\rm E}$ bands with a PSF FWHM of $0.49^{\prime\prime}$ and a $5\sigma$ point-source depth of 24.5 AB mag [Cuillandre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513496C/abstract). Careful on-ground and in-flight characterization of the H2RG detectors is required to account for spatial variations in interpixel capacitance (IPC) and conversion gain [Le Graët et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022SPIE12191E..1ML/abstract). Due to a limited initial calibration set, NISP absolute flux calibration in Q1 is currently accurate to $\sim 10\%$ [Cuillandre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513496C/abstract).

The extraction of photometric redshifts and physical galaxy parameters relies on the PHZ processing function, which combines Euclid's space-based photometry with ground-based [[ancillary-data|Ancillary data]]. The PHZ pipeline performs source classification (separating stars, galaxies, and quasars) and computes photometric redshifts. The calibration of these photometric redshift requirements heavily relies on foundational ground-based efforts like the Complete Calibration of the Color-Redshift Relation (C3R2) survey, which mapped the color-redshift relation down to a depth of $i \sim 24.5$ [Masters et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877...81M/abstract). Validation against the [[cosmos-galaxy-catalogue|COSMOS galaxy catalogue]] demonstrates that the precision ($\sigma_{\rm NMAD}$) of the photometric redshifts is fully compatible with mission requirements [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315306E/abstract). However, the literature explicitly notes that a bias correction must be applied to these initial estimates to achieve the stringent accuracy levels required for cosmological probes [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315306E/abstract). Furthermore, the PHZ pipeline estimates physical properties such as stellar mass and star-formation rates. Pre-launch preparations established that employing convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for image-based deep learning improves stellar mass estimates more effectively than traditional SED fitting [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.3529E/abstract). However, early Q1 outputs still exhibited an unrealistically large fraction of very young galaxies with extremely high specific star-formation rates; the application of appropriate priors during spectral energy distribution fitting is necessary to resolve this anomaly and obtain reliable physical parameters [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315306E/abstract).

For spectroscopic measurements, Euclid employs wide-field slitless spectroscopy via the NISP instrument. The translation of raw 2D spectrograms into science-grade 1D spectra is handled by the SIR processing function. This pipeline utilizes a photometric source catalog to model and subtract cross-contamination and self-contamination between overlapping spectral traces—a fundamental challenge in slitless spectroscopy [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315307E/abstract). The SIR processing function also rigorously corrects for detector effects, applies flux calibration, and performs precise wavelength calibration. Analysis of the Q1 data confirms that the wavelength calibration accuracy and the resolving power both meet mission requirements, reflecting the excellent optical quality of the telescope [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315307E/abstract). The Q1 release includes approximately three million validated 1D spectra from the red-grism observing mode, providing a massive dataset for immediate astrophysical use and laying the groundwork for Euclid's future large-scale clustering measurements [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315307E/abstract).

## Galaxy Morphology and Structural Parameters

The Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1) leverages the mission's high spatial resolution and multi-band photometric coverage to produce detailed morphological catalogs across 63.1 square degrees. The structural analysis of these galaxies utilizes a combination of automated parametric modeling, citizen science, and deep learning algorithms, providing robust metrics for galaxy classification and secular evolution.

**Sérsic Fits and Structural Parameters**
Automated single-component Sérsic profile fitting was performed for a magnitude-limited sample of galaxies ($I_{\rm E} < 23$) using the SourceXtractor++ software [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315309E/abstract). Comparing the visible (VIS) and near-infrared (NISP) bands reveals distinct chromatic structural variations: profiles measured in the NISP $Y_{\rm E}$, $J_{\rm E}$, and $H_{\rm E}$ bands exhibit systematically smaller effective radii ($R_{\rm e}$) and larger Sérsic indices ($n$) than those measured in the VIS $I_{\rm E}$ band. These structural parameters are highly consistent with archival high-resolution imaging from [[hst-observations|HST observations]]. Furthermore, a clear bimodality in the $(u-r)$ versus $\log(n)$ plane allows for a robust separation of early-type galaxies (ETGs) and late-type galaxies (LTGs). Within this parameter space, ETGs display a steeper size-stellar mass relation, reflecting fundamental differences in their mass assembly histories [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315309E/abstract). Complementing these structural distinctions, researchers have applied a probabilistic framework to categorize galaxies based on specific star-formation rate timescales, effectively dividing transitioning galaxies into 'Ageing', 'Quenched', and 'Retired' populations [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315315E/abstract). To further optimize the selection of these passive systems, the [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid]] Consortium developed the ARIADNE pipeline, which utilizes meta-learning to identify quiescent galaxies with significantly higher completeness than standard color-color cuts [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...671A..99E/abstract).

**Visual Morphology and Deep Learning**
Beyond parametric fitting, Q1 incorporates advanced machine learning techniques to catalog complex visual morphology. Building on the foundational application of deep learning for morphological classification established by predecessor catalogs like the SDSS-IV MaNGA PyMorph release [Fischer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.2057F/abstract), researchers fine-tuned the Zoobot foundation model on annotations provided by Galaxy Zoo volunteers. This effort resulted in a visual morphology catalog for 378,000 bright ($I_{\rm E} < 20.5$) or spatially extended (area $\geq 700$ pixels) galaxies [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315310E/abstract). This hybrid approach successfully identifies non-parametric features such as spiral arms, tidal disturbances, and stellar bars at scale. Exploratory work has also demonstrated that multi-modal foundation models (e.g., AstroPT), trained simultaneously on Euclid imaging and spectral energy distributions, can effectively capture morphological representations and isolate anomalous sources with minimal supervised training [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315312E/abstract).

**Stellar Bars and Cosmological Tensions**
The Q1 morphological catalogs provide critical constraints on secular evolution, particularly through the census of stellar bars. In a sample of 7,711 barred galaxies with stellar masses $M_* \gtrsim 10^{10} M_\odot$ at $z < 1$, the observed bar fraction ranges from 0.2 to 0.4 [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315311E/abstract). Massive galaxies exhibit consistently higher bar fractions than lower-mass systems at fixed redshift, with the lower-mass systems showing a steeper decline in bar prevalence at earlier cosmic times. Notably, there is an active tension between these observations and leading cosmological simulations (such as TNG50 and Auriga). The simulations overpredict the bar fraction in high-mass systems, suggesting that current theoretical models implement an overly efficient central stellar mass build-up that does not perfectly reflect the observed Universe [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315311E/abstract).

**Secondary Nuclei and Merger Signatures**
The exceptional resolution of the VIS instrument also enables the detection of sub-galactic structural anomalies linked to hierarchical merging. An analysis of massive ETGs in the Q1 footprint identified 666 candidate secondary nuclei across 504 host galaxies [Fabricius et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251102988F/abstract). While the majority exhibit projected separations of $3$ to $15\ \mathrm{kpc}$—typical of ongoing mergers—44 candidates are found at ultra-close separations of less than $2\ \mathrm{kpc}$. These compact systems, which are too massive to be standard globular clusters and remain unresolved even at Euclid's resolution, are theorized to be the dense remnants of merged galaxies sinking into a common gravitational potential, making them strong candidate hosts for secondary supermassive black holes [Fabricius et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251102988F/abstract).

## The Local Universe and Low Surface Brightness Features

The Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1) and its associated Early Release Observations (ERO) have demonstrated the observatory's exceptional capabilities for [[low-surface-brightness-astronomy|Low Surface Brightness Astronomy]]. The optical VIS instrument features an extended point spread function (PSF) that nearly matches a pure diffraction halo, making it one of the most stable and precise wide-field imagers for detecting diffuse emission [Cuillandre et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A...6C/abstract). Stacked images from the ERO reach remarkable $1\sigma$ surface brightness limits of $\sim 30.1-30.5$ mag arcsec$^{-2}$ in the optical $I_{\rm E}$-band and $\sim 29.2-29.4$ mag arcsec$^{-2}$ in the near-infrared NISP bands ($Y_{\rm E}$, $J_{\rm E}$, $H_{\rm E}$) measured over 100 arcsec$^2$ regions [Hunt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A...9H/abstract), [Cuillandre et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A...6C/abstract).

A focal point of the Q1 release is the Nearby Galaxy Showcase, which targeted six galaxies in the local Universe spanning distances from 0.5 to 8.8 Mpc: three dwarf galaxies (Holmberg II, IC 10, and NGC 6822) and three massive spirals (IC 342, NGC 2403, and NGC 6744) [Hunt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A...9H/abstract). [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid]]'s high spatial resolution enabled the extraction of approximately 1.3 million individual stars across these fields. By disentangling the distributions of young stars, asymptotic giant branch stars, and red giant branch populations, researchers reconstructed detailed star-formation histories on parsec scales [Hunt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A...9H/abstract). Furthermore, the large field of view allowed for comprehensive censuses of satellite systems and star clusters. For example, the data confirmed the satellite nature of a previously identified candidate, dw1909m6341, a nucleated dwarf spheroidal situated near a spiral arm of NGC 6744, as well as nine new globular cluster candidates around NGC 2403, most of which exhibit colours indicative of evolved stellar populations [Hunt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A...9H/abstract). While Euclid delivers unprecedented wide-field continuum sensitivity, characterizing these faint dwarf populations often benefits from synergy with medium-band ground-based observations; for example, the Merian Survey provides vital H-alpha and [O III] excess detection for dwarf galaxies at $z \sim 0.06-0.10$, heavily complementing Euclid's continuum imaging capabilities [Danieli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..110D/abstract).

In denser environments, ERO imaging of the Perseus cluster core provided an unprecedented view of diffuse intracluster light. The spatial distribution of this diffuse emission, along with its associated globular clusters, reveals an isophotal center displaced to the west of the bright cluster galaxies, tracing the complex assembly history of the Perseus core [Marleau et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A..12M/abstract). Furthermore, deep imaging of the cluster's spiral galaxies has revealed ultra-faint, filamentary tails indicative of ram-pressure stripping, illustrating Euclid's capability to capture the morphological transformation of galaxies undergoing environmental quenching [George et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A..40G/abstract).

## Galaxy Clusters and the Cosmic Web

The [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]] Quick Data Release (Q1) provides a high-resolution, multi-wavelength view of galaxy clusters and their embedding within the cosmic web. By combining Euclid's optical and near-infrared imaging with ancillary data from the [[spitzer-space-telescope|Spitzer Space Telescope]], Q1 extends the detection of massive structures to early epochs. Cross-matching Euclid data with Spitzer infrared overdensities has confirmed the presence of high-redshift galaxy clusters and protoclusters at $z > 1.3$, environments characterized by significant concentrations of passive galaxies [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315331E/abstract). Expanding the search for early cosmic structures, Euclid has also targeted Planck high star-forming galaxy protocluster candidates at $z > 1.5$ using the DETECTIFz overdensity finder, seeking to map the most intense sites of star formation at cosmic noon [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250321304E/abstract).

The nearby Perseus cluster serves as a primary testbed for Euclid's capabilities in [[deep-imaging-surveys|Deep imaging surveys]] of the local Universe. Q1 observations of the cluster's central $0.7$ square degrees reach an exceptional point-source depth of $I_E = 28.0$ AB mag ($5\sigma$) and a surface brightness limit of $30.1 \text{ mag arcsec}^{-2}$ [Cuillandre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513501C/abstract). To anchor these new space-based detections and large-scale scaling relations, Euclid researchers also rely on comprehensive spectroscopic baselines such as the SPIDERS catalogue, which compiled observations of 2,740 spectroscopically confirmed X-ray galaxy clusters [Kirkpatrick et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.5763K/abstract). This extensive sensitivity enables a comprehensive census of the low surface brightness Universe. Visual and automated inspections have identified approximately 1,100 dwarf galaxy candidates in Perseus, of which 606 represent new discoveries. The population is heavily dominated by dwarf ellipticals (96%), with 53% exhibiting nucleation and 8% classified as ultra-diffuse galaxies [Marleau et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A..12M/abstract).

The deep Perseus data allow for precise measurements of the cluster's luminosity function (LF) and stellar mass function (SMF). Observations reveal a dip in the LF at $M(I_E) \approx -19$ and a faint-end slope ($\alpha_S$) ranging between $-1.2$ and $-1.3$ [Cuillandre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513501C/abstract). Similarly, the low-mass end of the SMF exhibits a slope of $\alpha_S = -1.2$ to $-1.35$. Crucially, the literature notes a clear tension: these observed slopes are flatter than those predicted for dark matter halos in standard cosmological simulations. This discrepancy explicitly highlights that the mapping between dark matter halo mass and stellar mass at the faint end may require revision, suggesting that central stellar mass build-up in current models may be over-efficient [Cuillandre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513501C/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315311E/abstract).

Euclid Q1 also captures the physical mechanisms driving galaxy evolution and environmental quenching within dense cluster environments. Observations of spiral galaxies in Perseus, such as UGC 2665 and MCG +07-07-070, reveal extended, cometary filamentary structures characteristic of ram-pressure stripping [George et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A..40G/abstract). These low surface brightness stripped tails exhibit parsec-scale star-forming knots ($\sim 100 \text{ pc}$ in size) visible in the $I_E$-band imaging, demonstrating that star formation can occur within stripped gas in the intracluster medium. By combining Euclid colours with ancillary multi-wavelength data from facilities like the [[canada-france-hawaii-telescope|Canada-France-Hawaii Telescope]], researchers confirmed the presence of young stellar populations and stripped dust within these features [George et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A..40G/abstract). Beyond the local Universe, Euclid's high-resolution imaging of distant, intra-cluster medium (ICM)-selected clusters has captured extreme environmental quenching at earlier epochs, identifying the highest-redshift jellyfish galaxy candidate to date (EUCLJ035330.86−504347.6 at $z=1.32$) within the cluster SPT-CLJ0353−5043 [Klein et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250619566K/abstract).

Beyond individual clusters, Q1 data have been used to reconstruct the cosmic web and investigate how cluster connectivity—the number of large-scale cosmic filaments connected to a cluster node—shapes galaxy evolution. An analysis of 220 clusters at $0.2 < z < 0.7$ confirms that massive clusters are connected to a larger number of filaments, consistent with hierarchical structure formation models [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315332E/abstract). Furthermore, clusters hosting a higher fraction of early-type galaxies exhibit higher connectivity. At the galaxy scale, Q1 reveals a significant alignment effect: massive early-type galaxies ($M_* > 10^{10} M_\odot$) preferentially align their major axes with the spines of their host filaments [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315333E/abstract). In the broader context of mapping the cosmic web, Euclid's wide-field mapping will eventually complement novel large-scale structure tracers such as the LATIS survey, which has produced extensive intergalactic medium (IGM) tomography maps alongside thousands of spectra for galaxies at $z \sim 2-3$ [Newman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJS..281...32N/abstract).

## Strong Gravitational Lensing Discovery

The [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]] Quick Data Release (Q1) establishes a new paradigm for strong gravitational lensing discovery, marking a transition from targeted visual searches to automated, machine-learning-driven discovery engines applied over wide fields [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315324E/abstract). Benefiting from a spatial resolution capable of resolving Einstein radii $\theta_{\rm E} < 1''$, the Q1 release—covering just 63.1 square degrees—has doubled the total number of known lens candidates identified via space-based imaging [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315324E/abstract). 

The Q1 data release contains a vetted catalogue of 497 galaxy-galaxy strong lenses, of which 250 are considered high-confidence ("grade A") candidates exhibiting complete Einstein rings, extended gravitational arcs, or quadruply-imaged source configurations [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315324E/abstract). While deep learning algorithms are highly efficient, the literature explicitly notes a tension in their application: individual machine learning models generate significant false positives when processing millions of heterogeneous galaxy images. To achieve high completeness and purity, Euclid's discovery engine requires an ensemble approach. By combining outputs from eight different neural networks—including a fine-tuned version of the foundation model Zoobot—with citizen science visual inspection, the ensemble classifier achieves a purity of $52 \pm 2\%$ at a completeness of 50% [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315326E/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315328E/abstract). Additionally, expert visual inspection of pre-selected galaxies with high stellar velocity dispersions ($\sigma_\nu > 180 \text{ km s}^{-1}$) yielded 38 grade A candidates and provided critical "non-lens" examples to further train automated classifiers [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315325E/abstract). Extrapolating these Q1 yields suggests that the full Euclid Wide Survey will discover over 100,000 high-confidence galaxy-scale lenses [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315324E/abstract).

Among the most scientifically valuable discoveries in Q1 are four candidate galaxy-scale double-source-plane lenses (DSPLs) [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315327E/abstract). In these exceptionally rare systems, a single massive foreground galaxy simultaneously lenses two background sources located at different redshifts. DSPLs serve as powerful cosmological probes, as the ratio of their Einstein radii is tightly coupled to the dark energy equation of state via the cosmological scaling factor $\beta$. The identification of four DSPLs in the Q1 footprint aligns with forecasts predicting the discovery of approximately 1,700 such systems over the mission's lifetime [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315327E/abstract).

Beyond galaxy-scale systems, Q1 includes the first Euclid catalogue of strong-lensing galaxy clusters. Visual inspection of 1,260 cluster fields identified 83 gravitational lenses with a high probability ($\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{lens}} > 0.5$) of exhibiting giant tangential and radial arcs, only three of which had been previously observed from space [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315330E/abstract). Based on a measured number density of $\approx 0.3 \text{ deg}^{-2}$ for high-probability targets, Euclid is projected to discover over 4,500 strong lensing clusters [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315330E/abstract). To process these complex and extended features at scale, researchers developed the ARTEMIDE framework, which utilizes Mask Region-based Convolutional Neural Networks (Mask R-CNNs) for the automated segmentation of bright giant gravitational arcs within cluster fields [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251103064E/abstract).

To handle the unprecedented volume of lenses expected in future data releases, Q1 also served as a testbed for automated lens modeling using [[astronomical-software|Astronomical software]]. The Bayesian neural network LEMON (LEns MOdelling with Neural networks) was developed to rapidly estimate lens mass profile parameters, assuming a singular isothermal ellipsoid mass profile [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315329E/abstract). When LEMON's parameter predictions are utilized as initial priors for classical optimizers, the computational modeling time per lens is reduced by a factor of up to 26, enabling the mass-modeling of thousands of lenses simultaneously [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315329E/abstract).

## High-Redshift Galaxies and AGN Census

The Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1) provides an unprecedented window into the distant universe, enabling large-scale censuses of both early-universe galaxies and active galactic nuclei (AGN). By combining deep near-infrared (NIR) imaging with multi-wavelength ancillary data, [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid]] is fundamentally shifting the observational paradigm for high-redshift astrophysics from rest-frame ultraviolet (UV) searches to NIR and mid-infrared (MIR) selection. 

### High-Redshift and Massive Galaxies
Classical textbook descriptions of [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|High-Redshift Galaxy Observations]] have heavily relied on rest-frame UV drop-out techniques, which effectively identify unobscured star-forming galaxies but frequently miss dusty or evolved massive systems. Furthermore, standard Extremely Red Objects (EROs) have historically been constrained to $z \sim 0.7-1.5$. Euclid's NISP instrument, coupled with cross-matched MIR data from the [[spitzer-space-telescope|Spitzer Space Telescope]], has revealed a hidden population of High-redshift Infrared Extremely Red Objects (HIEROs) at much earlier epochs. Using a strict colour cut of
$$ H_E - \text{IRAC2} > 2.25 $$
researchers have identified approximately 27,000 HIERO candidates in the Euclid Deep Fields [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315322E/abstract). While some of these systems are projected to have extreme stellar masses ($M_* > 10^{11} M_\odot$) at $z > 3$, the literature remains in tension; current spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting tools suffer from significant degeneracies at $z > 3.5$, and lower-redshift solutions cannot be entirely ruled out without supplementary short-wavelength spectroscopy [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315322E/abstract). 

Pushing further into the Epoch of Reionization, Euclid Q1 has facilitated the discovery of 145 candidate massive galaxies at $z=5-7$ with $M_* > 10^{10.25} M_\odot$, including at least five extreme systems exceeding $10^{11} M_\odot$ [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251111943E/abstract). The inferred stellar masses of these early massive galaxies are found to be consistent with conventional galaxy formation models with standard star-formation efficiencies [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251111943E/abstract). Additionally, in the fields of massive clusters Abell 2390 and Abell 2764, Euclid has identified 30 Lyman-break galaxy candidates at $z > 6$ and 139 extremely red sources, further demonstrating its capability as a powerful discovery engine for the early universe [Atek et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A..15A/abstract). The robust validation of Euclid's photometric high-redshift candidates will be significantly bolstered by comparing them against ultra-deep, high-resolution spectroscopic baselines at $z > 5$, such as those provided by the JWST Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey (JADES) [D'Eugenio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJS..277....4D/abstract) and the Cosmic Evolution Early Release Science Survey (CEERS), which has provided a critical high-redshift baseline by identifying large samples of galaxies at $z \gtrsim 10$ and characterizing bulge and disk structures as early as $z > 3$ [Finkelstein et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983L...4F/abstract). Additionally, the Cosmic Dawn Survey (DAWN) will heavily complement deep Euclid imaging with matched multi-wavelength data to provide photometric catalogues and redshifts for galaxies up to $z \sim 10$ [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A.259E/abstract).

In the Euclid Deep Fields, robust photometry has provided a first comprehensive view of the star-forming main sequence (SFMS). Observations reveal a distinct bending of the SFMS relation at the high-mass end ($M_* > 10^{11} M_\odot$), shedding light on the onset of star-formation quenching in massive systems [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315314E/abstract). Furthermore, stacking analyses indicate that these Euclid-selected star-forming galaxies have a profound cosmological footprint, accounting for over 60% of the cosmic infrared background (CIB) at wavelengths of 250-500 $\mu$m [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251102989E/abstract).

### The AGN and Quasar Census
The high spatial resolution and wide-field coverage of Euclid also serve as a powerful engine for AGN discovery. A comprehensive multi-wavelength census has cross-matched Euclid photometry with major X-ray surveys (including [[erosita|eROSITA]], Chandra, and XMM-Newton), securely identifying counterparts to 11,286 X-ray sources [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315316E/abstract). Furthermore, a specific Q1 catalogue of over 229,000 candidate active galaxies has been released, utilizing multi-wavelength criteria from Euclid, [[wide-field-infrared-survey-explorer|WISE]], and [[gaia-mission-and-data-products|Gaia]] to achieve an unprecedented surface density of 3,641 AGN per square degree [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315320E/abstract).

While bright, unobscured quasars are readily identified through combined Euclid and [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]] colors, photometric-only identification remains a subject of active debate. Obscured or composite AGN—where the host galaxy contributes significantly to the emission—are notoriously difficult to separate from purely star-forming galaxies due to broad-band SED similarities in the $0.3-5 \ \mu\text{m}$ range [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240900175E/abstract). 

To overcome these photometric limitations, novel deep learning methods have been applied to Euclid's VIS imaging to isolate the central point-source contribution ($f_{\rm PSF}$) of AGN. By measuring the fraction of light originating from the central engine, models utilizing a threshold of $f_{\rm PSF} > 0.2$ identified $48,840 \pm 78$ AGN over a 63.1 square degree area [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315318E/abstract). Another highly innovative approach employs diffusion-based inpainting of VIS images; by masking the central pixels of a galaxy and using a generative model to reconstruct the expected normal galactic light profile, AGN are successfully identified as significant reconstruction errors where the central light exceeds the expected stellar profile [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315321E/abstract).

Finally, the combination of optical morphologies and radio data has illuminated the environmental drivers of nuclear activity. At $z < 1$, radio-emitting AGN demonstrate a strong preference for interacting environments, with up to 60% residing in merging galaxies, whereas radio-emitting star-forming galaxies are predominantly found in isolated systems [Magliocchetti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251102970M/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1) highlights several outstanding tensions and methodological challenges that will shape the path forward for the mission. For instance, photometric redshift (photo-$z$) estimates produced by the PHZ pipeline demonstrate precision compatible with mission requirements, but they currently exhibit biases that must be corrected to achieve the accuracy demanded by cosmological probes [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315306E/abstract). At high redshifts, the physical nature of High-redshift Infrared Extremely Red Objects (HIEROs) at $z > 3.5$ remains ambiguous; severe SED-fitting degeneracies make it currently unclear whether these are truly massive systems ($M_* > 10^{11} M_\odot$) or lower-redshift dust-obscured galaxies [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315322E/abstract). Additionally, the faint-end slopes of the Perseus cluster's luminosity and stellar mass functions are unexpectedly flatter than the predictions of standard dark matter halo simulations, raising questions about the physical drivers of dwarf galaxy formation and mass assembly [Cuillandre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513501C/abstract).

Addressing these challenges is a primary focus for the upcoming Euclid Data Release 1 (DR1). Managing the immense data volume—anticipated to include over 300 million galaxies—will necessitate highly optimized machine learning models and ensemble classifiers, particularly for the automated discovery of rare strong gravitational lenses where individual models still struggle with false positives [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315328E/abstract). Furthermore, DR1 will feature massive catalogues of slitless red-grism spectra. Providing millions of spectroscopic redshifts will reduce the current reliance on photo-$z$ estimates and drastically improve 3D maps of cosmic web connectivity and intrinsic galaxy alignments [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315332E/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250315333E/abstract).

To fully realize the mission's cosmological goals, [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid]] data must be synergized with wide-field campaigns, most notably the [[vera-c-rubin-observatory-lsst|Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST)]] and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope. Proposed Roman Space Telescope surveys like NANCY aim to provide high-spatial-resolution ($\sim 0.1^{\prime\prime}$) all-sky infrared mapping to directly complement Euclid's wide-field baseline [Han et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230611784H/abstract). Combining high-resolution space-based near-infrared photometry with deep ground-based optical data requires pixel-level Joint Survey Processing to handle mismatched resolutions and complex source deblending [Chary et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200810663C/abstract). Such processing can efficiently mitigate systematic errors, such as the chromatic point-spread function (PSF) variations in Euclid's VIS instrument, by leveraging external multi-band priors [Liu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...669A.128L/abstract). Additionally, hybrid machine learning approaches (e.g., SHAPE, which combines Self-Organizing Maps with SED fitting) are being developed to bridge different photometric systems and improve parameter estimation across overlapping surveys [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251000187W/abstract).

The scientific potential of these multi-observatory synergies is immense. The addition of Euclid's $J$ and $H$ band photometry to Rubin data is projected to reduce LSST photo-$z$ outlier fractions by approximately 40% for galaxies at $z > 1$ [Graham et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....159..258G/abstract). These refined catalogues will enable advanced multi-tracer methodologies that combine spectroscopic and photometric samples in an attempt to beat down cosmic variance, though researchers caution that significant correlations between the probes currently make the actual gain uncertain [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240412157E/abstract). Ultimately, Joint Survey Processing of Euclid, Rubin, and Roman will maximize the constraining power on the Dark Matter distribution and the equation of state of Dark Energy, overcoming the systematic limitations inherent to any single survey [Chary et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200810663C/abstract).
