The **warm neutral medium (WNM)** is a pervasive, diffuse phase of the [[neutral-interstellar-medium|Neutral Interstellar Medium]] composed primarily of neutral atomic hydrogen (H I). Characterized by kinetic temperatures modeled to be somewhat less than $10^4 \text{ K}$ and densities dictated by thermal pressure equilibrium [Wolfire et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...587..278W/abstract), the WNM is the volume-dominant neutral gas component in the disks of the Milky Way and other star-forming galaxies. Under the classical [[two-phase-model|Two-phase model]] of the interstellar medium, the WNM exists in a bistable thermal pressure equilibrium with the much cooler, denser [[cold-neutral-medium|Cold neutral medium]] (CNM) [Wolfire et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...443..152W/abstract). This thermodynamic balance is maintained by the equilibrium between ambient heating—dominated by the photoelectric ejection of electrons from small dust grains and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) via far-ultraviolet radiation—and radiative cooling through fine-structure lines, though observationally the vast majority of fine-structure emission such as [C II] arises from the denser cold phase.

Observationally, the WNM is largely optically thin to 21-cm line emission; consequently, it is primarily detected via broad, unabsorbed 21-cm emission profiles, as well as through ultraviolet spectroscopy of background quasars. While foundational models treated the WNM as a static thermodynamic phase, modern [[three-phase-models|Three-phase models]] and magnetohydrodynamic simulations reveal it to be a highly compressible, dynamically cycling environment. Because of the WNM's elevated thermal sound speed, internal turbulent velocity dispersions are typically subsonic or transonic. However, this turbulence, coupled with ambient magnetic fields, provides crucial non-thermal pressure support that regulates the fragmentation and transition of the WNM into the CNM and subsequent [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] [Vazquez-Semadeni et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1202.4498V/abstract), [McGuiness et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251204184M/abstract). Beyond the galactic disk, the WNM extends up to kiloparsec scales into the galactic halo, forming a complex transitional interface with the [[warm-ionized-medium|Warm Ionized Medium]] and the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]].

In a cosmological context, the WNM is central to the evolution of the early universe, constituting the bulk of the pristine gas mass density prior to and during the Epoch of Reionization [Fan et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ARA&A..44..415F/abstract). At high redshifts, it is the primary reservoir of atomic hydrogen observed in Damped Ly$\alpha$ systems (DLAs). Despite extensive multi-wavelength study, several fundamental properties of the WNM remain subjects of active research. Open questions include the physical origin of tiny-scale atomic structures (TSAS), the precise role of magnetic fields in bottlenecking the condensation of neutral gas into star-forming clouds, and the nature of "extra heating" sources required to explain observed line ratios in high-altitude extraplanar gas. Furthermore, recent hydrodynamic simulations suggest that standard static thermal equilibrium models may underestimate the median gas pressures of the neutral phases by up to $0.5 \text{ dex}$, highlighting an ongoing theoretical tension and the necessity of time-dependent, non-equilibrium frameworks to accurately capture the WNM's dynamic state.

## Physical Properties and Phase Definition

Observations and theoretical models establish that the WNM is the volume-dominant neutral component in the diffuse [[cosmic-media-and-interstellar-medium|interstellar medium]] of the Milky Way and other disk galaxies [Dickey et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988MNRAS.233..781D/abstract). [[physical-properties-of-galactic-gas|Physically]], it is characterized by its low density, elevated temperature, and large volume filling factor, contrasting sharply with the embedded clumps of the [[cold-neutral-medium|Cold neutral medium]] (CNM).

**Thermodynamic and Kinematic Properties**
The WNM is characterized by typical kinetic temperatures modeled at somewhat less than $10^4 \text{ K}$ [Wolfire et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...587..278W/abstract), with some models adopting temperatures near $5000 \text{ K}$ [Vallini et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.433.1567V/abstract). In the solar neighborhood, the total thermal pressure of the interstellar medium is approximately $P/k \approx 3000 \text{ K cm}^{-3}$, which dictates the characteristic WNM number density necessary to maintain equilibrium [Wolfire et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...587..278W/abstract). Locally, the Sun resides in a mixed-cloud environment (including the Local Interstellar Cloud and the G cloud) that exhibits structural inhomogeneities on spatial scales smaller than $4000 \text{ AU}$ [Linsky et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022SSRv..218...16L/abstract). This quiescent local environment can undergo dramatic fluctuations over geological timescales; for instance, evidence suggests the Solar System passed through the Local Ribbon of Cold Clouds 2-3 million years ago, directly exposing the Earth to dense neutral hydrogen environments with densities exceeding $3000 \text{ cm}^{-3}$ [Opher et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8..983O/abstract).

The interaction of partially neutral environments with energetic astrophysical sources can produce distinct observational signatures. For instance, the passage of high-velocity pulsars through partially neutral gas can produce dramatic bow shocks, such as the Guitar Nebula generated by PSR 2224+65 [Cordes et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993Natur.362..133C/abstract). Due to its elevated kinetic temperature, the thermal sound speed of the WNM is relatively large. Therefore, while macroscopic turbulence in the interstellar medium drives highly supersonic flows within the cold, dense CNM, the turbulent velocity dispersions remain largely subsonic or transonic relative to the WNM [Vazquez-Semadeni et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1202.4498V/abstract). This turbulence, operating in tandem with interstellar magnetic fields, provides critical non-thermal pressure support that regulates the fragmentation and gravitational collapse of the WNM into denser star-forming phases such as [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] [Vazquez-Semadeni et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1202.4498V/abstract), [McGuiness et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251204184M/abstract).

**Observational Signatures**
The physical properties of the WNM are predominantly mapped using the 21-cm line of neutral atomic hydrogen. Because of its low density and high temperature, the WNM is largely optically thin to 21-cm radiation; consequently, it is most readily detected as broad emission profiles, in contrast to the CNM, which is frequently observed through narrow absorption features [Dickey et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988MNRAS.233..781D/abstract), [Dutta et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JApA...40...41D/abstract). 

## Historical Development of Multiphase Models

The theoretical understanding of the Warm Neutral Medium (WNM) has its roots in the early stability criteria developed for interstellar gas. Early static frameworks, based on Field's 1965 criteria, demonstrated that gas at intermediate temperatures is thermally unstable and must rapidly segregate into either the warm, diffuse WNM or the dense CNM. This foundational [[two-phase-model|Two-phase model]] established that the WNM and [[cold-neutral-medium|CNM]] could coexist in a state of static thermal pressure equilibrium, dictated by rigorous thermal instability criteria [Ibanez S. et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...275..181I/abstract). 

Modern refinements of the two-phase framework demonstrated that this bistable equilibrium is maintained by a delicate balance between photoelectric heating and fine-structure radiative cooling [Wolfire et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...443..152W/abstract). These models established that the WNM and CNM can coexist only over a specific, narrow range of thermal pressures in the Galactic disk [Wolfire et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...587..278W/abstract). 

While the static two-phase framework successfully explained many bulk observational properties of interstellar clouds, the ubiquitous presence of supernovae and hot gas necessitated the development of [[three-phase-models|Three-phase models]] [Clayton et al. (1978)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1978prpl.conf...13C/abstract). Over time, the theoretical consensus shifted away from a strictly static, pressure-balanced medium toward a highly compressible, turbulence-driven multiphase environment [Vazquez-Semadeni et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1202.4498V/abstract). In this dynamic paradigm, interstellar phases are often transient. Thermal instability in post-shock layers—frequently generated by expanding supernova remnants—acts as a primary dynamical driver forcing the transition of the WNM into the CNM [Inutsuka et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AIPC..784..318I/abstract).

Simulating the evolution of these multiphase structures presents significant computational challenges. Accurately modeling the fragmentation of the WNM and the subsequent WNM-to-CNM transition requires numerically resolving the Field length, which is the critical spatial scale below which thermal conduction suppresses thermal instability [Inutsuka et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002Ap&SS.281...67I/abstract). As computational models have grown more sophisticated, certain conflicts have emerged between classic analytic theory and numerical results. Recent high-resolution hydrodynamic simulations incorporating time-dependent, non-equilibrium chemistry reveal that standard thermal equilibrium predictions may underestimate the median gas pressures of the neutral phases by up to $0.5 \text{ dex}$ [Ploeckinger et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543..891P/abstract), [Ploeckinger et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250615773P/abstract). Consequently, contemporary frameworks increasingly treat the WNM not merely as a distinct thermodynamic phase, but as an active participant in a continuously cycling, turbulent cascade.

## Thermal Balance and Energy Regulation

The thermal state of the Warm Neutral Medium (WNM) is governed by a delicate balance between energy injected into the gas by ambient radiation and particles, and energy radiated away through atomic fine-structure transitions. This thermodynamic balance is the foundational basis for the [[two-phase-model|Two-phase model]] of the [[neutral-interstellar-medium|Neutral Interstellar Medium]].

**Heating Mechanisms**
The dominant heat source for the WNM is the grain photoelectric effect. Far-ultraviolet (FUV) photons from the interstellar radiation field eject electrons from small dust grains and Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs) [Wolfire et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...443..152W/abstract), [Wolfire et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...587..278W/abstract). The excess kinetic energy of these ejected photoelectrons is subsequently thermalized through collisions with the ambient neutral gas, providing the primary heating rate required to sustain its warm temperatures. 

While photoelectric heating from PAHs dominates the global energy budget of the neutral phases, careful studies of PAH chemistry indicate that deuterium may be depleted onto these molecules at a level of $\sim 2.4\%$, a factor that has implications for both the global gas-phase D/H abundance and the precise mechanics of PAH-driven heating [Yang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..251...12Y/abstract). Cosmic ray heating provides a secondary, though occasionally dominant, energy source. Low-energy cosmic rays (LECRs) ionize neutral hydrogen and helium, transferring momentum and heat to the medium. While LECRs are predominantly recognized for maintaining the thermal balance of the adjacent [[warm-ionized-medium|Warm Ionized Medium]] [Walker et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...818...23W/abstract), their impact on the WNM becomes critical in environments with highly elevated cosmic ray fluxes. For example, in the Central Molecular Zone of the Milky Way, the cosmic-ray ionization rate is observed to reach $\zeta \sim 2 \times 10^{-14} \text{ s}^{-1}$, over an order of magnitude higher than in the local diffuse medium, drastically altering the local thermal equilibrium [Oka et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...883...54O/abstract).

**Radiative Cooling**
To maintain equilibrium, the FUV and cosmic-ray heating must be offset by radiative cooling. While fine-structure emission of singly ionized carbon, specifically the [C II] $158 \ \mu\text{m}$ transition, is a key cooling pathway in the interstellar medium, theoretical predictions and observations clarify that [C II] emission predominantly originates from the CNM rather than the WNM. Specifically, approximately $95\%$ of the [C II] line flux is attributed to the cold H I phase, with only $\sim 5\%$ arising from the warm neutral medium [Wolfire et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...443..152W/abstract), [Vallini et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.433.1567V/abstract). Because the ionization potential of neutral carbon is below the hydrogen-ionizing Lyman limit ($13.6 \text{ eV}$), most gas-phase carbon in the WNM exists as C$^+$. Collisional excitation of C$^+$ by neutral hydrogen atoms, followed by radiative de-excitation, efficiently carries thermal energy away from the gas. At elevated densities or in warmer shock-heated interfaces, the [O I] $63 \ \mu\text{m}$ line and Ly$\alpha$ emission can also contribute to cooling the bulk WNM.

## Bistable Pressure Equilibrium with the CNM

The exact shape of the heating and cooling curves dictates that the WNM and the [[cold-neutral-medium|Cold neutral medium]] (CNM, $T \approx 100 \text{ K}$) can coexist in stable pressure equilibrium over a narrow range of thermal pressures, bounded by a minimum ($P_{\rm min}$) and maximum ($P_{\rm max}$) threshold. Theoretical models demonstrate that the WNM and CNM can coexist in pressure equilibrium when the thermal pressure $P_{\rm th}/k_B$ lies roughly between $10^3$ and $10^4\ \mathrm{K\ cm^{-3}}$ [Wolfire et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...587..278W/abstract). Near the solar circle, the typical thermal pressure is estimated to be $P_{\rm th}/k_B \approx 3000\ \mathrm{K\ cm^{-3}}$, comfortably within the [[two-phase-model|two-phase regime]] [Wolfire et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...587..278W/abstract). 

Analytically, the existence of a stable minimum pressure $P_{\rm min}$ relies critically on the ionization state of the gas, specifically requiring that the abundance of $\mathrm{H}^+$ exceeds that of $\mathrm{C}^+$ at the pressure minimum [Wolfire et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...587..278W/abstract). If the ambient pressure drops below $P_{\rm th}/k_B \approx 1000\ \mathrm{K\ cm^{-3}}$, the two-phase equilibrium breaks down; the CNM phase disappears entirely, leaving the WNM as the sole neutral atomic phase [Smith et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524..873S/abstract).

The transition from the WNM to the CNM is governed by thermal instability. Because the WNM is highly compressible, large-scale compressional events—such as those induced by spiral density waves or the passage of supernova shock fronts—can easily push the WNM out of its thermal equilibrium. In these post-shock layers, the gas enters the thermally unstable regime and rapidly cools, condensing into dense CNM cloudlets embedded within the remnant WNM [Inutsuka et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AIPC..784..318I/abstract). 

Despite the theoretical success of static equilibrium models, the literature highlights ongoing tensions when these models are applied to dynamic, turbulent environments. The interstellar medium is subject to continuous turbulent mixing that replenishes gas in the thermally unstable temperature regimes between the WNM and CNM, meaning that these phases are often transient and dynamically cycling rather than strictly static [Vazquez-Semadeni et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1202.4498V/abstract). Furthermore, accurately capturing the transition between the WNM and CNM requires properly resolving the "Field length"—the critical spatial scale for thermal conduction—which remains a strict resolution limit in hydrodynamic simulations of the ISM [Inutsuka et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002Ap&SS.281...67I/abstract).

Consequently, there is active tension regarding the accuracy of pure thermal equilibrium models for determining ISM pressures. Recent hybrid non-equilibrium chemical simulations suggest that traditional thermal equilibrium predictions may actually underestimate the median gas pressures of the neutral phases by up to $0.5 \text{ dex}$ [Ploeckinger et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543..891P/abstract). Additionally, non-thermal pressure sources play a critical regulatory role during phase transitions. Magnetic fields have been shown to be energetically dominant both in the thermally unstable transition regime and within the CNM itself. By providing non-thermal pressure support, these magnetic fields slow the condensation of the WNM, thereby regulating the ultimate formation of [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] and subsequent star-forming cores [McGuiness et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251204184M/abstract).

## Observational Methodologies

The characterization of the warm neutral medium (WNM) relies heavily on a combination of radio astronomy, ultraviolet (UV) spectroscopy, and transient phenomena. Because the WNM exists in complex, multi-phase environments alongside the [[cold-neutral-medium|Cold neutral medium]] (CNM) and the [[warm-ionized-medium|Warm Ionized Medium]], isolating its specific physical properties requires techniques that can resolve gas across different temperatures, densities, and kinematics.

**Radio and Microwave Diagnostics**
The most direct probe of the [[neutral-interstellar-medium|Neutral Interstellar Medium]] is the 21-cm hyperfine transition of atomic hydrogen (H I). The WNM is readily detected in 21-cm emission due to its large volume filling factor and substantial column density across galactic disks [Dickey et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988MNRAS.233..781D/abstract). However, determining the thermodynamic state of the gas requires paired 21-cm emission and 21-cm absorption studies against bright background radio continuum sources. 

The optical depth of the 21-cm line is inversely proportional to the spin temperature, $\tau \propto N_{\rm HI} / (T_s \Delta v)$. Because the WNM has a high kinetic temperature and correspondingly high spin temperature, it produces very weak absorption features compared to the cooler, denser CNM. Consequently, separating the WNM from the CNM along a given line of sight relies on identifying broad 21-cm emission components that lack corresponding deep absorption features [Dutta et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JApA...40...41D/abstract). High-resolution 21-cm surveys utilizing this methodology have successfully mapped the multi-phase ISM in the Milky Way, nearby spirals like M31 [Dickey et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988MNRAS.233..781D/abstract), and low-metallicity dwarf galaxies, where high-resolution surveys have recently succeeded in isolating the embedded CNM from the surrounding WNM [Pingel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240713829P/abstract), [Stelea et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110602S/abstract). For example, observations of the low-mass dwarf Leo T reveal a surprisingly large CNM component comprising roughly 10% of its total H I mass, which may be a transient effect driven by interactions with the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|circumgalactic medium]] [Adams et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...612A..26A/abstract).

Additionally, Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) provide a novel tool for probing the WNM along cosmological sightlines. The average dispersion measure contributed by the neutral medium of a single intervening galaxy is found to be relatively low, roughly $\sim 0.25 \text{ pc cm}^{-3}$ [Prochaska et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474..318P/abstract). On even larger cosmological scales, the Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect serves as a complementary probe for mapping the warm and hot gas that pervades large-scale structure [Mroczkowski et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019SSRv..215...17M/abstract).

**UV, Infrared, and X-ray Spectroscopy**
At ultraviolet wavelengths, the WNM is studied through absorption features imprinted on the spectra of background continuum sources. Quasar absorption lines are particularly valuable for probing diffuse neutral gas in galactic halos, the circumgalactic medium (CGM), and high-redshift environments. The primary diagnostic is the Lyman alpha (Ly$\alpha$) transition at $1216 \text{ \AA}$. High column density Ly$\alpha$ absorbers, known as damped Ly$\alpha$ systems (DLAs), dominate the cosmological mass density of neutral gas at high redshift [Storrie-Lombardi et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.283L..79S/abstract). Analyses of fine-structure transitions in DLAs, such as upper limits on [C II] $158 \ \mu\text{m}$ absorption, indicate that the majority of these systems consist of gas in a WNM phase, heated by far-ultraviolet radiation from local star formation [Wolfe et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...615..625W/abstract).

In the low-redshift universe, UV spectroscopy using instruments like the *Hubble Space Telescope* Cosmic Origins Spectrograph (HST/COS) has been instrumental in mapping the extended WNM. Surveys such as COS-GASS and DIISC have demonstrated strong correlations between the Ly$\alpha$ equivalent width in the CGM and the neutral properties of the host galaxy. Specifically, COS-GASS found a strong correlation with the H I gas mass fraction ($M_{\rm HI}/M_\star$) [Borthakur et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...813...46B/abstract), while DIISC revealed a tight inverse correlation with the impact parameter normalized by the H I disk radius ($\rho/R_{\rm HI}$) [Borthakur et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240912554B/abstract). This indicates a physical connection between the extended neutral halo and the star-forming disk.

Furthermore, combining H I column densities with UV absorption lines from heavy elements (e.g., O I, Si II, C II) enables precise metallicity measurements of the WNM [Richter et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...607A..48R/abstract). Recent observations utilizing this technique have revealed that the neutral ISM in the solar neighborhood is not chemically homogeneous; it exhibits large metallicity variations (down to $\sim 17\%$ of solar abundances). This suggests that metal-poor gas accreting from high-velocity clouds does not efficiently mix with the ambient WNM, maintaining chemical inhomogeneities on scales of tens of parsecs [De Cia et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Natur.597..206D/abstract). High-resolution X-ray spectroscopy provides a complementary diagnostic; observations of X-ray binaries have revealed over-abundances of metals like oxygen, neon, and iron in the neutral ISM, consistent with the broader Galactic metallicity gradient [Pinto et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...521A..79P/abstract). Beyond the X-ray and UV regimes, multi-wavelength facilities like JWST continue to revolutionize our view of the multiphase ISM. In extreme low-metallicity environments, such as the dwarf galaxy I Zw 18, recent JWST/MIRI observations have revealed [[warm-molecular-gas|warm molecular hydrogen gas]] exhibiting anomalous ortho-to-para ratios ($>3$) co-existing with highly ionized gas, providing new insights into the multiphase structure of metal-poor systems [Hunt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250902690H/abstract).

## Galactic Distribution and Extraplanar Gas

The Warm Neutral Medium (WNM) is the volume-dominant neutral component of the diffuse [[cosmic-media-and-interstellar-medium|interstellar medium (ISM)]] within galactic disks [Dickey et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988MNRAS.233..781D/abstract), [Haffner et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009RvMP...81..969H/abstract). In contrast to the highly clumped [[cold-neutral-medium|Cold neutral medium]] (CNM), the WNM possesses a much larger volume filling factor and pervades the majority of the diffuse ISM [Oka et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...883...54O/abstract). Theoretical models and observations of the Milky Way indicate that the WNM can persist in two-phase thermal equilibrium with the CNM out to large galactocentric radii, reaching $R \approx 16-18 \text{ kpc}$ [Wolfire et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...587..278W/abstract). 

Vertically, the neutral medium exhibits a highly stratified structure. Locally, 3D maps of Na I and H I absorption have defined the complex cavity of the Local Bubble and its interconnected tunnels extending into the outer Galaxy [Vergely et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001A&A...366.1016V/abstract). Within this local volume, the observed 1/4-keV diffuse X-ray flux is likely a combination of thermal emission from the hot gas within the Local Bubble and charge exchange between the solar wind and the local neutral gas [Galeazzi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.512..171G/abstract). Similarly, charge exchange emission at the interface of neutral gas and jet-driven outflows accounts for a significant portion of diffuse X-ray emission in external galaxies like M51 [Yang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...894...22Y/abstract). Beyond the local bubble, recent high-resolution 21-cm absorption surveys constrain the vertical scale height of the CNM in the solar neighborhood to $\sigma_z \sim 50-90 \text{ pc}$. This is approximately a factor of two smaller than classical textbook estimates and tracks closely with molecular gas distributions [Rybarczyk et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..167R/abstract). By contrast, the WNM and its ionized counterpart, the [[warm-ionized-medium|Warm Ionized Medium]] (WIM), are significantly more extended, reaching kiloparsec scales above the galactic midplane and forming a complex transition zone with the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]]. High-resolution X-ray absorption studies have also revealed an extensive "warm ionized metal medium" (WIMM) permeating the galactic halo with a remarkably low temperature of $T \approx 2900 \pm 900 \text{ K}$ and a mean density of $\langle n_H \rangle \approx 0.023 \text{ cm}^{-3}$ [Nicastro et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457..676N/abstract).

The maintenance and thermal support of this extended, extraplanar warm gas remains an active area of debate. While photoionization from massive O-stars is the primary driver for the WIM, these ionizing photons must traverse low-density channels to reach kiloparsec altitudes [Mathis et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...544..347M/abstract). Furthermore, observed line ratios (e.g., [N II]/H$\alpha$) at high galactic latitudes require an "extra heating" source that contributes $\sim 15\%$ of the total energetic power [Mathis et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...544..347M/abstract). The literature is currently divided on the physical origin of this supplemental heating. Proposed mechanisms include the dissipation of supersonic turbulence or shocks, heating by low-energy cosmic rays (which efficiently heat plasma but not neutrals) [Walker et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...818...23W/abstract), and localized magnetic reconnection in halo current filaments [Birk et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.296..165B/abstract).

Beyond the stratified disk, warm neutral and ionized gases are prevalent in the halo as part of a global galactic gas cycle driven by galactic fountains and cosmological accretion [Braun et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005mpge.conf...23B/abstract). Measurements of the deuterium-to-hydrogen (D/H) abundance ratio in the warm neutral medium of the lower Galactic halo suggest that this extraplanar gas originates in the disk and is elevated by fountain processes that manage to preserve interstellar dust grains [Savage et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...659.1222S/abstract). Intense galactic feedback can even drive massive multi-phase outflows into the surrounding environment. For instance, observations of the massive compact galaxy SDSS J211824 reveal a tremendous wind feeding the circumgalactic medium, where neutral gas is detected out to distances of $20 \text{ kpc}$ traveling at extreme velocities of $1500 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Rupke et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.574..643R/abstract). High-velocity clouds (HVCs) represent another major reservoir of extraplanar gas. While HVCs are traditionally identified via 21-cm emission from their neutral hydrogen cores, recent cosmological simulations and ultraviolet absorption surveys demonstrate that these neutral structures are encased in massive ionized envelopes. The ionized mass in HVCs is typically $\sim 6$ times greater than the neutral mass, suggesting that ionized accretion ($\dot{M}_{\rm ion}$) plays a far more dominant role in sustaining galactic star formation than neutral accretion ($\dot{M}_{\rm neut}$) alone [Lucchini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250718687L/abstract). As these low-metallicity HVCs descend into the disk, they do not efficiently homogenize with the resident gas. This inefficient mixing sustains large, localized metallicity variations—often spanning an order of magnitude—within the otherwise well-mixed local ISM [De Cia et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Natur.597..206D/abstract).

Beyond isolated galaxies, the neutral medium is also subject to extreme environmental effects in galaxy clusters. In the Virgo cluster, simulations and observations have highlighted the existence of optically dark H I clouds that may reside in pressure equilibrium with the hot intracluster medium (ICM), although these structures are potentially short-lived on timescales of $\sim 100 \text{ Myr}$ [Taylor et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479..377T/abstract).

## Turbulence and Magnetic Field Interactions

The classical [[two-phase-model|Two-phase model]] of the interstellar medium posits a static pressure equilibrium between the Warm Neutral Medium (WNM) and the [[cold-neutral-medium|Cold neutral medium]] (CNM). However, modern paradigms view the neutral medium as a highly compressible, dynamic environment governed by the continuous injection of turbulent energy and magnetic field interactions [Vazquez-Semadeni et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1202.4498V/abstract).

**Turbulent Kinematics and Heating**
Turbulence acts as both a mixing agent and a driver of phase transitions in the interstellar medium. Large-scale compressions in the WNM—often triggered by spiral arm passages or supernova shocks—push gas into the thermally unstable regime, prompting localized cooling and condensation into the CNM [Vazquez-Semadeni et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1202.4498V/abstract). The characteristic Mach number of this turbulence strongly depends on the phase being considered. Because of its relatively high kinetic temperature, the WNM possesses a high sound speed. Consequently, turbulent flows are typically subsonic or transonic with respect to the WNM itself, even though these same bulk velocity dispersions are highly supersonic relative to the embedded cold clumps [Vazquez-Semadeni et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1202.4498V/abstract). For comparison, turbulence in the co-spatial [[warm-ionized-medium|Warm Ionized Medium]] is found to be mildly supersonic, with Mach numbers generally in the range of $1.4 \dots 2.4$ [Hill et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...686..363H/abstract).

While the dissipation of this turbulent kinetic energy contributes to the thermal budget of the gas, turbulent heating is generally secondary to the dominant photoelectric heating from dust grains and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. Theoretical models assessing the dimensionless parameter $\Upsilon \equiv t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm shock}$ (the ratio of the gas cooling time to the characteristic time between turbulent shocks) find that $\Upsilon < 1$ throughout the Galactic disk. This indicates that the WNM has sufficient time to radiate away excess energy and re-establish thermal balance between shock events, thereby preserving the viability of two-phase coexistence [Wolfire et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...587..278W/abstract).

**Magnetic Fields and MHD Density Waves**
Magnetic fields provide crucial non-thermal pressure support that regulates the structural evolution of the neutral medium. High-resolution magnetohydrodynamic simulations demonstrate that magnetic fields become energetically dominant in the thermally unstable transition regime (governing roughly 45% of the local mass budget) and even more so within the cold phase, thereby slowing the gravitational collapse of condensed WNM gas into star-forming cores [McGuiness et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251204184M/abstract). On sub-kiloparsec scales within normal disk galaxies, observations indicate that equipartition magnetic field strengths are strongly correlated with the local surface star formation rates, reinforcing the deep physical link between magnetic fields, the multiphase ISM, and ongoing star formation [Basu et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1402.2229B/abstract).

On galactic scales, magnetic fields couple the neutral gas to the broader dynamical potentials of the disk. The propagation of fast MHD density waves through the rotating composite disk (comprising stars and magnetized gas) can manifest as broad, trailing spiral structures in the neutral atomic hydrogen. These MHD density waves play a key role in organizing correlated spatial structures across the various interstellar medium components [Lou et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...567..289L/abstract).

Measurements of magnetic field strength in the neutral medium yield diverse results depending on the environment and epoch. In the local interstellar medium, the field strength is constrained to approximately $3.7 \dots 5.5\ \mu\mathrm{G}$ [Opher et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462.1036O/abstract). However, the cosmological evolution of these fields remains a subject of active debate. For instance, Zeeman splitting observations have detected an unexpectedly strong magnetic field of $84\ \mu\mathrm{G}$ in a damped Ly$\alpha$ absorbing galaxy at $z = 0.692$. This result strongly contradicts standard mean-field dynamo theories, which predict that galactic magnetic fields should be significantly weaker in the early universe [Wolfe et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455..638W/abstract). Such tensions highlight ongoing ambiguities in our understanding of magnetic field amplification and its historical interplay with the WNM.

## The WNM in the Cosmological Context

During the Cosmic Dawn and the subsequent Epoch of Reionization, the state of the neutral gas in the universe underwent a profound transformation. Following recombination, the [[warm-hot-intergalactic-medium|intergalactic medium (IGM)]] was pervasive, cold, and entirely neutral [Ferrara et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1409.4946F/abstract). As the first stars and accreting black holes formed, their ultraviolet and X-ray radiation initiated a phase transition, gradually heating and ionizing the surrounding primordial gas [Basu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251022043B/abstract). 

The reionization of the IGM was spatially inhomogeneous, characterized by the expansion and percolation of ionized H II bubbles within the neutral medium [Fan et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ARA&A..44..415F/abstract). Observations have spectroscopically confirmed the presence of overlapping ionized bubbles as early as $z=7.7$ [Tilvi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...891L..10T/abstract). During this epoch, the remaining neutral IGM was subject to early heating, likely driven by X-ray emission from the first collapsed objects. Measurements of the 21-cm power spectrum from the PAPER experiment place lower limits on the kinetic temperature of the neutral IGM at $z=8.4$, indicating temperatures of $> 5 \dots 10\ {\rm K}$ depending on the neutral fraction [Pober et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...809...62P/abstract). By $z \sim 6$, the reionization process was largely complete. Observations of early star-forming galaxies from this epoch frequently show a dearth of diffuse neutral gas; for example, the detection of an oxygen emission line from a high-redshift galaxy at $z=7.2$ indicates a surprisingly low dust-to-gas ratio and a potential lack of extended neutral material [Inoue et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Sci...352.1559I/abstract). 

As these primordial galaxies mature and star formation accelerates, the gas efficiently cools and transitions into denser phases. By $z \sim 2.2$, observations of primeval galaxies, such as IRAS 10214+4724, reveal the presence of [[warm-molecular-gas|warm, dense molecular gas]] directly associated with regions of high-mass star formation, marking the progression from diffuse atomic halos to star-forming disks [Solomon et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...398L..29S/abstract). The accretion of fresh gas to fuel this star formation is a critical aspect of galaxy evolution. Direct evidence for cold accretion streams has been found through observations of metal-poor ($[\text{Z/H}] \approx -2$) Lyman Limit System (LLS) absorbers with temperatures $< 20,000 \text{ K}$, located roughly $58 \text{ kpc}$ from a star-forming galaxy at $z = 2.4$ [Crighton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...776L..18C/abstract). 

Following the Epoch of Reionization, the global neutral fraction dropped dramatically to $x_{\rm HI} < 10^{-4}$ [Fan et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ARA&A..44..415F/abstract). The bulk of the universe's neutral atomic hydrogen became confined to the dense environments of galaxies and their immediate halos, predominantly observed as Damped Ly$\alpha$ systems (DLAs) [Storrie-Lombardi et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.283L..79S/abstract). These high-redshift absorbers provide a critical window into the [[neutral-interstellar-medium|Neutral Interstellar Medium]] of early galaxies. Studies of the fine-structure cooling line [C II] $158\ \mu{\rm m}$ in DLAs reveal that a local heating source is required to maintain thermal balance; the extragalactic background alone is insufficient. Interestingly, many DLAs exhibit only upper limits on [C II] absorption, implying that the gas in these systems exists predominantly as a warm neutral medium rather than a [[cold-neutral-medium|Cold neutral medium]]. The far-ultraviolet radiation fields required to explain these limits suggest that the majority of DLAs are heated by local star formation with surface densities between $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-2}\ M_\odot\ {\rm yr}^{-1}\ {\rm kpc}^{-2}$ [Wolfe et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...615..625W/abstract).

At the highest redshifts, the primordial warm neutral gas sets the initial conditions for galaxy assembly. Recent observations of a highly luminous galaxy at $z=14.179$ reveal a massive, pristine neutral hydrogen reservoir with a gas mass fraction $f_{\rm gas} \gtrsim 0.9$, indicating that the bulk of the earliest interstellar medium was composed of neutral atomic hydrogen [Heintz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206016H/abstract). As cosmic time progressed, the global cosmological mass density of neutral gas, $\Omega_g$, flattened and possibly turned over near $z \sim 3$ [Storrie-Lombardi et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.283L..79S/abstract). 

The survival of the high-redshift warm neutral medium is strongly dependent on the local radiation environment. In the host galaxies of the brightest quasars, extreme radiation fields can completely ionize the extended neutral gas. Observations show a complete dearth of 21-cm absorption when the central rest-frame UV luminosity exceeds a critical threshold of $L_{1216} \approx 10^{23}\ {\rm W\ Hz}^{-1}$ [Curran et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759..117C/abstract). Above this threshold, the large-scale atomic gas is fully ionized, effectively destroying the neutral medium and halting gas-fueled star formation in the host galaxy's extended disk.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite decades of multi-wavelength observations and theoretical modeling, several fundamental properties of the Warm Neutral Medium (WNM) remain poorly understood. A major unresolved question regarding the small-scale structure of the neutral ISM is the nature of tiny scale atomic structures (TSAS). Observed on scales of a few to $\sim 10^4 \text{ AU}$, these highly overpressured regions challenge standard equilibrium models. It remains an active debate whether TSAS represent discrete, localized phenomena driven by specific energy injections or if they are simply manifestations of a universal, magnetized turbulent cascade acting in concert with thermal instability [Stanimirović et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ARA&A..56..489S/abstract). 

Simulating the thermodynamics of the WNM and its transition to denser phases also presents severe computational challenges. The characteristic "Field length"—the scale below which thermal conduction suppresses thermal instability—remains a stringent resolution limit for hydrodynamic models seeking to accurately capture the phase transition [Inutsuka et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002Ap&SS.281...67I/abstract). Furthermore, standard thermal equilibrium models may underestimate median gas pressures in simulations of isolated galaxies by up to $0.5 \text{ dex}$, indicating that time-dependent, non-equilibrium chemistry and radiative cooling are critical for capturing the true state of the gas [Ploeckinger et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543..891P/abstract).

The transition from the WNM to the [[cold-neutral-medium|Cold neutral medium]] and eventually to molecular, star-forming gas is regulated by a complex interplay of turbulence, self-gravity, and magnetic fields. High-resolution magnetohydrodynamic simulations suggest that magnetic fields become energetically dominant in the thermally unstable regime and within the cold neutral phase, potentially slowing the collapse of [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] and creating a bottleneck for star formation [McGuiness et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251204184M/abstract). The precise extent to which these magnetic fields determine the transition rate into molecular clouds remains an open question. 

On larger galactic scales, the interaction between the WNM and the surrounding environment presents further ambiguities. In the galactic halo and extraplanar regions, the source of an "extra heating" component—accounting for $\sim 15\%$ of the total power required to explain observed line ratios in the high-latitude WNM and [[warm-ionized-medium|Warm Ionized Medium]]—is not fully identified, with candidates including turbulent dissipation, magnetic reconnection, and low-energy cosmic rays [Mathis et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...544..347M/abstract). Additionally, it is unclear whether pristine, low-metallicity gas accreting from high-velocity clouds efficiently mixes with the existing ISM or if it persists as long-lived chemical inhomogeneities [De Cia et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Natur.597..206D/abstract).

In a cosmological context, the distribution and kinematics of neutral gas provide unique constraints on dark matter and galaxy formation. For instance, warm dark matter models that truncate the power spectrum below a specific velocity dispersion cut-off have been proposed to simultaneously address the "Missing satellites" problem and the rest-frame UV luminosity cut-off observed during cosmic reionization [Hoeneisen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IJAA...13...25H/abstract). 

Future observational breakthroughs will rely heavily on next-generation radio interferometers. The Square Kilometer Array (SKA) will be instrumental in mapping the neutral intergalactic medium via 21-cm emission and tracing the evolution of H I absorption systems through the epoch of reionization and into cosmic noon ($1 < z < 3$) [Carilli et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004NewAR..48.1029C/abstract), [Wagh et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023sf2a.conf...71W/abstract). Concurrently, the Next Generation Very Large Array (ngVLA) will provide the unprecedented sensitivity and angular resolution necessary to resolve individual H I clouds beyond the Local Group. Within the Milky Way, the ngVLA is expected to yield a dense grid of H I absorption spectra, precisely constraining the temperature and density of the atomic gas as it transitions from the diffuse WNM into molecular star-forming cores [Pisano et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181006575P/abstract).
