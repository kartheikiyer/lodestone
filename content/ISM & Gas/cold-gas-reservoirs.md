In astrophysics, **cold gas reservoirs** represent the primary supply of [[atomic-gas-hi-studies|neutral atomic (H I)]] and [[dense-molecular-gas|molecular ($\text{H}_2$) hydrogen]] within galaxies and their surrounding [[extra-planar-and-circumgalactic-gas|Extra-planar and Circumgalactic Gas]]. These reservoirs dictate the star formation history of the Universe, serving as the essential fuel that governs a galaxy's growth across cosmic time. In the local Universe, the bulk of this material resides in extended galactic disks, where atomic gas generally dominates the total [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]], while the denser, colder molecular phase condenses into [[cold-molecular-clouds|Cold molecular clouds]] to actively form stars. Because the symmetric $\text{H}_2$ molecule lacks a permanent electric dipole moment, the molecular phase is traditionally quantified using [[dense-gas-tracers|Dense gas tracers]] such as carbon monoxide (CO) rotational transitions. In contrast, atomic hydrogen and its accretion flows are mapped directly via the 21-cm hyperfine emission line [Sancisi et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&ARv..15..189S/abstract). 

The evolution of these reservoirs is governed by the baryon cycle, a continuous process of [[cold-gas-accretion|Cold gas accretion]] from the cosmic web, internal star formation, and feedback-driven expulsion. Observational surveys reveal that the cosmic density of molecular gas peaked during "Cosmic Noon" ($z \sim 1.5 - 2.0$), driving star formation rates significantly higher than those seen today, before steadily declining by a factor of six to the present epoch [Tacconi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.463..781T/abstract), [Walter et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..111W/abstract). In contrast, the global mass density of atomic gas has remained remarkably stable over the last 10 billion years, a fact tightly constrained by absorption studies of damped Ly$\alpha$ systems [Wolfe et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ARA&A..43..861W/abstract). This dichotomy indicates a steady, integrated flow of material where [[hot-gas|ionized gas]] cools from the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]] to replenish H I disks, which subsequently funnel inward to sustain the central molecular reservoirs.

A galaxy's environment and internal feedback mechanisms play critical roles in regulating its [[gas-depletion-and-consumption|Gas Depletion and Consumption]]. External hydrodynamic forces begin to efficiently remove extended atomic gas in group-scale halos, creating populations of [[h-i-deficient-galaxies|H I-deficient galaxies]] well before they even reach dense galaxy clusters [Brown et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.1275B/abstract). Internally, energetic feedback from active galactic nuclei (AGN) and supernovae can heat or inject turbulence into the interstellar medium. Surprisingly, modern multi-wavelength studies show that classical early-type or post-starburst galaxies frequently retain substantial cold gas that cycles through [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]], suggesting that star formation quenching often proceeds via dynamical heating and turbulent suppression rather than the complete expulsion of the cold fuel supply.

Despite extensive surveys, significant open questions remain regarding the precise calibration of gas masses and the nature of high-redshift accretion. A major challenge arises in [[low-metallicity-environments|Low-Metallicity Environments]], where CO is easily photodissociated by ultraviolet radiation, leaving vast reservoirs of "CO-dark" molecular gas that must instead be probed with alternative fine-structure lines, such as [C II] [Geach et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757..156G/abstract). Furthermore, detecting faint 21-cm emission at high redshifts remains a fundamental observational bottleneck. Resolving these tensions and pushing the boundaries of [[atomic-gas-hi-studies|Atomic Gas (HI) Studies]] relies heavily on next-generation radio observatories, such as the Square Kilometre Array (SKA), which aim to directly trace the pristine neutral gas fueling the earliest [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]].

## Fundamental Components and Observational Tracers

The fundamental constituents of [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] reservoirs are [[atomic-gas-hi-studies|neutral atomic hydrogen (H I)]] and molecular hydrogen (H$_2$). In local disk galaxies like the Milky Way, H I comprises approximately 80% of the cold [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]], distributed in an extended disk with a typical scale height of $\sim 150$ pc and a velocity dispersion of $\sim 9$ km s$^{-1}$. The primary observational tracer for this phase is the HI 21-cm emission line. Because this hyperfine transition is intrinsically faint, direct [[atomic-gas-hi-studies|Atomic Gas (HI) Studies]] are challenging at high redshift, prompting the use of empirical calibrations based on [O I] or [C II] fine-structure lines to infer H I mass in distant [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] [Wilson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...685A..30W/abstract), [Heintz et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922..147H/abstract).

The remaining $\sim 20\%$ of the local cold gas mass is molecular, typically localized in central regions and [[cold-molecular-clouds|Cold molecular clouds]] characterized by a smaller scale height ($\sim 50$ pc) and a lower velocity dispersion ($\sim 7$ km s$^{-1}$). Because the symmetric H$_2$ molecule does not emit efficiently at the typical temperatures of the cold interstellar medium ($T \sim 10-30$ K), it is quantified using proxy [[dense-gas-tracers|Dense gas tracers]], predominantly CO rotational transitions (e.g., $J=1-0$ at 115 GHz). The total molecular mass is derived via the mass-to-luminosity conversion factor, expressed as $M_{H_2} = \alpha_{CO} L'_{CO}$. Furthermore, the ALMOND survey demonstrates that dense gas line ratios, such as HCN/CO, systematically increase with both [[molecular-gas-surface-density|Molecular gas surface density]] and dynamical pressure across local galaxies [Neumann et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693L..13N/abstract).

However, the literature demonstrates that $\alpha_{CO}$ is not a universal constant. It varies strongly with [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]] and cloud clumpiness [Ramambason et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...681A..14R/abstract), [Boselli et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...564A..66B/abstract). In [[low-metallicity-environments|Low-Metallicity Environments]], ambient ultraviolet radiation selectively photodissociates CO while H$_2$ remains protected by self-shielding, rendering CO rotational transitions highly inaccurate for total mass estimates and necessitating the use of alternative tracers such as the [C II] 158$\mu$m fine-structure line [Cormier et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...564A.121C/abstract), [Madden et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.141M/abstract). 

Finally, the [[g-s-ratio|dust-to-gas ratio]] provides a complementary method to estimate cold gas masses. Because interstellar dust is generally well-mixed with cold gas, far-infrared and submillimeter continuum observations of thermal dust emission can trace the total gas column. While this technique requires careful calibration—which typically scales with metallicity—it serves as a valuable independent diagnostic of the gas reservoir when H I or CO measurements are unavailable or heavily biased [Yıldız et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...636A...8Y/abstract), [Baes et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444L..90B/abstract).

## Scaling Relations and the Baryon Cycle

Galaxies grow and evolve through a continuous process of gas accretion, consumption, and expulsion known as the [[galaxy-gas-replenishment|Baryon cycle]]. The efficiency of this cycle is quantified by two fundamental parameters: the [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]], defined as the ratio of cold gas to total baryonic mass $f_{\rm gas} = M_{\rm cold} / (M_{\rm cold} + M_{\star})$, and the depletion timescale, defined as $\tau_{\rm SF} \equiv M_{\rm gas} / \dot{M}_{\star}$, which measures how quickly a galaxy converts its [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] reservoir into stars. 

From a gas perspective, a galaxy's position on the star-forming main sequence is governed by three primary factors: the total mass of its cold interstellar medium, the fraction of that gas which is molecular, and the rate at which that molecular gas is converted into stars [Saintonge et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ARA&A..60..319S/abstract). At "cosmic noon" ($z \sim 1.5 - 2.5$), [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] were significantly more gas-rich ($f_{\rm gas} \sim 40-50\%$) than local analogues, driving star formation rates an order of magnitude higher despite maintaining a similar [[average-star-formation-efficiency|star formation efficiency]] [Tacconi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.463..781T/abstract). While most CO-selected galaxies follow established scaling relations connecting $f_{\rm gas}$, $\tau_{\rm SF}$, and redshift, roughly 30% are offset from the main sequence due to variations in their gas reservoirs or depletion times [Aravena et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...882..136A/abstract).

The cosmic molecular gas density ($\rho_{\rm H_2}$) closely tracks the cosmic star formation history, peaking at $z \sim 1.5-2.0$ before declining by a factor of roughly six to the present day [Walter et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..111W/abstract), [Bollo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206778B/abstract). In contrast, the global [[atomic-gas-hi-studies|atomic gas density ($\rho_{\rm HI}$)]] has remained relatively constant over the same epoch [Lagos et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.1649L/abstract), [Walter et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..111W/abstract). This constancy necessitates a continuous replenishment paradigm—often referred to as the "bathtub model" of the Baryon cycle—which successfully predicts the observed ratio of molecular gas to stellar mass density across cosmic time [Bollo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206778B/abstract). This is observationally supported by a net infall of ionized gas from the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]] that refuels extended H I reservoirs, followed by a net [[galactic-gas-inflow-and-infall|inflow of H I]] toward the galaxy center where it condenses into $H_2$ [Walter et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..111W/abstract). Furthermore, simulations suggest that late-time star formation can rely on gas accreted into reservoirs 2-8 Gyr prior, allowing the star formation rate to temporarily surpass the current overall gas accretion rate [Woods et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442..732W/abstract).

Locally, the empirical Kennicutt-Schmidt relation links the [[cold-gas-surface-density|Cold gas surface density]] to the star formation rate surface density. This connection extends to the multiphase medium; for example, in M51, the efficiency of heating the [[hot-gas|hot X-ray gas]] correlates with the star formation rate surface density, highlighting core-collapse supernovae as the primary energy source linking the cold and hot phases of the interstellar medium [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...978L..15Z/abstract). However, scaling relations indicate that the coupling between cold gas and star formation is highly mass-dependent. In dwarf galaxies ($M_{\star} \lesssim 3 \times 10^9 M_{\odot}$), star formation struggles to keep pace with H I accretion. Intermediate-mass systems ($3 \times 10^9 \lesssim M_{\star} \lesssim 3 \times 10^{10} M_{\odot}$) exist in a gas-equilibrium state where both H I and $H_2$ are proportional to the star formation rate. In massive galaxies ($M_{\star} \gtrsim 3 \times 10^{10} M_{\odot}$), the system becomes gas-poor, and the H I reservoir largely ceases to participate directly in star formation [Hunt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.180H/abstract). In central spiral galaxies, the H I gas fraction remains nearly constant despite significant drops in the specific star formation rate, suggesting that quenching in these systems is primarily driven by the failure to convert H I into H$_2$ rather than an absolute lack of atomic gas [Dou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240903168D/abstract). Additionally, observations of lensed starburst galaxies at $z \sim 2.5$ reveal highly turbulent reservoirs of cool gas extending over 10 kpc, sustained by galactic winds that may extend the starburst phase rather than immediately quenching it [Falgarone et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017Natur.548..430F/abstract).

Deviations from the Kennicutt-Schmidt relation frequently highlight extreme ISM conditions rather than an underlying breakdown of the star formation process. For example, in low surface brightness galaxies, the atomic gas forms stars with extreme inefficiency, exhibiting median depletion times of $\tau_{\rm SF} \approx 18$ Gyr [Christie et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250920080C/abstract). Similarly, rare populations of [[h-i-rich-galaxies|H I-excess galaxies]] exhibit massive atomic reservoirs with surprisingly inefficient star formation, sometimes featuring counter-rotating disks or Malin 1-type extended structures [Geréb et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476..896G/abstract). Conversely, in low-metallicity dwarf galaxies, [[active-star-forming-regions|Active star-forming regions]] may initially appear to violate the relation due to an absence of CO emission; however, when the "CO-dark" molecular gas mass is properly inferred using [C II] emission, these galaxies fall back onto the standard Kennicutt-Schmidt relation [Madden et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.141M/abstract).

## Mechanisms of Gas Accretion and Inflow

The continuous [[galaxy-gas-replenishment|gas replenishment]] of [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] is a fundamental requirement of galaxy evolution. In local [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], the gas consumption timescale is typically $\sim 1-5 \times 10^9$ yr. Because this is significantly shorter than the Hubble time, galaxies must steadily acquire new gas to maintain their observed star formation histories. This replenishment occurs through three primary mechanisms: [[cold-gas-accretion|Cold gas accretion]] from the cosmic web, cooling from the circumgalactic medium, and gas-rich minor mergers.

Cosmological hydrodynamic simulations predict a bimodal nature for [[galactic-gas-inflow-and-infall|Galactic Gas Inflow and Infall]]. In massive halos or at low redshift, infalling gas [[super-virial-temperature-gas|shock-heats to the virial temperature]] before slowly cooling ("hot mode" accretion). Conversely, "cold mode" accretion dominates in low-mass halos and at high redshift ($z \gtrsim 2$). In this regime, unshocked gas streams along cosmic filaments directly into the central galaxy, maintaining temperatures of $T \sim 10^4$ K and fueling rapid stellar assembly [Dave et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..219D/abstract), [van de Voort et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421.2809V/abstract). Direct observational evidence for cold accretion flows has emerged at high redshift. For example, absorption studies of a $z=2.44$ star-forming galaxy revealed a cool ($T < 20,000$ K), highly metal-poor gas stream ($\log_{10}(Z/Z_{\odot}) = -2.0 \pm 0.17$) at an impact parameter of 58 kpc [Crighton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...776L..18C/abstract). Similarly, accretion of cold, chemically pristine gas has been detected via ultra-strong Mg II absorption toward galaxy overdensities at $z \sim 1.6$, providing evidence for the inflow of metal-poor material [Giavalisco et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...743...95G/abstract). Furthermore, the discovery of an enormous ($R > 100$ kpc) rotating structure of hydrogen gas at $z=2.843$ provides evidence of a massive "protodisk" actively forming from cold cosmic web gas [Martin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...824L...5M/abstract).

Galaxies also extract cold gas from their [[extra-planar-and-circumgalactic-gas|Extra-planar and Circumgalactic Gas]]. Observations from the COS-Halos survey have shown that [[oxygen-in-astrophysics|massive, ionized oxygen-rich halos]] surround star-forming galaxies, representing a major reservoir of galactic metals and potential fuel [Tumlinson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...334..948T/abstract). However, simulations such as Auriga reveal extreme diversity in circumgalactic medium properties even for fixed-mass halos, where metal covering fractions correlate more strongly with stellar mass and AGN luminosity than with star formation history [Hani et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488..135H/abstract). In massive early-type galaxies, cold reservoirs frequently originate from the cooling of the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]] and X-ray atmospheres [Werner et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.439.2291W/abstract), [Werner et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019SSRv..215....5W/abstract). Furthermore, the CLoGS survey found that a high fraction of group-dominant early-type galaxies contain cold gas disks, often exhibiting radio AGN activity but lacking a direct correlation between gas mass and radio power [O'Sullivan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...618A.126O/abstract). This cooling can trigger "chaotic cold accretion," where thermal instabilities cause cold molecular clouds to condense out of the hot halo. Observations of the Abell 2597 brightest cluster galaxy reveal a $3 \times 10^9 M_{\odot}$ filamentary nebula where cold clouds cast absorption shadows as they "rain" toward the central supermassive black hole at velocities of $\sim 300$ km s$^{-1}$ [Tremblay et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.534..218T/abstract), [Tremblay et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...865...13T/abstract). These dynamics are characteristic of [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]], wherein AGN jets and stellar feedback uplift gas that subsequently cools and cycles back onto the disk.

Finally, galaxies acquire gas through minor mergers with gas-rich satellites. This mechanism is particularly important for rebuilding disks in early-type and brightest group galaxies; for instance, direct cold gas deposition has been observed in a $z=0.25$ central galaxy undergoing a 1:56 mass-ratio merger, which produced $\sim 10$ kpc dust lanes in situ from tidally stripped satellite gas [Zhuang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251213932Z/abstract).

Despite the theoretical necessity of robust accretion, there is significant tension between predicted inflow rates and local Universe observations. Deep atomic gas surveys, such as HALOGAS, find that the average H I mass currently in a state of accretion provides an inflow rate of only $\sim 0.05 M_{\odot}$ yr$^{-1}$, with a stringent upper limit of $0.22 M_{\odot}$ yr$^{-1}$ [Kamphuis et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...668A.182K/abstract). Because these observed rates are substantially lower than the star formation rates of the surveyed galaxies, the literature suggests that local gas replenishment must rely heavily on the condensation of ionized coronal gas or internal recycling rather than the direct accretion of discrete cold H I clouds. However, highly sensitive MeerKAT observations of the isolated galaxy NGC 5068 have detected low-column-density H I ($6.4 \times 10^{17}$ cm$^{-2}$) and anomalous gas clumps, providing direct evidence for ongoing fresh accretion from the intergalactic medium [Healy et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...687A.254H/abstract).

## Environmental Impact and Gas Stripping

As galaxies infall into high-density environments, their [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] reservoirs are subjected to intense external hydrodynamic and gravitational forces. The interaction between a galaxy's interstellar medium and the [[hot-gas|hot, dense intracluster medium (ICM)]] primarily drives this transformation, efficiently removing gas and quenching star formation. 

The most vulnerable component of a galaxy's interstellar medium is the extended, [[atomic-gas-hi-studies|low-density atomic hydrogen (H I) disk]]. Observations of satellite galaxies demonstrate that the environmental suppression of H I begins well before galaxies reach the cluster core, initiating in group-scale halos with masses $\log (M_h/M_\odot) < 13.5$ [Brown et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.1275B/abstract). This environment-driven depletion is strongly associated with halo mass rather than local galaxy density, resulting in a large population of [[h-i-deficient-galaxies|H I-deficient galaxies]] in rich groups and clusters. Fast-acting mechanisms like ram-pressure stripping are required to explain the severe and rapid loss of atomic gas, as "starvation" alone—the gradual cessation of [[cold-gas-accretion|Cold gas accretion]]—operates too slowly to match the observed H I deficiencies in the local Universe [Brown et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.1275B/abstract).

While atomic gas is readily stripped, [[dense-molecular-gas|Dense molecular gas]] resides deeper within the galactic potential well, rendering it significantly more resilient to external ram pressure. Consequently, environmental processing strips H I much more efficiently and earlier than molecular hydrogen ($H_2$). However, the molecular phase is not entirely immune to environmental effects. High-resolution ALMA surveys of nearby clusters, such as the VERTICO survey of the Virgo cluster, reveal that highly H I-deficient galaxies also exhibit steep, truncated [[molecular-gas-surface-density|Molecular gas surface density]] profiles [Zabel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933...10Z/abstract). This truncation indicates that the low-surface-density molecular gas on the outskirts of galactic disks is actively removed by the ICM. Similarly, the ALMA Fornax Cluster Survey found that all detected low-mass galaxies ($M_\star < 3 \times 10^9 M_\odot$) possess disturbed molecular gas reservoirs, featuring asymmetric kinematics and gas tails in alignment with the direction of the cluster center—classic signatures of ongoing ram-pressure stripping [Zabel et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.2251Z/abstract). These interactions leave cluster members with overall gas fractions that can fall an order of magnitude below those of isolated field galaxies.

Gas stripped from infalling galaxies is deposited directly into the hot ICM. This, alongside major gas stream collisions and merging subclusters, induces sloshing motions that manifest as "cold fronts" within the cluster core. These cold fronts are observed as sharp discontinuities in X-ray surface brightness and temperature, where the denser side of the boundary is colder than the surrounding [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] [Zuhone et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JPlPh..82c5301Z/abstract), [Zinger et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476...56Z/abstract). Additionally, some optically dark, highly turbulent H I clouds have been detected in clusters like Virgo, existing in temporary pressure equilibrium with the ICM, though hydrodynamic simulations suggest such stripped structures are unstable and rapidly heat and disperse into the intracluster environment [Taylor et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479..377T/abstract).

The efficacy of gas stripping exhibits a notable redshift dependence, and the literature presents conflicting evidence regarding the survival of cold gas reservoirs at higher redshifts. While local clusters are highly effective at depleting cold gas, observations of low-mass clusters at $z \sim 1.3$ have uncovered overdensities of CO-emitting galaxies that retain field-like molecular gas reservoirs [Williams et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929...35W/abstract). Moreover, observations of the Spiderweb protocluster at $z=2.16$ reveal that extended molecular gas reservoirs (scales $>40$ kpc) are common, being present in approximately 30% of CO-selected galaxies, particularly in the cluster's dense core [Chen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.8950C/abstract). This implies that during the "cosmic noon," morphological transformation and quenching may be significantly delayed after a galaxy's first infall, allowing it to temporarily sustain star formation by consuming the cold gas tightly bound to its disk. However, at intermediate redshifts, environmental processing becomes increasingly efficient. The SEEDisCS survey has identified a unique population of galaxies infalling into clusters at $z \sim 0.5$ that maintain normal star-formation rates despite having significantly lower molecular gas content and short [[gas-depletion-and-consumption|Gas Depletion and Consumption]] timescales ($<1$ Gyr) [Spérone-Longin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...647A.156S/abstract). The exact timescale over which ram-pressure stripping overtakes internal [[gas-depletion-and-consumption|Gas Depletion and Consumption]] at high redshift remains an active subject of debate.

## Feedback and Quenching Processes

The regulation and termination of star formation in galaxies—a process known as quenching—is intimately tied to the dynamics of their [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] reservoirs. Internal feedback mechanisms, primarily driven by active galactic nuclei (AGN) and supernovae, are required to heat, disrupt, or expel this gas to prevent runaway star formation and to match the observed local galaxy mass functions. However, the exact mechanisms by which feedback couples to the interstellar medium remain a subject of active debate in the literature.

Supermassive black holes (SMBHs) are fueled by the accretion of cold gas. High-resolution observations have directly detected [[cold-molecular-clouds|Cold molecular clouds]] precipitating out of the [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] halo and falling toward central SMBHs via chaotic cold accretion [Tremblay et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.534..218T/abstract), [Rose et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518..878R/abstract). This influx of material can trigger profound AGN feedback. In extreme environments, such as hyper-luminous quasars at $z \sim 2-4$, powerful AGN-driven molecular outflows are observed to severely deplete the host's [[gas-fraction|molecular gas fraction]], leading to extremely short [[gas-depletion-and-consumption|Gas Depletion and Consumption]] timescales of $\lesssim 50\ {\rm Myr}$ [Bischetti et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...645A..33B/abstract).

Despite these dramatic examples, AGN feedback does not universally result in the immediate and total expulsion of a galaxy's cold gas. Radio jets and outflows often act to heat and redistribute gas rather than unbind it. In massive galaxy clusters, radio jets can uplift cold and warm multiphase gas; because the outflow velocities frequently remain below the galactic escape speed, the material rains back down, establishing long-lived [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]] [Tremblay et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...865...13T/abstract). Furthermore, some local powerful quasars hosting kpc-scale outflows still maintain massive molecular gas reservoirs ($\langle M_{\rm gas} \rangle \sim 1.3 \times 10^{10}\ M_\odot$) with depletion times consistent with those of normal [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] ($0.16 - 0.95\ {\rm Gyr}$) [Jarvis et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.1560J/abstract). Circumnuclear gas masses often show no strict correlation with concurrent AGN luminosity, indicating that fueling and feedback are highly episodic and do not necessarily destroy the global gas disk [Elford et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528..319E/abstract).

The persistence of cold gas during quenching is most evident in post-starburst galaxies. Classical textbook models dictate that quenching requires the exhaustion or complete blowout of gas reservoirs. However, recent observations and hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate that many post-starburst galaxies retain substantial molecular gas masses ($\sim 10^9 - 10^{10}\ M_\odot$) well after their transition to the red sequence [Davis et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.2447D/abstract), [Suess et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250614361S/abstract). In these systems, star formation is suppressed not by starvation, but by dynamical heating. Merger-induced shocks, stellar feedback, and AGN jets can inject significant turbulence into the gas, stabilizing it against gravitational collapse [Alatalo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812..117A/abstract). As a result, the [[high-star-formation-efficiency|High star formation efficiency]] typical of [[active-star-forming-regions|Active star-forming regions]] plummets, leading to extended depletion timescales of $> 10\ {\rm Gyr}$ [Alatalo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812..117A/abstract). This suggests a nuanced paradigm where quenching can occur via turbulence and heating that renders cold gas reservoirs inert, rather than requiring their absolute removal. However, note that some high-redshift quenched galaxies exhibit extremely low molecular gas fractions ($f_{H_2} < 7\%$), suggesting that in some systems, the depletion of the interstellar medium can be efficient and fairly complete [Bezanson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...873L..19B/abstract). Conversely, recent observations using the FAST radio telescope have shown that the majority of massive quiescent galaxies in the local Universe exhibit extreme H I deficiency ($\log(M_{\rm HI}/M_\star) < -3.46$). This deep depletion across diverse environments suggests that environmental stripping alone cannot fully explain quenching, and internal feedback or other mechanisms must actively prevent the retention or cooling of atomic gas [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251015145L/abstract). Structurally, the depletion of cold gas reservoirs is tightly coupled to bulge growth, with the molecular gas fraction exhibiting a significantly sharper decline than the atomic phase once galaxies surpass a threshold inner stellar mass surface density ($\Sigma_1$) [Shread et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251118227S/abstract). Furthermore, observations of the transition galaxy Hyde at $z=3.7$ suggest that quenching proceeds from the inside out, beginning before the galaxy has fully consumed or expelled its global gas reservoirs [Schreiber et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...650C...2S/abstract). Yet, recent MIGHTEE-HI results indicate that quiescent galaxies at intermediate redshifts ($z=0.4$) can retain significant atomic gas reservoirs, often just 40% lower than star-forming analogues if they contain dust [Bianchetti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250716917B/abstract).

## Cold Gas in Low-Metallicity Environments

In typical galactic environments, the rotational transitions of carbon monoxide (CO) are widely used as reliable proxies for the total [[dense-molecular-gas|molecular hydrogen ($\mathrm{H}_2$) mass]]. However, in [[low-metallicity-environments|Low-Metallicity Environments]], tracing [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] becomes exceptionally challenging due to the suppression of [[dense-gas-tracers|carbon monoxide (CO) emission]]. In [[low-metallicity-galaxies|Low-metallicity galaxies]], a lower dust abundance allows far-ultraviolet (FUV) radiation to penetrate deeper into molecular clouds. While $\mathrm{H}_2$ can effectively self-shield against this radiation, CO molecules rely heavily on dust shielding to survive. Consequently, CO is selectively photodissociated in the extended envelopes of these clouds. This creates a massive reservoir of "CO-dark" molecular gas that actively fuels star formation but cannot be detected via standard $\mathrm{CO}(J=1-0)$ observations [Cormier et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...564A.121C/abstract).

Dwarf irregular galaxies are characterized by their gas-rich but metal-poor disks, where star formation feedback has a significantly more profound impact on the interstellar medium compared to typical spiral galaxies [Hunter et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240217004H/abstract). The profound impact of selective photodissociation in these environments is evident in the CO-to-$\mathrm{H}_2$ conversion factor, $\alpha_{\mathrm{CO}}$. In metal-poor dwarf galaxies, $\alpha_{\mathrm{CO}}$ varies strongly and non-linearly with both [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]] and the internal "clumpiness" of the gas [Ramambason et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...681A..14R/abstract). Observations of local star-forming dwarfs reveal that equivalent $\alpha_{\mathrm{CO}}$ values can be a full order of magnitude higher than the standard Galactic calibration [Cormier et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...564A.121C/abstract). Because CO emission is restricted to the deepest, most shielded cores of molecular clouds, mass estimates relying solely on CO can overlook $70\%$ to $100\%$ of the total $\mathrm{H}_2$ mass in these systems [Madden et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.141M/abstract). Despite the harsh conditions, cold atomic precursors to molecular clouds can still be identified. The first extragalactic detection of [[atomic-gas-hi-studies|H I narrow self-absorption (HINSA)]] in the Large Magellanic Cloud revealed these cold atomic structures surviving even in low-metallicity environments [Liu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887..242L/abstract). Building on this, the Local Group L-Band Survey has provided the first unambiguous detections of localized [[cold-neutral-medium|cold neutral medium (CNM)]] absorption in the low-metallicity dwarf NGC 6822, revealing a mean spin temperature of $32 \pm 6$ K [Pingel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974...93P/abstract). Similarly, in the IC10 starburst, localized CNM detections show a strong kinematic coupling with high-density molecular tracers like CO and HCN, suggesting the cold atomic and molecular phases are more well-mixed than in the Milky Way [Stelea et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251110602S/abstract).

To overcome the limitations of CO, astronomers must utilize fine-structure cooling lines—specifically [C II] at $158\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ and [C I] at $609\ \mu\mathrm{m}$—to trace the CO-dark $\mathrm{H}_2$ residing in large photodissociation regions (PDRs). The [C II] line is a particularly robust alternative. In extreme low-metallicity starbursts, the luminosity ratio of [C II] to $\mathrm{CO}(1-0)$ can exceed $10^4$, emphasizing the overwhelming dominance of the $\mathrm{C}^+$ emitting region over the CO core [Cormier et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...564A.121C/abstract). Advanced radiative transfer and multiphase theoretical modeling confirm that the total $\mathrm{H}_2$ mass in these environments is much better traced by [C II] and [C I] emission than by CO [Ramambason et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...681A..14R/abstract), [Geach et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757..156G/abstract). Furthermore, similar multiphase calibrations have recently been developed to trace neutral atomic hydrogen (H I) using [C II] [Heintz et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922..147H/abstract) and [O I] fine-structure lines [Wilson et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...685A..30W/abstract) in high-redshift systems.

Accurately quantifying CO-dark gas using these alternative tracers significantly alters our understanding of star formation in dwarf galaxies. When relying exclusively on CO-derived gas masses, low-metallicity dwarfs appear to exhibit anomalously high star formation efficiencies. However, once the total $\mathrm{H}_2$ reservoir is properly accounted for using [C II] observations, these galaxies are found to align with the standard Schmidt-Kennicutt star formation scaling relations. This alignment demonstrates that their global star formation efficiency is entirely normal given the actual mass of the available molecular reservoir [Madden et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.141M/abstract).

## Cosmic Evolution of Gas Reservoirs

The cosmic history of star formation is fundamentally tied to the availability and evolution of [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] reservoirs across cosmic time. Observational efforts to trace the redshift evolution of the cosmic gas density generally divide the gas into its [[multi-gas-phase-components|molecular ($\rho_{\rm H_2}$) and atomic ($\rho_{\rm HI}$) components]], which exhibit strikingly different evolutionary pathways.

The cosmic molecular gas mass density strongly tracks the star formation history of the Universe. Observations from extensive (sub)millimeter campaigns, such as the ASPECS survey in the Hubble Ultra Deep Field [Aravena et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...882..136A/abstract) and the ALMACAL survey [Bollo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206778B/abstract), reveal that [[dense-molecular-gas|$\rho_{\rm H_2}$]] peaked during "Cosmic Noon" ($z \sim 1.5-2.0$). At this epoch, roughly 8 to 9 billion years ago, the progenitors of Milky Way-mass galaxies were gas-dominated, with cold gas masses often exceeding their stellar masses [Papovich et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016NatAs...1E...3P/abstract). Typical massive [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] hosted exceedingly large molecular reservoirs, with [[gas-fraction|gas mass fractions]] ($f_{\rm gas} \equiv M_{\rm gas} / (M_{\rm gas} + M_\star)$) averaging $40-50\%$, which is roughly an order of magnitude higher than in local counterparts [Tacconi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.463..781T/abstract). However, mass-complete stacking analyses from ALMA suggest that gas fractions for galaxies at $z=1-3$ may be systematically lower than estimates biased toward individual bright detections, particularly in the low-mass regime [Mérida et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..64M/abstract). In fact, studies of strongly magnified low-mass star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 2$ suggest a significant molecular gas deficit compared to scaling relations, with atomic hydrogen potentially accounting for up to 91% of their total cold gas budget [Catán et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240807849C/abstract). Beyond this peak, $\rho_{\rm H_2}$ gradually declines by roughly $1$ dex toward $z \sim 6$ [Bollo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206778B/abstract). 

From Cosmic Noon to the present day ($z=0$), $\rho_{\rm H_2}$ has plummeted by a factor of roughly six [Walter et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..111W/abstract). This dramatic depletion highlights a "downsizing" effect, where massive galaxies consumed or quenched their gas reservoirs earlier in cosmic time than lower-mass systems [Liu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887..235L/abstract). Despite this severe drop in the total gas mass fraction, the global [[gas-depletion-and-consumption|gas depletion timescale]] ($\tau_{\rm dep} = M_{\rm H_2} / {\rm SFR}$) appears to remain relatively constant across these epochs [Bollo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206778B/abstract). This implies that the precipitous drop in the cosmic star formation rate is primarily driven by the diminishing availability of molecular gas, rather than a fundamental shift in star formation efficiency. Despite the dramatic rise in molecular gas at high redshift, recent ALMA studies show that atomic hydrogen has dominated the total cold gas budget of star-forming galaxies for at least the past 3 billion years, even as these galaxies host rotating disks similar to their local analogs [Cortese et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848L...7C/abstract).

In contrast to the highly variable molecular gas density, the cosmic mass density of neutral atomic hydrogen ($\rho_{\rm HI}$) has remained remarkably stable over the last $\sim 10$ Gyr [Walter et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..111W/abstract). At high redshift, [[atomic-gas-hi-studies|$\rho_{\rm HI}$]] is most rigorously constrained by absorption studies of Damped Ly$\alpha$ systems (DLAs) observed against background quasars and gamma-ray bursts [Wolfe et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ARA&A..43..861W/abstract), [Lagos et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.1649L/abstract). The REBELS-IFU survey detections of significant DLA absorption at $z \sim 7$ demonstrate that massive galaxies already hosted substantial, enriched reservoirs of neutral gas and dust during the [[primordial-gas-and-chemistry|Epoch of Reionization]] [Rowland et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251011351R/abstract). Furthermore, high-column-density H I gas in these DLAs can span large continuous areas (up to $100\ {\rm kpc}^2$), consistent with them being massive, low-luminosity primeval galaxies [Cooke et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812L..27C/abstract). Even at the earliest epochs, substantial gas reservoirs existed. Observations of JADES-GS-z14-0, the most distant known galaxy at $z = 14.179$, reveal it hosts a massive neutral H I reservoir ($M_{\rm gas} \sim 10^{9.5} - 10^{9.8}\ M_\odot$) that dominates its total baryonic content [Heintz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...987L...2H/abstract). 

The relative constancy of $\rho_{\rm HI}$ presents a stark dichotomy with $\rho_{\rm H_2}$. While the atomic and molecular gas densities were roughly equal at $z \sim 1.5$, the sharp decline in $\rho_{\rm H_2}$ means that the present-day cold gas budget is heavily HI-dominated [Walter et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..111W/abstract). To reconcile the constant $\rho_{\rm HI}$, the declining $\rho_{\rm H_2}$, and the continuous growth of stellar mass, models invoke continuous baryonic cycling—often framed as the "bathtub model" [Bollo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206778B/abstract). In this paradigm, galaxies are fueled by a two-step [[cold-gas-accretion|Cold gas accretion]] process: ionized gas from the circumgalactic and intergalactic mediums cools to replenish extended HI reservoirs, which subsequently flow inward and condense into H$_2$ in the dense galactic centers [Walter et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..111W/abstract). Because the cosmic infall rate of fresh gas has slowed by nearly an order of magnitude since $z \sim 2$, the molecular reservoirs cannot be replenished at the rate they are consumed. The intersecting evolutionary tracks of these baryon components culminated at $z \sim 1.5$, the transition epoch when the cosmic stellar mass density formally surpassed the total (atomic plus molecular) cold gas density [Walter et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...902..111W/abstract).

## Kinematics and Sub-Galactic Structure

In the local Universe, the bulk of [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] within star-forming galaxies resides in [[luminous-gas-rich-disc-galaxies|macroscopic rotating disks]]. The overall gas kinematics in these systems are typically dominated by ordered circular motion, supplemented by a turbulent velocity dispersion that varies by gas phase. In the Milky Way, the widespread [[atomic-gas-hi-studies|atomic hydrogen (H I)]] exhibits a vertical scale height of $\sim 150$ pc and a velocity dispersion of $\sim 9 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, whereas the more centrally concentrated molecular gas has a narrower scale height of $\sim 50$ pc and a lower velocity dispersion of $\sim 7 \text{ km s}^{-1}$. Extragalactic observations of low-mass systems confirm that the coldest H I ($T < 1400$ K) components are kinematically quiescent, exhibiting narrow velocity dispersions of $< 6 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Warren et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757...84W/abstract). Furthermore, in extremely faint dwarf galaxies, cold atomic gas identified through brightness temperature tracing is found to be significantly more abundant than that identified via standard Gaussian decomposition of line profiles [Patra et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.2467P/abstract).

On sub-galactic scales, the interstellar medium is not uniform but heavily structured by [[gas-clumping|Gas clumping]]. The molecular phase organizes into [[cold-molecular-clouds|Cold molecular clouds]] with typical masses of $10^5 - 10^7 M_\odot$ and number densities of order $100 \text{ cm}^{-3}$. Direct observations of clump formation at $z \sim 2$ show that massive star-forming clumps can form through the rapid gravitational collapse of over a billion solar masses of gas within high-redshift disks [Zanella et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.521...54Z/abstract). This [[gas-clumping|clumping]] is highly sensitive to the local environment and gas-phase metallicity. In [[low-metallicity-environments|Low-Metallicity Environments]], models and observations indicate that H$_2$ resides in small, dense clumps surrounded by diffuse, "CO-dark" envelopes. Consequently, the total molecular gas mass and the CO-to-H$_2$ conversion factor ($\alpha_{\text{CO}}$) become strongly dependent on the geometric clumpiness parameter of the gas [Ramambason et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...681A..14R/abstract).

While late-type galaxies feature aligned stellar and gaseous disks, early-type galaxies (ETGs) present more complex gas kinematics. Roughly 40% of local ETGs host detectable molecular or atomic gas reservoirs, which frequently settle into compact rotating disks or rings. However, these gas structures are frequently misaligned with the underlying stellar kinematics [Young et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.3408Y/abstract), [Glass et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.5524G/abstract). While the exact origin of ETG gas remains debated, such kinematic misalignments are strong signatures of external gas acquisition via minor mergers or interactions, contrasting with internal origins like cooling flows. In dense environments, rotating disks are further disrupted by ram-pressure stripping. Resolved observations of cluster members show truncated molecular gas profiles and disturbed kinematics [Zabel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933...10Z/abstract), [Zabel et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.2251Z/abstract), with cold gas occasionally removed into extended tidal or stripped tails reaching tens of kiloparsecs [D'Onofrio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250721249D/abstract).

Beyond the galactic midplane, cold gas is frequently observed in complex extra-planar geometries. [[extra-planar-and-circumgalactic-gas|Extra-planar and Circumgalactic Gas]] structures are often driven by [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]], where mechanical feedback from supernovae or active galactic nuclei (AGN) ejects gas into the halo. In massive elliptical galaxies and brightest cluster galaxies, this manifests as a "galaxy-scale fountain" where buoyant X-ray bubbles uplift cold gas, which subsequently rains back toward the central supermassive black hole via chaotic cold accretion [Tremblay et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.534..218T/abstract), [Tremblay et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...865...13T/abstract). At higher redshifts, gas kinematics also reveal the signatures of initial assembly; observations of Ly$\alpha$ and molecular emission have identified massive "protodisks" (radii $> 100$ kpc) with organized rotational velocity profiles. These giant structures represent the direct infall and angular momentum deposition of cold streams from the cosmic web, maintaining a distinct kinematic signature prior to condensing into a central galaxy [Martin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...824L...5M/abstract). The ALMA-CRISTAL survey recently discovered a 15 kpc-long [C II] gas plume in a Lyman-$\alpha$ blob at $z=4.54$, which may represent a massive cold accretion stream or an interaction-driven structure actively feeding the high-redshift system [Solimano et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240104919S/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While recent multi-wavelength surveys have revolutionized our understanding of [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] in galaxies, several fundamental questions remain unresolved. A primary source of tension lies in the physical calibrations used to infer total gas masses from observational tracers, alongside debates regarding the mechanisms that regulate and deplete these reservoirs.

**Uncertainties in Gas Conversion Factors**
The conversion of CO luminosity to molecular hydrogen mass, parameterized by the conversion factor $\alpha_{\rm CO}$, is highly sensitive to the physical state of the interstellar medium. In [[low-metallicity-environments|Low-Metallicity Environments]], CO is easily photodissociated by ultraviolet radiation, leaving vast reservoirs of "CO-dark" $\text{H}_2$ [Madden et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.141M/abstract), [Cormier et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...564A.121C/abstract). Recent theoretical frameworks demonstrate that $\alpha_{\rm CO}$ varies strongly and non-linearly with both [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]] and the internal [[gas-clumping|clumpiness]] of the gas [Ramambason et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...681A..14R/abstract). Resolving the true distribution of this dark gas and incorporating fine-structure lines like [C II] $158\ \mu\text{m}$ as standard tracers [Heintz et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922..147H/abstract) remains a major hurdle in accurately mapping the cosmic molecular gas density over time. For instance, a full baryonic census of the $z=5.65$ galaxy HZ10 indicates that standard [C II] conversion factors may overpredict gas masses relative to dynamical constraints, suggesting the need for a calibrated [C II]-to-total ISM mass conversion factor [Algera et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251202320A/abstract). Theoretical models also require advancements; tools like the Hybrid-CHIMES model have recently been developed to more accurately simulate the chemical transition between atomic and molecular hydrogen in numerical galaxy formation models [Ploeckinger et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250615773P/abstract).

**Turbulence and Quenching Mechanisms**
Another active debate centers on the mechanisms that quench [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]]. The exact role of active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback and environmental processing in regulating [[cold-molecular-clouds|Cold molecular clouds]] remains ambiguous. While AGN are known to drive massive outflows, it is unclear to what extent they physically expel cold gas versus suppressing star formation through turbulent injection [Alatalo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812..117A/abstract). Observations of compact groups and post-starburst galaxies reveal that significant molecular reservoirs can persist even when star formation is highly suppressed. This suggests that shock-driven turbulence may prevent the gas from collapsing efficiently, meaning star formation can be quenched without starving the galaxy of its cold gas first [Alatalo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812..117A/abstract), [Davis et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.2447D/abstract).

**Next-Generation Facilities**
In the realm of [[atomic-gas-hi-studies|Atomic Gas (HI) Studies]], tracing neutral gas across cosmic time presents an observational bottleneck due to the intrinsic faintness of the 21-cm hyperfine transition. Currently, direct 21-cm emission mapping is largely confined to the local universe, though recent simulations predicted the detectability of lensed H I [Blecher et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.tmp.1668B/abstract), leading to the successful direct detection of H I in an individual galaxy at $z \sim 0.7$ using gravitational lensing [Lawrie et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251101715L/abstract). To push these boundaries, the astronomy community relies on the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) and its pathfinders. Upcoming large-scale blind surveys with the SKA are expected to dramatically improve constraints on the high-redshift H I mass density via direct absorption studies [Dutta et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JApA...40...41D/abstract), [Wagh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963..101W/abstract). Concurrently, new indirect methods are being developed to estimate cold gas surface densities from optical depth and metallicity in large spectroscopic surveys, potentially bypassing the need for direct 21-cm or CO detections entirely [Scholte et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518..353S/abstract).

Simultaneously, in the molecular and high-density regimes, the Next-generation VLA (ngVLA) will offer unprecedented spatial resolution and sensitivity. It will enable the characterization of complex organic molecules and spatially resolve emission from compact envelopes down to sub-parsec accretion disks [Beltrán et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ASPC..517..249B/abstract). Collectively, these future observatories will be critical for distinguishing between different modes of cosmic accretion, such as identifying pristine, metal-poor inflows of primordial gas that directly feed the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]] and galactic disks [Cresci et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.467..811C/abstract), [Crighton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...776L..18C/abstract). By combining these next-generation radio and sub-millimeter facilities, researchers aim to firmly link large-scale cosmic web accretion and [[primordial-gas-and-chemistry|Primordial gas and chemistry]] to the localized physics of star formation.
