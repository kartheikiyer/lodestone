In extragalactic astronomy, a **stellar halo** is a diffuse, spatially extended, and extremely low-surface-brightness [[stellar-component|Stellar component]] of a galaxy that envelops its main [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] and [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]]. Composed primarily of older, pressure-supported stars and [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]], the stellar halo extends deep into the host galaxy's [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]], often reaching out to hundreds of kiloparsecs and blending into the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]]. Because collisionless stellar systems retain a long-term dynamical memory of their origins, stellar halos serve as crucial "fossil records" of a galaxy's [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]], preserving the kinematic and chemical signatures of its earliest [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]]. 

Within the modern cosmological paradigm of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], stellar halos are understood to possess a dual origin. The vast majority of a halo's mass—particularly in its extended outer regions—is formed *ex situ* through the hierarchical accretion and tidal disruption of [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]]. Cosmological simulations and observations indicate that this accreted component is heavily dominated by debris from a small number of massive [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] events [Bullock et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...635..931B/abstract). Conversely, the denser inner halo frequently harbors an *in situ* [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]]: stars that formed from smoothly accreted cooling gas within the central [[parent-galaxy|Parent galaxy]] and were subsequently displaced into eccentric orbits by early, violent major mergers. Together, these complementary processes weave a complex spatial architecture characterized by ubiquitous [[stellar-substructure|Stellar substructure]], such as tidal streams and shells.

The chemical and kinematic properties of stellar halos provide powerful diagnostics of both the underlying [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]] and the statistical connections between galaxies and their environments. The median [[stellar-populations-by-metallicity|Stellar Populations by Metallicity]] of a stellar halo generally correlates tightly with the [[total-galactic-halo-luminosity|Total Galactic halo luminosity]] of its host galaxy, although local anomalies—such as the unusually metal-poor halo of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]]—indicate that individual accretion histories can vary significantly [Mouhcine et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...633..821M/abstract). [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] tagging of distinct $\alpha$-element abundances allows astrophysicists to reconstruct the specific star formation histories of disrupted progenitors. Furthermore, the kinematics of halo stars and their associated [[galactic-globular-cluster-system|Galactic globular cluster system]] are indispensable for mapping the deep [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]] of the host. By analyzing these extended populations, researchers can constrain the overarching [[galaxy-halo-scales|Galaxy-halo scales]], defining how the efficiency of star formation scales with [[dark-matter-halo-scales|Dark Matter Halo Scales]] across cosmic time [Behroozi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...717..379B/abstract), [Wechsler et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ARA&A..56..435W/abstract).

Despite a broad theoretical consensus on their hierarchical origins, deep low-surface-brightness imaging reveals immense structural diversity among stellar halos in the local universe. A major open question is whether the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] is a statistical outlier among massive spiral galaxies when compared to the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]]. Additionally, the discovery of some disk galaxies with virtually no detectable stellar halo challenges the assumption that extended hierarchical accretion is completely ubiquitous. Resolving these tensions, alongside related small-scale cosmological puzzles like the core-cusp problem in [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]], remains a primary driver for next-generation deep imaging surveys and refined hydrodynamic simulations.

## Historical Development and Theoretical Paradigms

The conceptual foundation of stellar halos originates with Walter Baade's observational work during World War II at the Mount Wilson Observatory. By resolving stars in the Local Group, Baade realized that the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] consists of distinct kinematic and chemical components, dividing them into Population I (disk stars of various ages and metallicities) and Population II (spheroidal components, including the [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] and the stellar halo). Population II stars were initially characterized as uniformly old and metal-poor, serving as a fossil record of the earliest epochs of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]].

Early theoretical frameworks for galaxy formation within dark matter halos were guided by models such as the "two-stage theory" proposed by White and Rees (1978), which naturally integrated into the modern $\Lambda$CDM cosmology and the hierarchical assembly paradigm. In this picture, stellar halos are built predominantly from the tidal disruption of [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]] (an *ex situ* origin). Cosmological simulations demonstrate that the vast majority of an accreted halo's mass ($80\%-90\%$) is typically contributed by a small number ($\lesssim 15$) of the most massive accretion events [Bullock et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...635..931B/abstract). Before this dark-matter-dominated framework was universally accepted, early baryonic dark matter models even proposed that halos could consist of massive clusters of stellar remnants like neutron stars, which would theoretically produce observable X-ray signals [Silk et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991Sci...251..537S/abstract). Concurrently, characterizing the [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]] themselves presented unique challenges; fundamental cosmological constraints demonstrated that standard NFW dark matter halo concentrations were often too high to match the rotation curves of low-surface brightness [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] without adopting low-density cold dark matter parameters [Navarro et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..117..404N/abstract). 

While early [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] models traditionally defined stellar halos as exclusively old, metal-poor, and accreted, contemporary research emphasizes a dual-origin paradigm. High-resolution cosmological simulations reveal a substantial *in situ* component, consisting of stars that formed within the main progenitor galaxy from smoothly accreted cold gas, and were subsequently displaced into halo orbits by violent major mergers [Zolotov et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...702.1058Z/abstract). 

This dual origin creates a strong spatial dichotomy in [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]]. The inner halo (typically $r < 20-50 \text{ kpc}$) harbors this dense, older *in situ* population, whereas the outer halo is built almost entirely through pure accretion and is heavily structured by recent merger debris [Zolotov et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...702.1058Z/abstract), [Elias et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.4004E/abstract), [Wright et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...70W/abstract). Furthermore, the classical view of halos as uniformly metal-poor has been challenged by observations of massive disk galaxies; for instance, the halo of the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] is significantly more metal-rich than that of the Milky Way, underscoring the extreme diversity of halo assembly histories driven by hierarchical growth [Mouhcine et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...633..821M/abstract).

## Structural and Morphological Characteristics

The structural and morphological properties of stellar halos reflect the complex, stochastic assembly histories of their host galaxies. As the highly extended [[galaxy-subcomponents|Galaxy subcomponents]] of a galaxy, the stellar halo is characterized by an exceptionally low surface brightness. Deep imaging reveals that stellar halos typically begin to dominate the galactic light distribution only at surface brightness levels of $\mu \approx 28-30$ mag arcsec$^{-2}$ [Bakos et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1204.3082B/abstract), [Merritt et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...62M/abstract).

The three-dimensional [[stellar-density-profile|Stellar density profile]] of these halos is most commonly parameterized using power laws, $\rho(r) \propto r^{-\alpha}$. In the outer regions ($r \gtrsim 30$ kpc), typical logarithmic slopes range from $\alpha \approx 3$ to $4$ [Bullock et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...635..931B/abstract), though a generalized $\rho \propto r^{-3}$ scaling has also been noted as a ubiquitous feature in modified gravity (MOND) frameworks [Hernandez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770...83H/abstract). Alternatively, halo mass distributions are frequently modeled using a [[hernquist-profile|Hernquist profile]], or utilizing Sérsic and Einasto profiles—particularly for the extended envelopes of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], where the fits indicate a smooth continuation between the central bulge and the inner halo [de Jong et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IAUS..254P..19D/abstract), [Remus et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..321...84R/abstract). Morphologically, these halos are not perfectly spherical; observations indicate they are often highly flattened, with minor-to-major axis ratios of $c/a \approx 0.6$ [de Jong et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IAUS..254P..19D/abstract). Detailed simulations of galaxy major mergers further indicate a structural shape dichotomy: post-merger stellar remnants are mostly oblate, while their encompassing dark matter halos are prolate or triaxial, with the axes of the two components typically oriented perpendicular to one another [Novak et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...646L...9N/abstract).

The radial architecture of stellar halos often exhibits distinct transition zones. The density profile itself can exhibit sharp morphological changes: the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] displays a "broken" profile that steepens significantly beyond a break radius of $\sim 25$ kpc. By contrast, deep kinematic and photometric mapping of the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] explicitly led to the discovery of an enormous metal-poor stellar halo exhibiting a continuous, unbroken profile out to radii beyond $160$ kpc, significantly larger than any of its previously mapped structural components [Guhathakurta et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..2366G/abstract). Simulations suggest that the presence or absence of density profile breaks is fundamentally tied to the orbital apocenters of the specific satellite galaxies that built the halo [Deason et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763..113D/abstract).

At the extreme outer limits of [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]] within groups and clusters, the stellar halo eventually blends into the diffuse intracluster light (ICL). The scale radius or transition radius marking the boundary between the gravitationally bound stellar halo and the unbound ICL scales strongly with the mass of the [[parent-halo|Parent halo]], ranging from $\sim 20$ kpc for Milky Way-mass systems up to $\sim 250$ kpc for massive cluster centrals [Contini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...692L...9C/abstract). Historically, this transition radius remains remarkably stable at $\sim 30-40$ kpc across much of cosmic time, only expanding to reach $\sim 400$ kpc in the most [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]] after $z=0.5$ [Contini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251101164C/abstract). Correspondingly, models quantitatively predict that the [[galaxy-mass-fractions|Galaxy mass fractions]] rises dramatically as a function of [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]], increasing from approximately $0.5\%$ in typical spiral galaxies to nearly $30\%$ in massive galaxy clusters where shredded galaxies supply an immense volume of intrahalo light [Purcell et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...666...20P/abstract). Within this environmental buildup, models predict that the fraction of stars stripped from satellites into the diffused intrahalo component peaks at roughly $40\%$ in halos reaching $10^{14} M_\odot$ [Yang et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...693..830Y/abstract). Furthermore, the ultimate physical boundary encompassing these halos is increasingly conceptualized as the splash-back radius—the orbital apocenter of recently accreted dark matter and satellite material—which typically extends well beyond the conventional virial radius ($R_{\rm 200m}$) [Xu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971..157X/abstract).

## The Dual Origin: In-situ vs. Ex-situ Formation

The canonical framework of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] describes stellar halos as the fossil records of hierarchical merging, built predominantly through the tidal disruption of [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]]. However, high-resolution cosmological hydrodynamical simulations have necessitated a paradigm shift, revealing that stellar halos are composed of both an "ex-situ" population of accreted stars and an "in-situ" population of stars that originally formed within the central potential well of the [[parent-galaxy|Parent galaxy]] [Zolotov et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...702.1058Z/abstract), [Wright et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...70W/abstract).

**Ex-situ Mass and Satellite Accretion**
The ex-situ component constitutes the vast majority of the total halo mass, typically accounting for $80\% - 90\%$ of the stellar halo in Milky Way-mass systems. This ex-situ mass is not assembled through a continuous drizzle of low-mass objects, but is instead heavily dominated by a small number (typically $\sim 2 - 15$) of the most massive [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] that the host has accreted over its history [Bullock et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...635..931B/abstract), [Wright et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...70W/abstract). The debris from these disrupted systems dominates the outer stellar halo (typically beyond $r \sim 30-50 \text{ kpc}$) and leaves distinct kinematic and chemical signatures, such as extended streams and specific $\alpha$-element abundance patterns [Bullock et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...635..931B/abstract), [Gonzalez-Jara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.282G/abstract).

**In-situ Stars and Dynamical Heating**
In contrast, the inner regions of the stellar halo often host a substantial fraction of in-situ stars. These stars originally formed at high redshifts out of smoothly accreted cold gas within the primordial disk or core of the host galaxy. Subsequently, major mergers dynamically heated or "kicked" these stars out of their central orbits and into the extended halo [Zolotov et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...702.1058Z/abstract). Furthermore, gas recycling—where evolving [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] continuously return mass to the interstellar medium over cosmic time—can sustain star formation and alter the structural evolution of the inner galaxy, creating a dense reservoir of stars susceptible to displacement during later turbulent interactions [Martig et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract).

**Quantifying the Dual Components**
Estimates of the exact ratio of in-situ to ex-situ stars vary significantly and depend heavily on a galaxy's specific merger history. Simulations suggest that within the inner halo, the in-situ mass fraction ranges from roughly $5\%$ in galaxies with active, recent major mergers, up to $20\% - 50\%$ in galaxies with quiescent recent histories [Zolotov et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...702.1058Z/abstract). Recent models, such as the FOGGIE simulations, find that globally, in-situ stars can contribute $30\% - 40\%$ of the total stellar halo mass, though this material remains highly centrally concentrated and does not heavily influence the diffuse light in the halo outskirts [Wright et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...70W/abstract). 

## Hierarchical Assembly and Tidal Disruption

Under the standard cosmological paradigm, extended stellar halos are primarily built through the hierarchical accretion and tidal disruption of smaller [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]]. As dark matter subhalos fall into the primary potential well, strong tidal forces strip them of their stars and gas. Because collisionless stellar systems retain a long-term memory of their dynamical origins, stellar halos serve as an archeological record of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] [Bullock et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...635..931B/abstract).

High-resolution $N$-body and hydrodynamic simulations demonstrate that halo assembly proceeds essentially from the inside out. The resulting accreted stellar density profiles are generally well-described by broken power laws, $\rho \propto r^{-\alpha}$ with $\alpha \sim 3-4$, or by Einasto profiles at larger radii [Bullock et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...635..931B/abstract), [Remus et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..321...84R/abstract). 

This tidal disruption process leaves ubiquitous morphological signatures—such as streams, shells, and other [[stellar-substructure|Stellar substructure]]—that dictate the non-uniformity of the halo. Substructures in the outer halo generally correspond to recent minor mergers occurring within the last $\sim 8$ Gyr, whereas earlier accretion events have largely phase-mixed into a smoother background distribution [Johnston et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...689..936J/abstract). 

Deep imaging surveys highlight an immense structural diversity among [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]; the peak-to-peak variation in stellar halo mass fractions spans over two orders of magnitude [Merritt et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...62M/abstract). Furthermore, while standard hierarchical models suggest that halos should be ubiquitous, some massive spirals (such as M101) exhibit almost no detectable stellar halo. This presents an ongoing tension with baseline cold dark matter expectations and implies that galaxy assembly histories are highly non-uniform [Merritt et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...62M/abstract), [Elias et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.4004E/abstract).

## Stellar Substructure and Kinematic Fossils

In the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm, the tidal disruption of [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]] leaves behind an archaeological footprint characterized by distinct spatial and kinematic substructures. Because stellar halos typically begin to dominate a galaxy's surface brightness profile only at extremely faint levels, detecting these features requires exceptionally deep imaging [Bakos et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1204.3082B/abstract), [Merritt et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...62M/abstract). Consequently, large photometric surveys have catalyzed a major paradigm shift, transitioning the field from viewing the outer [[milky-way|Milky Way]] halo as a smooth, diffuse component to recognizing it as a highly substructured and complex environment [Helmi et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&ARv..15..145H/abstract).

Recent and ongoing accretion events manifest as coherent spatial features such as stellar streams, shells, and diffuse tidal debris [Johnston et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...689..936J/abstract). The morphological appearance of this debris provides direct constraints on the orbital parameters and properties of the progenitor. For instance, $N$-body simulations indicate that satellite galaxies with a rotationally supported disky morphology tend to produce more prominent, well-defined stellar streams than spheroidal progenitors [Milošević et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022SerAJ.205...33M/abstract). In some systems, such as NGC 4449, massive stellar streams indicate "stealth" mergers with mass ratios of approximately $1:50$ that alone can deposit stellar mass comparable to the entire pre-existing halo [Martínez-Delgado et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748L..24M/abstract). Alongside stellar kinematics, observations of Mg II absorption in quasar spectra suggest that "stirred up" gas within extended halos may also be correlated with the morphological asymmetries and recent interaction histories of the parent galaxies [Churchill et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..331..387C/abstract). These multi-phase interactions are directly observable; for example, UV absorption spectra have detected metal-rich gas physically associated with the [[giant-stellar-stream|Giant Stellar Stream]] in the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]]. This gas, reflecting a specific ionization state and solar-like metallicity, explicitly traces the disruption and environment of its massive progenitor [Koch et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...807..153K/abstract).

As tidal debris orbits within the host's [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]], it eventually becomes spatially well-mixed and visually indistinguishable from the smooth halo. However, the debris retains coherence in multi-dimensional phase space, forming distinct kinematic groups [Helmi et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002Ap&SS.281..351H/abstract). These phase-space correlations are highly sensitive to the underlying [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]]. Theoretical models suggest that the long-term survival and detectability of specific kinematic fossils may be tied to the triaxiality of the dark matter potential, which can induce stabilizing orbital resonances [Rojas-Niño et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...805...29R/abstract).

The reconstruction of a galaxy's accretion history is further enhanced by combining kinematics with chemical abundances (chemodynamical tagging). In the [[milky-way|Milky Way]], coherent variations in the ratio of blue horizontal branch stars to main-sequence turn-off stars map directly onto distinct tidal streams, revealing the varying ages and metallicities of their respective progenitor dwarfs [Bell et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AJ....140.1850B/abstract). Similarly, in the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]], the kinematics of halo [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] exhibit two distinct, perpendicularly rotating populations, preserving the signature of two major discrete accretion epochs separated by billions of years [Mackey et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.574...69M/abstract).

The literature currently debates the degree to which these substructures map onto a global structural dichotomy. While some observational studies argue that the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] exhibits a sharp, fundamental division between a metal-rich, prograde inner halo and a metal-poor, retrograde outer halo [Carollo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Natur.450.1020C/abstract), other kinematic and cosmological models suggest the halo is a complex, continuous superposition of stochastic accretion events. In this latter view, the observed structural "break" in the galaxy's density profile is caused by the physical buildup of stars at the orbital apocenters resulting from a relatively early and massive accretion event [Deason et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763..113D/abstract). Furthermore, astronomers caution that local samples acquired within a few kiloparsecs of the Sun may significantly bias global halo reconstructions, as massive, highly retrograde components like the Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus merger remnant can be missing entirely from small, localized volumes [Sharpe et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963..162S/abstract).

## Chemical Abundances and Metallicity Gradients

The [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] of stellar halos serve as a crucial "fossil record" for understanding the hierarchical assembly of galaxies. By analyzing the [[halo-metallicity-distribution-function|Halo metallicity distribution function]] (MDF) of a halo, astronomers can reconstruct the mass spectrum and accretion epochs of its progenitor satellites. The shape of the MDF—ranging from single-peaked distributions dominated by one or two massive progenitors to broad or bimodal forms—reflects the highly stochastic nature of halo assembly [Mondal et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251123452M/abstract).

Observations of nearby spiral galaxies reveal a positive correlation between the total luminosity of the [[parent-galaxy|Parent galaxy]] and the average metallicity of its stellar halo [Mouhcine et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...633..821M/abstract), [Renda et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.363L..16R/abstract). Within this scaling relation, the median metallicity of an accreted halo is determined primarily by the mass of its most massive progenitor [Oman et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017Galax...5...33O/abstract). However, the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] appears to be an outlier in this regard; its halo is approximately $1\text{ dex}$ more metal-poor than that of the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]], falling nearly a full decade below the global luminosity-metallicity relation expected for massive spirals [Mouhcine et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...633..821M/abstract), [Freeman et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..165..167F/abstract), [Rich et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004oee..symp..255R/abstract). By contrast, massive elliptical galaxies such as NGC 5128 exhibit metal-rich outer halos with median metallicities of $[\text{M/H}] \approx -0.4$ and broad dispersions of $\sim 0.77\text{ dex}$ [Choi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929..113C/abstract), [Harris et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....122.3065H/abstract). 

Alpha-enrichment, typically denoted as $[\alpha/\text{Fe}]$, provides an independent and sensitive probe of the star formation histories of [[accreted-stellar-populations|Accreted stellar populations]]. The $[\alpha/\text{Fe}]$ abundance distributions directly correlate with the dominant epoch of accretion and the properties of the progenitor satellites, allowing the ratio to act as a chemical clock. Simulations indicate that halos dominated by very early accretion exhibit higher average $[\alpha/\text{Fe}]$, whereas halos built by high-luminosity, longer-lived satellites possess higher $[\text{Fe/H}]$ [Johnston et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...689..936J/abstract). Furthermore, ex situ stars that dominate the outer halo tend to be more $\alpha$-rich and older, while "endo-debris" stars deposited deeper in the potential well are generally less $\alpha$-rich [Gonzalez-Jara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.282G/abstract).

Chemical tagging capitalizes on these diverse abundance patterns to identify distinct progenitor systems. Methods that model CARDs as linear combinations of empirical templates can successfully recover the mass spectrum and quenching times of disrupted dwarf galaxies [Cunningham et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934..172C/abstract). At smaller scales, chemical tagging of light-element anticorrelations is used to identify halo stars that migrated from evaporating [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] [Martell et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..334...38M/abstract).

Structurally, halos often exhibit a negative metallicity gradient. In the outermost halo of the Milky Way ($r > 35\text{ kpc}$), the frequency of very metal-poor stars ($[\text{Fe/H}] < -2.0$) reaches up to $60\%$, strongly supporting inside-out hierarchical assembly [Dietz et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...894...34D/abstract). These spatial-chemical correlations are increasingly quantified using information-theoretic metrics; the mutual information between stellar angular position and metallicity increases with radius, tracing the transition from well-mixed inner regions to the incomplete phase mixing characteristic of recent, distant accretion events [Mondal et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251123452M/abstract). Interestingly, kinematic substructures retain distinct chemical signatures: prograde stars in the outer Milky Way halo show a strong metallicity-distance dependence (suggesting accretion of multiple small galaxies), whereas retrograde stars exhibit a roughly constant metallicity, likely linked to the singular accretion of the massive Sequoia progenitor [Dietz et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...894...34D/abstract).

## Multi-wavelength Observations

While stellar halos are primarily studied through optical and near-infrared imaging of their diffuse stellar populations, observing halos across the electromagnetic spectrum reveals additional multiphase components closely tied to galaxy evolution. In many [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], extended gaseous and non-thermal components manifest as [[intermediate-radio-halo|Intermediate radio halo]].

The existence and physical size of these radio halos are directly correlated with the extent of active star-forming regions within the galactic disk. Their ability to "break out" from the interstellar medium and expand into the halo depends significantly on the [[stellar-surface-density|Stellar surface density]] of the host galaxy, as the outward pressure from cosmic rays and magnetic fields must overcome the central [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]] [Dahlem et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...457..121D/abstract). In more extreme environments, such as local [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|Starburst Galaxy Evolution]], radio halos become particularly prominent. Observations of starburst systems demonstrate that their radio halos exhibit typical scale heights of approximately $1\text{ kpc}$, and their intrinsic radio luminosity scales linearly with both the galaxy's star formation rate and its infrared luminosity [Galante et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...685A.157G/abstract). These multi-wavelength counterparts underscore that galactic halos are dynamic, multiphase environments shaped by both continuous external accretion and vigorous internal feedback.

## Globular Clusters as Halo Tracers

[[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] are indispensable tracer objects for investigating the extended properties of galactic halos. Because they are highly luminous and compact, they can be observed at large galactocentric radii where the diffuse stellar light is too faint and cold H I gas is absent. As collisionless test particles, their kinematics provide direct probes of the underlying dark matter potential, allowing for robust dynamical mass measurements well into the halo outskirts. Furthermore, their orbital evolution, which contracts over time due to dynamical friction, provides a beyond-mean-field test of the cold dark matter (CDM) paradigm [Ben-Yeda et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250402476B/abstract).

Globular cluster systems typically exhibit a bimodal color and metallicity distribution, broadly dividing them into metal-poor ([[blue-globular-clusters|Blue globular clusters]]) and metal-rich ([[red-globular-clusters|Red globular clusters]]) subpopulations. The prevailing framework posits that blue GCs formed at high redshifts ($z > 3$) within small protogalactic fragments that were subsequently accreted to form the extended halo, whereas red GCs formed predominantly during later, dissipational major mergers that built up the host galaxy's central regions [Harris et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806...36H/abstract). As structural fossils, GC kinematics preserve the memory of these hierarchical assembly events. For example, distinct populations of GCs in the halo of the Andromeda Galaxy exhibit orthogonal rotation axes, pinpointing two separate major accretion epochs separated by billions of years [Mackey et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.574...69M/abstract).

One of the most striking relationships in near-field cosmology is the remarkably tight GC mass-scaling relation between the total mass of a galaxy's globular cluster system ($M_{\rm GCS}$) and its host dark matter halo mass ($M_h$). Observations indicate that this scaling holds across five orders of magnitude in halo mass and across all galaxy morphological types, described by:
$$M_{\rm GCS} \propto M_h^{1.03 \pm 0.03}$$
When partitioned by metallicity, the [[blue-globular-clusters|Blue globular clusters]] scale almost exactly linearly ($M_{\rm GCS, blue} \propto M_h^{0.96 \pm 0.03}$), while the [[red-globular-clusters|Red globular clusters]] exhibit a slightly steeper dependence ($M_{\rm GCS, red} \propto M_h^{1.21 \pm 0.03}$) [Harris et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806...36H/abstract). The linearity of the blue GC scaling suggests that the mass of gas available for metal-poor GC formation at high redshift was directly proportional to the total dark matter potential well. This contrasts sharply with the highly nonlinear stellar mass-to-halo mass relation governing the bulk of the galaxy's field [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]], making GCs uniquely direct tracers of total halo mass [Harris et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806...36H/abstract).

In addition to tracing the gravitational potential, globular clusters actively contribute to the stellar mass of the halo itself. Over cosmic time, tidal stripping and internal evaporation gradually dissolve some globular clusters, depositing their stars into the diffuse halo. These "migrant" stars can be identified in the field via unique chemical signatures, notably the light-element abundance anticorrelations that characterize globular cluster populations but are typically absent in standard halo field stars [Martell et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..334...38M/abstract). Such chemical tagging allows researchers to quantify the exact fraction of the stellar halo built from disrupted clusters, further tightening the observational link between halo assembly and GC evolution.

## The Galaxy-Halo Connection and Scaling Relations

The statistical relationship between the stellar content of a galaxy and its host dark matter halo—known as the stellar mass-halo mass (SMHM) relation—is a cornerstone of modern [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] models. Techniques such as abundance matching and halo occupation distribution (HOD) modeling are widely used to map galaxies to dark matter halos. These statistical frameworks inherently distinguish the evolutionary pathways of [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]], which reside at the bottom of the halo potential well, from orbiting [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] [Moster et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...710..903M/abstract), [Wechsler et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ARA&A..56..435W/abstract).

A robust feature of the SMHM relation is a strongly mass-dependent star formation efficiency. The efficiency of converting available baryons into stars peaks at a [[pivot-mass|Pivot mass]] of approximately $M_h \sim 10^{12} M_\odot$, where up to $10\%-20\%$ of the universal baryon fraction is locked in stars [Behroozi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...717..379B/abstract), [Wechsler et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ARA&A..56..435W/abstract). At both lower and higher mass regimes, feedback mechanisms efficiently suppress star formation. In low-mass halos, stellar mass scales steeply with halo mass, roughly as $M_* \propto M_h^{2.3}$. Conversely, in the high-mass regime ($M_h > 10^{13} M_\odot$), stellar mass growth is much shallower, following $M_* \propto M_h^{0.3}$, and is driven predominantly by hierarchical merging rather than *in situ* star formation [Brown et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...682..937B/abstract), [Behroozi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...717..379B/abstract). Further observations at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 1$) reveal that the high-mass end slope of the SMHM relation is significantly steeper than it is at $z \sim 0$, exhibiting a very low intrinsic scatter of approximately $0.15$ dex [Shankar et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...797L..27S/abstract). Scaling relations generally evolve over cosmic time; recent JWST observations from the COSMOS-Web survey indicate that at high redshifts ($z > 3.5$), early galaxy assembly outpaces halo growth, as large gas reservoirs sustain elevated star formation efficiencies even when the growth of the host dark matter halos begins to stagnate [Shuntov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A..20S/abstract).

The intrinsic scatter of the SMHM relation provides unique insights into the regularity of galaxy assembly. For massive halos ($M_h > 10^{14} M_\odot$), the scatter in central stellar mass at a fixed halo mass is remarkably tight ($\lesssim 0.2$ dex) and is primarily governed by the statistics of hierarchical accretion [Gu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833....2G/abstract), [Wechsler et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ARA&A..56..435W/abstract). Furthermore, the number of [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] within a given halo depends strongly on the halo's formation time, with older halos hosting significantly fewer satellites than younger halos of the exact same mass [Zhu et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...639L...5Z/abstract). In group and cluster environments, a significant portion of the variance in stellar properties correlates with the magnitude gap between the brightest central galaxy and its satellites [Golden-Marx et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...860....2G/abstract). In contrast, the scatter increases dramatically for dwarf galaxies residing in low-mass halos, sometimes reaching nearly 1 dex. This large variance at the faint end is attributed to stochastic *in situ* star formation, varying degrees of tidal stripping, and the existence of "dark dwarfs" completely devoid of stars [Gu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833....2G/abstract), [Munshi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170506286M/abstract). In these low-mass halos, the overall halo growth rate—rather than just halo concentration—serves as a primary driver of scatter in the SMHM relation, such that faster-growing halos host correspondingly lower stellar masses [Feldmann et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...871L..21F/abstract).

The galaxy-halo connection is not strictly parameterized by mass alone; secondary properties induce notable tensions and sub-trends. For instance, at a given stellar mass, the clustering amplitude and inferred host halo mass differ significantly between [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] and [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]]. Quiescent centrals tend to inhabit systematically more massive halos than their star-forming counterparts, implying that quenching is tightly linked to halo mass and assembly history [Coil et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838...87C/abstract), [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..233W/abstract). At a fixed stellar mass, the stellar populations of passive central galaxies also vary robustly with halo mass; centrals residing in more massive dark matter halos tend to be systematically older and more $\alpha$-enriched (e.g., higher $[\text{Mg/Fe}]$), pointing to earlier and more rapid quenching epochs [Oyarzún et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933...88O/abstract). Furthermore, the structural properties of the stellar component reflect the dark matter potential: halo spin has been shown to correlate inversely with stellar surface density, as high-spin halos suppress central gas condensation and lead to more diffuse stellar distributions [Rong et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241112210R/abstract).

Finally, while total stellar mass is traditionally matched to halo mass, recent numerical models suggest that the stellar mass located specifically in the outer halo is a superior tracer of the host's total dark matter mass. For massive central galaxies ($M_* \geq 10^{11.2} M_\odot$), the 2D outskirt stellar mass measured between $50$ and $100\text{ kpc}$ correlates more tightly with halo mass than either the total stellar mass or the total accreted (*ex situ*) mass. This underscores that the diffuse outer stellar halo is uniquely and deeply coupled to the hierarchical assembly of the broader dark matter halo [Xu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990..127X/abstract).

## Diversity and Variability in the Local Universe

Expanding beyond our immediate neighbors, deep imaging campaigns have revealed immense structural diversity across the local universe. Observations from the Dragonfly survey targeting Milky Way-mass spirals report a mean stellar halo mass fraction of $0.009 \pm 0.005$, but with a massive peak-to-peak scatter exceeding a factor of $100$ (an rms scatter of $1.01_{-0.26}^{+0.9}$ dex) [Merritt et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...62M/abstract). While some galaxies display highly structured and massive halos resembling the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]], others possess almost no detectable stellar halo [Merritt et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...62M/abstract). For example, massive spirals like M101 (the Pinwheel Galaxy) show effectively no diffuse stellar envelope, a feature difficult to reconcile with some standard expectations of continuous hierarchical growth [Elias et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.4004E/abstract). Simulated analogues of these "halo-less" galaxies suggest they inhabit older dark matter halos that assembled early and have evolved unperturbed by major mergers over the last $\sim 10$ Gyr [Elias et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.4004E/abstract).

This structural diversity extends comprehensively down to the lowest mass regimes. Evidence for extended stellar halos is found even in dwarf irregular galaxies like IC 1613, which possesses an extended component of metal-poor stars tracing out to five half-light radii [Pucha et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...880..104P/abstract). Deep Hubble Space Telescope (HST) observations resolving the red giant branch stars in starburst dwarfs, such as NGC 1569 and NGC 4449, confirm the presence of extended stellar envelopes with density profiles consistent with distinct, ancient stellar halos [Ryś et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...530A..23R/abstract). In low-mass field galaxies, simulated accreted stellar halos show immense variability, often exhibiting scale lengths that are smaller than their *in-situ* [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] yet remaining distinctly diffuse rather than bulge-like [Cooper et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250113317C/abstract). Furthermore, environmental density heavily impacts these dwarf systems even prior to their eventual accretion; dwarfs located near massive halos tend to grow faster and exhibit higher stellar masses for a given halo mass compared to their isolated field counterparts [Christensen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..236C/abstract). While low-mass halos are typically associated with extremely low star formation efficiencies, it has been proposed that dwarf galaxies may have experienced a reversed phase of high star-formation efficiency during periods of early, fast [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]]. This intense early assembly potentially reverses the low-efficiency trends typically seen in such environments at later epochs [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...790L..17M/abstract).

Deep surface brightness profiles from programs like the GHOSTS survey and SDSS Stripe 82 confirm that stellar halos typically only begin to dominate a galaxy's light profile at extremely faint surface brightness limits of $\sim 28 - 30$ mag arcsec$^{-2}$ [Bakos et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1204.3082B/abstract), [de Jong et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..241..503D/abstract). At these limits, the morphological variety—ranging from pristine, unperturbed [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] to systems engulfed in complex tidal streams and shells—underscores the stochasticity of [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]]. Ultimately, the overarching consensus in [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] models is that the vast scatter in halo mass and structure at a fixed total galaxy mass is fundamentally driven by the highly varied merger and accretion histories of individual dark matter halos [Merritt et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...62M/abstract), [Renda et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150604238R/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the standard hierarchical paradigm provides a robust framework for understanding the assembly of stellar halos, significant theoretical tensions and observational limitations persist. Unraveling the complete history of galaxy assembly requires addressing several open questions regarding the ubiquity of halos, the exact nature of their progenitor systems, and their underlying dark matter distributions.

**Structural Diversity and "Halo-less" Galaxies**
Recent deep imaging surveys reveal an immense structural diversity, with peak-to-peak variations in halo mass fractions exceeding a factor of $100$ among galaxies of similar mass [Merritt et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...62M/abstract). Notably, some massive spiral galaxies (such as M101) exhibit almost no detectable stellar halo, which is difficult to reconcile with standard cold dark matter expectations [Merritt et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...62M/abstract), [Elias et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.4004E/abstract). It remains ambiguous whether these "halo-less" galaxies represent a fundamentally distinct evolutionary pathway or simply extreme stochastic variations in accretion histories.

**The In Situ vs. Ex Situ Debate and Metallicity Tensions**
The precise origins of halo stars remain a subject of active debate. While hierarchical models predict that halos are primarily composed of [[accreted-stellar-populations|Accreted stellar populations]], the exact contribution of "in situ" stars—those formed within the main galaxy and subsequently dynamically heated—remains highly conflicting depending on host merger history [Zolotov et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...702.1058Z/abstract), [Wright et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...70W/abstract). Furthermore, it is currently unclear whether the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] is a statistical outlier among spiral galaxies. The Galactic halo falls significantly below the global luminosity-metallicity relation established for other nearby spirals [Mouhcine et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...633..821M/abstract), [Renda et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150604238R/abstract). Resolving whether this discrepancy stems from an atypical assembly history or falls within normal hierarchical scatter requires larger observational samples.

**Dwarf Galaxies, Missing Satellites, and the Core-Cusp Problem**
At the lowest mass scales, the relationship between [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] and their dark matter halos presents ongoing challenges. The scatter in the stellar mass-halo mass relation increases dramatically for $M_{\rm halo} \lesssim 10^9 M_\odot$. This high variance is driven in part by the predicted existence of dark dwarfs—low-mass dark matter halos that are entirely devoid of stars [Munshi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170506286M/abstract). Additionally, observations of dark-matter-dominated low surface brightness galaxies consistently favor "cored" central density profiles, contradicting the "cuspy" profiles predicted by collisionless dark matter simulations. The extent to which stellar feedback can account for these cored profiles and the broader baryon deficiency in dwarf halos remains a complex subject of active research [Kuzio de Naray et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741L..29K/abstract), [Yu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251205584Y/abstract).

**Future Directions**
The resolution of these tensions relies heavily on the next generation of wide-field observatories. Upcoming deep imaging with the LSST at the Vera C. Rubin Observatory will probe the ultra-faint end of the stellar mass function, mapping diffuse substructures and potentially quantifying the population of ultra-faint satellites and dark dwarfs [Munshi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170506286M/abstract). Concurrently, the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will provide unprecedented views of stellar halos out to $\sim 100$ kpc, utilizing both integrated light and resolved color-magnitude diagrams to untangle complex accretion histories [Wright et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...70W/abstract), [Walder et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...994...36W/abstract). Additionally, the 4DWARFS survey will serve as a key spectroscopic project, mapping the chemo-kinematics of over 140,000 stars in dwarf galaxies and stellar streams to significantly advance our understanding of substructure and chemical evolution [Skúladóttir et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Msngr.190...19S/abstract). Together, these facilities will definitively constrain the properties of [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]] and test the hierarchical assembly paradigm across cosmic time.
