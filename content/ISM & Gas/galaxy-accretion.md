## Article

**Galaxy accretion** is the fundamental mechanism by which galaxies assemble their mass, dictating their structural, chemical, and dynamical evolution across cosmic time. Operating within the broader framework of the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], mass assembly proceeds via two primary channels: the continuous [[smooth-accretion|Smooth accretion]] of baryonic gas from the intergalactic medium, and the stochastic, discrete capture of [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]] and dark matter subhalos. While early [[monolithic-collapse|Monolithic collapse]] theories posited a rapid, singular formation epoch, modern cosmological models demonstrate that galaxies are fundamentally open systems. [[smooth-gas-infall|Smooth gas infall]] accounts for the vast majority of a typical galaxy's total mass assembly, fueling [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] and sustaining [[continuous-star-formation|Continuous star formation]], whereas the discrete accretion of minor satellites and major mergers primarily makes galaxies more concentrated and triggers intense starbursts [Combes et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..4121C/abstract).

The thermodynamics and geometry of infalling gas are classically divided into a bimodal framework that governs large-scale [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. In low-mass dark matter halos and at high redshifts, gas accretes primarily via [[cold-gas-accretion|Cold gas accretion]], penetrating the halo along dense cosmic web filaments on dynamical timescales without supporting a stable virial shock [Dave et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..219D/abstract). As halos grow more massive and cross a critical threshold, infalling material is subject to virialization heating. This transitions the system to [[hot-mode-accretion|Hot Mode Accretion]], where star formation is subsequently fueled by the gradual, subsonic cooling of a hot circumgalactic reservoir. This bimodal supply of intergalactic fuel regulates the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] of galaxies, dictates the [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]] of extended galactic disks, and drives the continuous dilution and enrichment of the interstellar medium [Fraternali et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.2166F/abstract).

Simultaneously, the stochastic arrival of mass through hierarchical merging leaves indelible structural and kinematic signatures. The tidal disruption of infalling satellites constructs extended [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]] and populates the galaxy with distinct [[accreted-stellar-populations|Accreted stellar populations]] and globular clusters, which serve as crucial fossil records of the assembly process [Freeman et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ASPC...92....3F/abstract), [West et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..245..420W/abstract). However, the accretion cycle is heavily regulated by [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]] processes. Mechanical feedback from supernovae and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] can eject gas or prevent it from cooling, balancing the continuous inflow. Furthermore, a galaxy's position within the larger cosmic web—its [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]]—dictates its accretion efficiency. Dense cluster environments actively shock-heat infalling intergalactic gas and physically strip existing gas reservoirs from satellite galaxies, ultimately leading to durable [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]].

This article provides a comprehensive overview of galaxy accretion physics, tracing the historical development of mass assembly theory from early monolithic collapse models to the modern [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] scenario [Gott et al. (1977)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977ARA&A..15..235G/abstract). It examines the multi-wavelength observational tracers of both gas inflows and stellar substructure, the intricate fueling dynamics of central [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]], and the environmental mechanisms that alter accretion pathways in galaxy clusters. Finally, it addresses the critical open questions currently challenging the field, including the elusive direct observational detection of cold streams, the physical degeneracies between AGN-driven and halo-mass-driven quenching mechanisms, and conflicting observations regarding the correlation between dark matter accretion and star formation in the local Universe.

## Historical Development of Galaxy Assembly Theory

The theoretical framework of galaxy assembly has undergone a series of profound paradigm shifts over the past half-century. Early conceptualizations of galaxy formation were dominated by the [[monolithic-collapse|Monolithic collapse]] model. Inspired by the classic Eggen-Lynden-Bell-Sandage model for the Milky Way's formation, this scenario posited that galaxies—particularly [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]]—formed rapidly at high redshift through the single, dissipative collapse of a massive gas cloud, followed by passive stellar evolution [Evans et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200709474E/abstract). While this model successfully explained the homogeneously old stellar populations observed in massive spheroids, its initial conditions were difficult to motivate physically, and it struggled to naturally reproduce the systematic properties and cosmological distribution of galaxies.

During the late 1970s and early 1980s, the emergence of the dark matter paradigm catalyzed a fundamental shift in astrophysical thought. Observations of extended, flat rotation curves in [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] demanded that galaxies reside within massive, invisible dark matter halos. This realization led to the foundational two-stage theory of galaxy formation: collisionless dark matter halos assemble "bottom-up" via a [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], after which baryonic gas falls into these potential wells, shock-heats, and radiatively cools to form luminous galaxies. Concurrently, Toomre's merger hypothesis proposed that major mergers of disk galaxies could directly produce elliptical remnants. This established a framework where the morphological transformation and mass assembly of massive galaxies were viewed primarily through the lens of discrete, violent merger events. In the context of this hierarchical formation scenario, classical estimates suggest that systems like the Milky Way gathered the bulk of their mass roughly $8-10\text{ Gyr}$ ago [Combes et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.265..417C/abstract). However, this standard cold dark matter framework has faced persistent theoretical challenges, particularly concerning low surface brightness galaxies; here, dark matter models require extensive feedback hypotheses to match the predictive success achieved by Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) [McGaugh et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021SHPSA..88..220M/abstract).

As cosmological hydrodynamical simulations and deep multi-wavelength surveys matured, the purely merger-driven view of stellar mass growth encountered severe observational tensions. Measurements of high and sustained star formation rates at $z \sim 2-3$ required a continuous resupply of fuel that discrete mergers could not provide [Erb et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.430.1051C/abstract). While major mergers are highly efficient at triggering intense starbursts and fueling [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]], subsequent demographic analysis revealed that only about one-third of the present-day stellar mass in the Universe is directly attributable to merging events [Kaviraj et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609136K/abstract). 

Consequently, modern theory has synthesized these views into a hybrid paradigm where [[smooth-accretion|Smooth accretion]], rather than discrete merging, dominates the baryonic assembly of most galaxies. Multi-zoom cosmological simulations demonstrate that smooth, continuous gas accretion from the cosmic web accounts for approximately $77\%$ of the total mass assembly in typical galaxies, leaving major mergers to dominate only at the highest mass scales [L'Huillier et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...544A..68L/abstract). 

A defining theoretical breakthrough of the modern era was the recognition of bimodal gas accretion dynamics [Dave et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..219D/abstract). In this updated framework, accreting gas does not universally shock-heat to the halo's virial temperature. Instead, gas in low-mass halos and at high redshifts accretes primarily via a "cold mode" along dense cosmic filaments on dynamical timescales. Conversely, massive halos at lower redshifts are fed predominantly by a "hot mode" via cooling from a shock-heated circumgalactic reservoir. This continuous, bimodal accretion framework successfully resolves the gas consumption timescale paradox, explains the transition from highly turbulent, gas-rich disks at high redshift to secularly evolving spirals today, and provides the physical foundation for the [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]] of galactic disks.

## Modes of Gas Accretion: Cold vs. Hot Flows

The mass assembly and evolution of galaxies are heavily governed by a bimodal framework of [[smooth-accretion|Smooth accretion]], which is fundamentally divided by the thermodynamic state of the infalling intergalactic gas. Cosmological hydrodynamical [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]] demonstrate that gas enters dark matter halos via two distinct pathways: [[cold-gas-accretion|Cold gas accretion]] and [[hot-mode-accretion|Hot Mode Accretion]] [Dave et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..219D/abstract), [Woods et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442..732W/abstract). The dichotomy between these modes is primarily dictated by the ratio of the gas cooling time to the dynamical free-fall time of the halo ($t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff}$), which varies strongly as a function of halo mass and redshift.

**Cold-Mode Accretion**
In halos slightly smaller than the Milky Way and below, the gas cooling time is substantially shorter than the free-fall time. Consequently, gas flows from the cosmic web along dense, narrow filaments directly into the central regions of the halo, penetrating the environment without supporting a stable virial shock. This cold-mode gas typically maintains temperatures of $T < 10^5$ K, radiating its acquired gravitational energy via line emission on dynamical timescales [Dave et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..219D/abstract), [Woods et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442..732W/abstract). Because it supplies fuel efficiently and rapidly, cold-mode accretion dominates the global mass assembly of galaxies in the early Universe ($z > 2$), driving the prompt formation of massive [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] [Dave et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..219D/abstract). Furthermore, the turbulent, high-velocity influx of cold gas has been dynamically linked to the structural assembly of thick galactic disks [Noguchi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494L..37N/abstract), [Noguchi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190508993N/abstract). Importantly, the kinetic energy from this rapid mass inflow can drive fast turbulent motions in young galaxy disks, acting as the primary source of turbulence before stellar feedback becomes dominant [Elmegreen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...712..294E/abstract). Theoretical modeling demonstrates that converting just 20% of the accretion energy into random motion is sufficient to match the high velocity dispersions observed in high-redshift galaxies [Khochfar et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009RvMA...21...87K/abstract).

**Hot-Mode Accretion and Virialization Heating**
As halos grow more massive, the infalling gas encounters a stable accretion shock near the virial radius. The kinetic energy of the infall is dissipated, shock-heating the gas to near the halo's virial temperature [Dave et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..219D/abstract). This process of virialization heating creates a quasi-static, hot circumgalactic reservoir. Star formation must then be fueled by the gradual, subsonic cooling of this hot halo gas. Heating rates can be computed analytically using the extended Press-Schechter formalism, linking the thermodynamic state of the gas to the underlying dark matter merger tree without relying on instantaneous heating approximations [Wang et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007astro.ph..1363W/abstract), [Wang et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...672..752W/abstract). Hot-mode accretion overtakes cold streams to become the dominant pathway for mass assembly at low redshifts ($z \lesssim 1-2$). The slow, angular momentum-conserving condensation of this hot gas is theorized to govern the subsequent formation of extended thin disks [Noguchi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494L..37N/abstract).

**Evolutionary Implications and Observational Tensions**
The transition from cold to hot mode accretion represents a primary physical driver of morphological downsizing in [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|Galaxy and Universe evolution]] [Dave et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..219D/abstract). Once a halo crosses the transition mass, the cessation of rapid cold flows starves the galaxy of immediate fuel, reducing specific star formation rates and leaving the galaxy susceptible to durable [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] via [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] and stellar feedback [Noguchi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.4691N/abstract), [Woods et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442..732W/abstract).

Despite the robust theoretical consensus regarding this bimodality, there is an ongoing tension in the literature due to the lack of ubiquitous, direct observational evidence for cold accretion. As noted in observational reviews, directly detecting these diffuse gas flows remains exceptionally challenging [Kacprzak et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..145K/abstract). Current empirical support remains largely indirect, relying on kinematic signatures in background quasar absorption spectra [Bouché et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Sci...341...50B/abstract), localized metallicity drops in starbursting regions , and the necessity of continuous gas replenishment to match the observed [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and gas consumption timescales of local spirals [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430...67S/abstract).

## Stochastic Growth via Satellite and Stellar Accretion

While smooth gas accretion accounts for the majority ($\sim 77\%$) of the total mass assembled by galaxies across cosmic time, the stochastic arrival of mass via discrete merging events is a fundamental driver of structural transformation and [[ex-situ-galaxy-growth|Ex-situ galaxy growth]] [L'Huillier et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...544A..68L/abstract). In the standard [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] model, an initial period of rapid in-situ star formation is followed by a protracted phase of stochastic accretion, where galaxies grow through a hierarchical sequence of minor and [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] [Remus et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935...37R/abstract). Although only about one-third of the total stellar mass in the local Universe is directly attributable to merging [Kaviraj et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609136K/abstract), this process is primarily responsible for building the extended [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]] and central bulges of mature systems.

The continuous infall and tidal disruption of [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]] leaves long-lasting fossil records in the outskirts of their hosts. As these satellites orbit within the host's dark matter potential, tidal forces strip their dark matter, gas, and stars. The remnants of these accreted satellites produce kinematic over-densities in velocity space—such as stellar streams—which remain coherent significantly longer than spatial over-densities [Smith et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..420..113S/abstract). Observational studies tracking surface brightness profiles show that the majority of structural evolution in galaxy outskirts ($2\text{--}10 R_e$) between $z=1.1$ and $z=0.2$ is driven by this ongoing accretion of stellar material [Williams et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241203662W/abstract).

The relative contribution of this ex-situ growth is strongly mass-dependent. High-mass galaxies systematically exhibit larger accreted stellar mass fractions than low-mass galaxies [Remus et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935...37R/abstract). Furthermore, at fixed stellar mass, quiescent galaxies show a larger fractional increase in their ex-situ stellar populations over recent cosmic time compared to star-forming galaxies, suggesting that minor mergers are a dominant ongoing assembly mechanism for massive, passive systems [Williams et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241203662W/abstract). Satellites that avoid complete tidal disruption in the halo undergo orbital decay via dynamical friction. If a satellite is sufficiently dense and its mass is roughly $\sim 10\%$ of the primary disk mass, it can sink to the center and deposit its stars, contributing to the growth of a central bulge without completely destroying the pre-existing disk. During these violent merger and accretion events, expelled stellar and gaseous material can also fragment and self-gravitate, leading to the formation of Tidal Dwarf Galaxies—a notable mechanism for recycling shed mass back into new, collapsed structures within the intergalactic environment [Duc et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002Ap&SS.281..347D/abstract).

Globular cluster populations provide an independent, crucial diagnostic of this stochastic accretion history. The globular cluster systems of massive galaxies typically display a bimodal color distribution. Theoretical models and observations dictate that metal-rich (red) clusters form dissipatively in-situ during the early collapse or during gas-rich major mergers. In contrast, the metal-poor (blue) clusters are accreted dissipationlessly during the tidal disruption of smaller satellite galaxies [Beasley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.555..483B/abstract). The power of this diagnostic is starkly illustrated by compact relic galaxies like NGC 1277, which possesses a uniformly red globular cluster system; the complete absence of blue clusters observationally confirms that the galaxy has undergone virtually no stochastic accretion or minor mergers since its initial primordial collapse [Beasley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.555..483B/abstract).

## Structural Evolution and the Inside-Out Formation Scenario

The structural assembly of galaxies into distinct morphological components—such as thin disks, thick disks, and central bulges—is intimately linked to their gas accretion histories. Smooth, continuous accretion from the cosmic web is required to sustain observed star formation rates across cosmic time, simultaneously supplying the requisite [[angular-momentum-in-galaxy-formation|Angular Momentum in Galaxy Formation]] to support rotationally dominated structures. 

Spiral galaxies typically assemble via an [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]] scenario. In this paradigm, gas accretion time-scales are shortest in the central regions and progressively longer in the outer disk, driving a radial gradient in age and metallicity [Fraternali et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.2166F/abstract), [Nuza et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.3089N/abstract). Cold gas accretion plays a crucial role in providing the requisite angular momentum for extended thin disks, helping to solve the long-standing angular momentum problem in disk formation, whereas discrete mergers tend to aggravate it [Combes et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..390..369C/abstract). Furthermore, significant amounts of external gas accretion from cosmic filaments are required to explain the observed high frequency of galactic bars across cosmic time [Combes et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AIPC..783...43C/abstract).

Observations and chemical evolution models of the Milky Way suggest that the system acts as a "leaky accretion disk," wherein continuous gas inflow is balanced by local star formation and galactic outflows [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...34W/abstract). Governed by this steady-state equilibrium, the Milky Way has formed approximately two-thirds of its stellar mass since $z \approx 1$, while its total cold gas mass has remained remarkably constant over time [Fraternali et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.2166F/abstract). For instance, the Milky Way's present-day gas accretion rate is estimated at a few solar masses per year, which is sufficient to sustain its current observed star-formation rate without depleting its reservoir [Richter et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430...15R/abstract).

The bipartite nature of gas accretion—cold versus hot modes—provides a physical framework for the structural diversity of disks. Theoretical models suggest that geometrically thick, metal-poor, and older stellar disks form primarily from the rapid, direct accretion of cold gas streams in low-mass halos at high redshift [Noguchi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190508993N/abstract), [Noguchi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494L..37N/abstract). In contrast, dynamically colder thin disks form at later epochs as shock-heated gas in more massive halos gradually cools and settles with higher angular momentum [Noguchi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494L..37N/abstract). Furthermore, the concentrated star formation required to build central bulges is better explained by low-angular-momentum gas cooling directly from the hot halo; the accretion of satellite dwarf galaxies alone cannot reproduce the steep central accretion profiles required by systems like the Milky Way [Peek et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...698.1429P/abstract). Semi-empirical modeling further demonstrates that this bulge/disk bimodality is a direct consequence of two overarching halo accretion modes: central bulges form vigorously during an early, fast-growth phase of the halo, while extended disks assemble gradually during a later, slow-accretion phase [Boco et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954...97B/abstract).

As disks mature, their growth transitions from a violent, instability-driven phase to a period dominated by the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]. In this stage, continuous mass return from stellar populations and the radial transport of gas via gravitational torques help stabilize the disk against destruction from minor mergers, significantly moderating the overall bulge-to-disk ratio [Martig et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract), [Forbes et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.1552F/abstract). The ultimate morphological fate of a galaxy is thus dictated by the competition between smooth cosmic gas infall—which promotes extended [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]]—and merger-driven disruption. In massive halos, active galactic nuclei (AGN) feedback plays a critical role in enforcing this dichotomy. By shutting off the cooling of hot halo gas, AGN feedback prevents subsequent disk regrowth, thereby durably quenching the galaxy and "freezing" the elliptical morphology of post-merger remnants [Dubois et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.3948D/abstract).

## The Impact of Accretion on Star Formation Histories

The continuous accretion of fresh gas is the fundamental driver of a galaxy's [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]]. Observations of local spiral galaxies reveal typical [[gas-depletion-and-consumption|Gas Depletion and Consumption]] timescales of $\tau_{\rm SF} \equiv M_{\rm gas}/\dot{M}_\star \approx 1-5 \times 10^9$ yr. Because this timescale is significantly shorter than the Hubble time, galaxies must continuously replenish their cold gas reservoirs to avoid rapid quenching [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430...67S/abstract).

To explain these sustained star formation rates, theoretical models often employ an equilibrium formalism, where the inflow of gas balances star formation and outflows: $\dot{M}_{\rm in} \approx \dot{M}_\star + \dot{M}_{\rm out}$. Smooth gas accretion from the cosmic web is the dominant mass delivery mechanism, accounting for approximately $77\%$ of the total mass assembled in galaxies across cosmic time [L'Huillier et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...544A..68L/abstract). At $z \sim 1.5-3$, during the peak of the cosmic star formation history, the average gas accretion rate into massive galaxies is estimated to be $\dot{M} \approx 96 \pm 19 \ M_\odot \ {\rm yr}^{-1}$, directly fueling the majority of their intense star formation [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.430.1051C/abstract). 

As the apparent cosmic accretion rate onto massive halos declines exponentially toward $z=0$, models typically predict a corresponding decline in the global star formation rate [Fraternali et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.2166F/abstract), [Woods et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442..732W/abstract). However, recent measurements of H I and H$_2$ mass densities present a striking counter-perspective, suggesting that the cosmic gas accretion rate density has actually remained relatively constant from $z=5$ to $z=0$. This implies that the observed decline in the cosmic star formation rate may not be primarily driven by a simple lack of intergalactic fuel, but rather by internal regulatory processes [Spring et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471L.101S/abstract). Despite these competing views, the delicate balance between inflow and consumption continues to regulate [[equilibrium-star-formation-rates|Equilibrium star formation rates]] and dictate the inside-out assembly of galactic disks.

While smooth accretion drives steady disk growth, discrete and episodic accretion events—such as major mergers—destabilize the gas and drive it toward the galactic nucleus. This sudden influx dramatically reduces the local gas consumption timescale to $\tau_{\rm SF} \sim 10^7-10^8$ yr, triggering violent starbursts that can temporarily elevate the specific star formation rate by orders of magnitude [Combes et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..4121C/abstract), [Segovia Otero et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.516.2272S/abstract). Such events are pivotal in [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|Starburst Galaxy Evolution]] and the rapid buildup of central stellar bulges.

Accretion history also dictates a galaxy's late-stage evolution. Rather than undergoing a single, permanent quenching event, many galaxies experience long-term [[rejuvenation-episodes|Rejuvenation episodes]] on gigayear timescales. Modulated by the geometry of gas accretion at the halo outskirts and complex feedback cycles, these episodes allow galaxies to transition back onto the star-forming main sequence multiple times [Fortun\'e et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320858F/abstract). 

Despite the clear theoretical link between cosmological accretion and star formation, observational evidence at low redshift presents an ongoing tension. Theoretical models generally predict a strong positive correlation between dark matter halo accretion and star formation. However, observational constraints for isolated Milky Way-mass galaxies at $z \sim 0.1$ rule out a positive correlation between recent dark matter accretion and star formation activity with $\gtrsim 85\%$ confidence [O'Donnell et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.1253O/abstract), [O'Donnell et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.3285O/abstract). This discrepancy suggests that while fresh cosmic accretion is the primary driver of star formation at high redshift, internal processes such as stellar mass loss and gas recycling may dominate the fuel supply for star formation in massive galaxies in the local Universe [Martig et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract), [O'Donnell et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.3285O/abstract).

## Chemical Enrichment and the Fundamental Metallicity Relation

Galaxies do not evolve as closed boxes; rather, their [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|Galaxy chemical enrichment mechanisms]] are heavily regulated by the continuous exchange of mass with the intergalactic medium. The accretion of pristine or metal-poor gas from the cosmic web acts to dilute the metallicity, $Z$, of the interstellar medium while simultaneously supplying the raw fuel for ongoing star formation. This dynamic is typically described by the [[equilibrium-recycling-model|Equilibrium recycling model]] (often referred to as a "bathtub" model), which posits that galaxies live in a slowly evolving equilibrium state governed by a balance between mass inflows, star formation, and outflows [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract).

In this framework, the basic mass-balance driving chemical evolution can be approximated by a steady-state condition where the gas accretion rate, $\dot{M}_{\rm acc}$, balances the star formation rate (${\rm SFR}$) and the galactic outflow rate ($\dot{M}_{\rm out}$):
$$ \dot{M}_{\rm acc} \approx {\rm SFR} + \dot{M}_{\rm out} $$
Models employing this balance, often further constrained by assuming an outflow rate roughly equal to the ${\rm SFR}$, successfully reproduce the observed mass-metallicity relation of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] at $z \sim 2$ [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract). At the stellar level in the early Universe, the fundamental metallicity of ancient stars is also governed by accretion and feedback; for instance, metal accretion onto low-mass Population III stars is effectively prevented by their own stellar winds, ensuring they remain chemically pristine over cosmic time despite residing in increasingly enriched environments [Tanaka et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844..137T/abstract).

While the equilibrium state predicts a tight relation between stellar mass and metallicity, observed galaxies exhibit intrinsic scatter. At a fixed stellar mass, a galaxy's ${\rm SFR}$ is observed to be anticorrelated with its gas-phase metallicity—a defining feature of the Fundamental Metallicity Relation. This anticorrelation naturally emerges from the stochastic nature of gas accretion: a temporary enhancement in the infall of metal-poor gas dilutes the existing metal abundance while simultaneously increasing the gas fraction and fueling a corresponding spike in the ${\rm SFR}$ [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443..168F/abstract). Conversely, deviations that depress the ${\rm SFR}$ at a given mass are correlated with a lower gas fraction and a higher metallicity [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). 

The Fundamental Metallicity Relation and the scatter in these scaling relations provide strict quantitative constraints on galactic feedback and accretion physics. Theoretical modeling indicates that the galaxy-to-galaxy variation in the [[mass-loading-factor|Mass loading factor]] at a fixed mass must be remarkably small ($\lesssim 0.1$ dex) to reproduce the observed scatter in star formation and metallicity. This suggests that the intrinsic scatter in cosmic accretion rates onto star-forming galaxies is narrower than what is expected from pure dark matter $N$-body simulations alone [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443..168F/abstract). Recent spatially resolved studies utilizing MaNGA survey data further reveal that accretion rate histories vary systematically with mass and star formation state: more massive galaxies generally accreted more gas at higher redshifts, while star-forming galaxies at $z=0$ exhibit more persistent and continuous accretion histories regardless of their total mass [Camps-Fariña et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...678A..65C/abstract).

Furthermore, [[galaxy-gas-replenishment|Galaxy gas replenishment]] from previous stellar generations complicates the simple accretion-dilution picture. While metal-poor inflows drive the long-term mass growth and chemical dilution of the disc, the reaccretion of previously ejected, metal-enriched gas helps set the effective chemical yield of the system [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). Observationally, the direct signatures of these accretion-driven enrichment cycles manifest as localized metallicity drops in the starburst regions of galaxies and heavily influence the abundance of $\alpha$-enhanced gas observed in the local Universe .

## Accretion onto Galactic Nuclei and Supermassive Black Holes

The fueling of a central supermassive black hole links large-scale galaxy dynamics to sub-parsec physical processes within the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]]. The resultant [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] depends intricately on the rate, angular momentum, and geometry of gas inflows. Across a broad population of star-forming galaxies, the average black hole accretion rate correlates strongly with the host galaxy's global star formation rate [Chen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1401.2179C/abstract), suggesting that large-scale gas availability ultimately regulates both phenomena on long timescales. However, modeling the full history of black hole growth presents theoretical challenges; standard hierarchical models have historically over-predicted early black hole accretion while under-predicting late-time growth [Somerville et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..399..391S/abstract).

In the local Universe, most massive galactic nuclei—including Sgr A* in the Milky Way—are quiescent. While gas is present, the observed accretion luminosities satisfy $L_{\rm acc} \ll 0.1 \dot{M}_{\rm Bondi} c^2$, indicating that the inflow must proceed via a radiatively inefficient accretion flow (RIAF) or advection-dominated accretion flow (ADAF) [Narayan et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002luml.conf..405N/abstract), [Yuan et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006JPhCS..54..427Y/abstract). In these two-temperature collisionless plasmas, thermal energy is advected across the event horizon rather than radiated away. Three-dimensional hydrodynamical simulations of stellar wind accretion onto Sgr A* reveal that this low-rate fueling is highly variable on $10-100$ year timescales, producing cold gas clumps and filaments embedded in hot X-ray emitting gas [Cuadra et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006JPhCS..54..436C/abstract). However, a notable tension persists in the literature: the mass accretion rates predicted by multi-dimensional hydrodynamical simulations of the Galactic center are typically a factor of $10-20$ higher than the rates required by standard ADAF spectral models [Yuan et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...537..236Y/abstract). This discrepancy may be alleviated by invoking outer boundary conditions with very low specific angular momentum, which substantially alters the sonic radius and dynamics of the flow [Yuan et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...537..236Y/abstract).

At the opposite extreme, systems such as Ultraluminous X-ray sources (ULXs) emit X-ray luminosities often exceeding $10^{40}\text{ erg s}^{-1}$. Optical spectroscopic studies show that ULXs possess hot, optically thick outflows, marking them as a homogeneous class powered by supercritical accretion disks [Fabrika et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015NatPh..11..551F/abstract), [Fabrika et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASPC..510..395F/abstract). These supercritical accretion disks act as scaled-up analogs to the Galactic microquasar SS 433, demonstrating that black holes can substantially exceed the classical Eddington limit when fed at extreme rates [Fabrika et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160105971F/abstract).

Super-Eddington accretion is particularly relevant for explaining rapid black hole growth in the early Universe. The overmassive black holes recently observed at high redshift ($z > 6$) likely assembled their mass through short, repeated episodes of super-Eddington accretion triggered by major [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]] [Trinca et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241214248T/abstract). Semi-analytical models suggest these extreme bursts last between $0.5-3\text{ Myr}$ and operate with a duty cycle of just $1-4\%$ [Trinca et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241214248T/abstract). In the lower-redshift Universe ($z \lesssim 2$), [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]] continue to drive significant central inflows, enhancing specific black hole accretion rates for $\sim 1\text{ Gyr}$ after the initial encounter [Schechter et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...989..149S/abstract).

The characteristic timescales of central accretion vary significantly across the AGN population. While highly luminous quasars exhibit accretion timescales of $10^7-10^8\text{ yr}$, the timescales for less luminous AGN (with hard X-ray luminosities $< 10^{44}\text{ erg s}^{-1}$) are not fixed; instead, they depend on both the black hole mass and the mass of the accreted gas, dictated by specific angular momentum loss mechanisms within the circumnuclear torus or disk [Shirakata et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.4846S/abstract).

Beyond supermassive black holes, the interstellar medium also hosts isolated stellar-mass black holes that accrete gas directly from their surroundings. Models of low-rate accretion onto these isolated black holes indicate that adiabatic heating within collisionless flows is approximately 25% more efficient than in non-magnetized cases [Beskin et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...440..223B/abstract). Furthermore, magnetic field line reconnection within discrete current sheets during this isolated accretion can generate a hard, highly variable spectral component stretching from ultraviolet to gamma-ray energies [Beskin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AdSpR..42..523B/abstract). With advances in high-resolution astronomy, current facilities like Chandra and JWST now hold strong potential for detecting the electromagnetic signatures of wandering isolated black holes (such as OGLE-2011-BLG-0462) as they accrete from the warm neutral medium [Kimura et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250301172K/abstract).

## Environmental Influences and Cluster-Scale Accretion

The rate and physical mode of gas accretion onto galaxies are fundamentally dictated by the [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]]. While galaxies residing in cosmic voids or low-density filaments predominantly grow via the smooth, steady inflow of cold gas, mass assembly in dense groups and clusters is governed by deep gravitational potential wells and the presence of a hot, diffuse medium [Combes et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..4121C/abstract). As large dark matter halos collapse, gravitational shock heating raises the entropy of the infalling gas to levels where radiative cooling becomes highly inefficient. Consequently, galaxies migrating into these dense environments are starved of the fresh gas required to sustain star formation, a phenomenon that drives the differential evolution of specific star formation rates across cosmic time [Cen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741...99C/abstract).

**Cluster-Scale Accretion and the ICM**
[[intracluster-and-intragroup-phenomena|Intracluster and Intragroup Phenomena]] are driven by the continuous accretion of both diffuse gas and sub-halos from the surrounding cosmic web. As intergalactic gas falls into a massive cluster, it is decelerated and heated at an accretion shock, effectively marking its integration into the intracluster medium (ICM). While hydrodynamical simulations show an alignment between the accretion shock and the splashback radius, self-similar spherical collapse models reveal this alignment holds primarily for moderate mass accretion rates and an adiabatic index of $\gamma \approx 5/3$ [Shi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461.1804S/abstract). Another prominent byproduct of this cluster-scale accretion is the generation of Intracluster Light (ICL). As tidal forces strip stars from infalling host galaxies, this material accumulates into extended, diffuse halos that permeate the intracluster environment [Mihos et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..317...27M/abstract).

The global mass accretion rates of galaxy clusters govern their outer structure and can break the self-similarity of the ICM in cluster outskirts, leading to a $\sim 10\%$ departure in the gas-to-dark matter density ratio compared to the cosmic mean [Lau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806...68L/abstract). Observationally, cluster mass accretion rates can now be constrained to within $33\%$ using a combination of X-ray surface brightness asymmetries and thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich data, independent of fully relaxed [[galaxy-cluster-mass-profiles|Galaxy-cluster mass profiles]] [Soltis et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985..212S/abstract).

**Kinematics and Entropy Production**
The dynamics of the infalling gas reflect the complex interplay of cosmic accretion and cluster-scale feedback. In cluster outskirts, gas velocity distributions are markedly asymmetric and double-peaked, directly tracing the kinematic signature of cosmic accretion, whereas central cluster regions exhibit more balanced, Gaussian inflow and outflow velocities [Ayromlou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A..20A/abstract). The velocity structure function of this multiphase gas generally follows a Kolmogorov-like slope of $\sim 1/3$, except in the innermost core regions where feedback heavily disrupts the flow [Ayromlou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A..20A/abstract). 

The entropy of the accreted gas is further modified by internal feedback mechanisms. For instance, galactic outflows can smooth the density distribution of accreting baryons, boosting entropy production at the accretion shock by factors of $2$ to $4$ at radii near $r_{500}$ [Voit et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...594L..75V/abstract). Such models also indicate that baryon accretion onto lower-mass groups is comparatively smoother than the highly turbulent accretion onto massive clusters [Voit et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...594L..75V/abstract).

**Environmental Stripping and Quenching**
For individual [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] orbiting within these structures, the hostile cluster environment actively destroys their existing gas reservoirs. The relative motion between a satellite and the dense ICM generates a strong hydrodynamical drag force. When this drag exceeds the satellite's gravitational restoring force, its cold interstellar gas is physically ablated through ram-pressure stripping. Alongside tidal stripping and strangulation—the removal of the extended circumgalactic gas reservoir—these environmental processes efficiently truncate any further gas accretion onto the satellite [Cen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741...99C/abstract). The combination of shock-heated environmental gas precluding new accretion and active stripping removing existing fuel rapidly drives [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]], transforming star-forming disk galaxies into passive spheroids as they sink toward the cluster core.

## Regulation and Feedback in the Baryonic Cycle

Galaxy evolution is governed by a slowly evolving equilibrium between the accretion of intergalactic gas, star formation, and feedback-driven ejection [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). The continuous supply of fresh gas into the interstellar medium dictates the available [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]], but without regulation, galaxies would rapidly exhaust their gas reservoirs in massive starbursts or succumb to the catastrophic overcooling of halo gas [Dave et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..219D/abstract).

In low-mass and typical disk galaxies, self-regulation is primarily achieved via mechanical energy from supernovae. This stellar feedback drives [[galaxy-outflows|Galaxy outflows]], cycling gas from the interstellar medium into the circumgalactic medium and preventing excessive early star formation [Woods et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442..732W/abstract). The efficiency of these mass-loss processes is frequently parameterized by the [[mass-loading-factor|Mass loading factor]], $\eta = \dot{M}_{\rm out} / \dot{M}_\star$. Chemical evolution models of the Milky Way and nearby disk galaxies suggest that a mass loading factor of $\eta \approx 0-2$ successfully reproduces observed radial metallicity profiles [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...34W/abstract). Furthermore, constraints on the fundamental metallicity relation indicate that the intrinsic scatter in the mass loading factor at fixed mass is remarkably small, $\lesssim 0.1$ dex [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443..168F/abstract). The competition between gravitational instability and this mechanical feedback helps maintain a nearly constant atomic hydrogen surface density across a wide variety of galaxies [Chakraborti et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732..105C/abstract).

In high-mass systems (halo masses $\gtrsim 10^{12} M_\odot$), stellar feedback is insufficient to unbind gas from deeper potential wells. Here, the energetic output from central supermassive black holes—governed by [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]]—becomes the dominant regulatory mechanism. Furthermore, stellar and AGN feedback are often fundamentally coupled via strong interaction terms; these combined feedback mechanisms regulate the cold gas available for black hole accretion and heavily influence the overall baryon cycling efficiency of the galaxy [Iyer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250821152I/abstract). Cosmological simulations demonstrate that AGN feedback is critical for durably suppressing quiescent star formation and preventing the reformation of gas-rich discs after major mergers, thereby establishing the morphological sequence of massive elliptical galaxies [Dubois et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.3948D/abstract). 

Traditionally, this feedback-driven suppression of accretion was viewed as a permanent transition, abruptly moving systems into a passive state via [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]]. However, recent hydrodynamical simulations indicate that a large fraction of field galaxies actually undergo continuous, gigayear-timescale cycles of quenching and [[rejuvenation-episodes|Rejuvenation episodes]], driven by the fluctuating geometry of gas accretion at the halo outskirts [Fortun\'e et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320858F/abstract). Furthermore, the literature remains conflicted over the primary driver of massive galaxy quenching; semi-empirical models reveal a significant physical degeneracy between the disruptive effects of AGN feedback (often tied empirically to the $M_{\rm BH} - \sigma_\star$ relation) and "halo quenching" mechanisms governed purely by the thermal state and mass of the parent dark matter halo [Fu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251026305F/abstract). Recent morphological studies provide additional insight into these transformations, demonstrating that kinematic morphology (as measured by normalized specific angular momentum) exhibits a universal bimodality across all star-formation states. This indicates that galaxy quenching ultimately proceeds via divergent evolutionary pathways, distinct for rotationally supported cold disks versus pressure-supported hot spheroids [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..165W/abstract).

## Observational Evidence and Tracers of Inflow

Direct observation of continuous gas accretion from the intergalactic medium is notoriously difficult, as the inflowing gas is typically diffuse, multi-phase, and has a low emission measure. While cold-mode accretion is a robust prediction of cosmological simulations, ubiquitous direct detection remains elusive, meaning that evidence for mass inflow largely relies on a combination of indirect multi-wavelength tracers, kinematic modeling, and chemical signatures [Kacprzak et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..145K/abstract).

**Gas Kinematics and Absorption Lines**
One of the most effective methods for directly probing gas inflows utilizes background quasar absorption lines to map the circumgalactic medium of foreground galaxies. Kinematic modeling of these absorption features has successfully isolated the distinct signatures of cool, infalling gas delivering angular momentum to star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 2.3$, where the estimated mass accretion rate is found to be comparable to the star formation rate [Bouché et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Sci...341...50B/abstract). Additionally, theoretical studies suggest that large-scale Kelvin-Helmholtz turbulence at the disk-halo interface could serve as an indirect observational probe of coplanar gas accretion onto galaxy disks [Goldman et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.2949G/abstract). 

In the local Universe, deep 21-cm radio observations of [[h-i-rich-galaxies|H I-rich galaxies]] reveal morphological and kinematic signatures of neutral hydrogen (H I) accretion. Galaxies such as NGC 3359 and NGC 4565 exhibit asymmetric gas kinematics and extra-planar H I structures indicative of ongoing, low-level gas infall [van der Hulst et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..331..139V/abstract). Beyond H I mapping, asymmetric structural features such as lopsidedness, large-scale warps, and polar rings serve as robust kinematic and morphological tracers of external gas accretion funneling in from cosmic filaments [Combes et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319...57C/abstract). To extend these studies into deeper cosmic time, the upcoming Optical, Radio Continuum and HI Deep Spectroscopic Survey (ORCHIDSS) will serve as a key facility for tracing the evolution of neutral hydrogen beyond $z > 0.25$, aiming to robustly map the gas-consumption cycle and accretion history across a broader span of cosmic history [Duncan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Msngr.190...25D/abstract).

**Chemical Signatures and Metallicity Drops**
The accretion of pristine or metal-poor gas from the cosmic web leaves a distinct chemical imprint on the host galaxy. Observations show a fundamental anticorrelation between the star formation rate and gas-phase metallicity at a fixed stellar mass. Theoretical frameworks suggest this relationship is driven by the stochastic inflow of metal-poor gas, which simultaneously dilutes the interstellar medium and fuels new star formation [Forbes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443..168F/abstract). On spatially resolved scales, localized metallicity drops are frequently observed in the starburst regions of dwarf and spiral galaxies. These localized chemical dilutions provide strong indirect evidence that massive star-forming knots can be fueled by recently accreted cosmic gas rather than well-mixed, metal-enriched local material [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430...67S/abstract), .

**Stellar Halos and Globular Clusters**
While gas tracers probe smooth accretion, the hierarchical assembly of galaxies via the merging of minor satellites is recorded in [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]]. [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]] are disrupted by tidal forces, leaving behind [[stellar-substructure|Stellar substructure]] in the form of tidal streams. Because kinematic overdensities in velocity space remain coherent much longer than spatial features, large-scale astrometric and spectroscopic surveys are highly effective at identifying these accretion relics [Smith et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..420..113S/abstract). 

Globular cluster populations serve as another crucial fossil record of a galaxy's accretion history. The color bimodality observed in many massive galaxies reflects a dual formation pathway: metal-rich (red) clusters form *in situ* during the initial dissipative collapse, whereas metal-poor (blue) clusters are accreted later alongside infalling satellite galaxies. As discussed previously, this paradigm is powerfully confirmed by massive relic galaxies like NGC 1277; its purely red globular cluster system serves as direct observational proof that it has undergone virtually no mass accretion since its initial formation [Beasley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.555..483B/abstract).

**Multi-wavelength Tracers of Co-evolution**
Finally, the influx of gas regulates not only the stellar disk but also [[galaxy-black-hole-assembly|Galaxy-black hole assembly]]. Infrared and X-ray surveys reveal a strong linear correlation between the average supermassive black hole accretion rate and the host galaxy's star formation rate [Chen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1401.2179C/abstract). This synchronicity indicates that the overarching cosmological accretion processes govern both the buildup of the galaxy and the growth of the central black hole on long timescales [Jones et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881..110J/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions in Accretion Physics

Despite the theoretical success of hierarchical assembly, observational validation of gas accretion models remains fundamentally constrained by the "missing baryon" problem. Cosmological mass inventories indicate that more than 50% of the baryonic material in the local Universe is unaccounted for in collapsed structures, residing instead in the diffuse intergalactic medium. Understanding how this reservoir cycles into galaxies via accretion and is subsequently expelled constitutes a major frontier in astrophysics. While [[cold-gas-accretion|Cold gas accretion]] along cosmic filaments is a robust prediction of cosmological simulations, ubiquitous direct observational detection remains elusive [Kacprzak et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..145K/abstract). Current empirical evidence relies predominantly on indirect kinematic signatures in background quasar absorption spectra—which trace gas delivering both mass and angular momentum to the host halo [Bouché et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Sci...341...50B/abstract)—or on metallicity drops in starburst regions [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430...67S/abstract), .

Resolving the physical state of accreting gas requires probing the epoch where cold flows are predicted to dominate. Next-generation facilities, particularly JWST, are poised to revolutionize the study of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. High-redshift galaxies frequently exhibit extraordinary star formation rates that demand continuous, rapid gas replenishment [Erb et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract), [Papovich et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412.1123P/abstract). Observations with JWST are expected to map the cooling pathways of this accreted multi-phase gas, explicitly testing the role of warm molecular hydrogen ($H_2$) line emission driven by turbulent dissipation from large-scale accretion and feedback [Guillard et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015sf2a.conf...81G/abstract). Furthermore, early JWST observations have revealed a ubiquitous population of overmassive accreting black holes in the early Universe. Theoretical models suggest these systems undergo episodic, super-Eddington accretion bursts triggered by major mergers, reaching high specific black hole accretion rates before transitioning to secular growth [Trinca et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241214248T/abstract), [Schechter et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250701092S/abstract).

A significant theoretical tension lies in disentangling the regulatory mechanisms that halt gas accretion, ultimately leading to [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]]. Current literature highlights a profound degeneracy between AGN-driven feedback and halo-mass-driven quenching mechanisms. Semi-empirical models demonstrate that applying a simple quenching threshold based on the observed $M_{\rm BH} - \sigma_\star$ relation can reproduce the quenched fraction of galaxies just as effectively as models invoking halo shock-heating [Fu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251026305F/abstract). Unraveling whether massive galaxies cease accreting cold gas strictly because the halo reaches a critical transition mass threshold, typically around $M_{\rm vir} \sim 10^{12.5} M_\odot$, or because AGN outflows physically disrupt the incoming filaments remains an actively debated question [Noguchi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.4691N/abstract), [Dubois et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.3948D/abstract). 

Finally, the fossil record of accretion histories encoded in the [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]] of nearby galaxies offers a complementary constraint to direct high-redshift observations. The morphology, kinematics, and chemical abundances of [[accreted-stellar-populations|Accreted stellar populations]] and extragalactic globular clusters reflect the cumulative history of hierarchical merging and smooth accretion. Exploring [[subhalo-physics-and-galactic-substructure|Subhalo Physics and Galactic Substructure]]—such as coherent phase-space streams and the spatial distribution of accreted versus in-situ stellar components—enables the precise reconstruction of progenitor masses and accretion timelines [Apfel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250705239A/abstract), [Williams et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241203662W/abstract). Validating these localized, near-field accretion records against the predictions of large-volume cosmological simulations is essential for formulating a unified theory of galaxy assembly across all mass scales and environments.
