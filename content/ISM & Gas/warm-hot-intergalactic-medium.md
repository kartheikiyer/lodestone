The **Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM)** is a tenuous, highly ionized [[hot-astrophysical-plasma|Hot Astrophysical Plasma]] with temperatures ranging between $10^5$ and $10^7$ K that permeates the [[cosmic-and-molecular-filaments|filamentary structures]] of the cosmic web. At high redshifts, the universe's baryonic matter is mostly accounted for in the photoionized intergalactic medium and dense galactic structures. However, in the low-redshift universe ($z \lesssim 2$), the observed mass of stars, cold gas, and cluster plasma falls significantly short of the cosmological baryon budget. Cosmological hydrodynamic simulations resolve this "[[hot-gas-mass-fraction|missing baryon problem]]" by predicting that 30% to 50% of the modern universe's baryons reside within the WHIM [Davé et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...552..473D/abstract) [Meiksin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009RvMP...81.1405M/abstract). This vast reservoir is generated primarily by supersonic gravitational [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|shock heating]]; as [[low-density-gas|diffuse gas]] collapses into [[cosmic-and-molecular-filaments|Cosmic and Molecular Filaments]], the kinetic energy of the infalling material is converted into thermal energy, fundamentally altering the [[temperature-density-relation|Temperature-density relation]] of the intergalactic medium [Davé et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.11518D/abstract).

Because of its extremely low density and high ionization state, the WHIM is notoriously difficult to observe directly. Its presence is most reliably traced through ultraviolet and X-ray absorption line spectroscopy of bright background quasars, where [[highly-ionized-inter-stellar-media|highly ionized metals]] such as O VI, O VII, and O VIII imprint their absorption signatures onto traversing light [Diaferio et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008SSRv..134....7D/abstract). More recently, astronomers have also constrained the thermal and pressure profiles of the WHIM using the thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect and statistical analyses of [[diffuse-x-ray-emitting-gas|Diffuse X-ray emitting gas]]. These observations confirm that the intergalactic medium is not merely a pristine, primordial gas, but a complex environment that has been chemically enriched over cosmic time. Heavy elements are transported into the WHIM via supernovae-driven winds and active galactic nuclei outflows, processes that also inject [[extragalactic-cosmic-rays|cosmic rays]] and [[magnetized-intergalactic-matter|Magnetized intergalactic matter]] into the cosmic web. 

This article details the physical formation and evolutionary history of the WHIM, tracing its development from the [[epoch-of-reionization|Epoch of Reionization]] to the modern shock-heated universe. It explores the diverse observational techniques used to detect this hidden mass and examines the highly dynamic transition zone between the intergalactic filaments and the denser [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] of individual galaxies. Finally, the article highlights the critical open questions remaining in the field—such as the relative importance of non-gravitational [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|Heating mechanisms (astrophysics)]] and the ongoing challenge of distinguishing the true intergalactic WHIM from bound galactic coronas—and discusses how next-generation X-ray microcalorimeters and fast radio burst surveys will definitively map these missing baryons in the coming decades.

## The Missing Baryon Problem and the Low-Redshift Universe

Observations of the high-redshift universe ($z \sim 3$), primarily through the absorption signatures of the Ly$\alpha$ forest, successfully account for the expected cosmic baryon density. However, in the low-redshift universe ($z \lesssim 2$), the combined mass of observed baryons—residing in stars, [[cold-neutral-medium|cold neutral gas]], and [[diffuse-x-ray-emitting-gas|X-ray emitting cluster gas]]—accounts for only roughly half of the predicted cosmological baryon budget. Cosmological hydrodynamic simulations consistently resolve this "[[hot-gas-mass-fraction|missing baryon problem]]" by predicting that a massive fraction of the Universe's baryons reside in the WHIM [Davé et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...552..473D/abstract), [Meiksin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009RvMP...81.1405M/abstract), [Génova-Santos et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806..113G/abstract). This diffuse [[hot-astrophysical-plasma|Hot Astrophysical Plasma]] is distributed along the extensive [[cosmic-and-molecular-filaments|filamentary structures]] of the cosmic web, typically occupying a median [[low-density-gas|overdensity]] of $10-30$ relative to the mean cosmic density [Davé et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...552..473D/abstract).

The WHIM is characterized by [[highly-ionized-inter-stellar-media|highly ionized gas]] with temperatures spanning $10^5 \lesssim T \lesssim 10^7$ K. The primary mechanism driving [[nearby-intergalactic-gas|intergalactic gas]] into this phase is [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|shock heating]] from gravitational collapse. As gas falls into mildly non-linear structures and dark matter filaments, the cooling time of the low-density plasma exceeds the Hubble time, allowing it to retain its shock-heated thermal energy [Davé et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...552..473D/abstract), [Diaferio et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008SSRv..134....7D/abstract). However, the exact contribution of non-gravitational heating mechanisms remains an active area of debate. While simulations universally agree on the WHIM's existence, the global impact of active galactic nucleus (AGN) and supernova feedback on the thermodynamical properties of the intergalactic medium is disputed, though it is generally considered modest compared to shock heating [Gheller et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..981G/abstract). Furthermore, observations of [[super-virial-temperature-gas|Super-virial temperature gas]] ($T > 10^7$ K) in the local [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] pose a specific challenge to pure gravitational heating models, indicating that complex feedback outbursts or non-thermal processes may be required to explain the highest-temperature components of the missing baryons [Das et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...918...83D/abstract).

Historically, the WHIM was considered extremely difficult to detect due to its low density and high ionization state. However, recent observational campaigns have moved past marginal detections to robust empirical confirmations. High-confidence X-ray absorption studies utilizing highly ionized metals, particularly O VII and O VIII, have successfully traced the WHIM in [[extragalactic-phenomena|large-scale structures]]. A notable example is the detection of O VII K$\alpha$ absorption associated with the Sculptor Wall, providing direct evidence that the WHIM traces the filamentary cosmic web [Buote et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...695.1351B/abstract). 

In addition to X-ray spectroscopy, metallicity-independent probes using the thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect (tSZE) have cross-correlated cosmic microwave background maps with galaxy catalogs. These tSZE measurements confirm a power-law [[temperature-density-relation|pressure-density relation]] consistent with shock-heated WHIM gas [Lim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.4017L/abstract). Such constraints indicate that the average IGM temperature ranges from $\sim 10^4$ K in mean-density regions to $>10^6$ K in overdense filaments, independently verifying that a significant portion of the low-redshift baryon budget is locked in the warm-hot cosmic web [Génova-Santos et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806..113G/abstract), [Lim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.4017L/abstract).

## Formation Physics: Shock Heating and the Cosmic Web

Following [[recombination-cosmology|Recombination (cosmology)]], the diffuse intergalactic gas cooled adiabatically as the Universe expanded. During the [[epoch-of-reionization|Epoch of Reionization]], the gas was re-heated to $\sim 10^4$ K by [[photoionization-and-radiative-heating|Photoionization and Radiative Heating]], establishing a baseline [[temperature-density-relation|Temperature-density relation]] governed by the general radiative processes, heating, and cooling mechanisms that dictate the physics of the [[nearby-intergalactic-gas|intergalactic medium]] [Draine et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011piim.book.....D/abstract). At even earlier times, photoionization instabilities could have created early structure in the [[ambient-medium|pre-galactic medium]] on scales larger than the photon path lengths [Hogan et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992Natur.359...40H/abstract). This early thermal history leaves a "thermal memory" in the low-density intergalactic medium, parameterized as a power-law equation of state:
$$ T = T_0(1 + \delta)^{1 - \gamma} $$
where $\delta$ is the baryonic overdensity and $\gamma \approx 1.5$ [Davies et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489..977D/abstract). Because the exponent $1 - \gamma$ is negative, this specific parameterization implies that denser regions of the intergalactic gas can be correspondingly cooler [Davies et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489..977D/abstract). 

At redshifts $z < 2$, the thermodynamic evolution of the intergalactic medium departs significantly from this photoionized equilibrium. As dark matter halos grow and merge to form the [[cosmic-and-molecular-filaments|filamentary cosmic web]], baryons are pulled into these continuously deepening potential wells. When the infalling gas encounters the mildly non-linear structures of the cosmic web—such as filaments and sheets—it undergoes supersonic compression. In these regions, the local gas density is sufficiently low that the radiative cooling time exceeds the Hubble time. Consequently, the kinetic energy of the collapsing gas cannot be efficiently radiated away, and the gas undergoes extensive [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|shock heating]] [Davé et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...552..473D/abstract), [Diaferio et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008SSRv..134....7D/abstract).

This gravitational shock heating drives the gas temperatures up to $10^5 - 10^7$ K, transforming the cool intergalactic gas into the WHIM. Cosmological hydrodynamic simulations consistently predict that this mechanism is so ubiquitous that the WHIM ultimately comes to serve as the primary reservoir for the "[[hot-gas-mass-fraction|missing baryons]]" in the local Universe [Davé et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...552..473D/abstract), [Meiksin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009RvMP...81.1405M/abstract), [Génova-Santos et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806..113G/abstract). When modeling these diffuse components analytically to describe their distribution, halo models require a scale-dependent bias for the diffuse gas component to accurately describe WHIM clustering [Fedeli et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014JCAP...08..028F/abstract). 

The resulting shock-heated gas exhibits a modified [[temperature-density-relation|Temperature-density relation]]. The WHIM resides in structures with a median overdensity around $10 - 30$ relative to the mean cosmic density [Davé et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...552..473D/abstract). Thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect cross-correlation measurements verify this strong environmental dependence: gas residing in regions of mean cosmic density ($\delta \sim 1$) maintains temperatures around $10^4$ K, but temperatures steepen sharply to $\sim 10^5$ K at overdensities of $10$, and exceed $10^6$ K in denser environments where overdensities reach $100$ [Lim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.4017L/abstract). 

While gravitational collapse is universally recognized as the dominant heating mechanism for the WHIM, the contribution of non-gravitational processes—such as galactic winds, supernovae, and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback—adds complexity to the cosmic baryon budget. Simulations indicate that the global thermodynamic properties of the large-scale WHIM are only modestly affected by feedback [Davé et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...552..473D/abstract), [Gheller et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..981G/abstract). However, feedback is crucial for understanding the chemical enrichment of the filaments and plays a highly significant role in the denser, multiphase gas of the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]], where extreme feedback can drive localized pockets of gas to super-virial temperatures [Johnson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884L..31J/abstract), [Das et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...918...83D/abstract). In addition to gravitational shocks and standard galactic feedback, some models also suggest that neutrino decay may act as a possible non-standard heating source for the IGM gas, particularly in massive cluster environments [Chan et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...658..859C/abstract).

## Observational Tracers: Absorption Line Spectroscopy

Because the WHIM consists of a diffuse, [[low-density-gas|low-density plasma]], it is notoriously difficult to map directly in emission. Consequently, the primary method for detecting and characterizing the WHIM is through absorption line spectroscopy of bright background sources, particularly active galactic nuclei and quasars. As these background photons traverse the cosmic web, the intervening WHIM leaves absorption signatures corresponding to [[highly-ionized-inter-stellar-media|highly ionized metals]] and [[neutral-interstellar-medium|neutral hydrogen]].

At the cooler end of the WHIM temperature range ($T \sim 10^5 - 10^{5.5} \text{ K}$), the gas is most effectively probed in the far-ultraviolet (FUV) band. The O VI doublet is a critical tracer of this phase, providing robust observational support for the presence of [[hot-gas-mass-fraction|warm-hot baryons]] in the intergalactic medium [Davé et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...552..473D/abstract). Additionally, broad H I Lyman-$\alpha$ absorption systems, which exhibit Doppler widths of $b = 40-80 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, offer a valuable metallicity-independent probe of the WHIM at temperatures of $T \lesssim 4 \times 10^5 \text{ K}$ [Johnson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884L..31J/abstract). This is complemented by observations of low-brightness H I detections in the local universe that likely represent components of the filamentary cosmic web [Popping et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhDT.......260P/abstract).

For the [[hot-gas|hot phase]] of the WHIM ($T > 10^6 \text{ K}$), hydrogen and lower-ionization metals are almost completely stripped of electrons. In this regime, researchers must rely on soft X-ray spectroscopy. The most reliable tracers of this hot phase are the K$\alpha$ transitions of He-like oxygen (O VII) and H-like oxygen (O VIII) [Bogdán et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953...42B/abstract). While Ne IX absorption is also observed in soft X-ray studies, it specifically traces the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|hot halo]] and [[cosmic-media-and-interstellar-medium|interstellar medium]] of the Milky Way rather than the broader cosmological WHIM [Yao et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...624..751Y/abstract). Because these highly ionized states peak in abundance at typical WHIM temperatures, their absorption lines encode critical information about the ionization state, temperature, and column density of the hidden baryons. 

The transition from marginal hints to high-confidence detections of the X-ray WHIM has been achieved through simultaneous observations using high-resolution grating spectrometers on missions like *Chandra* and *XMM-Newton*. A landmark result in this domain was the $3\sigma$ detection of redshifted O VII K$\alpha$ absorption toward the blazar H 2356-309. This absorber is spatially associated with the Sculptor Wall—a large-scale supercluster filament—and exhibits an O VII column density of $N \gtrsim 10^{16} \text{ cm}^{-2}$, consistent with the filamentary WHIM predicted by cosmological simulations [Buote et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...695.1351B/abstract). To further aid in mapping these structures, WHIM signatures can be predicted and traced spatially in the local universe by mapping the density field of galaxy light under the assumption of local, monotonic biasing [Viel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.360.1110V/abstract).

Despite these successes, interpreting WHIM absorption spectra is complicated by the challenge of distinguishing true intergalactic filaments from bound halo gas. A persistent debate concerns the origin of $z=0$ highly ionized absorption systems observed along numerous sightlines—whether they trace a hot, extended [[warm-corona|Galactic corona]] or a broader [[nearby-intergalactic-gas|intragroup medium]] of the Local Group (see Section: The IGM-CGM Interface) [Williams et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ESASP.604E...2W/abstract). Similar biases complicate other low-redshift detections, as absorbers originally attributed to the diffuse filamentary WHIM are sometimes shown to arise from the denser intragroup medium of intervening galaxy groups [Johnson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884L..31J/abstract). Studies explain that the intracluster medium in spiral-rich galaxy groups is significantly multiphased and can be reconciled with Ly$\alpha$ forest absorption measurements around galaxies [Zhan et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...592L...1Z/abstract). Furthermore, resonant scattering in bright X-ray lines can cause surface brightness distortions in the hot intergalactic medium, providing an important diagnostic tool to study gas velocity fields across these structures [Churazov et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010SSRv..157..193C/abstract).

## Emission Studies and the Soft X-ray Background

Gas in the WHIM cools primarily through thermal bremsstrahlung and collisionally excited metal line emission in the soft X-ray regime. However, because the WHIM occupies regions of [[low-density-gas|low overdensity]] ($10 \lesssim \delta \lesssim 30$), the surface brightness of this diffuse emission is exceedingly faint, making direct imaging of the [[cosmic-and-molecular-filaments|cosmic web]] a profound observational challenge.

Historically, the WHIM was considered too diffuse to detect in direct emission, prompting researchers to search for its signature statistically within the unresolved soft X-ray background. By cross-correlating the [[diffuse-x-ray-emitting-gas|Diffuse X-ray emitting gas]] observed by ROSAT and XMM-Newton with large-scale galaxy distributions, studies have detected an extended X-ray excess around galaxies that extends up to 1.5 Mpc [Sołtan et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...436...67S/abstract). This excess constitutes $1.3 \pm 0.2 \%$ of the total background flux in the 0.3-0.5 keV band, with a spectrum well-described by thermal bremsstrahlung at $kT \lesssim 0.5$ keV—consistent with theoretical predictions for the WHIM [Sołtan et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...436...67S/abstract). At cooler boundary temperatures, far-ultraviolet (FUV) spectroscopy from experiments like the Diffuse Ultraviolet Experiment (DUVE) provides essential constraints on the emission parameters of [[warm-ionized-medium|interstellar plasma]] interacting at the WHIM interface [Korpela et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...495..317K/abstract). Moreover, non-gravitational feedback heating leaves unique signatures in the soft X-ray background emissivity, allowing for measurements of the energy balance between feedback and gravity [Zhang et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...588..704Z/abstract). Similar localized thermal emission ($kT < 1$ keV) has also been correlated with [[extragalactic-phenomena|large-scale galaxy structures]] at $z \sim 0.1 - 0.45$ [Zappacosta et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph..1202Z/abstract).

Isolating this faint extragalactic signal is heavily complicated by local foregrounds. For instance, roughly $40\%$ of the local $1/4$ keV X-ray flux is generated by solar wind charge exchange within the heliosphere, with the remainder largely originating from the Local Hot Bubble [Galeazzi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.512..171G/abstract). Despite these bright foregrounds, recent analytical advances have yielded high-confidence indirect detections of the WHIM in emission. By applying power spectrum analysis to XMM-Newton and eROSITA data, researchers have successfully separated the spatial fluctuation signatures of filamentary WHIM gas from other X-ray background sources [Cerini et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230714411C/abstract). Recent breakthroughs have also occurred at other wavelengths; for instance, direct images obtained by the Condor Array Telescope have successfully mapped the cosmic web by detecting Lyman-$\alpha$ emission and absorption from cosmic dust within intergalactic filaments [Lanzetta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241210081L/abstract).

In denser environments, the WHIM is also thought to contribute to the soft X-ray excess (0.2-1 keV) frequently observed above the [[hot-gas|intracluster medium]] in galaxy clusters. Cosmological simulations suggest that this excess is driven by a combination of dense clumps in the warm [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] and the diffuse WHIM filaments that connect to the cluster outskirts, particularly extending beyond the [[mean-galactic-virial-temperature|virial radius]] [Gouin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250618459G/abstract). Correspondingly, prominent galaxy bridges connecting major clusters, such as those observed in the Shapley Supercluster, serve as primary targets for identifying the WHIM in X-ray emission [Ursino et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806..211U/abstract). Simulations of metal-line emission from the circumgalactic medium and WHIM predict that the brightest emission (such as C IV and O VI) closely follows the fundamental filamentary structure of the halo, extending up to 120 kpc at low redshift [Corlies et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827..148C/abstract). For example, HaloSat observations of the full Virgo Intracluster Medium measured a temperature of $kT = 1.39$ keV, illustrating the thermal state of the massive, dense nodes connecting to the WHIM [Hewitt et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021RNAAS...5..185H/abstract).

Definitively mapping the cosmic web in emission requires isolating specific diagnostic lines against the bright Milky Way foreground. Current X-ray CCDs lack the spectral resolution necessary to disentangle these components. Consequently, future missions featuring large-area X-ray microcalorimeters are required. With anticipated spectral resolutions of 1-2 eV, these instruments will be capable of directly mapping O VII and O VIII emission in the WHIM and [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|extended galactic halos]] out to $R_{500}$ or $R_{200}$, enabling a precise kinematic and thermodynamic census of the universe's missing baryons [Kraft et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221109827K/abstract), [Schellenberger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969...85S/abstract).

## Thermal Probes via the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich Effect

The thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (tSZE) effect provides a powerful, metallicity-independent method to probe the integrated pressure and thermal energy of the WHIM. Arising from the inverse Compton scattering of cosmic microwave background (CMB) photons by [[hot-gas|hot electrons]], the tSZE signal—characterized by the Compton $y$ parameter—is directly proportional to the line-of-sight integral of the electron pressure.

Because the diffuse WHIM produces a much fainter tSZE signal compared to the dense intracluster medium, its detection requires cross-correlating maps of CMB anisotropies (such as those from the *Planck* and *WMAP* satellites) with [[extragalactic-phenomena|large-scale structure]] templates derived from galaxy catalogs [Suarez-Velásquez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...769...25S/abstract), [Génova-Santos et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806..113G/abstract). This cross-correlation technique necessitates sophisticated spatial templates to separate the diffuse intergalactic gas in the cosmic web from the bound gas residing inside [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|galaxy halos]] [Suarez-Velásquez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...769...25S/abstract). 

Current observational constraints from these cross-correlations confirm that the WHIM accounts for a significant portion of the cosmological baryon budget, though the detections remain challenging. An analysis of *Planck* data cross-correlated with Two-Micron All-Sky Redshift Survey (2MASS) templates yielded a marginal signal at zero lag of $0.064 \pm 0.051 \, \mu\mathrm{K}$ [Génova-Santos et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806..113G/abstract). The literature is explicit that such signals possess low signal-to-noise ratios (typically $S/N \sim 0.8$ to $1.4$) and can be affected by Galactic foregrounds or random spatial alignments [Suarez-Velásquez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...769...25S/abstract), [Génova-Santos et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806..113G/abstract). Nevertheless, the upper limits derived from these measurements are consistent with approximately $45\%$ of all baryons residing in filamentary structures at overdensities of $1$ to $100$ times the cosmic mean, possessing temperatures in the range of $10^{4.5}$ to $10^{7.5}$ K [Génova-Santos et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806..113G/abstract), [Cao et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MPLA...21.2233C/abstract).

The tSZE data also illuminate the underlying [[temperature-density-relation|Temperature-density relation]] of the intergalactic medium. Cross-correlation studies reveal that the pressure profiles of the IGM roughly follow a power law consistent with an adiabatic equation of state, which steepens at densities greater than $10$ times the mean cosmic density ($\bar{\rho}_m$) [Lim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.4017L/abstract). The implied average gas temperatures track the environmental density: from $\sim 10^4$ K in regions of mean density, rising to $\sim 10^5$ K at $10 \bar{\rho}_m$, and exceeding $10^6$ K in overdense filaments at $\sim 100 \bar{\rho}_m$ [Lim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.4017L/abstract). Furthermore, the thermal energy content is found to be elevated in regions with stronger tidal fields, corroborating the paradigm that the WHIM is predominantly powered by [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|shocks]] generated during large-scale structure formation [Lim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.4017L/abstract).

Looking ahead, joint analyses of the tSZE Compton $y$ parameter and the CMB [[optically-thin-gas|optical depth]] ($\tau$) offer a pathway to constrain both the density and pressure profiles simultaneously. Theoretical models indicate that joint cross-correlations with galaxy clustering can measure the [[hot-gas-mass-fraction|Hot gas mass fraction]] and pressure profiles with uncertainties of $\sim 5.3\%$ and $\sim 13\%$, respectively, at low redshift [Roy et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951...50R/abstract). In the most extreme density peaks, [[non-thermal-sunyaev-zel-dovich-effect|relativistic corrections to the SZ effect]] (rSZe) provide an additional diagnostic tool. Measurements of the rSZe at sub-millimeter wavelengths have been successfully used to determine the temperatures of superheated gas ($T_{\mathrm{rSZe}} = 15.1_{-3.3}^{+3.8}$ keV) in major cluster mergers, offering a complementary probe to [[diffuse-x-ray-emitting-gas|Diffuse X-ray emitting gas]] observations [Vaughan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251008837V/abstract).

## Chemical Enrichment and Galactic Feedback

The concept of intergalactic pollution has deep historical roots; early models proposed that quasi-stellar sources enriched the [[nearby-intergalactic-gas|primordial intergalactic medium]], recycling matter to account for Population II stellar abundances [Silk et al. (1972)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1972ApJ...175....1S/abstract). Today, it is recognized that the WHIM and the closely associated [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] are fundamentally tied to the lifecycle of galaxies. Although [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|shock heating]] from the gravitational collapse of large-scale structures is the primary energetic driver forming the WHIM, its chemical enrichment is governed by non-gravitational galactic feedback [Davé et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...552..473D/abstract), [Meiksin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009RvMP...81.1405M/abstract). 

Heavy elements synthesized within the [[neutral-interstellar-medium|Neutral Interstellar Medium]] and [[warm-ionized-medium|Warm Ionized Medium]] of galaxies are continuously expelled into the intergalactic environment. Core-collapse and Type Ia supernovae power galactic winds that inject both thermal energy and metals into the circumgalactic medium and eventually the broader cosmic web [Böhringer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004IAUS..217...92B/abstract), [Böhringer et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MmSAI..85..396B/abstract). Importantly, UV diagnostics are critical for tracing this low-density gas as it is dispersed by galactic winds and interactions [Martin et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..164...66M/abstract). Specifically, the hot X-ray emitting phase of these galactic "superwinds" contains the majority of the energy and newly synthesized metals that are likely to escape the galactic potential and enrich the IGM [Strickland et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S.289S/abstract). In emission-line galaxies, for instance, metal absorption is observed to be strongly anisotropic, exhibiting a spatial excess along the galactic minor axis that is consistent with starburst-driven outflows [Lan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...866...36L/abstract). In early cosmic chemical evolution models, it was suggested that up to 80% of baryons may be lost to the diffuse IGM via such supernova-driven winds during the earliest stages of star formation [Hartwick et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...640...41H/abstract). Additionally, radiation pressure-driven dust efflux serves as a complementary mechanism; dust grains expelled from galaxies and subsequently destroyed in the intergalactic space can account for a significant fraction of the carbon and silicon enrichment observed in the distant universe [Aguirre et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...556L..11A/abstract). Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) feedback also plays a vital role in heating and mixing the gas, though the relative contributions of AGN versus stellar feedback in enriching the intergalactic medium far from galaxy centers remains an active open question.

The intergalactic gas was already polluted with metals at early epochs. By redshift $z \sim 2.5$, the median intergalactic abundance is measured to be $[O,C/H] \approx -2.82$, following an approximately log-normal distribution with a scatter of $\sigma = 0.75$ dex [Simcoe et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...606...92S/abstract). Observations of the Ly$\alpha$ forest indicate that roughly $60\%$ to $70\%$ of absorbers at this epoch are enriched to $[O,C/H] \gtrsim -3.5$, implying that typical galaxies recycled $0.1\%-0.4\%$ of their mass back into the intergalactic medium as heavy elements during the first 3 Gyr of cosmic time [Simcoe et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...606...92S/abstract). Furthermore, intergalactic supernovae—potentially associated with gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) lacking host galaxies—may erupt directly within these voids and filaments, significantly contributing to the iron enrichment of the IGM [Dado et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ChJAS...8..366D/abstract). Importantly, the lack of a universal metallicity floor suggests that the bulk of these intergalactic metals were produced by standard galactic populations rather than solely by early Population III stars [Simcoe et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...606...92S/abstract).

Despite the clear signature of feedback, the spatial extent to which these metals penetrate the lowest-density filaments of the WHIM is highly debated. While the circumgalactic medium holds a substantial reservoir of enriched gas, some studies utilizing broad Ly$\alpha$ absorbers as metallicity-independent WHIM probes suggest that galactic feedback may not enrich the gas far beyond isolated galaxy or group halos to levels currently detectable in UV or X-ray metal lines [Johnson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884L..31J/abstract). Consequently, the literature is ambiguous regarding the exact metallicity distribution within the most diffuse, large-scale segments of the cosmic web. Future missions deploying high-resolution X-ray microcalorimeters aim to map these extended abundance ratios in detail, providing the precision needed to disentangle the physical imprints of stellar winds, AGN outbursts, and gravitational shocks across the WHIM [Kraft et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221109827K/abstract), [Schellenberger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969...85S/abstract).

## Magnetism and Non-Thermal Components

The WHIM is a weakly collisional, [[hot-astrophysical-plasma|Hot Astrophysical Plasma]] permeated by [[magnetized-intergalactic-matter|Magnetized intergalactic matter]]. Observations and cosmological simulations indicate that magnetic fields are ubiquitous throughout the cosmic web, with field strengths varying dramatically by environment. While void regions may host exceedingly weak fields on the order of $\sim 10^{-16}$ G, magnetic fields of roughly $10^{-7}$ G are consistently found in association with intergalactic hot gas and galaxies, potentially probed by Faraday rotation and high-energy [[extragalactic-cosmic-rays|cosmic rays]] . These fields scale up to several $\mu\mathrm{G}$ within the denser intracluster medium [Subramanian et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016RPPh...79g6901S/abstract), [Anderson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.tmp.2002A/abstract).

The origin of these magnetic fields remains an active area of debate. It is currently unresolved whether the initial seed fields were generated by primordial mechanisms—such as during cosmic inflation or electroweak phase transitions—or by astrophysical "batteries" and subsequently distributed by galactic outflows [Subramanian et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016RPPh...79g6901S/abstract). Regardless of their origin, these seed fields are significantly amplified by large-scale structure formation. Accretion shocks and turbulence drive dynamo action, while kinetic plasma instabilities, such as the magnetothermal instability (MTI), can modify the temperature profile of the gas and amplify the local magnetic energy by factors of over 50. It is important to note, however, that the MTI is strictly a mechanism for amplifying magnetic fields within the [[hot-gas|Intracluster Medium]] (ICM) where the requisite condition—temperature declining with radius in the direction of gravity—exists, rather than a general mechanism for all WHIM filaments [Parrish et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...688..905P/abstract). 

Observational constraints on the WHIM's magnetic topology rely heavily on Faraday rotation measures (RM) of background polarized radio sources. Recent large-scale RM grids, such as those from the POSSUM survey, have successfully isolated the signature of magnetized thermal gas at the interfaces of galaxy groups. The extended WHIM (spanning $2 - 7$ splashback radii) contributes a measurable excess to the residual RM dispersion of $4.2 \pm 1.2 \;\mathrm{rad\,m^{-2}}$ [Anderson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.tmp.2002A/abstract). These RM values suggest that the magnetic pressure within WHIM filaments and the outer circumgalactic medium may be dynamically significant, potentially rivaling the thermal pressure (i.e., driving the plasma $\beta$ toward unity) and influencing the bulk flow of baryons.

In addition to magnetic fields, the WHIM hosts a population of relativistic [[ultra-high-energy-particles|Ultra-High-Energy Particles]] and cosmic rays. As large-scale structure forms, gravitational collapse and associated accretion shocks accelerate electrons to relativistic energies. As these electrons propagate through the $\sim \mu\mathrm{G}$ turbulent fields of the WHIM and intracluster medium, they produce non-thermal emission, most notably observable as steep-spectrum diffuse radio halos and radio relics [van Weeren et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...508...75V/abstract), [Cassano et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ASPC..407..223C/abstract). Additionally, bent-double radio sources serve as effective probes of the intragroup medium density, as the ram pressure from the intergalactic medium's interaction shapes the morphology of these galactic jets as they traverse the cosmic web [Freeland et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..386...39F/abstract). Furthermore, these magnetized environments act as scattering media for hadronic [[extragalactic-cosmic-rays|Extragalactic cosmic rays]], rendering galaxy clusters highly opaque to the propagation of ultra-high-energy cosmic ray nuclei [Condorelli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957...80C/abstract). On an even broader scale, the intergalactic infrared radiation field causes pair-production absorption of high-energy gamma-rays traversing the cosmic web, serving as an independent probe of the diffuse IGM background [Stecker et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...476..712S/abstract).

The interplay between the thermal WHIM and non-thermal relativistic populations has sparked ongoing debate regarding the exact physical origin of soft X-ray and EUV excesses observed in several massive structures. While earlier models attributed this excess to non-thermal inverse Compton scattering of the cosmic microwave background by relativistic electrons, competing models suggest the excess is produced by synchrotron radiation from ultra-relativistic, multi-TeV electrons [Timokhin et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004A&A...417..391T/abstract). Furthermore, competing theories suggest the emission is primarily thermal, originating from unresolved, cooler clumps within the WHIM [Gouin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250618459G/abstract). Future high-resolution X-ray spectroscopy missions will be required to unambiguously disentangle the thermal and non-thermal energy budgets of the cosmic web.

## The IGM-CGM Interface

The boundary between the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] (CGM) and the broader intergalactic filaments represents a highly dynamic transition zone. Galaxies do not evolve as isolated, closed boxes; rather, this interface regulates the fundamental exchange of mass, energy, and heavy elements between the dense galactic environments and the tenuous cosmic web. 

On one hand, the IGM-CGM interface serves as the primary reservoir for [[cold-gas-accretion|cosmological gas accretion]]. Observations of low-redshift galaxies reveal that the physical extent and column density of circumgalactic [[neutral-interstellar-medium|neutral hydrogen]], measured via Ly$\alpha$ equivalent widths, correlate strongly with the host galaxy's internal atomic gas fraction at impact parameters extending up to $\sim 200$ kpc [Borthakur et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...813...46B/abstract). This correlation scales dynamically with the normalized H I radius ($r/R_{\rm HI}$), supporting a paradigm wherein the galactic disk is continuously nourished by the accretion of diffuse intergalactic gas [Borthakur et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240912554B/abstract).

Conversely, this region is heavily shaped by galactic feedback, manifesting as a [[multiphase-circumgalactic-medium|Multiphase circumgalactic medium]] that encompasses cool ($T \sim 10^4$ K), warm, and hot ($10^5 \text{--} 10^7$ K) gas phases [Tumlinson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55..389T/abstract). Cool gas, traced by Mg II and Fe II absorption, often exhibits an anisotropic spatial distribution with an absorption excess along the minor axis of star-forming galaxies. This geometry is a strong signature of enriched material being ejected by supernova-driven [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|galactic outflows]] [Lan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...866...36L/abstract). The transport of metals from the [[cosmic-media-and-interstellar-medium|ISM]] across the CGM and into the IGM may be further aided by radiation pressure acting on dust grains, contributing significantly to the observed intergalactic chemical enrichment [Aguirre et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...556L..11A/abstract). For the cooler components, data from the DESI Year 1 survey demonstrate that the covering fraction of cool gas (traced by Mg II) around massive red galaxies evolves significantly with redshift, closely tracking the global star formation rate density [Chang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251203845C/abstract). Furthermore, UV absorption line ratios in the CGM, specifically the N V to O VI ratio, provide a sensitive test for local radiation fields and constrain nitrogen abundance models in this transition region [Cen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160903583C/abstract).

The thermal state of the hot, volume-filling gas at this interface is generally assumed to be governed by gravitational shock heating. In this standard model, the density profile of the extended hot halo is often parameterized by an isothermal $\beta$-model:
$$ \rho(r) = \rho_0 \left[ 1 + \left(\frac{r}{r_c}\right)^2 \right]^{-3\beta/2} $$
where observations of massive spirals typically yield $\beta \approx 0.35 \text{--} 0.55$, and the gas is heated to the host halo's [[mean-galactic-virial-temperature|virial temperature]], $T_{\rm vir}$ [Li et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJS..233...20L/abstract). Recent observations from the SRG/eROSITA all-sky survey have utilized image stacking to detect hot CGM emission out to 50 kpc around nearby L* galaxies, yielding results consistent with predictions from IllustrisTNG simulations [He et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260116499H/abstract). Understanding the warm-hot CGM also relies on detailed comparisons of analytic models—such as isentropic and cooling flow models—which yield differing entropy profiles and column density predictions for highly ionized oxygen ions [Singh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.tmp.1670S/abstract).

However, the literature presents notable tensions regarding this purely gravitational heating scenario. High-resolution X-ray absorption studies have identified "[[super-virial-temperature-gas|supervirial]]" gas temperatures ($T > 10^7$ K) in the Milky Way's CGM, which cannot be explained by gravitational infall alone and likely require complex non-gravitational feedback or outburst mechanisms [Das et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...918...83D/abstract). Similar non-isothermal, supervirial components have been detected out to $100$ kpc in other local spiral galaxies, such as NGC 3221 [Das et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...897...63D/abstract). At higher redshifts ($z \sim 2.8$), direct high-resolution observations with JWST have resolved [O III] emission from narrow ($\sim 2.5$ kpc) filamentary structures surrounding interacting galaxies, indicating strong mechanical heating driven by episodic feedback [Peng et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241010993P/abstract).

Furthermore, defining the exact outer boundary of the CGM remains an observational challenge. Because both the hot CGM and the warm-hot IGM share similar temperatures and ionization states, there is an active and unresolved debate over whether highly ionized $z=0$ absorption systems (e.g., O VII, O VIII) originate primarily within a localized [[warm-corona|Galactic corona]] or represent the more extended, diffuse medium of the Local Group [Williams et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ESASP.604E...2W/abstract). Disentangling these overlapping components requires mapping both the kinematic and thermal structure of the interface with next-generation X-ray microcalorimeters.

## Evolutionary History: From Reionization to the Modern IGM

Historically, the emergence of the WHIM was anticipated by early cold dark matter (CDM) simulations, which predicted that a significant fraction of cosmic gas would be heated to temperatures between $10^5$ and $10^6$ K as large-scale structures formed [Chiang et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ApJ...339..603C/abstract). Even earlier, a historical clumpy IGM model suggested that binding gas within galaxy groups and clusters would contribute significantly to the [[diffuse-x-ray-emitting-gas|diffuse X-ray background]], laying the conceptual groundwork for a multiphase intergalactic environment [Silk et al. (1973)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1973ApJ...183..387S/abstract). Additionally, early constraints from the X-ray background and cosmic microwave background fluctuations suggested that any highly energetic intergalactic medium must be smoothly distributed on scales larger than galaxies [Barcons et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991Natur.350..685B/abstract). 

Following [[recombination-cosmology|Recombination (cosmology)]] at $z \approx 1300$, the universe entered the "Dark Ages," dominated by a cooling, neutral gas. Early constraints from the [[21-cm-cosmology|21 cm]] power spectrum utilizing the PAPER array have set important lower limits on the IGM kinetic temperature, indicating it was heated above $\sim 5-10$ K by redshift $z=8.4$ [Pober et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...809...62P/abstract). The formation of the first stars and black holes initiated the Cosmic Dawn, injecting ultraviolet radiation into the surrounding environment. This marked the beginning of the [[epoch-of-reionization|Epoch of Reionization]], a spatially inhomogeneous and patchy phase transition. Rather than a sudden event, this cosmic reionization represented a gradual phase transition driven by UV and X-ray radiation from the first stars and black holes, which transformed the neutral intergalactic gas into a highly ionized plasma [Ferrara et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1409.4946F/abstract), [Tilvi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...891L..10T/abstract), [Fan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ARA&A..61..373F/abstract). Constraints from Lyman-$\alpha$ absorption and cosmic microwave background optical depth indicate that [[re-ionization|hydrogen reionization]] was largely completed by $z \approx 6.8$ [Davies et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489..977D/abstract). The exact galactic populations driving this transition remain actively debated. It has been noted that the required ionizing photon emissivity to satisfy the hydrogen Gunn-Peterson constraint often exceeds that provided by observed high-redshift quasars [Giroux et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJS..102..191G/abstract). While traditional models favor a "democratic" process led by numerous faint, low-mass galaxies [Muñoz et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...729...99M/abstract), recent "oligarchic" models argue that a small population of massive, UV-bright galaxies with high ionizing photon escape fractions ($f_{\rm esc} \sim 0.2$) contributed the bulk of the required radiation budget [Naidu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...892..109N/abstract). Even during the Epoch of Reionization, the interstellar media of early galaxies were undergoing complex chemical evolution; ALMA detections of [O III] 88 micron emission from a galaxy at $z \sim 7$ indicate the presence of a metal-enriched but uniquely dust-deficient environment [Inoue et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Sci...352.1559I/abstract).

Following hydrogen reionization, the intergalactic medium (IGM) retained a "thermal memory" of the heating events, eventually relaxing into a power-law [[temperature-density-relation|Temperature-density relation]] approximated by $T = T_0(1+\delta)^{1-\gamma}$, where $\gamma \approx 1.5$ [Davies et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489..977D/abstract). The subsequent proliferation of quasars at $z \sim 3$ drove [[he-ii-reionization|He II reionization]], which injected additional thermal energy and further modified the equation of state of the cosmic gas [Faucher-Giguère et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...703.1416F/abstract). By $z \sim 2.5$, [[photoionization-and-radiative-heating|Photoionization and Radiative Heating]] had established a baseline temperature of $\sim 10^4$ K for the IGM. Observations of the high-redshift Lyman-$\alpha$ forest reveal that this early medium was already chemically enriched, possessing a median abundance of $\mathrm{[O,C/H]} \approx -2.8$, likely distributed by early [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|supernova winds]] and radiation pressure-driven dust efflux [Aguirre et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...556L..11A/abstract) [Simcoe et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...606...92S/abstract).

At high redshifts ($z \gtrsim 2$), the average cosmic density was sufficiently high that any gas shock-heated by gravitational collapse could radiatively cool back to its baseline photoionized temperature. The bulk of the universe's baryons thus resided in the $10^4$ K photoionized phase. However, as the universe expanded, the mean density of the IGM dropped, causing the radiative cooling time of the gas to exceed the Hubble time at $z \lesssim 2$. 

During this later epoch, the continuous collapse of matter into [[cosmic-and-molecular-filaments|Cosmic and Molecular Filaments]] and sheets drove pervasive shock heating. The kinetic energy of infalling gas breaking against mildly non-linear structures was converted into thermal energy, heating the IGM to temperatures between $10^5$ K and $10^7$ K [Davé et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...552..473D/abstract) [Diaferio et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008SSRv..134....7D/abstract). This transition from a purely photoionized medium to a predominantly shock-heated one gave rise to the Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM). Cosmological hydrodynamic simulations consistently predict that this hot, diffuse network grew to become the primary repository for baryons at $z < 1$ [Davé et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...552..473D/abstract) [Meiksin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009RvMP...81.1405M/abstract) [Génova-Santos et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806..113G/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Frontiers

Despite robust predictions from hydrodynamic simulations, completing the cosmic baryon census remains an outstanding observational challenge. Current X-ray absorption studies rely heavily on rare, bright background quasars, which restricts probing the WHIM to one-dimensional pencil beams. Furthermore, interpreting observations remains subject to significant ambiguities; for instance, it remains heavily debated whether $z=0$ highly ionized absorption systems (e.g., O VII and O VIII) originate primarily from a [[warm-corona|Warm corona]] within the Milky Way's [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] or if they represent the broader Local Group intergalactic medium [Yao et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...624..751Y/abstract), [Williams et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ESASP.604E...2W/abstract). Another major open question concerns the thermodynamics of the WHIM. While shock heating from structure formation is the dominant heating mechanism, the extent to which non-gravitational feedback from active galactic nuclei (AGN) and galactic winds enriches and heats the broader cosmic web is actively debated [Johnson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884L..31J/abstract).

Closing the [[hot-gas-mass-fraction|missing baryon problem]] and mapping the cosmic web in emission demands a generational leap in future instrumentation. Direct imaging of diffuse WHIM emission is severely hampered by low surface brightness and confusion with the bright local [[diffuse-x-ray-emitting-gas|Diffuse X-ray emitting gas]]. Overcoming this limitation requires large-area X-ray microcalorimeters capable of 1-2 eV spectral resolution in the 0.2-2.0 keV band [Kraft et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221109827K/abstract). To achieve the necessary resolving power ($>3,000$) for effectively mapping the WHIM, future spectrometers will heavily rely on advancements such as Critical-Angle Transmission (CAT) grating technology [Heilmann et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016SPIE.9905E..1XH/abstract). Proposed and upcoming observatories, particularly the Line Emission Mapper (LEM) and the Athena mission, are specifically designed to meet these requirements. By utilizing high-resolution spectroscopy, these missions will be able to disentangle faint cosmological emission lines from the Milky Way foreground [Kraft et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221109827K/abstract), [Schellenberger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969...85S/abstract). This capability will enable two-dimensional mapping of gas kinematics, outflow structures, and abundance ratios, definitively bridging the gap between individual galactic halos and the large-scale WHIM [Mathur et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908...69M/abstract). Alongside Athena and LEM, the proposed Xenia mission has been put forward as a dedicated tool for studying the WHIM and [[global-reionization|cosmic reionization]] utilizing cryogenic imaging spectrometers [Burrows et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010SPIE.7732E..1TB/abstract).

Beyond X-ray wavelengths, radio and sub-millimeter frontiers offer highly complementary probes of this [[hot-astrophysical-plasma|Hot Astrophysical Plasma]]. Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) have recently emerged as a powerful tool for measuring the distribution of intergalactic matter. The dispersion measures of a localized sample of $\mathcal{O}(10-100)$ FRBs can constrain the distribution of baryons between the CGM and IGM to $\sim 10\%$ accuracy [Ravi et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...88R/abstract). Simultaneously, the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) will probe the interface of the WHIM and the [[cold-neutral-medium|Cold neutral medium]] by mapping H I column densities down to $N_{\rm HI} \approx 10^{18} \text{ cm}^{-2}$ at sub-arcminute resolution [Popping et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015aska.confE.132P/abstract). Concurrently, upcoming Line Intensity Mapping experiments targeting H-$\alpha$ will probe the IGM during the Epoch of Reionization, providing tests for predictions derived from simulations such as CoDa III [Lee et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025RNAAS...9...96L/abstract). Finally, advancements in cross-correlating the thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (tSZE) effect with large-scale galaxy surveys will allow precision mapping of the WHIM [[temperature-density-relation|pressure-density relation]], while relativistic corrections to the SZ effect (rSZe) show promise for measuring superheated gas ($T > 10$ keV) generated by shocks in major cluster mergers [Lim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.4017L/abstract), [Vaughan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251008837V/abstract).
