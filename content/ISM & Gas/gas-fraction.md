## Article

In astrophysics, the **gas fraction** ($f_{\rm gas}$) is a fundamental parameter that quantifies the relative mass of gaseous material within a galaxy or galaxy cluster compared to its total baryonic or stellar mass. Formally defined as $f_{\rm gas} = M_{\rm cold} / (M_{\rm cold} + M_*)$—where $M_{\rm cold}$ is the mass of the [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] reservoir (typically comprising atomic hydrogen, H I, and molecular hydrogen, H$_2$) and $M_*$ is the mass of the [[stellar-component|Stellar component]]—this ratio measures the efficiency with which a system has converted its primordial and accreted baryons into stars over cosmic time. In the local universe, the gas fraction exhibits robust [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] with stellar mass, luminosity, and surface brightness. [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] are overwhelmingly gas-rich and often dominated by H I, whereas [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] have largely exhausted or expelled their cold gas, evolving into gas-poor systems. By incorporating the gas fraction alongside stellar mass, kinematic scaling laws such as the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] can successfully unify the scaling between total baryonic mass and rotational velocity across five orders of magnitude.

The gas fraction is not a static quantity but rather a dynamic tracer of the galactic baryon cycle, regulated by the continuous interplay between [[net-gas-inflows|Net gas inflows]] from the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]], internal star formation, and [[galaxy-and-gaseous-outflows|Galaxy and gaseous outflows]] driven by stellar and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback. Theoretical frameworks, such as equilibrium "bathtub" models, demonstrate that this cycle governs both the available fuel for star formation and the observed scatter in [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]] scaling relations [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1354D/abstract). Across cosmic time, galactic gas fractions have evolved dramatically. During the "cosmic noon" ($z \sim 2$), early [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] possessed significantly higher $f_{\rm gas}$ values, dominated by immense molecular reservoirs that fueled the peak of the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]] [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract). Observations indicate that while atomic gas mass has declined modestly since this epoch, the cosmic molecular gas mass has dropped by more than an order of magnitude [Popping et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..477P/abstract).

A galaxy's cold gas fraction directly dictates its star formation rate via the [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]], though recent studies highlight that the [[dense-molecular-gas|Dense molecular gas]] fraction is the most direct physical prerequisite for starbirth [Gao et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..237..331G/abstract). Furthermore, the [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]] plays a critical role in shaping gas reservoirs; galaxies falling into dense groups and clusters are subject to ram-pressure stripping and starvation, mechanisms that rapidly deplete their gas and drive them toward becoming [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]]. On the macroscopic scale of massive clusters, the [[hot-gas-mass-fraction|Hot gas mass fraction]] of the intracluster medium frequently dominates the total baryonic mass budget, serving as a vital probe for cosmological parameters.

This article explores the formal definitions and empirical scaling relations of the gas fraction, traces its redshift evolution and role in chemical enrichment, and examines the physical phase transitions between atomic, molecular, and dense gas. Finally, it addresses ongoing challenges and open questions in the field. These include resolving the theoretical tension of "baryon-deficient dwarfs" that fall significantly below predicted mass retention models, calibrating the [[h2-conversion-factor|H2 conversion factor]] to detect "dark" molecular gas in [[low-metallicity-environments|Low-Metallicity Environments]], and determining whether the observed decline in the cosmic star formation rate is primarily driven by an absolute depletion of gas reservoirs or a fundamental decrease in star formation efficiency [Popping et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..477P/abstract), [Kudritzki et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910...87K/abstract).

## Definitions and Scaling Relations

The cold gas mass fraction, $f_{\rm gas}$, is formally defined as the ratio of the cold gas mass to the total baryonic mass of the galaxy disk (excluding hot halo gas):

$$ f_{\rm gas} = \frac{M_{\rm cold}}{M_{\rm cold} + M_*} $$

where $M_{\rm cold}$ is the total mass of [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] (typically the sum of atomic hydrogen, $M_{\rm H I}$, and molecular hydrogen, $M_{\rm H_2}$) and $M_*$ is the mass of the [[stellar-component|Stellar component]]. This parameter expresses the efficiency with which a galaxy has converted its available gas reservoir into stars over cosmic time. Note that some literature alternatively employs the [[g-s-ratio|G/S ratio]] ($M_{\rm cold}/M_*$) to characterize a galaxy's relative gas budget. 

In the local universe, $f_{\rm gas}$ exhibits robust [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] with stellar mass, luminosity, and star formation rate (SFR). Observations establish that $f_{\rm gas}$ strongly anticorrelates with stellar mass and surface brightness, reflecting the "downsizing" paradigm of galaxy evolution [McGaugh et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...481..689M/abstract), . [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] ($M_* < 3 \times 10^9 M_\odot$) are systematically less evolved and highly gas-rich; their gas fractions frequently exceed 90% and are overwhelmingly dominated by atomic hydrogen, residing in a regime where star formation often cannot keep pace with steady H I accretion [McGaugh et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...481..689M/abstract), [Hunt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.180H/abstract). Conversely, [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] ($M_* > 3 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$) have largely consumed or expelled their cold gas reservoirs, typically exhibiting $f_{\rm gas} \sim 0.1$ (similar to the Milky Way) or lower. In these high-mass systems, H I often still dominates the mass of the gas budget but does not actively participate in star formation [Hunt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.180H/abstract). Interestingly, the gas fraction also leaves distinct imprints on stellar sub-populations; for instance, the mean [[globular-clusters|globular cluster]] metallicity at a given age is found to be higher for early-type dwarfs compared to gas-rich dwarf irregulars [Sharina et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010vsgh.conf..181S/abstract).

While morphological type is traditionally linked to gas content, the literature reveals tensions regarding which structural parameters truly drive gas depletion. At a fixed $M_*$ and SFR, standard parametric morphological indicators like the Sérsic index or the C-index show no significant correlation with H I content [Namiki et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...918...68N/abstract). Instead, visual "smoothness" and the inner [[stellar-surface-density|Stellar surface density]] within the central 1 kpc ($\Sigma_1$) serve as more robust predictors. Both atomic and molecular gas fractions decline with increasing $\Sigma_1$, but the molecular gas fraction declines particularly sharply above a mass-relative threshold ($\Delta\Sigma_1 = 0$), closely tracking the onset of galactic quenching [Shread et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251118227S/abstract). Furthermore, internal galactic structures are sensitive to these gas budgets; for instance, the likelihood of a galaxy hosting a strong bar is anti-correlated with its gas mass fraction, as high gas richness tends to inhibit the formation of bar structures [Cervantes Sodi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835...80C/abstract).

The gas fraction is also a vital component of kinematic scaling laws. Incorporating $f_{\rm gas}$ into the Tully-Fisher relation yields the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]], which successfully unifies the scaling between total baryonic mass and rotational velocity across five orders of magnitude in mass [Marongwe et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251120188M/abstract). Despite the predictive power of these [[observable-mass-scaling-relations|Observable-mass scaling relations]], substantial diversity remains at the extremes of the [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|galaxy mass function]]. Recent local volume studies have identified a population of "baryon-deficient dwarfs" exhibiting stellar masses 1.0 to 1.5 orders of magnitude lower than expected from standard models, highlighting significant unresolved scatter in the baryon retention fractions of low-mass halos [Mancera Piña et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522727M/abstract).

## Phase Transitions: Atomic, Molecular, and Dense Gas

The partitioning of the [[neutral-interstellar-medium|Neutral Interstellar Medium]] into atomic (H I) and molecular (H$_2$) phases is a fundamental regulator of galactic star formation. The physical mechanism driving this transition remains a subject of active debate; theoretical models typically emphasize either large-scale hydrostatic pressure driven by the combined gravity of stars and gas, or local gas and dust shielding combined with metallicity [Fumagalli et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...722..919F/abstract), [Krumholz et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...699..850K/abstract). High-resolution observations of [[low-metallicity-environments|low-metallicity dwarf galaxies]] suggest that local shielding models more accurately capture the phase transition on spatial scales below 100 pc [Fumagalli et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...722..919F/abstract).

The relative abundance of these phases evolves significantly across cosmic time. In the local universe, low-mass galaxies are overwhelmingly dominated by atomic gas [Hunt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.180H/abstract), whereas high-redshift ($z > 1$) galaxies host much larger [[cold-gas-reservoirs|Cold gas reservoirs]] that are primarily molecular [Combes et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..413C/abstract), [Walter et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1101.4022W/abstract). Cosmological models indicate that while the global H I mass in galaxies has decreased by merely $\sim 0.2$ dex since $z = 1.5$, the total H$_2$ mass has precipitously declined by $>1$ dex over the same period [Popping et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..477P/abstract).

Quantifying molecular gas relies heavily on the [[h2-conversion-factor|H2 conversion factor]] ($\alpha_{\rm CO}$), which translates CO emission luminosity into total molecular mass. Recent local universe surveys demonstrate a steep metallicity dependence for this factor, following $\alpha_{\rm CO} \propto (Z/Z_\odot)^{-1.55}$ [Hunt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.180H/abstract). Furthermore, recent integral field unit mapping from the ALMaQUEST Survey demonstrates that the global molecular-to-atomic gas ratios ($R_{\rm mol}$) exhibit significant correlations with both local oxygen abundance and galaxy ionization states, as identified via spatially resolved [[strong-line-diagnostics|BPT diagnostics]] [Yu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240319447Y/abstract). In extreme low-metallicity environments where CO fails to adequately trace H$_2$, mid-infrared rotational H$_2$ emission can directly probe [[warm-molecular-gas|Warm molecular gas]] down to $\sim 80$ K, recovering mass often missed by traditional tracers [Togi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...18T/abstract). While dense gas concentrates centrally, the extent of the molecular phase can be vast; CO emission can trace spiral arms out to 15 kpc in the outskirts of galaxies, serving as a critical probe for extended UV disks and the effects of ram-pressure stripping [Watson et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161205275W/abstract).

While total molecular gas is a prerequisite, star formation is most tightly linked to the [[dense-molecular-gas|Dense molecular gas]] phase, typically observed using high-dipole moment [[dense-gas-tracers|Dense gas tracers]] like HCN and HCO$^+$. On smaller scales, spectroscopically traced dense gas fractions (e.g., HCN/CO ratios) are found to correlate strongly with the mass-weighted average cloud-scale (roughly 120 pc) [[molecular-gas-surface-density|molecular gas surface densities]] across galaxy disks [Gallagher et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...868L..38G/abstract). The correlation between the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] and dense gas mass is remarkably linear across nine orders of magnitude, spanning from local dense cores to hyperluminous starbursts [Gao et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..237..331G/abstract). However, spatially resolved maps reveal a complex environmental dependency. The [[dense-gas-fraction|Dense gas fraction]] (often parameterized by the HCN/CO ratio) generally correlates with stellar surface density and dynamical equilibrium pressure. Paradoxically, the star formation efficiency of this dense gas (traced by IR/HCN) is frequently anti-correlated with the dense gas fraction itself [Bigiel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...822L..26B/abstract), [Jiménez-Donaire et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...880..127J/abstract). This suggests that while high-pressure environments—such as the [[central-molecular-zone|Central Molecular Zone]] of the Milky Way—favor the physical formation of dense gas, the actual efficiency of converting that gas into stars is suppressed, potentially due to elevated interstellar turbulence or higher density thresholds required for gravitational collapse [Bigiel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...822L..26B/abstract). Furthermore, the conversion factor for dense gas tracers ($\alpha_Q$) is not universal; for HCN, it varies by a factor of 3 between the inner and outer Milky Way, scaling as $Z^{-1.53}$ [Patra et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250307931P/abstract).

Finally, the spatial distribution of stellar mass strongly influences these phase transitions. In massive local galaxies, both the atomic and molecular gas fractions decline with increasing central stellar mass surface density ($\Sigma_1$), but the molecular fraction drops much more sharply above a critical density threshold, hinting at a connection between bulge growth, active galactic nucleus feedback, and the rapid depletion of molecular fuel [Shread et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251118227S/abstract). However, while active galactic nucleus (AGN) radiation can profoundly influence molecular gas properties, certain massive molecular reservoirs can remain surprisingly resilient to energetic AGN feedback, as observed in the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|nuclear region of galaxies]] like NGC 2110 [Rosario et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...875L...8R/abstract).

## Redshift Evolution and Cosmic Gas History

The gas content of galaxies has undergone profound changes over cosmic time, tightly coupling [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] to the global assembly of stellar mass. Observations tracking the [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]] of galaxies reveal that cold gas fractions ($f_{\rm gas}$) were substantially higher during the "cosmic noon" at $z \sim 2-3$ [Erb et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract), [Combes et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015llg..book..413C/abstract). During this epoch, these elevated gas supplies fueled the peak of the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]].

A defining characteristic of this early epoch is the shifting balance between the atomic and molecular gas phases. While the cold gas budget of the local universe is dominated by atomic hydrogen, high-redshift galaxies possessed massive molecular (H$_2$) reservoirs [Walter et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1101.4022W/abstract). Models and semi-empirical constraints indicate that the molecular gas mass in galaxies has decreased by $>1$ dex since $z = 1.5$, whereas the atomic H I mass has experienced a comparatively modest decline of $\sim 0.2$ dex over the same period [Popping et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..477P/abstract). Consequently, the molecular gas-to-stellar mass ratio, $\mu_{\rm gas} \equiv M_{\rm H_2}/M_*$, scales strongly with redshift, following the empirical relation $\mu_{\rm gas} \propto (1+z)^{2.5}$ [Tacconi et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...853..179T/abstract). However, observations of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] at $z \sim 0.2$ from the HIGHz survey demonstrate that their cold gas budgets are still dominated by atomic hydrogen, indicating that H I dominance has persisted for at least the last 3 billion years [Cortese et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848L...7C/abstract).

This elevated high-redshift gas content is particularly evident in typical massive star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 2-3$. These systems hosted exceptionally large cold gas reservoirs sustained by continuous [[galaxy-accretion|cosmological accretion]] [Cresci et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.467..811C/abstract), [Bouché et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Sci...341...50B/abstract). During this epoch, the relative contribution of different growth channels depends strongly on mass; for massive galaxies at $z \sim 1-3$, mergers and continuous gas accretion are approximately equally important for mass assembly, whereas accretion likely dominates the growth of lower mass systems [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract). This rapid infall of pristine primordial gas heavily diluted central metallicities, producing distinct chemical signatures unique to the early universe [Cresci et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.467..811C/abstract). However, this extremely gas-rich phase was short-lived. By $z \sim 0.7$, massive [[passive-galaxies|passive galaxies]] already exhibit heavily depleted molecular gas fractions ($f_{\rm gas} \lesssim 0.1$). This indicates that many early galaxies consumed or expelled their gas, evolving into gas-poor systems without requiring subsequent large-scale gas accretion or dynamical stabilization [Spilker et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...860..103S/abstract). In the local universe, surveys such as the Atlas3D project have further illuminated how cold gas data provides valuable constraints for understanding the assembly and star formation history of modern early-type galaxies [Young et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..309...47Y/abstract). Conversely, low-mass galaxies show clear evidence for progressive gas loss over cosmic time, having gradually lost nearly all their cold baryons compared to massive systems that evolved and quenched more rapidly [Calura et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...682..252C/abstract).

The dramatic reduction in cosmic gas fractions is directly tied to the [[number-density-evolution|Number density evolution]] of actively star-forming galaxies versus quiescent systems. It remains a subject of ongoing debate whether the order-of-magnitude decline in the cosmic star formation rate density since $z \sim 2$ is primarily driven by galaxies physically depleting their absolute gas reservoirs or by a secular decrease in the efficiency with which cold gas is converted into stars [Popping et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..477P/abstract). Recent cosmological simulations and look-back models suggest that the decline is not solely due to galaxies running out of total gas. Instead, the fraction of the cold interstellar medium that is physically capable of gravitationally collapsing to form stars has systematically decreased over time [Kudritzki et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910...87K/abstract).

## The Baryon Cycle: Accretion, Outflows, and Recycling

The evolution of galaxy gas fractions is best understood through a slowly evolving equilibrium framework, which has largely superseded the traditional [[closed-box-model|Closed box model]] [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). In this paradigm, a galaxy's gas reservoir is regulated by a continuous baryon cycle operating between the interstellar medium and the extended [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] [Tumlinson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55..389T/abstract). The gas content at any given epoch is governed by the dynamical balance between [[net-gas-inflows|Net gas inflows]], star formation, and [[galaxy-and-gaseous-outflows|Galaxy and gaseous outflows]] [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1354D/abstract). 

Because typical gas consumption timescales ($t_{\rm depl} = M_{\rm gas}/\text{SFR}$) are significantly shorter than the Hubble time, galaxies must continually accrete gas to sustain their observed star formation rates [Erb et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract), [Richter et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430...15R/abstract). Observational evidence for this process includes the detection of kinematic signatures from cool gas accreting onto star-forming galaxies at $z = 2.3$, where the mass inflow rate is closely matched to the star formation rate [Bouché et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Sci...341...50B/abstract). At high redshift ($z \sim 3$), the rapid funneling of pristine, metal-poor gas into galactic centers can overwhelm local enrichment, driving distinct "inverse" chemical gradients [Cresci et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.467..811C/abstract). Accretion also persists in seemingly quiescent systems; approximately 37% of star-forming early-type galaxies likely fuel their residual star formation via external gas accretion from minor mergers or the cosmic web [Davis et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489L.108D/abstract). Direct observations further corroborate this; for example, large-scale H I accretion from the cosmic web onto nearby systems like IC 10 has been observed, leading to localized, sharp drops in metallicity [Elmegreen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..317..204E/abstract).

To prevent galaxies from converting all their accreted baryons into stars, stellar and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback must drive material out of the dense disk. The efficiency of this ejection is frequently parameterized by the [[mass-loading-factor|Mass loading factor]], defined as the ratio of the outflow rate to the star formation rate ($\eta = \dot{M}_{\rm out} / \text{SFR}$). Cosmological simulations demonstrate that the balance set by the mass loading factor fundamentally shapes the mass-metallicity relation [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1354D/abstract). Furthermore, temporary fluctuations in the gas accretion rate drive the observed scatter around this relation; an influx of cold gas dilutes the metallicity while fueling star formation, whereas a drop in accretion allows enrichment to proceed unhindered [De Lucia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.3215D/abstract). 

Not all accreting gas is primordial. [[recycling-astrophysics|Recycling (astrophysics)]] of enriched material via stellar mass loss (e.g., from asymptotic giant branch stars and supernovae) plays a critical role in replenishing the cold gas supply. Theoretical models, such as the three-dimensional "Galactic fountain" model, describe this process as gas escaping the galactic disk via supernovae, cooling in the lower halo, and subsequently falling back to replenish the interstellar medium [de Avillez et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..166..103D/abstract). Theoretical models indicate that the continuous return of gas from aging stellar populations can govern the structural evolution of massive galaxies, facilitating the survival of late-type disks and suppressing excessive bulge growth [Martig et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract). Direct evidence for these complex flows includes serendipitous MaNGA discoveries of H-$\alpha$ tails in starbursting galaxies, which trace gas accretion events via recycled wind material that help regulate the low-mass end of the [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass function]] [Cheung et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832..182C/abstract). While the relative importance of fresh cosmic accretion versus recycled gas remains an active area of debate, recent hydrodynamical simulations suggest that recycled material from dying stars in the inner galaxy serves as the primary fuel source for central supermassive black holes [Choi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964...54C/abstract). Ultimately, the entire baryon cycle may be governed by a precipitation limit in the halo, where feedback heats the circumgalactic medium and suspends it at a threshold pressure, regulating the rate at which cold clouds can condense and "rain" back onto the galaxy [Voit et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190311212V/abstract).

## Environmental Influences and Quenching Mechanisms

The gas fraction of a galaxy is profoundly influenced by its surrounding [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]]. Observations consistently show that galaxies residing in dense environments, such as galaxy groups and clusters, are systematically less gas-rich, redder, and exhibit lower specific star formation rates compared to their isolated counterparts in the field. This environmental dependence is a primary driver of the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]], wherein dense regions are dominated by gas-poor early-type galaxies, while late-type star-forming galaxies predominate in the field. As galaxies fall into the gravitational potential of a cluster or massive group, they transition from central to satellite galaxies and become subject to a variety of environmental processes that deplete their cold gas reservoirs and lead to galaxy quenching [Ai et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862...48A/abstract). The reach of this environmental impact can be vast; observations demonstrate that star-forming galaxies located in and around $z \sim 0.5$ clusters often exhibit anomalously low gas-to-stellar mass ratios out to distances of 8 virial radii [Spérone-Longin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021IAUS..359..158S/abstract).

The mechanisms responsible for environmentally driven gas depletion are generally divided into hydrodynamical and gravitational processes. Ram-pressure stripping occurs when a satellite galaxy moves through the dense [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]] or intracluster medium (ICM), exerting a pressure that can rapidly sweep away the galaxy's interstellar medium. This process preferentially removes the extended atomic hydrogen (H I) disk, resulting in a population of [[h-i-deficient-galaxies|H I-deficient galaxies]] within clusters. Interestingly, while ram pressure efficiently removes diffuse atomic gas, the compression of the interstellar medium can temporarily promote the formation of dense molecular gas in stripped "jellyfish" tails, as observed in galaxies such as JW100 [Moretti et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889....9M/abstract). 

Alternatively, starvation (or strangulation) involves the stripping of a galaxy's extended hot gas halo rather than its cold disk. Without a reservoir of hot gas to cool and replenish the cold interstellar medium, ongoing star formation gradually consumes the remaining gas over several gigayears. Studies of the correlation between group crossing times and H I gas fractions suggest that these long-timescale starvation mechanisms may play a more dominant role than rapid stripping in depleting the atomic gas of typical group galaxies [Ai et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862...48A/abstract). Gravitational processes, including tidal stripping and high-speed harassment, further contribute to the removal of both gas and stellar mass. Beyond gas removal, gravitational interactions such as the tidal fields generated during mergers can drive large fractions of a galaxy's gas into its inner regions, frequently triggering intense starbursts and fueling nuclear activity [Hernquist et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989Natur.340..687H/abstract).

Stacking analyses of H I spectra demonstrate that the atomic gas fraction, $f_{\rm H I} = M_{\rm H I} / M_*$, is on average significantly lower for group and pair members than for isolated field galaxies at a fixed stellar mass, shifting them below the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] [Roychowdhury et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927...20R/abstract). However, in low-mass groups ($M_* \lesssim 10^{9.5} M_\odot$), substantial amounts of H I can be found stripped and residing in the intra-group medium rather than within cataloged member galaxies [Roychowdhury et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927...20R/abstract). 

On the scale of massive clusters, the total gas mass fraction of the ICM provides a proxy for the universal baryon fraction and is used to constrain cosmological parameters. Nevertheless, using [[galaxy-cluster-properties-and-scaling-relations|Galaxy Cluster Properties and Scaling Relations]] for precision cosmology is complicated by complex ICM physics. Phenomena such as non-thermal pressure support and [[gas-clumping|Gas clumping]] in the cluster outskirts—as observed in the northern outskirts of the Virgo cluster—can lead to overestimates of the local gas mass fraction if not properly corrected [Mirakhor et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506..139M/abstract). However, X-ray methods can systematically overestimate the gas mass by $5\%-10\%$, meaning that alternative measurements—such as those utilizing the Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect (SZE)—are often more accurate and less biased estimators of cluster gas mass [Hallman et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...648..852H/abstract).

## Relationship to Star Formation Laws

The relationship between a galaxy's gas reservoir and its star formation rate (SFR) is empirically codified by the [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]] (KS law). This power-law scaling relation links the surface density of star formation, $\Sigma_*$, to the surface density of cold gas, $\Sigma_{\rm gas}$, typically taking the form $\Sigma_* \propto \Sigma_{\rm gas}^N$. While standard formulations evaluating the total or molecular gas yield an index of $N \approx 1.4$, observations specifically tracking dense molecular gas (traced by HCN emission) reveal a roughly linear correlation ($N \approx 1$) that stretches across nine orders of magnitude in infrared luminosity, from local dense cores to high-redshift extreme starbursts [Gao et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..237..331G/abstract). Furthermore, this relation can vary spatially; the star formation rate to dense gas correlation index may follow a standard Kennicutt-Schmidt slope ($N \approx 1.5$) within galaxy nuclei, but flattens to a linear relationship when measured globally across the entire galaxy [Narayanan et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...681L..77N/abstract). Consequently, the global SFR depends more intimately on the dense gas fraction than on the total molecular gas budget. However, spatially resolved observations complicate this simple picture; regions with high gas surface density and pressure, such as the centers of spiral galaxies, can exhibit surprisingly lower dense gas star formation efficiencies compared to outer disk environments [Bigiel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...822L..26B/abstract). For instance, the high effective pressure in central galactic bulges (such as in M51) can shift molecular clouds from self-gravitating to more diffuse configurations, explaining the observed sublinear star formation relations in these dense inner regions [Heyer et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930..170H/abstract).

The timescale on which a galaxy converts its molecular reservoir into stars is quantified by the [[depletion-time|Depletion time]], defined as $t_{\rm depl} = M_{\rm gas} / \text{SFR}$. Studies mapping molecular gas fractions across cosmic time demonstrate that $t_{\rm depl}$ is tightly correlated with a galaxy's position relative to the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] (MS). Using large samples spanning $z=0$ to $4$, the molecular gas depletion time is found to scale as $t_{\rm depl} \propto (1+z)^{-0.6} (\delta \text{MS})^{-0.44}$, where $\delta \text{MS}$ is the specific SFR normalized to the main sequence [Tacconi et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...853..179T/abstract). This universal correlation indicates that starbursting galaxies lying significantly above the main sequence exhibit markedly higher star formation efficiencies and shorter depletion times than typical main-sequence disks [Koyama et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...847..137K/abstract), [Silverman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...867...92S/abstract). Conversely, massive passive galaxies at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 0.7$) show molecular gas fractions ($\lesssim 10\%$) and depletion times that deviate significantly from extrapolations of star-forming scaling relations, suggesting either a break or a large increase in scatter in gas consumption behaviors during quenching [Spilker et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...860..103S/abstract).

The balance of [[gas-depletion-and-consumption|Gas Depletion and Consumption]] also shifts fundamentally as a function of stellar mass. In the local universe, dwarf galaxies ($M_* \lesssim 3 \times 10^9 M_\odot$) are overwhelmingly gas-rich and often dominated by ongoing H I accretion, yet their overall star formation is unable to keep pace with the available gas supply [Hunt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.180H/abstract). Intermediate-mass galaxies ($3 \times 10^9 \lesssim M_* \lesssim 3 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$) operate in a state of equilibrium where both atomic and molecular gas masses scale linearly with the SFR. In the highest mass regime ($M_* \gtrsim 3 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$), galaxies become systematically gas-poor, and the remaining atomic gas often decouples from the active star formation process [Hunt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.180H/abstract). Across all mass regimes and redshifts, the continuous conversion of gas into stars dictated by the KS law necessitates steady gas accretion. Without continuous replenishment from the circumgalactic medium, the short theoretical depletion times compared to the Hubble time dictate that star-forming galaxies would rapidly exhaust their gas reservoirs [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract).

## Gas Fraction in Hot Halos and the CGM

While cold interstellar gas dictates the instantaneous star formation within galactic disks, the majority of galactic baryons and metals actually reside outside the disk in the extended [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] (CGM) [Tumlinson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55..389T/abstract). In massive systems, this material primarily takes the form of a [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]] with temperatures ranging from $10^5\text{ K}$ to over $10^7\text{ K}$. For luminous [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], this hot X-ray emitting gas frequently dominates the total interstellar medium, contributing up to $\sim 10^{10} M_\odot$ to the overall system mass. In the Milky Way, this hot gas permeates the disk and extends into a large spherical volume spanning 60 to 200 kpc. Cosmological simulations reveal that the [[density-profiles|density profiles]] of this hot galactic halo gas exhibit a non-trivial dependence on the total mass profile of the underlying dark matter halo [Hansen et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...653L..17H/abstract). The mass of this extended halo peaks roughly 6 kpc from the Galactic center—likely a relic of historical active galactic nucleus (AGN) activity—and provides a sufficient baryonic reservoir ($M_b \approx 0.8-4 \times 10^{11} M_\odot$) to close the local baryon census [Nicastro et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160708364N/abstract).

The [[hot-gas-mass-fraction|Hot gas mass fraction]] is a dynamic component of the galactic baryon cycle. Cosmological simulations indicate that warm-hot ($10^5 \text{ K} < T < 10^6 \text{ K}$) and hot ($T > 10^6 \text{ K}$) ionized gas governs the overall mass accretion onto Milky Way-mass galaxies, flowing inward along filamentary structures [Joung et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759..137J/abstract). On larger scales, extensive Mg II absorption studies, such as the MAGG survey, reveal that the majority of metal-enriched cool halo gas around galaxies at $z \sim 1$ is actually associated with multiple-galaxy associations and groups rather than isolated single systems [Dutta et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.5022D/abstract). The thermodynamic stability of this halo is often modeled via a "precipitation limit." Both theory and observation suggest that when the ambient CGM pressure exceeds a critical threshold, thermal instabilities cause cold clouds to condense and "rain" toward the galactic center. This inflow provides fresh fuel for star formation and central black holes, which in turn trigger energetic feedback. This feedback reheats the [[super-virial-gas|Super-virial gas]], lowering the pressure and maintaining a self-regulating loop that suspends the CGM near the precipitation threshold [Voit et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190311212V/abstract).

On the extreme scales of galaxy clusters, the hot gas fraction ($f_{\rm gas}$) overwhelmingly dominates the baryonic mass budget. While the mass fraction of stars in rich clusters is typically only $\sim 1\%$, the X-ray emitting intracluster medium (ICM) makes up the vast majority of the remaining ordinary matter. In maintaining the thermodynamic state of this gas, active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback plays a crucial role; AGN-driven bubbles in clusters act as catalysts, effectively converting sound and shock wave energy into heat via a vortex field [Heinz et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...634L.141H/abstract). Because the deep gravitational potential wells of rich clusters are thought to retain a fair sample of the universe's matter, the cluster gas mass fraction is frequently utilized as a standard candle to constrain cosmological parameters. By equating the measured cluster $f_{\rm gas}$ to the universal baryon fraction $\Omega_b/\Omega_m$, astronomers can place constraints on the dark matter density and the Hubble constant [Wilson et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MPLA...21.2197W/abstract), [Samushia et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0706.1963S/abstract), [Gonzalez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513665G/abstract). To trace these cosmological fractions over time, the peak position of the Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect (SZE) power spectrum serves as a sensitive probe for evaluating the cosmic evolution, or relative constancy, of the cluster gas mass fraction across different epochs [Majumdar et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...555L...7M/abstract).

Despite its cosmological utility, the assumption that cluster gas fractions follow simple self-similar scaling laws is a subject of active tension in the literature. The complex physics of the ICM—including cool cores, non-thermal pressure support, and radial temperature dependencies—induces variations in $f_{\rm gas}$ that can bias cosmological parameter estimates, particularly when measurements are taken well inside the virial radius [Ferramacho et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007A&A...463..423F/abstract), [Sadat et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..3426S/abstract). Furthermore, [[gas-clumping|Gas clumping]] in cluster outskirts can lead to significant overestimates of the gas mass fraction if the local density structure is not properly modeled, as demonstrated by observations of the Virgo cluster [Mirakhor et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506..139M/abstract). To better capture these distributions, simple physical models for cluster gas profiles have been developed that utilize fewer parameters than the standard beta-profile while providing superior fits to the observed density structures [Patej et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...798L..20P/abstract). Consequently, while the hot gas fraction remains a powerful probe of both galaxy evolution and cosmology, extracting precise physical constraints requires rigorous accounting of the multiphase substructure.

## Chemical Evolution and the Effective Yield

The chemical properties of a galaxy provide a fossil record of its star formation history and the extent to which it has exchanged matter with the circumgalactic medium. In this context, the cold gas mass fraction ($f_{\rm gas}$) serves as a primary parameter regulating [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]].

In the idealized [[closed-box-model|Closed box model]], where a galaxy evolves in isolation without inflows or outflows, the metallicity $Z$ of the gas phase is entirely determined by the instantaneous gas fraction and the true nucleosynthetic yield $y$ (the mass of newly synthesized metals returned to the interstellar medium per unit mass locked in stars). Under the instantaneous recycling approximation, this relationship is expressed as:
$$Z = -y \ln(f_{\rm gas})$$

Observations, however, show that real galaxies rarely adhere to closed-box evolution. To quantify deviations from this model, it is useful to define the effective yield, $y_{\rm eff}$:
$$y_{\rm eff} = -\frac{Z}{\ln(f_{\rm gas})}$$
If a galaxy loses metal-enriched gas through feedback-driven outflows, the observed $y_{\rm eff}$ will be lower than the true yield $y$. Empirically, low-mass dwarf and disk galaxies—which typically maintain high gas fractions—exhibit substantially reduced effective yields. Because their shallow gravitational potential wells cannot efficiently retain supernova-heated gas, they experience significant metal-enriched outflows. Conversely, massive galaxies with deeper potential wells retain a larger fraction of their metals. This differential ability to retain enriched gas directly drives the fundamental mass-metallicity relation observed across galaxy populations.

Modern theoretical frameworks have largely shifted from closed-box models to the [[equilibrium-recycling-model|Equilibrium recycling model]] or "baryon cycle" paradigm [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). In this scenario, galaxies exist in a slowly evolving equilibrium balancing cosmological gas accretion, star formation, and feedback-driven outflows. The gas fraction acts as a buffer that regulates the scatter in observed [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]]. Fluctuations in pristine cold gas accretion drive coupled variations in both $f_{\rm gas}$ and $Z$: an increase in pristine gas supply temporarily elevates the gas fraction and dilutes the gas-phase metallicity, moving the galaxy below the mean mass-metallicity relation [De Lucia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.3215D/abstract). Conversely, a suppression of inflow allows star formation to consume the existing gas, simultaneously lowering $f_{\rm gas}$ and enriching the remaining interstellar medium, thereby driving the galaxy above the median relation.

The regulatory effect of gas flows on metallicity is even more pronounced in the early Universe. While local galaxies generally exhibit [[negative-metallicity-gradients|negative radial metallicity gradients]] (metal-rich centers and metal-poor outskirts), observations of star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 3$ occasionally reveal "inverse" gradients. In these systems, the central regions are less enriched than the outskirts, a phenomenon attributed to the rapid funneling and dilution by massive streams of primordial cold gas [Cresci et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.467..811C/abstract). In the local universe, spatially resolved studies demonstrate that gas-phase metallicity gradients vary systematically across the mass-size plane, where smaller galaxies at a fixed stellar mass exhibit flatter [[abundance-gradients|gradients]] than their larger counterparts [Boardman et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501..948B/abstract). Furthermore, high-redshift gas reservoirs show distinct chemical signatures, such as high $\alpha$-enhancement (e.g., $\text{O/Fe} \approx 2.2$ solar at $z \sim 2$). This indicates that the chemical evolution of these highly gaseous, early-Universe galaxies is dominated by the rapid recycling of core-collapse supernova ejecta, occurring well before Type Ia supernovae have time to contribute significantly to the iron peak [Strom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...925..116S/abstract). Interestingly, the chemical abundances observed in some of these high-redshift massive galaxies imply extremely short star formation timescales, which challenge standard evolutionary models of early chemical enrichment [Kobayashi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.540..205K/abstract). Over longer timescales, the detailed chemical evolution—such as the tracking of the gas-phase deuterium abundance—demonstrates the critical importance of stellar mass loss. Material returned from intermediate-mass stars profoundly affects these abundances and can ultimately dominate the fuel supply for sustained star formation in high-mass galaxies [van de Voort et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477...80V/abstract).

## Historical Development of Theoretical Frameworks

The historical understanding of the galactic gas fraction ($f_{\rm gas}$) was initially rooted in the [[closed-box-model|Closed box model]]. In this simplified theoretical framework, a galaxy begins its life with a fixed initial mass of pristine gas and evolves as an isolated system, slowly consuming its reservoir through star formation without exchanging mass with its environment. Under the instantaneous recycling approximation, the gas-phase [[metallicity|Metallicity]] ($Z$) is analytically tied to the instantaneous gas mass fraction via the relation $Z = y_Z \ln(f_{\rm gas}^{-1})$, where $y_Z$ is the true nucleosynthetic yield. However, observations of local disk galaxies and the cosmic chemical evolution of galaxies rapidly exposed the limitations of this model. Low-mass galaxies exhibit effective yields systematically lower than $y_Z$, requiring metal-enriched outflows, while the sustained [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] of high-redshift systems requires continuous gas replenishment from the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] to prevent them from rapidly exhausting their fuel [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract).

Recognizing that galaxies are fundamentally open systems, the theoretical paradigm shifted toward equilibrium frameworks, commonly referred to as [[bathtub-models|Bathtub models]]. In these models, a galaxy's gas fraction and star formation rate are governed by a slowly evolving balance between cosmic gas inflow, star formation, and feedback-driven outflows [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). The governing principle posits that the time derivative of the gas mass approaches zero over cosmological timescales, such that the inflow rate matches the sum of the star formation rate and the mass outflow rate. This continuous baryon cycle naturally explains the observed inverse correlation between stellar mass and gas fraction, as well as the fundamental scaling relations observed across cosmic time [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1354D/abstract). 

While the equilibrium scenario captures the median trends of galaxy evolution, real galaxies exhibit scatter around these scaling relations. [[stochastic-chemical-evolution-models|Stochastic chemical evolution models]] demonstrate that this scatter is primarily driven by fluctuations in gas accretion rates. An anomalous increase in the supply of cold gas temporarily drives $f_{\rm gas}$ up and dilutes the [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]], moving the galaxy below the median mass-metallicity relation. Conversely, a reduction in gas supply allows star formation to consume the remaining gas, lowering $f_{\rm gas}$ and enriching the interstellar medium as the galaxy slowly returns to equilibrium [De Lucia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.3215D/abstract).

Today, these analytic and [[idealized-galaxy-models|Idealized galaxy models]] are routinely integrated into comprehensive cosmological simulations and semi-analytical frameworks, such as the Munich galaxy formation model. In these advanced models, the overarching dark matter halo establishes the initial gravitational potential and sets the gas cooling timescales [White et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997gsr..proc....3W/abstract). Recent computational advancements include two-fluid frameworks, such as the Cold Gas Subgrid Model (CGSM), which are designed to better capture unresolved multi-phase gas interactions and thermodynamic transitions within the galactic baryon cycle [Butsky et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240203419B/abstract). Ultimately, it is the complex interplay of ejective feedback (such as supernovae blowing cold gas out of the disk) and preventive feedback (such as active galactic nuclei heating the halo to suppress further accretion) that dictates the retained gas fraction and the long-term evolutionary trajectory of the galaxy [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite the establishment of robust local scaling relations for galactic gas fractions, several critical challenges remain in understanding the cosmic baryon cycle. A primary observational hurdle is accurately tracing the molecular gas mass ($M_{\rm H_2}$) in [[low-metallicity-environments|Low-Metallicity Environments]]. In these regimes, the traditional CO-to-H$_2$ conversion factor ($\alpha_{\rm CO}$) breaks down or scales steeply with metallicity ($Z^{-1.55}$) [Hunt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.180H/abstract). Independently, the conversion factor for dense gas tracers (such as HCN) exhibits a similarly steep metallicity scaling ($Z^{-1.53}$) [Patra et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250307931P/abstract). At metallicities as low as $12+\log(\text{O/H}) \approx 7.8$, CO-blind or "dark" molecular gas can dominate the cold gas budget, with true H$_2$ masses up to a factor of $\sim 100$ larger than CO-based estimates [Togi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...830...18T/abstract). Future observations utilizing direct mid-infrared rotational H$_2$ emission to probe warm gas ($T \gtrsim 80$ K) with the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), combined with high-resolution sub-millimeter mapping from ALMA, are essential for penetrating these elusive reservoirs. Furthermore, the exact physical driver of the atomic-to-molecular transition—specifically whether it is governed by global hydrostatic pressure or local gas and dust shielding—remains a subject of active theoretical debate requiring high-resolution validation [Fumagalli et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...722..919F/abstract).

At the low-mass end of the galaxy spectrum, the efficiency of baryon retention poses a major theoretical puzzle. Recent rotation curve decompositions have revealed a population of "baryon-deficient dwarfs" exhibiting stellar masses $1$ to $1.5$ orders of magnitude lower than predicted by current hydrodynamical simulations [Mancera Piña et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522727M/abstract). In dwarf galaxies where $M_* \lesssim 3 \times 10^9 M_\odot$, the interstellar medium often appears to be overwhelmed by continuous H I accretion, creating a bottleneck where star formation cannot keep pace with the gas supply [Hunt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.180H/abstract). Resolving why these systems fail to convert their gas into stars—and determining their potential evolutionary links to nearly starless [[dark-galaxies|Dark galaxies]]—requires a more precise accounting of the [[total-mass-astrophysics|Total mass (astrophysics)]] and feedback processes operating in shallow potential wells.

Scaling relations that link gas kinematics to mass also present unresolved tensions. The [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] (BTFR) universally correlates baryonic mass with rotational velocity across five orders of magnitude for individual galaxies, but galaxy clusters often exhibit a parallel, offset relation. It remains ambiguous whether this offset is an observational artifact driven by intracluster medium (ICM) physics, such as non-thermal pressure support and [[gas-clumping|Gas clumping]], which bias gas mass fraction measurements [Ferramacho et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007A&A...463..423F/abstract), [Mirakhor et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506..139M/abstract). Alternatively, some specific alternative quantum gravity frameworks, such as the Nexus Paradigm, propose this offset could reflect an exponential evolution of the BTFR normalization depending on the formation epoch [Marongwe et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251120188M/abstract), though this explanation remains entirely outside mainstream astrophysical consensus.

Finally, the ultimate cause of the cosmic decline in star formation since $z \sim 2$ remains contested. While early universe galaxies possessed substantially higher gas fractions, it is unclear if the observed drop in the cosmic star formation rate density is primarily due to galaxies progressively depleting their total gas reservoirs, or if there is a systematic decrease in the specific efficiency with which the available cold gas is converted into stars [Popping et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449..477P/abstract), [Kudritzki et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910...87K/abstract). Unraveling whether gas supply or star formation efficiency dictates quenching will be a major directive for next-generation multi-wavelength surveys.
