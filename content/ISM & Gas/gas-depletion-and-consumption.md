## Article

**Gas depletion and consumption** govern the lifecycle of a galaxy's [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] reservoir, dictating its capacity to sustain star formation over cosmic time. At the core of this framework are two fundamental metrics: the [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]] ($f_{\rm gas}$), which quantifies the relative mass of available interstellar fuel, and the [[depletion-time|Depletion time]] ($\tau_{\rm dep}$), defined as the ratio of the available gas mass to the current [[instantaneous-star-formation-rate|Instantaneous star formation rate]] ($\tau_{\rm dep} = M_{\rm gas}/\dot{M}_\star$). In local [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], a tight linear correlation exists between the surface density of [[molecular-gas-and-cloud-evolution|molecular hydrogen]] and the star formation rate, yielding a remarkably uniform molecular gas depletion timescale on kiloparsec scales [Bigiel et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...730L..13B/abstract). However, this timescale is observed to decrease systematically at higher redshifts, reflecting the heightened [[average-star-formation-efficiency|Average star formation efficiency]] characteristic of the early Universe [Genzel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800...20G/abstract). Together, these metrics delineate the empirical foundation of [[h2-regulated-star-formation|H2-regulated star formation]] and are used to distinguish quiescent galactic disks from extreme [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|starburst environments]].

Because the characteristic depletion time of a typical spiral galaxy is substantially shorter than the Hubble time, galaxies cannot evolve as closed systems; they require continuous [[galaxy-gas-replenishment|Galaxy gas replenishment]] to survive. Modern theoretical frameworks, such as [[bathtub-models|Bathtub models]] or the [[equilibrium-recycling-model|Equilibrium recycling model]], posit that galaxies exist in a slowly evolving steady state. In this paradigm, the interstellar medium is regulated by a continuous baryon cycle: the consumption of gas via star formation is balanced by [[galactic-gas-inflow-and-infall|Galactic Gas Inflow and Infall]] from the cosmic web, and simultaneous [[gas-expulsion|Gas expulsion]] driven by [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]]. Energetic input from stellar feedback and active galactic nuclei (AGN) can expel substantial amounts of gas or heat the halo to prevent future accretion [Lu et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150402109L/abstract). This intricate balance regulates the chemical enrichment of the galaxy, leaving a fossil record of gas consumption encoded in its stellar [[abundance-gradients|Abundance gradients]].

When gas depletion and feedback-driven ejective processes irreversibly outpace accretion, galaxies undergo [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]], transitioning from active star formation to become [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]]. This terminal exhaustion of fuel can be triggered by internal structural drivers, such as massive gas inflows driven by gravitational torques toward the perturbed galactic nucleus during major mergers [Barnes et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998giis.conf..275B/abstract), or by external [[environmental-quenching|Environmental quenching]] mechanisms like ram-pressure stripping and [[suffocation-astrophysics|Suffocation (astrophysics)]] in dense galaxy clusters [Kimm et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...729...11K/abstract), [Nichols et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732...17N/abstract). This article explores the full evolutionary trajectory of galactic gas reservoirs, from fundamental scaling relations and equilibrium models to the ultimate death of galaxies. Furthermore, it surveys active frontiers and open questions in the field, including the physical breakdown of depletion scaling laws on sub-cloud scales [Schruba et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...722.1699S/abstract), tensions surrounding the star formation efficiencies of dwarf galaxies, and the elusive physics of the multiphase circumgalactic medium that ultimately controls the galactic gas supply [Tumlinson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55..389T/abstract).

## Fundamental Metrics: Gas Mass Fraction and Depletion Timescale

To quantify the consumption of [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] reservoirs in galaxies, two fundamental metrics are universally employed: the gas mass fraction and the gas depletion timescale. These parameters dictate the evolutionary trajectory of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] and provide the empirical foundation for models of galactic baryon cycling. For large surveys where direct [[molecular-gas-and-cloud-evolution|molecular gas]] measurements are observationally expensive or unavailable, empirical "dirt-cheap" scaling relations have been developed that use proxy measurements such as dust attenuation ($A_v$) and stellar half-light radius ($R_{50}$) to efficiently predict molecular gas masses [Yesuf et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884..177Y/abstract).

The cold [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]] characterizes the relative mass of the fuel reservoir available for star formation, typically defined as:
$$ f_{\rm gas} = \frac{M_{\rm cold}}{M_{\rm cold} + M_\star} $$
where $M_{\rm cold}$ is the mass of the cold interstellar medium (typically atomic and molecular hydrogen, including a correction for helium) and $M_\star$ is the [[galaxy-mass-fractions|stellar mass]]. In the local Universe, $f_{\rm gas}$ varies strongly with galaxy morphology, luminosity, and surface brightness, presenting a strong correlation that indicates galaxies evolve at a roughly fixed size [McGaugh et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...481..689M/abstract). While massive ellipticals have nearly negligible gas fractions, the Milky Way operates with $f_{\rm gas} \sim 0.1$, and some low surface brightness disk galaxies can exhibit $f_{\rm gas} > 0.9$. Furthermore, the fact that many gas-rich galaxies are still actively forming stars at $z=0$ implies either a lengthy, continuous formation history or a [[delayed-star-formation|delayed onset of star formation]] within tenuous disks [McGaugh et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...481..689M/abstract). Observational surveys have shown that $f_{\rm gas}$ was systematically higher in the past, scaling approximately as $(1+z)^{2.5}$ to $(1+z)^{3}$, meaning high-redshift main-sequence galaxies were overwhelmingly gas-dominated compared to their local counterparts [Genzel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800...20G/abstract), [Tacconi et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...853..179T/abstract). However, recent ALMA stacking analysis for a mass-complete sample at $1 \le z \le 3$ suggests that gas fractions for blue, main-sequence objects are lower than those estimated by earlier studies biased toward individually detected bright sub-millimeter galaxies [Mérida et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..64M/abstract).

The gas [[depletion-time|Depletion time]], $\tau_{\rm dep}$, measures how long a galaxy could sustain its current [[instantaneous-star-formation-rate|Instantaneous star formation rate]] (SFR) before exhausting its gas reservoir, assuming no further gas accretion:
$$ \tau_{\rm dep} = \frac{M_{\rm gas}}{\dot{M}_\star} = \frac{\Sigma_{\rm gas}}{\Sigma_{\rm SFR}} $$
This timescale is the inverse of the [[average-star-formation-efficiency|Average star formation efficiency]]. For [[quiescent-galaxies|quiescently]] star-forming spiral galaxies, the total gas depletion timescale is typically $1-5$ Gyr. Theoretical models propose that these characteristically long gas depletion times physically originate from the continuous cycling of gas into and out of star-forming states multiple times before it is ultimately converted into stars [Semenov et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...845..133S/abstract). In contrast, in merger-driven [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|starburst environments]], $\tau_{\rm dep}$ drops precipitously to $\sim 10^7 - 10^8$ yr, which is comparable to the dynamical free-fall time of the cold gas [Gao et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...512L..99G/abstract). Furthermore, evaluating these timescales accurately depends strongly on the methodologies employed. In massive $z \sim 1$ galaxies undergoing [[galaxy-quenching|quenching]], inferred depletion times can vary significantly (by up to a factor of 6) based on the specific [[star-formation-rate-tracers|star formation rate tracer]] used, with spectral fitting methods providing more consistent results for these transitioning systems [Belli et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909L..11B/abstract).

Studies resolving the [[volumetric-star-formation-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]] on kiloparsec scales demonstrate that star formation correlates much more tightly with the molecular hydrogen (H$_2$) surface density than with the total or atomic gas. In the disks of nearby, normal star-forming galaxies, the molecular gas depletion timescale is remarkably uniform, with a median value of $\tau_{\rm dep, H_2} \approx 2.35$ Gyr [Bigiel et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...730L..13B/abstract). This global uniformity suggests that star formation proceeds in a regulated, roughly universal manner within giant molecular clouds across different galactic disks. Over cosmic time, the molecular depletion timescale decreases moderately, scaling as $\tau_{\rm dep} \propto (1+z)^{-0.3}$ to $(1+z)^{-0.6}$ [Genzel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800...20G/abstract), [Tacconi et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...853..179T/abstract). To unify these metrics and reduce intrinsic scatter across diverse environments, a multi-freefall star formation law has been proposed. This framework defines the gas consumption rate relative to a turbulence-regulated maximum possible rate, successfully spanning Milky Way clouds to high-redshift disk and starburst galaxies [Salim et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806L..36S/abstract).

Despite the tight linear correlation on kiloparsec scales, the relationship breaks down at spatial resolutions below $\sim 300$ pc. On these small scales, $\tau_{\rm dep}$ exhibits massive scatter driven by the discrete evolutionary lifecycles of individual star-forming regions and molecular clouds. Furthermore, variations in $\tau_{\rm dep}$ exist globally across different galactic environments. For instance, the [[central-molecular-gas|central regions]] of many galaxies exhibit shorter depletion times ($\sim 1$ Gyr) compared to their extended disks, a phenomenon likely associated with enhanced molecular gas compression by the steep stellar gravitational potential [Utomo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...849...26U/abstract). 

The behavior of $\tau_{\rm dep}$ in [[dwarf-galaxy-physical-processes|dwarf galaxies]] remains a subject of active debate. While traditional star formation models and standard Kennicutt-Schmidt implementations assume that dwarfs have fundamentally lower star formation efficiencies, some revised frameworks—such as those incorporating an integrated galactic initial mass function (IGIMF)—suggest that dwarf irregulars actually possess depletion timescales ($\sim 3$ Gyr) that are entirely consistent with those of large spiral disks [Pflamm-Altenburg et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...706..516P/abstract). Explicitly resolving this tension is critical for understanding whether low-mass galaxies are inherently inefficient at forming stars or simply subject to different stellar feedback and initial mass function constraints.

## The Cold Gas Reservoir: Atomic vs. Molecular Phases

The cold interstellar medium of galaxies is primarily partitioned into [[multi-gas-phase-components|two phases]]: neutral atomic gas (HI) and molecular gas (H$_2$). Because star formation occurs almost exclusively within the dense, shielded environments of [[molecular-gas-and-cloud-evolution|molecular clouds]], the galaxy-wide [[instantaneous-star-formation-rate|star formation rate]] (SFR) is fundamentally governed by the molecular reservoir rather than the total gas content—a paradigm often referred to as [[h2-regulated-star-formation|H2-regulated star formation]].

The partitioning of the atomic and molecular phases relies heavily on the physical conditions of the interstellar medium. Theoretical models propose two primary drivers for the HI-to-H$_2$ conversion: hydrostatic pressure dictated by the local gravitational potential of gas and stars, and self-shielding mediated by gas [[column-density-threshold|column density]] and dust ([[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|metallicity]]). Observations on sub-100 pc scales indicate that gas and dust shielding is the dominant factor controlling the molecular [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]], particularly in low-metallicity environments [Fumagalli et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...722..919F/abstract), [Krumholz et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...699..850K/abstract).

On kiloparsec scales, the tight coupling between H$_2$ and star formation manifests as a linear [[volumetric-star-formation-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt relation]], yielding the remarkably constant molecular [[depletion-time|Depletion time]] observed in nearby massive disk galaxies [Bigiel et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...730L..13B/abstract). As noted above, this coupling evolves over cosmic time, reflecting the highly efficient star formation characteristic of high-redshift galaxies [Genzel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800...20G/abstract), [Tacconi et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...853..179T/abstract).

While bulk molecular gas is traced by CO emission, identifying the highest-density cloud cores requires [[dense-gas-tracers|Dense gas tracers]] such as HCN and HCO$^+$ [Kepley et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...780L..13K/abstract). It has historically been assumed that the luminosity of these dense gas tracers converts to mass via a universal constant. However, recent studies reveal that this conversion factor strongly depends on the local environment, scaling inversely with metallicity (e.g., $\alpha_{\rm HCN} \propto Z^{-1.53}$). Applying a uniform conversion factor may therefore systematically bias estimates of dense gas mass and star formation efficiency across different galactic environments [Patra et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250307931P/abstract).

Despite the supremacy of H$_2$ in driving star formation, HI constitutes a vital component of [[cold-gas-reservoirs|Cold gas reservoirs]]. Observations demonstrate that atomic hydrogen has dominated the total cold gas mass budget of star-forming disks for at least the past three billion years ($z \sim 0.2$) [Cortese et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848L...7C/abstract). Yet, in HI-dominated regions at higher redshifts ($z \sim 1-3$), the SFR efficiency is observed to be merely $1\%-3\%$ of the efficiency predicted by the standard Kennicutt-Schmidt relation [Rafelski et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...825...87R/abstract). Thus, atomic gas primarily acts as a diffuse, intermediate buffer: it accretes from the [[extra-planar-and-circumgalactic-gas|circumgalactic environment]], slowly condenses into H$_2$, and only then fuels the actively star-forming molecular phase [Hunt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.180H/abstract).

## Gas Replenishment: Accretion and Infall Mechanisms

Normal disk galaxies exhibit gas consumption times of [[depletion-time|$\tau_{\rm dep} \simeq 1-5$ Gyr]], yet they maintain active star formation over the much longer Hubble time. This apparent paradox necessitates a continuous process of [[galaxy-gas-replenishment|Galaxy gas replenishment]] from the [[extra-planar-and-circumgalactic-gas|circumgalactic medium]] (CGM) and the broader cosmic web [Beckman et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..119B/abstract), [Richter et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430...15R/abstract). The modern paradigm for galaxy evolution has largely shifted away from closed-box chemical models toward a "baryon cycle" or [[equilibrium-recycling-model|equilibrium framework]], where galaxies exist in a slowly evolving balance between [[net-gas-inflows|Net gas inflows]], ongoing star formation, and feedback-driven [[galaxy-outflows|outflows]] [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract).

Two primary mechanisms govern the infall of fresh gas: cosmological [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] and the re-accretion of previously ejected material. Cosmological accretion is broadly divided by a temperature threshold near $T \sim 10^5$ K. At high redshifts ($z \sim 2-4$), galaxy growth is dominated by [[gas-accretion-modes|Cold-mode accretion]]—anisotropic inflows of relatively unshocked, pristine gas moving along cosmic web filaments directly into the galaxy's potential well [Woods et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442..732W/abstract), [Stewart et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..249S/abstract). In contrast, hot-mode accretion involves gas that shock-heats to the halo virial temperature upon entry, forming a diffuse reservoir that gradually cools to fuel late-time star formation. At higher redshifts, [[gaseous-halos|Damped Ly$\alpha$ systems (DLAs)]] act as vast neutral-gas reservoirs that provide crucial observational evidence for continuous gas accretion from the intergalactic medium to replenish galactic consumption [Wolfe et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ARA&A..43..861W/abstract).

Alongside pristine accretion, [[wind-recycling|Wind recycling]] plays a critical role in sustaining galaxies at late epochs. Gas ejected by [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|stellar feedback]] or AGN feedback is often enriched with heavy elements, allowing it to cool more efficiently than pristine gas. This material can fall back onto the disk via [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]] [de Avillez et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..166..103D/abstract), [van de Voort et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..301V/abstract). Simulations indicate that in low-redshift systems, a substantial fraction of the inner CGM has previously cycled through the galactic interstellar medium, making recycling a dominant source of late-time star-forming fuel [Mitchell et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.2600M/abstract). Direct morphological evidence for gas accretion from recycled wind material has been captured by surveys such as SDSS-IV MaNGA, which serendipitously observed a large accreting H$\alpha$ complex falling back onto its host [Cheung et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832..182C/abstract). Similarly, at high redshift, observations utilizing the Keck Cosmic Web Imager (KCWI) have revealed luminous Ly$\alpha$ nebulae at $z=2.6$, demonstrating the presence of recycled metal-rich gas extending to enormous spatial scales of 122 kpc [Zhang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952..124Z/abstract).

Kinematically, accreting gas tends to approach the galaxy with specific angular momentum comparable to the disk edge. Analyses of observed [[abundance-gradients|metallicity gradients]] favor a scenario in which gas accretes onto disks with rotation velocities approximately 20-30% lower than the local circular speed [Fraternali et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181108432F/abstract). It typically settles in the galactic outskirts before migrating inward via slow radial transport, with [[net-accretion-rates|net mass fluxes]] of roughly $\sim 1 M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$ and radial speeds of $1-3$ km s$^{-1}$ [Trapp et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.4149T/abstract). The dissipation of accretion energy is also thought to be a significant driver of supersonic turbulence within the star-forming disk [Khochfar et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009RvMA...21...87K/abstract), [Goldman et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.2949G/abstract). For the Milky Way, the current net accretion rate is estimated to be a few solar masses per year, which is sufficient to offset its current star formation rate [Beckman et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..119B/abstract), [Richter et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430...15R/abstract). Moreover, the Milky Way's hot [[gaseous-halos|gaseous halo]] permeates a vast spherical volume extending out to at least 60-200 kpc. This hot reservoir features a density peak at roughly 6 kpc, which potentially acts as a relic of nuclear activity from 6 Myr ago [Nicastro et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160708364N/abstract).

While theoretical models overwhelmingly require continuous accretion, direct observational confirmation remains challenging due to the diffuse nature of the CGM. However, strong indirect evidence is found in chemical abundance patterns. Most notably, observations of $z \sim 3$ galaxies have revealed "inverse" metallicity gradients, where the central star-forming regions are less metal-enriched than the outskirts. This is interpreted as the direct dilution of central gas by pristine [[gas-accretion-modes|cold flows]] [Cresci et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.467..811C/abstract). More recently, direct kinematic signatures of inspiraling cool gas have been detected around high-redshift star-forming galaxies using background quasar sightlines, and corroborated by mock sightlines in cosmological simulations, yielding mass inflow rates dynamically comparable to the host galaxies' star formation rates [Bouché et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Sci...341...50B/abstract), [Ho et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...875...54H/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Sci...380..494Z/abstract). 

Despite this emerging observational consensus, tensions remain in the literature. It is still heavily debated what the exact proportional breakdown is between pristine cold accretion and enriched wind recycling as a function of redshift and halo mass, and precisely how highly anisotropic cosmic filaments couple to the rotating disks of massive galaxies [van de Voort et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..301V/abstract), [Trapp et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.4149T/abstract).

## Galactic Feedback and Ejective Processes

To prevent the rapid over-consumption of [[cold-gas-reservoirs|Cold gas reservoirs]] and to regulate galaxy growth over cosmic time, mechanisms that drive [[gas-expulsion|Gas expulsion]] are required. In the absence of such [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]], standard cooling models predict that gas would collapse and form stars on a free-fall time, severely overpredicting the baryonic mass fraction locked in stars. Instead, galaxies exist in a slowly evolving equilibrium regulated by a combination of ejective feedback—which physically removes gas from the interstellar medium—and preventive feedback, which heats halo gas to suppress further accretion [Davé et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract), [Lu et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150402109L/abstract).

In star-forming galaxies, stellar feedback driven by supernovae, stellar winds, and radiation pressure serves as the primary engine for gas ejection. The efficiency of this process is parameterized by the [[mass-loading-factor|Mass loading factor]], $\eta$, defined as the ratio of the mass outflow rate to the star formation rate:
$$ \eta = \frac{\dot{M}_{\rm out}}{\dot{M}_\star} $$
Observations of high-redshift ($z > 5$) starbursts reveal massive [[galaxy-outflows|molecular outflows]] reaching velocities up to $800 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, carrying mass at rates comparable to the concurrent [[instantaneous-star-formation-rate|star formation rate]], implying $\eta \approx 1-2$ [Spilker et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Sci...361.1016S/abstract). In the local universe, hydrodynamic simulations focusing specifically on the [[central-molecular-zone|Central Molecular Zone]] of the Milky Way indicate that stellar feedback drives a multiphase wind where the warm phase dominates the mass flux—carrying $100\%$ to $200\%$ of the mass converted into stars within that nuclear region—though much of this material ultimately falls back onto the disk via [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]] [Armillotta et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.4401A/abstract). In low-mass [[dwarf-galaxy-physical-processes|dwarf galaxies]], bursty stellar feedback can induce "breathing modes": rapid fluctuations in the global gravitational potential that drive correlated variations in gas kinematics, size, and star formation [Luo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251017996L/abstract). Over cosmic time, these ejective processes are highly efficient; in simulated halos of mass $10^{11} < M_{200}/\text{M}_\odot < 10^{13}$, more than half of the accreted baryons are eventually ejected beyond the virial radius [Mitchell et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.2600M/abstract).

In massive galaxies, AGN feedback is widely considered the dominant mechanism driving [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] [Ma et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941..205M/abstract). Energy released during supermassive black hole accretion can inject immense kinetic and thermal energy into the surrounding gas. However, the precise coupling efficiency between AGN luminosity and the molecular gas reservoir remains a subject of active debate in the literature. While cosmological simulations require aggressive AGN feedback to match the observed properties of [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]], different models yield conflicting cold gas properties. For instance, the SIMBA simulation achieves agreement with observations by aggressively depleting the global cold gas reservoir, whereas IllustrisTNG retains excess cold gas due to a kinetic feedback implementation that redistributes, rather than ejects, the inner gas [Ma et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941..205M/abstract).

Direct observational evidence regarding AGN feedback efficiency is similarly mixed. Detailed multi-wavelength observations of local quasar hosts and [[galactic-nuclear-activity-sequence|Seyfert galaxies]] occasionally show a surprisingly low coupling efficiency between the AGN and the molecular outflows [Fei et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976..201F/abstract). In some cases, the molecular gas remains remarkably resilient to the AGN's energetic glare, maintaining smooth kinematics across the irradiated region [Rosario et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...875L...8R/abstract). Furthermore, large statistical samples indicate that while AGN frequently drive strong non-gravitational kinematics, galaxies with prominent outflows often exhibit specific star formation rates comparable to normal star-forming galaxies [Woo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839..120W/abstract). This suggests a distinct time delay: AGN-driven [[gas-expulsion|Gas expulsion]] may not immediately suppress star formation, requiring a dynamical timescale for negative feedback to fully propagate through the galaxy and halt the consumption of the gas reservoir. Moreover, the physical characteristics of these winds can self-limit their effectiveness; for example, the AGN-driven outflow in systems like NGC 1266 can reach extremely high electron densities (up to $4.2 \text{ cm}^{-3}$), making them among the densest known winds where short cooling timescales may lead to the rapid stalling of the outflow [Lopez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251001321L/abstract).

## Equilibrium Models and the 'Bathtub' Framework

Historically, the chemical and physical evolution of galaxies was often described using a [[closed-box-model|Closed box model]], which assumes that a galaxy evolves as an isolated system gradually consuming its initial gas reservoir. However, observational evidence, such as the persistent star formation in disk galaxies and the classical G-dwarf problem, demonstrated that isolated evolution is insufficient to explain the long-term star formation history of galaxies. The modern theoretical paradigm has thus shifted to open-system architectures, commonly referred to as [[bathtub-models|Bathtub models]] or the [[equilibrium-recycling-model|Equilibrium recycling model]] [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract).

In this framework, the interstellar gas mass ($M_{\rm gas}$) is regulated by a continuous baryon cycle consisting of gas accretion, star formation, and outflow-driven depletion. The mass balance of the [[cold-gas-reservoirs|Cold gas reservoirs]] can be expressed as a differential equation:
$$ \frac{dM_{\rm gas}}{dt} = \dot{M}_{\rm in} - \dot{M}_{\rm out} - (1 - R) \text{SFR} $$
where $\dot{M}_{\rm in}$ represents the [[net-accretion-rates|Net accretion rates]] of fresh or recycled gas, $\dot{M}_{\rm out}$ is the [[mass-loss-processes|mass loss rate]] due to [[galaxy-and-gaseous-outflows|Galaxy and gaseous outflows]], $R$ is the return fraction of gas recycled into the interstellar medium from dying stars, and SFR is the star formation rate. In addition to instantaneous feedback, delayed gas recycling from long-term stellar mass loss plays a profound role in regulating structural evolution. The return of gas from evolving stellar populations can reduce galactic bulge-to-disk ratios by up to a factor of 3, strongly promoting the survival of star-forming disks over cosmic time [Martig et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...714L.275M/abstract).

The outflow rate is often parameterized via the [[mass-loading-factor|Mass loading factor]], $\eta = \dot{M}_{\rm out}/\text{SFR}$. The exact mechanisms governing feedback—specifically whether it primarily ejects existing interstellar gas ($\dot{M}_{\rm out}$) or heats the circumgalactic medium to prevent new gas from accreting ($\dot{M}_{\rm in}$)—remain a subject of active debate, though both serve to regulate the galaxy's overall gas budget [Lu et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150402109L/abstract).

The core tenet of the bathtub framework is that galaxies naturally self-regulate toward a slowly evolving equilibrium state where the gas reservoir remains roughly constant over short timescales ($dM_{\rm gas}/dt \approx 0$). In this steady-state regime, the SFR is dictated entirely by the net supply of gas, leading to [[equilibrium-star-formation-rates|Equilibrium star formation rates]]:
$$ \text{SFR} = \frac{\dot{M}_{\rm in}}{\eta + 1 - R} $$
Consequently, the cosmic decline in star formation rates from $z \sim 2$ to the present day is interpreted not merely as galaxies running out of a finite initial gas supply, but rather as a result of the diminishing cosmic [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] rate relative to the gas consumption rate [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1354D/abstract). In support of this nuanced view, galaxy look-back evolution models suggest the cosmic decline in star formation is not driven primarily by the total exhaustion of gas reservoirs, but rather by a significant reduction in the fraction of the cold gas phase that remains capable of forming stars [Kudritzki et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910...87K/abstract). 

This equilibrium balance governs both the [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]] and the chemical evolution of galaxies. Metallicities are established by the competition between the enrichment from [[astration|stellar star formation]], dilution from pristine inflowing gas, and the expulsion of metals via outflows [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1354D/abstract), [Finlator et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..221F/abstract). When galaxies are perturbed from this equilibrium by stochastic variations in gas inflow, they tend to move temporarily off the main scaling relations, but are dynamically driven back toward the equilibrium state on the timescale required to process the excess gas [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract).

Quantitative constraints on the components of the bathtub model have been derived by fitting chemical evolution models to observed galaxy scaling relations. For example, observations of main-sequence [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] at $z \sim 1.4$ suggest an inflow rate of $\dot{M}_{\rm in} \approx 1.7 \times \text{SFR}$ and an outflow rate of $\dot{M}_{\rm out} \approx 0.4 \times \text{SFR}$ [Seko et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833...53S/abstract). This balance supports the assumption that the bulk of inflowing gas is efficiently processed by a combination of star formation and feedback.

## Chemical Evolution and Consumption Tracers

The chemical properties of a galaxy act as a fossil record of its formation history, encoding the ongoing balance between gas consumption, heavy element nucleosynthesis, and large-scale gas flows. Early analytical frameworks, such as the [[closed-box-model|Closed box model]], posited that galaxies evolve as isolated systems that gradually consume their initial gas reservoir. However, these models famously over-predict the number of metal-poor stars in the local Milky Way—the so-called 'G-dwarf problem'. Observations of stellar metallicities and light element abundances strongly indicate that the continuous accretion of low-metallicity gas onto the galactic disk is required to ameliorate this discrepancy and sustain star formation over Hubble times [Gibson et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..273..205G/abstract), [Beckman et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..119B/abstract).

To resolve these mass-budget and chemical issues, modern theories often treat galaxies as open systems governed by an [[equilibrium-recycling-model|Equilibrium recycling model]]. In this framework, a galaxy's [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]] and global metallicity are established by a slowly evolving balance between [[galactic-gas-inflow-and-infall|Galactic Gas Inflow and Infall]], star formation, and gas expulsion via [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]] [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). The global [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|mass-metallicity relation]] naturally emerges in such models because the mass outflow rate—and thus the retention fraction of synthesized metals—scales inversely with the depth of the galaxy's gravitational potential [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1354D/abstract). Simple analytical models that balance the gas accretion rate with combined star formation and outflow rates can successfully reproduce the mass-metallicity relation observed out to $z \sim 2$ [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract). Furthermore, models regulating chemical evolution directly by the star formation history (SFH) have successfully reproduced the metallicity enrichment history of dwarf galaxies assuming a uniform wind efficiency ($\eta \sim 1.0$) [Yin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958...34Y/abstract). Detailed measurements of the physical imprints of gas flows reveal that more massive galaxies have historically accreted more gas, and experienced the peak of this accretion at higher redshifts compared to lower-mass systems [Camps-Fariña et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...678A..65C/abstract).

Spatially resolved metallicities provide further constraints on localized gas consumption via [[abundance-gradients|Abundance gradients]]. Massive, star-forming galaxies typically exhibit negative [[o-h-radial-gradient|O/H radial gradient]]s, consistent with an inside-out disk formation scenario where gas is consumed and enriched more rapidly in the dense inner regions. However, the exact behavior of these gradients varies systematically across the galaxy mass-size plane. At a given stellar mass above $10^{10} M_\odot$, compact galaxies display flatter gradients than their more extended counterparts [Boardman et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501..948B/abstract). While galactocentric radius is a strong predictor of local metallicity in extended, lower-mass systems, local stellar mass density becomes equally predictive in compact, high-mass galaxies. This variation suggests that major mergers can flatten pre-existing negative gradients through radial gas mixing [Boardman et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.520.4301B/abstract).

At high redshifts, the canonical picture of negative gradients can break down entirely. Observations of rotationally supported star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 3$ have revealed "inverse" metallicity gradients, where the central star-forming regions are actually less enriched than the outskirts [Cresci et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.467..811C/abstract). This reversal represents a significant paradigm shift, providing compelling indirect evidence for the rapid accretion of pristine, primordial [[gas-accretion-modes|cold flows]] directly into the galactic center, which heavily dilutes the central enriched gas despite vigorous local star formation.

Finally, the interpretation of gas consumption timescales relies heavily on the assumed metallicity of the [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] reservoirs. The conversion factors used to derive molecular gas mass from emission tracers—such as the CO-to-H$_2$ conversion factor $\alpha_{\rm CO}$ and the dense gas tracer $\alpha_{\rm HCN}$—are strongly metallicity-dependent. For instance, observations suggest $\alpha_{\rm CO} \propto (Z/Z_\odot)^{-1.55}$ [Hunt et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...643A.180H/abstract), and a similar scaling is found for HCN in the Milky Way [Patra et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250307931P/abstract). Consequently, understanding [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|Galaxy chemical enrichment mechanisms]] is critical not only for tracking galaxy evolution but also for accurately calibrating these tracers to measure true gas depletion times.

## Structural Drivers: Mergers, Bars, and the Central Molecular Zone

The structural evolution of galaxies profoundly influences their gas kinematics, altering both the spatial distribution of the interstellar medium and the rate at which it is consumed. Internal non-axisymmetries and external tidal forces both act to remove angular momentum from galactic gas, funneling it toward the nucleus and triggering rapid periods of star formation. 

Internal dynamical instabilities, particularly Bar-driven inflow, are highly efficient at torqueing gas and driving it inward. In the Milky Way, the galactic bar channels [[molecular-gas-and-cloud-evolution|molecular material]] into the [[central-molecular-zone|Central Molecular Zone]] (CMZ), a dense region occupying the innermost $\sim 500$ pc of the Galaxy characterized by a unique high-density, high-temperature physical state compared to the broader galactic disk [Morris et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ARA&A..34..645M/abstract). Hydrodynamical simulations indicate that gas accumulates in dense streams or rings at radii of 200-300 pc, initiating oscillatory burst/quench cycles of [[star-formation-processes|Star Formation Processes]] with periods of tens to hundreds of Myr [Armillotta et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.4401A/abstract). In these systems, gas accretion plays a dual role in internal dynamics, both preventing inward flows at the outer Lindblad resonance during strong bar phases and later replenishing the disk to initiate multiple cycles of bar formation and destruction [Bournaud et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...392...83B/abstract). 

The extreme physical conditions within galactic centers significantly alter gas consumption metrics. Spatially resolved observations show that the molecular gas [[depletion-time|Depletion time]], defined as 
$$ \tau_{\rm dep} \equiv \frac{\Sigma_{\rm H2}}{\Sigma_{\rm SFR}} $$
is systematically shorter in galactic centers ($\sim 1$ Gyr) compared to extended disks ($\sim 2.4$ Gyr). This accelerated consumption is likely driven by the compression of molecular gas within the deep stellar gravitational potential [Utomo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...849...26U/abstract). However, the exact star formation efficiency in the CMZ remains subject to debate. Some evidence suggests that dense clouds in the CMZ exhibit a lower star formation rate per unit dense gas mass compared to the solar neighborhood [Parmentier et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903...56P/abstract). This tension may be partially alleviated by adopting metallicity-dependent conversion factors for [[dense-gas-tracers|Dense gas tracers]] like HCN; extending these scaling relations to the CMZ can reduce the inferred dense gas mass by factors of 2 to 3 [Patra et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250307931P/abstract). Additionally, observations reveal that the Schmidt index for dense gas in galactic nuclei appears significantly steeper ($N \sim 1.5$) than in global measurements, suggesting that the underlying [[volumetric-star-formation-law|Volumetric star formation law]] is what primarily drives nuclear gas consumption [Narayanan et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...681L..77N/abstract).

External structural drivers, notably [[gas-rich-galaxy-interactions|Gas-rich galaxy interactions]], provoke even more dramatic gas evolution. The framework of [[galaxy-merger-physics|Galaxy Merger Physics]] demonstrates that major mergers subject gas to severe gravitational torques and shocks. In "wet" mergers, the gas loses kinetic energy through radiative cooling and rapidly funnels into the nuclear region [Hernquist et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989Natur.340..687H/abstract). This sudden influx dilutes the central metallicity and triggers intense [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|Starburst Galaxy Evolution]] [Sparre et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.2720S/abstract). In [[luminous-gas-rich-disc-galaxies|luminous infrared galaxy (LIRG)]] mergers, the molecular gas content strongly correlates with the projected separation of the nuclei; as the merger advances, the global star formation efficiency surges, and the central gas reservoir is rapidly exhausted [Gao et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...512L..99G/abstract). Consequently, gas depletion times in starbursting merger remnants drop to $\sim 10^7 - 10^8$ yr, an order of magnitude faster than the $\sim 1 - 5 \times 10^9$ yr typical of quiescent disk galaxies.

While interactions drive massive nuclear inflows, they simultaneously redistribute gas to the extreme outskirts of the system. Recent high-resolution molecular gas observations of post-starburst galaxies reveal that major mergers can dynamically eject $\sim 50\%$ of the molecular gas reservoir into extended tidal tails reaching lengths of up to 65 kpc [D'Onofrio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250721249D/abstract), [D'Onofrio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990..166D/abstract). This severe tidal [[gas-expulsion|gas removal]] efficiently starves the central galaxy of its [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]], acting as a rapid mechanism for [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]] that operates in tandem with internal stellar and AGN feedback.

## Environmental Depletion: Ram Pressure and Strangulation

Galaxies evolving in dense cluster or group environments are subject to external mechanisms that accelerate gas depletion, collectively driving [[environmental-quenching|Environmental quenching]]. The passage of a galaxy through the dense, hot intracluster medium (ICM) subjects its interstellar medium to ram pressure. When the external ram pressure exceeds the restoring gravitational force of the galaxy, the cold gas reservoir can be completely and abruptly swept away. This process of [[gradual-gas-stripping|ram pressure stripping]] typically transforms star-forming spirals into gas-poor S0 galaxies by removing the fuel for star formation while leaving the stellar disk largely intact. If the ram pressure is weaker, it may only strip the lower-density outer regions of the gas disk. In this scenario, star formation can continue in the galactic center until the remaining fuel is exhausted. Observations of molecular gas in the extended outskirts of galaxies have proven to be sensitive tracers of ongoing ram pressure stripping in clusters [Watson et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161205275W/abstract). To quantify these environmental effects, recent analytical galactic chemical evolution (GCE) models have been developed to explicitly incorporate ram pressure stripping in dwarf galaxies, operating alongside continuous gas inflow and stellar winds [Kvasova et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982...92K/abstract).

A related but distinct process is strangulation, or [[suffocation-astrophysics|Suffocation (astrophysics)]]. Rather than directly stripping the cold gas disk, strangulation involves the removal of a galaxy's extended hot gaseous halo. In massive early-type galaxies, observations from the SLUGGS survey show that the X-ray gas luminosity scales linearly with total dynamical mass, suggesting that this extended hot gas is primarily shock-heated during initial halo infall rather than being purely sustained by internal feedback [Forbes et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464L..26F/abstract). Without this reservoir of shock-heated gas to cool and replenish the interstellar medium, the galaxy is forced to consume its existing cold gas reservoir over its standard gas [[depletion-time|depletion timescale ($\tau_{\rm dep}$)]]. Early semi-analytic models often assumed that a galaxy's hot [[gaseous-halos|halo]] was completely and instantaneously stripped upon accretion into a larger host system. However, this assumption predicted a stark overabundance of red, passive satellite galaxies—a tension known as the over-quenching problem [Kimm et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...729...11K/abstract). To resolve this discrepancy, modern hydrodynamical simulations and empirical models favor [[gradual-gas-stripping|Gradual gas stripping]], where the removal of the hot reservoir is delayed by roughly $\sim 2 \text{ Gyr}$, allowing satellites to retain up to $\sim 30\%$ of their initial hot gas for several gigayears after accretion.

In addition to hydrodynamic stripping, major mergers and strong tidal encounters can dynamically unbind gas, expelling substantial fractions of the molecular reservoir into extended tidal tails [D'Onofrio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990..166D/abstract).

The exact efficiency of these environmental stripping mechanisms remains a subject of active debate, particularly in low-density group environments where ram pressure is inherently weaker. In such regimes, theoretical models suggest that external stripping must operate in tandem with internal feedback. For example, heating from ancient stellar populations or early starbursts can decrease the gas binding energy, puffing up the interstellar medium to facilitate its removal by the tenuous coronal gas of a host halo [Nichols et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732...17N/abstract). Conversely, if the rate of gas return from evolving stellar populations is sufficiently high, the outflowing ejecta may act to resist ram-pressure ablation, potentially delaying environmental quenching for billions of years [Gisler et al. (1979)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1979ApJ...228..385G/abstract).

## The Path to Quiescence: Quenching and Galaxy Death

The cessation of star formation marks a galaxy's evolution into the population of [[passive-and-quiescent-galaxies|Passive and Quiescent Galaxies]]. This transition, driven by [[galaxy-quenching|Galaxy quenching]], requires the irreversible exhaustion, heating, or expulsion of the cold gas reservoir. As the interstellar medium is depleted, galaxies migrate off the star-forming main sequence, often passing through the intermediate regime of [[green-valley-galaxies|Green Valley galaxies]] before reaching a permanent quiescent state. Across cosmic time, the demographic fraction of these terminally quenched galaxies steadily grows at later epochs, strongly correlating with higher stellar mass and denser local environments [De Lucia et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250201724D/abstract).

A critical structural proxy for the quenching transition is the central stellar surface density within the inner 1 kpc, denoted $\Sigma_1$. Observations and hydrodynamical simulations reveal a tight correlation between $\Sigma_1$, the mass of the central supermassive black hole, and the depletion of the cold gas reservoir [Ma et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941..205M/abstract). As a galaxy undergoes central bulge growth, its molecular gas fraction undergoes a sharp decline once $\Delta\Sigma_1 > 0$. This threshold strongly coincides with a rise in active galactic nucleus (AGN) activity, demonstrating that $\Sigma_1$ acts as a reliable structural tracer of [[mass-quenching-efficiency|Mass quenching efficiency]] [Shread et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251118227S/abstract).

In massive galaxies, AGN feedback serves as the primary mechanism for quenching. It acts through ejective feedback (physically removing gas via outflows) and preventative feedback (heating the circumgalactic medium to halt [[galaxy-gas-replenishment|Galaxy gas replenishment]]). The cumulative energy released during black hole growth is largely responsible for global cold gas depletion [Ma et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941..205M/abstract). However, as discussed previously, the exact coupling efficiency of this feedback remains a subject of active debate, with some observations suggesting a delayed response governed by galactic dynamical timescales before star formation is fully suppressed [Rosario et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...875L...8R/abstract), [Woo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839..120W/abstract). Furthermore, cosmological simulations display varying degrees of success in capturing this gas clearance, heavily depending on how their kinetic feedback implementations redistribute or eject the inner gas [Ma et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941..205M/abstract).

Beyond internal AGN feedback, [[environmental-quenching|Environmental quenching]] accelerates gas exhaustion. In dense cluster environments, ram-pressure stripping and tidal interactions violently remove the interstellar medium, a process that can transform gas-rich spirals into gas-poor S0 galaxies [Gisler et al. (1979)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1979ApJ...228..385G/abstract), [Nichols et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732...17N/abstract). [[galaxy-merger-physics|Galaxy Merger Physics]] also dictates late-stage gas depletion, as major tidal interactions can strip a large portion of a galaxy's molecular gas into extended tails, severely stunting future star formation capacity in the remnant [D'Onofrio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250721249D/abstract).

The culmination of these quenching pathways is the emergence of "dead" galaxies with terminally exhausted gas supplies. However, these systems are not entirely devoid of complex baryonic physics; in massive elliptical galaxies, the hot gas reservoirs remain highly dynamic, shaped by stellar enrichment from aging populations and episodic heating by radio lobes [Mathews et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ARA&A..41..191M/abstract). In the most massive of these quenched systems ($M_* > 10^{11.5} M_\odot$), the signature of cold gas depletion extends well beyond the stellar disk into the [[gaseous-halos|Gaseous halos]]. This is evidenced by a marked suppression of H I Ly$\alpha$ absorption in the circumgalactic medium [Smailagić et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957...91S/abstract). Without a cool circumgalactic reservoir to fuel future accretion, the baryon cycle is broken, locking the galaxy into a passive state.

## Open Questions: The Multi-Phase CGM and Sub-Cloud Physics

While global scaling relations successfully describe [[integrated-star-formation|Integrated star formation]] on kiloparsec scales, a major open question remains the physical breakdown of the [[volumetric-star-formation-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt (KS) law]] at sub-kiloparsec scales. Observations reveal that below spatial scales of $\sim 300$ pc, the tight, linear correlation between the star formation rate surface density ($\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$) and molecular gas surface density ($\Sigma_{\rm H2}$) breaks down, exhibiting order-of-magnitude scatter in the local [[depletion-time|Depletion time]] [Schruba et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...722.1699S/abstract). This divergence reflects the discrete, highly dynamic lifecycle of individual star-forming regions. Recent high-resolution surveys of [[molecular-gas-and-cloud-evolution|Molecular Gas and Cloud Evolution]] indicate that giant molecular clouds (GMCs) collapse on free-fall timescales of $10-30$ Myr, but operate with remarkably low efficiencies per free-fall time ($\epsilon_{\rm ff} \approx 0.5\%$) before experiencing rapid ($< 5$ Myr) gas clearing by stellar feedback [Schinnerer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ARA&A..62..369S/abstract). The exact mechanisms regulating this inefficiency remain actively debated. Specifically, it is ambiguous whether internal cloud self-gravity or external galactic potentials dominate gas motions; analytical models emphasize that forces arising from the galaxy's gravitational potential (such as shear and Coriolis forces) can provide significant structural support that is distinct from conventional turbulence [Meidt et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...854..100M/abstract). (It is worth noting that while extreme supersonic turbulence, with Mach numbers peaking at $1.6-3.3$, has occasionally been cited in this context, such violent kinematics are characteristic of turbulent gas bridges in major galaxy mergers rather than typical GMCs in standard star-forming disks [Sparre et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.2720S/abstract).) Complementary analytical frameworks suggest that these star-forming regions form on timescales closely tied to the vertical turbulent crossing time of the disk ($\sim 3-30$ Myr), a timescale which naturally decreases in high-density environments as turbulence increases [Kocjan et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260202657K/abstract).

Scaling up to the galactic halo, the interface between the interstellar medium and [[extra-planar-and-circumgalactic-gas|Extra-planar and Circumgalactic Gas]] presents significant observational and theoretical frontiers. The circumgalactic medium (CGM) is a multiphase reservoir responsible for sustaining [[galaxy-gas-replenishment|Galaxy gas replenishment]] [Tumlinson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55..389T/abstract). However, the characterization of its lowest-density, small-scale components is an ongoing challenge. High-resolution cosmological simulations (e.g., FOGGIE) predict severe [[gas-clumping|Gas clumping]] in the CGM, resolving long-lived, cool ($\sim 10^4$ K) gas structures down to spatial scales of $R \sim 0.25$ kpc at $z=1$ [Augustin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250106551A/abstract). While these dense clumps are often metal-poor compared to the ambient diffuse halo, highly ionized species like O VI are predicted to glow brightly at the warm interfaces of these accreting clouds as they mix in a metal-enriched [[galactic-fountains|galactic fountain]] [Lochhaas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251025844L/abstract). Robustly observing this multi-phase accretion structure is vital for differentiating between pristine cold flows and recycled material. Theoretical models suggest the existence of a "precipitation limit", where the pressure of the circumgalactic medium is regulated by an internal feedback loop that suspends the diffuse gas precisely at the thermodynamic threshold for cold cloud condensation [Voit et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190311212V/abstract).

Further complications in our understanding of gas consumption arise during high-redshift evolution and in massive cluster environments. For instance, observations of damped Ly$\alpha$ systems at $z \sim 1-3$ show that the star formation efficiency in neutral atomic-dominated gas is anomalously low—roughly $1\%-3\%$ of the KS relation prediction. This creates a direct conflict in the literature: while many theoretical models predict that reduced star formation efficiency at high redshift is driven primarily by low metallicity, recent analyses suggest it is instead governed by a fundamental lack of molecular gas content [Rafelski et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...825...87R/abstract). Conversely, in the most massive environments, the exact mechanisms halting gas accretion remain unclear. Observations of luminous red galaxies with $M_* > 10^{11.5} M_{\odot}$ exhibit a distinct "cool gas depletion" signature, wherein H I Ly$\alpha$ absorption in the CGM is severely suppressed, indicating a truncation of the cold gas supply [Smailagić et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957...91S/abstract). How magnetic fields, active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback, and shock heating couple to selectively deplete specific thermal phases of the CGM remains an active area of investigation [Ayromlou et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231106339A/abstract), [Smailagić et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957...91S/abstract).
