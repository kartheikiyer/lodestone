**Submillimeter astronomy** is a branch of observational astrophysics that studies the universe at wavelengths between approximately 300 μm and 1 mm (frequencies from 300 GHz to 1 THz). Situated between [[far-infrared-astronomy|Far-infrared astronomy]] and [[millimeter-wave-astronomy|Millimeter-wave Astronomy]], this spectral window is uniquely sensitive to the cold, dense components of the interstellar medium (ISM). The primary physical processes observed in this regime include the thermal continuum emission from cold interstellar dust—typically modeled as a modified blackbody—and the rotational transitions of abundant molecules such as carbon monoxide (CO), which act as standard proxies for total molecular gas mass. Unlike [[visible-light|Visible light]] or [[near-ultraviolet-astronomy|Near-ultraviolet astronomy]], which predominantly trace unobscured stellar photospheres, submillimeter observations penetrate regions of high optical extinction to reveal the obscured, dusty nurseries where the majority of cosmic star formation occurs.

While observational astronomy was largely limited to optical wavebands until the mid-20th century, foundational interpretative frameworks for obscured star formation developed rapidly in the 1980s alongside pioneering space missions like IRAS [Neugebauer et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984Sci...224...14N/abstract). The field was fully revolutionized in the late 1990s by the deployment of multi-pixel bolometer arrays on single-dish observatories like the [[james-clerk-maxwell-telescope|James Clerk Maxwell Telescope]]. These instruments discovered a massive population of heavily obscured, high-redshift star-forming systems known as submillimeter galaxies (SMGs) [Sanders et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.269..381S/abstract)[Blain et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhR...369..111B/abstract). Though often completely invisible to deep optical surveys, SMGs are now recognized to make a major contribution to the cosmic star formation rate density at $z \sim 2-4$ and account for the bulk of the extragalactic background light at 850 μm. Today, studying these faint populations is frequently facilitated by gravitational lensing from massive foreground clusters, which magnifies background SMGs to observable flux thresholds and allows for detailed structural analysis on sub-kiloparsec scales.

Beyond the high-redshift universe, submillimeter [[astrophysical-spectroscopy|Astrophysical spectroscopy]] is a vital tool for probing the local universe, ranging from the complex astrochemical environments of nearby Seyfert galaxies and dwarf irregulars to the Milky Way's own Galactic Center. By observing high-critical-density molecules (e.g., HCN, CS) and fine-structure lines, astronomers can tightly constrain the thermodynamics and chemical enrichment of star-forming clouds. However, accurately interpreting these systems requires [[synergy-in-multi-wavelength-astronomy|Synergy in multi-wavelength astronomy]]. By anchoring the Rayleigh-Jeans tail of the spectral energy distribution (SED) with submillimeter data and combining it with [[near-infrared-astronomy|Near-Infrared Astronomy]], X-ray observations, and radio maps, observers can successfully disentangle the bolometric contributions of active galactic nuclei from intense nuclear starbursts.

Despite significant instrumental advances, such as the deployment of the [[herschel-space-observatory|Herschel Space Observatory]] and the [[atacama-large-millimeter-array-alma|Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA)]], several major open questions remain. A historic challenge in the field has been the "confusion limit," where the large beam sizes of single-dish telescopes artificially blended multiple discrete sources into single hyperluminous detections; high-resolution interferometry later revealed that source multiplicity significantly biased early cosmic star formation estimates [Hodge et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...768...91H/abstract). Additionally, astronomers are actively debating the exact redshift distribution of newly discovered "optically dark" and radio-dim SMG populations [Younger et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704..803Y/abstract), [Smail et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958...36S/abstract), as well as the physical reality of a reported 500 μm "submillimeter excess" in low-metallicity dwarf galaxies. Resolving these tensions and mapping the complete cosmic baryon cycle will rely on next-generation, large-aperture facilities like the [[fred-young-submillimeter-telescope|Fred Young Submillimeter Telescope]], which are designed to overcome current bottlenecks in survey speed and mapping depth.

## Introduction and Physical Foundations

Submillimeter astronomy spans the wavelength range between the far-infrared and the millimeter, typically defined as $300\ \mu\text{m}$ to $1\ \text{mm}$ (or frequencies from roughly $300\ \text{GHz}$ to $1\ \text{THz}$). Unlike [[visible-light|Visible light]] or [[near-ultraviolet-astronomy|near-ultraviolet observations]], which predominantly trace direct stellar emission, the submillimeter regime is fundamentally sensitive to the cold, dense components of the interstellar medium. 

The dominant continuum process observed in this window is thermal dust emission. Dust grains in the interstellar medium absorb rest-frame ultraviolet and optical light from young, massive stars and re-radiate this energy at longer wavelengths. This thermal emission is typically modeled as a modified blackbody spectrum:
$$ S_\nu \propto \nu^\beta B_\nu(T_d) $$
where $S_\nu$ is the flux density, $B_\nu(T_d)$ is the Planck function for a dust temperature $T_d$, and $\beta$ is the dust emissivity index. Observational studies utilizing facilities like the [[atacama-large-millimeter-array-alma|Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA)]] and the [[herschel-space-observatory|Herschel Space Observatory]] indicate that $\beta$ generally falls between $1.5$ and $2.0$ for both local galaxies and high-redshift sources, which is consistent with classical dust-grain models and Milky Way dust properties [da Cunha et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919...30D/abstract). The bulk of the dust mass in normal star-forming spiral galaxies is relatively cold ($T_d \approx 15\ \text{K}$ to $35\ \text{K}$ ), producing an emission profile that peaks in the [[far-infrared-astronomy|Far-infrared astronomy]] regime but provides a massive, optically thin Rayleigh-Jeans tail in the submillimeter [Alton et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...507L.125A/abstract). Empirical calibrations of galaxy SEDs demonstrate that the 60-to-100 μm flux ratio is an effective parameter for describing the global interstellar radiation field and modeling this resulting submillimeter emission [Dale et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...576..159D/abstract). Conversely, extreme environments—such as the hyperluminous "Hot DOGs" (Dust-Obscured Galaxies)—can exhibit heavily obscured dust temperatures ranging from $60\ \text{K}$ to $120\ \text{K}$ [Wu et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...756...96W/abstract).

In addition to the dust continuum, the submillimeter window hosts a rich spectrum of molecular rotational transitions, which govern the thermodynamics and cooling of dense molecular clouds. The most abundant trace molecule, carbon monoxide (CO), features a ladder of transitions that act as standard proxies for tracing the total molecular gas mass. Observations of CO transitions up to $J=5\rightarrow 4$ reveal widely varying excitation conditions across different cosmic environments, ranging from Milky Way-like low-excitation gas in some [[ultrabright-lensed-submillimeter-galaxy|ultrabright lensed submillimeter galaxies]] to highly excited, turbulent gas in major mergers [Sulzenauer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923L..27S/abstract). Furthermore, molecules with higher dipole moments, such as HCN, HCO$^+$, and CS, trace significantly denser gas and are heavily utilized to map the chemical and physical conditions of circumnuclear disks and starburst rings around active galactic nuclei [Izumi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...811...39I/abstract)[Takano et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PASJ...66...75T/abstract).

While observational astronomy was largely limited to optical wavebands until the mid-20th century, foundational interpretative frameworks for obscured star formation developed rapidly in the 1980s alongside pioneering space missions like IRAS [Neugebauer et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984Sci...224...14N/abstract). These foundational observations drove a paradigm shift in the understanding of the interstellar medium and cosmic star formation history. Findings from instruments such as DIRBE and FIRAS on the COBE satellite demonstrated that the energy in the submillimeter cosmic infrared background is comparable to the integrated light of all galaxies in UV and optical wavelengths [Hauser et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001IAUS..204..101H/abstract). In summary, they demonstrated that heavily obscured, dusty systems emit nearly half of the total cosmic energy density, marking a major phase of galaxy evolution that is entirely missed by traditional optical observations [Genzel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005mmgf.conf..112G/abstract)[Sanders et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.269..381S/abstract).

## Historical Development and Instrumentation

The historical development of submillimeter astronomy has been driven by the dual imperatives of overcoming severe atmospheric attenuation and achieving sufficient angular resolution to unambiguously identify deeply obscured cosmic sources. 

**Early Single-Dish Telescopes and the Bolometer Revolution**
In the late 1990s, the advent of multi-pixel **Bolometers** transformed the field. The Submillimeter Common-User Bolometer Array (SCUBA) on the [[james-clerk-maxwell-telescope|James Clerk Maxwell Telescope]] (JCMT) revolutionized extragalactic astrophysics by discovering a massive population of dusty, high-redshift star-forming galaxies, now known as submillimeter galaxies (SMGs) [Sanders et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.269..381S/abstract), [Blain et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhR...369..111B/abstract). These early arrays were limited to a few tens of pixels but proved that the submillimeter sky was rich with discrete sources, which are now known to contribute up to half of the cosmic far-infrared background. 

Instrumentation advanced rapidly to increase mapping speeds and focal plane coverage. Specialized adaptations were also made to existing facilities; for instance, a unique re-imaging device for the [[caltech-submillimeter-observatory|Caltech Submillimeter Observatory]] (CSO) successfully converted the 10.4-meter telescope into a small off-axis system to efficiently map large-scale [C I] emission in the Milky Way [Plume et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995PASP..107..488P/abstract). The quest to mitigate atmospheric interference also highlighted the potential of Antarctic-based infrared and submillimeter surveys, which benefit from extremely low backgrounds in their respective atmospheric windows [Bailey et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996PASA...13....7B/abstract). The transition from monolithic bolometers to highly multiplexed arrays yielded instruments like Bolocam and SHARC-II at the CSO [Haig et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004SPIE.5498...78H/abstract), [Khan et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...631L...9K/abstract). Using SHARC-II, astronomers discovered the first galaxy selected at 350 μm, identifying a population of star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 1$ with moderate dust temperatures ($\sim 30$ K) that might easily be missed by surveys at longer submillimeter wavelengths [Khan et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...631L...9K/abstract). This evolution culminated in instruments like **SCUBA-2** on the JCMT, which mapped the sky orders of magnitude faster than its predecessor [Robson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793...11R/abstract), [Wang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200107447W/abstract). More recently, the development of Kinetic Inductance Detectors (KIDs) has allowed for arrays exceeding $2,300$ detectors, such as the MUSIC camera, massively parallelizing data acquisition at submillimeter wavelengths [Duan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020SPIE11453E..1ZD/abstract).

**The Shift to Interferometry**
A fundamental limitation of single-dish submillimeter observations is the "confusion limit"—the blending of multiple faint sources within a large, diffraction-limited beam (typically $\sim 15''$ for a 15-meter dish at $850\,\mu\mathrm{m}$). To bypass this, the field embraced **Interferometry**. Early arrays like the Submillimeter Array (SMA) and the [[northern-extended-millimeter-array|Northern Extended Millimeter Array]] ([[noema|NOEMA]]) provided the critical sub-arcsecond localization required to match submillimeter sources with their optical and radio counterparts [Iono et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...640L...1I/abstract), [Smail et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958...36S/abstract). 

The commissioning of the [[atacama-large-millimeter-array-alma|Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA)]] represented a paradigm shift. With beam areas roughly 200 times smaller than typical single-dish surveys, ALMA definitively resolved the confusion limit. ALMA observations demonstrated that 35-50% of the ultra-luminous sources previously detected as single objects by single-dish telescopes are in fact blends of multiple, physically distinct galaxies [Hodge et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...768...91H/abstract).

**Space-based Observatories and Future Facilities**
Because atmospheric water vapor is highly opaque at submillimeter wavelengths, **Space-based observatories** have been essential for providing continuous spectral coverage. The Submillimeter Wave Astronomy Satellite (SWAS), launched in 1998, pioneered space-based heterodyne spectroscopy targeting fundamental molecular coolants like $\mathrm{H_2O}$ and $\mathrm{O_2}$ [Melnick et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...539L..77M/abstract). Subsequently, the [[herschel-space-observatory|Herschel Space Observatory]] provided unprecedented sensitivity across the far-infrared and submillimeter regime, mapping dust mass and temperature in nearby galaxies and confirming the ubiquity of cold ($\sim 15-25\,\mathrm{K}$) dust [Dale et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...745...95D/abstract), [Kirkpatrick et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...778...51K/abstract).

Today, submillimeter astronomy increasingly relies on multi-wavelength synergies. ALMA's high-resolution dust continuum and molecular line maps are routinely paired with near- and mid-infrared data from the [[spitzer-space-telescope|Spitzer Space Telescope]] and the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] to characterize the stellar masses and dust attenuation of heavily obscured galaxies [Schulz et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023pcsf.conf..330S/abstract), [McKinney et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...979..229M/abstract). 

Looking forward, there is a recognized trade-off between the atmospheric stability of space-based platforms and the raw mapping speed and angular resolution of large ground-based apertures. Future ground facilities like the proposed 50-meter Atacama Large Aperture Submillimeter Telescope (AtLAST) [Liu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240301202L/abstract) and the 6-meter [[fred-young-submillimeter-telescope|Fred Young Submillimeter Telescope]] (FYST) [Chapman et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022SPIE12190E..05C/abstract) are currently being developed to bridge this gap, aiming to conduct massive spectroscopic and continuum surveys that will overcome current bottlenecks in field-of-view and mapping speed.

## High-Redshift Submillimeter Galaxies (SMGs)

Submillimeter galaxies (SMGs) are a population of massive, heavily dust-obscured galaxies in the high-redshift universe characterized by extreme star formation rates [Blain et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhR...369..111B/abstract). Typically boasting a [[far-infrared-luminosity|Far-infrared luminosity]] of $L_{\text{IR}} > 10^{12} L_{\odot}$, these systems form stars at rates frequently exceeding $1000\ M_{\odot}\ \text{yr}^{-1}$ [Frayer et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...506L...7F/abstract). An early prototype for this high-redshift SMG population was the extremely red object HR 10 (ERO J1645) at $z=1.44$, which provided early evidence of a dusty, interacting galaxy capable of such extreme star formation rates [Dey et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...519..610D/abstract). Another early example of a heavily obscured submillimeter galaxy is SMM J00266+1708, which exhibited a faint red counterpart ($K=22.5$) while remaining otherwise invisible to deep optical surveys [Frayer et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AJ....120.1668F/abstract). The discovery of SMGs prompted a paradigm shift in [[high-redshift-galaxy-observations|High-Redshift Galaxy Observations]]; while the cosmic star formation history was previously charted primarily via rest-frame ultraviolet and [[visible-light|Visible light]] dropouts, it is now established that SMGs account for roughly $30\%$ to $50\%$ of the extinction-corrected star formation rate density at $z = 1 - 5$ [Sanders et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.269..381S/abstract), [Barger et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761...89B/abstract). Furthermore, they are responsible for nearly all of the $850\ \mu\text{m}$ extragalactic background light [Sanders et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.269..381S/abstract).

Early single-dish submillimeter surveys were heavily restricted by the confusion limit, leading to the assumption that bright submillimeter detections corresponded to single, hyperluminous galaxies. However, high-resolution interferometry has revealed that a large fraction (up to half) of single-dish submillimeter sources resolve into multiple, discrete fainter galaxies [Barger et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761...89B/abstract), [Hodge et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...768...91H/abstract). This multiplicity significantly complicates the derivation of intrinsic luminosities and necessitates revisions to previously calculated cosmic star formation rate densities [Chen et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...789...12C/abstract).

The bulk of the SMG population resides at "Cosmic Noon" with a median redshift of $z \sim 2$ [Chapman et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...548L.147C/abstract). However, the exact redshift distribution of the faintest, most heavily obscured sources remains a subject of active debate. Observations have identified a high-redshift tail ($z > 4$) of "optically dark" SMGs that were previously missed by [[radio-surveys|Radio surveys]] and optical selection techniques. For example, some ultramassive SMGs at $z > 4$ are virtually invisible in [[hubble-space-telescope-deep-surveys|Hubble Space Telescope Deep Surveys]] and remain undetected at $\lambda \lesssim 2\ \mu\text{m}$ even by the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] [Smail et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958...36S/abstract). The existence of such hidden giants illustrates the severe incompleteness of rest-frame optical catalogs.

SMGs are widely considered to be the progenitors of present-day massive elliptical and spheroidal galaxies [Sanders et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.269..381S/abstract), [Genzel et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005mmgf.conf..112G/abstract). [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral field spectroscopy]] further supports this evolutionary link, indicating that SMGs are the high-redshift precursors to massive local bulges [Eisenhauer et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Msngr.113...17E/abstract). Moreover, deep optical imaging of SMGs reveals a predominance of irregular and elongated morphologies, suggesting that major mergers are the primary drivers of their extreme star formation rates [Chapman et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...599...92C/abstract). They frequently reside in compact overdense regions (spanning $\sim 170\ \text{kpc}$) and serve as signposts for high-redshift protoclusters [Aravena et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...708L..36A/abstract). Gravitational lensing by foreground clusters is often employed to probe these faint populations down to sub-mJy flux levels [Johansson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...527A.117J/abstract). Despite their general association with overdensities, the environmental bias of SMGs is not strictly uniform and remains a point of tension in the literature. Some extremely luminous, high-redshift sources, such as HFLS3 at $z=6.34$, show no significant evidence of residing in biased environments, suggesting they may represent short-lived, highly luminous phases in average-density regions rather than the cores of massive nascent clusters [Robson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793...11R/abstract). Conversely, studies of the environment surrounding the radio galaxy 4C41.17 at $z=3.8$ have revealed significant overdensities of submillimeter galaxies. The same field also happens to contain a physically unrelated, massive 110 kpc Ly$\alpha$ blob in the foreground at $z=2.672$ [Greve et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.382...48G/abstract). Finally, detailed studies of SMG properties remain a pillar of [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]].

## Astrochemistry and Molecular Tracers

Submillimeter [[astrophysical-spectroscopy|Astrophysical spectroscopy]] provides crucial diagnostic tools for probing the density, temperature, and chemical composition of the interstellar medium (ISM) in both local galaxies and high-redshift sources. Because cold molecular hydrogen ($\text{H}_2$) lacks a permanent dipole moment, its rotational transitions are invisible at typical molecular cloud temperatures. Consequently, submillimeter astronomy relies on a suite of secondary molecular tracers and fine-structure lines to map the molecular universe.

**CO Transitions and Molecular Gas Mass**
The rotational transitions of carbon monoxide (CO) are the standard proxy for total molecular gas mass. Multi-level observations, from CO($J=1-0$) up to CO($J=6-5$) and beyond, are used to constrain the excitation conditions of the gas. For example, in the luminous infrared galaxy NGC 1614, CO line modeling indicates the presence of cold ($T \approx 20-40\text{ K}$) and dense ($n > 10^3\text{ cm}^{-3}$) molecular gas with a $^{12}\text{CO}$-to-$^{13}\text{CO}$ abundance ratio exceeding 130 [Sliwa et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...796L..15S/abstract). Converting CO luminosities to $\text{H}_2$ mass relies on the empirically derived CO-to-$\text{H}_2$ conversion factor, $\alpha_{\text{CO}}$. However, the literature notes significant ambiguity in this factor; $\alpha_{\text{CO}}$ is highly uncertain in low-metallicity environments, such as dwarf galaxies, which complicates gas mass estimates in objects like the host galaxy of FRB 121102 [Bower et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018AJ....155..227B/abstract). Furthermore, extrapolating total gas masses exclusively from high-$J$ CO transitions can introduce significant uncertainties; for instance, observations incorporating foundational CO($1-0$) detections often reveal that the gas is sub-thermally excited in some SMGs [Hainline et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008PhDT.......251H/abstract).

**Fine-Structure Lines and PDRs**
Atomic carbon ([C I]) and ionized carbon ([C II]) are critical submillimeter tracers of photon-dominated regions (PDRs) and "CO-dark" molecular gas. The [C II] $158\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ fine-structure line is a dominant coolant of the ISM, accounting for approximately $0.2\%$ of the total bolometric luminosity in nearby starburst galaxies [Benford et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998SPIE.3357..278B/abstract). It serves as an excellent probe of interstellar matter in phases where CO does not emit [van der Tak et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35....2V/abstract). Additionally, observations with facilities like the [[caltech-submillimeter-observatory|Caltech Submillimeter Observatory]] have mapped the neutral carbon [C I] $^3P_1 \to ^3P_0$ transition at $492\text{ GHz}$, revealing that starbursts such as IC 342 require PDR models with higher gas densities than the Milky Way [Buettgenbach et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...397L..15B/abstract).

**Chemical Composition and Dense Gas Tracers**
Molecules with high critical densities, such as HCN, HCO$^+$, and CS, trace the dense cores ($n > 10^4\text{ cm}^{-3}$) of star-forming clouds. High-resolution interferometry with the [[atacama-large-millimeter-array-alma|Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA)]] resolves complex chemical gradients within galactic nuclei. In the Seyfert galaxy NGC 1068, species like SO and HC$_3$N are concentrated in the circumnuclear disk, while $^{13}$CO and C$^{18}$O dominate the surrounding starburst ring [Takano et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PASJ...66...75T/abstract). Submillimeter interferometry is also powerful for dissecting merging systems; for example, observations of the ultra-luminous infrared galaxy Arp 220 have resolved a binary nucleus, revealing that line ratios of HCN and CO differ between the components and indicate varying physical conditions across the merging nuclei [Wiedner et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...581..229W/abstract).

In the luminous active nucleus of NGC 7469, the overall spatial distribution of dense gas tracers is anti-correlated with optical light, effectively tracing dust-obscured star formation. However, elevated HCN($4-3$)/HCO$^+$($4-3$) and HCN($4-3$)/CS($7-6$) ratios are found highly localized to the AGN position itself, indicating that these elevated ratios are likely influenced by AGN heating rather than starburst activity. In similar Seyferts like NGC 1097, such ratios are speculated to be driven by AGN-related mechanical heating from a jet [Izumi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...811...39I/abstract). Furthermore, SiO serves as a well-established tracer of interstellar shocks; its abundance is significantly enhanced in regions undergoing cloud-cloud collisions, such as the Galactic Center [Huettemeister et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&A...334..646H/abstract).

**High-Redshift Applications**
In high-redshift submillimeter galaxies (SMGs), molecular tracers help constrain the extreme star formation rates and baryon cycles of the early universe, frequently revealing massive gas reservoirs ($M_{\text{gas}} \sim 10^{10} - 10^{11} M_\odot$) [Frayer et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...506L...7F/abstract). While many SMGs harbor highly excited, compact gas reservoirs, recent observations reveal a diversity of physical conditions. For instance, the highly magnified z=1.6 SMG WISE J122651.0+214958.8 exhibits "Milky Way-like" low-excitation molecular gas. Despite an apparent star formation rate of $\sim 2960\text{ }M_\odot\text{ yr}^{-1}$, its CO($J=5-4$) to CO($J=2-1$) luminosity ratio is remarkably low ($r_{5,2} = 0.11 \pm 0.02$), suggesting an extended, main-sequence mode of star formation during the epoch of peak cosmic star formation [Sulzenauer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923L..27S/abstract).

## Gravitational Lensing as a Tool for Submillimeter Astronomy

Gravitational lensing by massive foreground galaxy clusters acts as a "natural telescope" for submillimeter astronomy, magnifying the flux and expanding the solid angle of faint background sources. This phenomenon enables the detection of distant, heavily dust-obscured galaxies that reside far below the normal confusion limits of single-dish submillimeter telescopes.

**Probing the Faint Submillimeter Population**
By targeting the fields of well-modeled lensing clusters (such as Abell 370 and Abell 2390) at $850~\mu\mathrm{m}$, observers have successfully constrained the faint end of the submillimeter number counts [Cowie et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002AJ....123.2197C/abstract), [Chen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...733...64C/abstract). Once intrinsic fluxes are recovered using magnification maps derived from cluster mass models (e.g., superpositions of Navarro-Frenk-White halos), these surveys can probe down to sub-mJy levels ($S_{850} < 1~\mathrm{mJy}$) [Johansson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...527A.117J/abstract). Pioneering efforts such as the SCUBA Cluster Lens Survey summarized key multi-wavelength properties of this faint submillimeter population across wavebands spanning from X-rays to the radio [Smail et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001dmsi.conf....3S/abstract). These faint, lensed submillimeter galaxies possess moderate total infrared luminosities ($L_{\mathrm{IR}} \sim 10^{10}-10^{12}~L_{\odot}$) comparable to local luminous infrared galaxies, and they are estimated to account for roughly $70\%$ of the $850~\mu\mathrm{m}$ extragalactic background light [Chen et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...789...12C/abstract). While massive clusters are primarily utilized as foreground lenses to study background objects, the central galaxies within massive cooling-flow clusters can themselves be significant submillimeter sources, exhibiting dust emission fueled by intense star formation or obscured AGN [Edge et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.306..599E/abstract). Beyond merely acting as gravitational lenses, massive galaxy clusters are studied using the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect at millimeter and submillimeter wavelengths. High-resolution SZ imaging with facilities like CARMA allows astronomers to probe the thermal energy and resolve merger-induced structures within dense cluster environments, such as RX J1347.5-1145 [Plagge et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770..112P/abstract).

**High-Resolution Imaging on Sub-Kiloparsec Scales**
The magnification of angular size by gravitational lensing is particularly valuable for high-resolution imaging. When paired with interferometers like the [[atacama-large-millimeter-array-alma|Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA)]] and the Submillimeter Array (SMA), lensing facilitates studies of the interstellar medium and galaxy structure on sub-kiloparsec scales. In systems at $z > 4$, strong lensing has resolved individual structural components, revealing spiral arms, linear bars, and compact stellar cores ($R_{\mathrm{e}} \sim 1~\mathrm{kpc}$) hidden by massive optical attenuation ($A_V \sim 5$) [Smail et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958...36S/abstract). Pushing this synergy forward aims to ultimately resolve the kinematics of giant molecular clouds on physical scales of $40\text{--}100~\mathrm{pc}$ in the distant universe [Negrello et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PASA...37...25N/abstract). Additionally, lensing allows detailed molecular gas studies even with single-dish facilities. For instance, observations with the IRAM 30 m and Yebes 40 m telescopes of the "Cosmic Seahorse" (WISE J122651.0+214958.8), magnified by a factor of $\mu = 9.5 \pm 0.7$, permitted detailed measurements of low-excitation molecular gas in a starburst at $z=1.6$ [Sulzenauer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923L..27S/abstract).

**Ambiguities and Lensing Bias**
Despite its utility, lensing magnification can complicate the interpretation of submillimeter data. The literature contains active debate regarding whether some hyperluminous, high-redshift sources represent intrinsically extreme starbursts in average environments, or ordinary galaxies whose fluxes have been artificially boosted by an unrecognized foreground lens. For example, the intrinsic nature of the highly luminous $z=6.34$ galaxy HFLS3 remains ambiguous; a lack of dusty companions challenges the assumption that it marks a massive protocluster overdensity, leaving open the possibility of line-of-sight magnification [Robson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...793...11R/abstract). 

Similarly, evaluating the true luminosity of objects like the $z=1.3$ starburst MIPS J142824.0+352619 required targeted submillimeter interferometry to firmly bound the source size ($< 1.2''$) and confirm that the lensing amplification from a foreground elliptical galaxy was relatively modest [Borys et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...636..134B/abstract), [Iono et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...640L...1I/abstract). Accurate lens modeling is therefore essential to prevent lensing bias from skewing calculations of cosmic star formation rate densities and intrinsic galaxy properties in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]].

## Probing the Local Universe and Galactic Center

Observations of nearby galaxies at submillimeter wavelengths provide critical spatial resolution for dissecting the thermodynamics and vertical distribution of the interstellar medium. High-resolution submillimeter maps of edge-on spiral galaxies like NGC 891 have revealed massive reservoirs of cold ($\sim 15$ K) dust at large galactic radii that were largely invisible to earlier [[far-infrared-astronomy|Far-infrared astronomy]] surveys [Alton et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...507L.125A/abstract). These observations also confirm the presence of "dust chimneys"—structured outflows escaping the primary midplane absorption layer and extending to $z$-heights of nearly $2$ kpc [Alton et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...507L.125A/abstract), [Serabyn et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..177..207S/abstract). Similarly, $850$ $\mu$m imaging of the local starburst M82 tracks extended dust continuum emission driven by outflows up to $1.5$ kpc into the galactic halo [Leeuw et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AJ....137..517L/abstract).

Mapping the diffuse submillimeter emission in large angular-scale targets like M31 Andromeda presents a severe observational challenge due to the atmospheric filtering inherent to ground-based single-dish telescopes. The HASHTAG survey successfully recovered this large-scale structure by combining [[james-clerk-maxwell-telescope|James Clerk Maxwell Telescope]] SCUBA-2 data with space-based observations, producing high-fidelity submillimeter maps of M31 Andromeda and quantifying CO($J=3-2$) line contamination in the $850$ $\mu$m band at up to $28\%$ in the galaxy's $10$ kpc ring [Smith et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..257...52S/abstract). 

In galactic nuclei, high-resolution interferometry with the [[atacama-large-millimeter-array-alma|Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA)]] allows detailed chemical mapping of circumnuclear disks (CNDs). In the Seyfert 2 galaxy NGC 1068, shock-related species like SO and HC$_3$N are localized specifically to the CND, while tracers such as $^{13}$CO and C$^{18}$O dominate the surrounding starburst ring [Takano et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PASJ...66...75T/abstract). Submillimeter continuum observations of the same galaxy trace a massive reservoir of diffuse $\text{H}_2$ that coexists with atomic HI. In this extended, diffuse component outside the central starburst region, the gas-to-dust ratio is $M_{gas}/M_{dust} \sim 70-150$, which contrasts with a much larger ratio in the inner kiloparsec [Papadopoulos et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514L..95P/abstract). Submillimeter observations are also key in mapping local interacting systems. For instance, Submillimeter Array (SMA) observations of the merging system Arp 302 reveal an asymmetric molecular gas distribution and strong variations in the CO $J=2-1/1-0$ line ratio across the star-forming regions [Ao et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....136.1118A/abstract). Similarly, $350$ $\mu$m observations of ultraluminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs) at intermediate redshifts ($0.1 < z < 0.5$) confirm that the far-IR-radio correlation holds robustly in this regime, suggesting their dust is primarily heated by intense starbursts rather than active nuclei [Yang et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...660.1198Y/abstract).

Our own Milky Way provides a unique laboratory for submillimeter astrophysics. Submillimeter polarimetry of Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*) between $230$ and $690$ GHz has been used to measure intraday variability and Faraday rotation. The derived rotation measure of $(-5.6 \pm 0.7) \times 10^5 \text{ rad m}^{-2}$ restricts the supermassive black hole's accretion rate to a narrow range of $2 \times 10^{-7}$ to $2 \times 10^{-9} M_{\odot} \text{ yr}^{-1}$ [Marrone et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhDT........32M/abstract). Furthermore, mapping of SiO in the Galactic Center traces extensive cloud-cloud collisions, with dense molecular cores ($n_{H_2} \ge 10^4 \text{ cm}^{-3}$) surprisingly maintaining cool kinetic temperatures of $\sim 25$ K compared to the hotter cores typically found in the galactic disk [Huettemeister et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&A...334..646H/abstract). Additionally, the MSX galactic plane survey led to the discovery of 'infrared dark clouds' silhouetted against the bright mid-infrared background emission; these cold, dense regions contain submillimeter cores indicative of early Class 0 protostars [Price et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002AdSpR..30.2027P/abstract).

Submillimeter observations are also crucial for understanding the origins of cosmic dust. Observations of Supernova 1987A with the [[herschel-space-observatory|Herschel Space Observatory]] revealed a large reservoir of cold dust grains ($\sim 0.4-0.7\ M_{\odot}$), suggesting that supernova ejecta are primary producers of cosmic dust [Matsuura et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...333.1258M/abstract).

Finally, the study of nearby dwarf irregular galaxies has sparked an ongoing debate regarding dust emissivity at low metallicities. Initial observations of low-metallicity galaxies identified anomalous spectral energy distributions, such as abnormally low emissivity indices at 450 and 850 $\mu$m [Kiuchi et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....128.2743K/abstract), while later data from the [[herschel-space-observatory|Herschel Space Observatory]] revealed a specific, pronounced 500 $\mu$m "submillimeter excess" relative to standard thermal dust models [Dale et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...745...95D/abstract). However, the literature remains conflicting: more recent analyses using updated photometry from the KINGFISH survey argue that this perceived excess largely disappears within $1\sigma$ measurement uncertainties, suggesting it may be a modeling artifact rather than a physical phenomenon [Kirkpatrick et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...778...51K/abstract). Deriving total gas masses in these metal-poor environments is further complicated by severe uncertainties in the CO-to-H$_2$ conversion factor ($\alpha_{CO}$), which drastically alters interpretations of the molecular gas reservoir in systems such as the dwarf host of FRB 121102 [Bower et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018AJ....155..227B/abstract). High-resolution studies of other dwarf galaxies, such as NGC 5253, have additionally resolved a hot giant molecular cloud surrounding a forming super star cluster, revealing a gas-to-dust ratio significantly lower than expected for such low-metallicity environments [Turner et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150908531T/abstract).

## Synergies with Multi-wavelength Astronomy

The true physical nature of dust-enshrouded galaxies and active galactic nuclei (AGN) cannot be fully unraveled using submillimeter data alone. Instead, [[synergy-in-multi-wavelength-astronomy|Synergy in multi-wavelength astronomy]] is required to penetrate varying layers of obscuration and construct a complete picture of galaxy evolution. By bridging the gap between high-energy X-ray observations, rest-frame ultraviolet/optical data, and low-frequency radio surveys, astronomers can accurately constrain the baryon cycle, chemical enrichment, and star formation histories of the universe's most extreme environments.

**Optical/Infrared Integration and JWST Follow-up**
Submillimeter galaxies (SMGs) represent a population of luminous systems undergoing intense star formation, yet they are notoriously faint or completely invisible in rest-frame ultraviolet and optical bands [Genzel et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005mmgf.conf..112G/abstract), [Chapman et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...548L.147C/abstract). To derive precise stellar masses, photometric redshifts, and optical attenuation ($A_V$), submillimeter continuum maps must be paired with deep [[near-infrared-astronomy|Near-Infrared Astronomy]] and mid-infrared imaging. Recent [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] follow-up of ALMA sources has revolutionized SED modeling by spatially resolving the heavily attenuated stellar populations of SMGs. For instance, joint JWST and ALMA analyses in the COSMOS-Web survey revealed that roughly 30% of SMGs lacked any prior optical or near-infrared detections, with the most heavily obscured sources residing at $z > 4$ [McKinney et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...979..229M/abstract). Cross-matching [[atacama-large-millimeter-array-alma|ALMA]] sources with the Spitzer SMUVS catalog shows that ALMA-detected galaxies at $z > 2$ are systematically more massive and redder than non-detected galaxies, which is highly consistent with extreme high dust extinction [Suzuki et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...959...82S/abstract). Pixel-level SED modeling of systems such as the $z=4.26$ galaxy 850.1 demonstrates that some ultramassive ($M_* \sim 10^{11.8} M_\odot$), heavily obscured ($A_V \sim 5$) SMGs remain invisible even in deep JWST/NIRCam imaging, underscoring the necessity of submillimeter data to complete the census of massive galaxy progenitors [Smail et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958...36S/abstract).

**The Radio-FIR Correlation**
Centimeter-wavelength [[radio-astronomy-and-sources|Radio astronomy and sources]] provide a natural complement to submillimeter mapping. Because radio emission is unaffected by dust extinction, high-resolution radio imaging provides a critical census of galaxy evolution that precisely complements submillimeter mapping, especially in localizing sources that remain entirely invisible at optical bands [Richards et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999alma.confE..51R/abstract). Empirical studies utilizing high-resolution interferometry confirm that SMGs closely obey the local Radio-FIR correlation out to at least $z \sim 5$ [Younger et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704..803Y/abstract), [Barger et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761...89B/abstract). [[radio-surveys|Radio pre-selection]] has historically been used to isolate dusty starbursts and estimate their redshift distributions, finding a median redshift of $z \sim 2$ for the bulk of the 850 $\mu$m population [Chapman et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...548L.147C/abstract). However, this multi-wavelength synthesis also exposes tensions. On one hand, deep interferometric surveys have identified a subset of "radio-dim" or optically dark SMGs that feature a high-redshift tail extending beyond $z > 4$, indicating that [[low-frequency-radio-astronomy|low-frequency radio surveys]] systematically miss a higher-redshift segment of the dusty universe [Younger et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704..803Y/abstract), [Smail et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958...36S/abstract). Completely separately, surveys of extremely bright, millimeter-selected hyperluminous sources discovered by the [[south-pole-telescope|South Pole Telescope]] and observed with the single-dish APEX telescope have revealed a population with a high median redshift of $z \sim 3.0$ [Greve et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...756..101G/abstract).

**X-ray Observations and AGN Activity**
Integrating submillimeter maps with data from the [[chandra-x-ray-observatory|Chandra X-ray Observatory]] is critical for decoupling starburst from AGN activity. While many bright SMGs lack strong X-ray emission—suggesting their bolometric output is overwhelmingly dominated by star formation [Genzel et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005mmgf.conf..112G/abstract)—multi-wavelength characterizations of specific sources reveal complex co-evolution. In the protoquasar SSA22-AzTEC1 ($z = 3.09$), hard X-ray emission indicates a heavily obscured AGN with a hydrogen column density of $N_H \sim 10^{24}$ cm$^{-2}$. Despite this active nucleus, SED modeling confirms the AGN contributes only $\sim 10\%$ of the bolometric luminosity, meaning the submillimeter emission is still driven by a massive starburst [Tamura et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724.1270T/abstract). Furthermore, multi-wavelength combinations reveal important scaling relations. Observations with ALMA suggest that in SMGs hosting unobscured QSOs, the central black holes grow approximately three times faster than their host galaxies compared to present-day mass ratios [Fu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844..123F/abstract). In the local universe, ALMA observations of Seyfert galaxies show that dense molecular gas tracers (like HCN and HCO$^+$) physically correlate with X-ray and mid-infrared continuum peaks but anti-correlate with optical light, mapping the exact geometry of dust-obscured nuclear star formation [Izumi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...811...39I/abstract). Comprehensive modeling often utilizes archives from the [[spitzer-space-telescope|Spitzer Space Telescope]] to further characterize these populations.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite the rapid evolution of [[millimeter-wave-astronomy|Millimeter-wave Astronomy]] and submillimeter observational techniques, several fundamental questions regarding the physical properties and demographics of dusty star-forming galaxies remain unresolved. Furthermore, observers must continuously account for the significant role of selection effects—particularly those dictated by the diverse intrinsic spectral energy distributions (SEDs) of galaxies—when interpreting multi-wavelength submillimeter survey results and determining absolute number counts [Blain et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001dmsi.conf..195B/abstract). One active debate centers on the exact redshift distribution of the faintest, optically dark submillimeter galaxies (SMGs). While classical [[radio-surveys|radio-selected SMG populations]] typically peak at $z \sim 2-3$ [Chapman et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...548L.147C/abstract), the discovery of new populations has revised this picture. Deep interferometric surveys highlight a significant, previously missed tail of radio-dim and optically dark sources extending beyond $z > 4$ [Younger et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704..803Y/abstract), [Smail et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958...36S/abstract), while single-dish surveys of extremely bright millimeter sources report median redshifts as high as $z \sim 3$ [Greve et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...756..101G/abstract). Another active area of study concerns "500 micron risers"—galaxies whose spectral energy distributions rise sharply from 250 to 500 $\mu$m. High-resolution follow-up suggests these extreme sources are often either gravitationally lensed or reside in forming cluster cores [Clements et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.tmp.1962C/abstract). Furthermore, the internal physics of local low-metallicity dwarf galaxies presents a lingering puzzle: as noted earlier, the existence of a 500 $\mu$m submillimeter excess relative to standard models [Kiuchi et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....128.2743K/abstract), [Dale et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...745...95D/abstract) remains highly contested due to conflicting photometry and modeling uncertainties [Kirkpatrick et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...778...51K/abstract).

A critical bottleneck in current submillimeter continuum surveys is the spatial confusion limit. Because of the relatively large beam sizes of existing single-dish observatories, what appears as a single hyperluminous source is frequently a blend of multiple fainter galaxies. As previously noted, high-resolution interferometric follow-up using the [[atacama-large-millimeter-array-alma|Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA)]] and the Submillimeter Array (SMA) has revealed that many bright single-dish detections resolve into multiple discrete SMGs [Barger et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761...89B/abstract), [Hodge et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...768...91H/abstract), [Chen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...789...12C/abstract). This multiplicity significantly complicates the derivation of source counts, luminosity functions, and the cosmic star-formation rate density. To disentangle this, projects like the FOSSILS survey are currently using ALMA to resolve SMG sub-structures on sub-kiloparsec scales, discovering detailed morphological features such as dusty spiral arms in galaxies at "Cosmic Noon" ($z \sim 2.5$) [Ikeda et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250920720I/abstract).

To simultaneously overcome the confusion limit and the slow mapping speeds of current interferometers, the next decade of submillimeter astronomy will be defined by a new generation of wide-field, large-aperture single-dish facilities. Two major 50-meter class observatories are currently in development: the Atacama Large Aperture Submillimeter Telescope (AtLAST) and the [[large-millimeter-telescope|Large Millimeter Telescope]] (LST). Equipped with $2^{\circ}$ fields of view and highly multiplexed spectroscopic capabilities, AtLAST will perform direct millimeter spectroscopy and deep dust continuum maps, surveying millions of galaxies to securely map the cosmic baryon cycle up to $z \sim 7$ [Liu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240301202L/abstract), [van Kampen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240302806V/abstract). Similarly, the LST is optimized for wide-area imaging and spectroscopic surveys across the 70-420 GHz atmospheric windows, utilizing active surface control to correct for gravitational and thermal deformations, which will potentially push observations up to 1 THz [Kawabe et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016SPIE.9906E..26K/abstract), [Kawabe et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018alas.confE..18K/abstract). Observations with the [[south-pole-telescope|South Pole Telescope]] will continue to play a foundational role in these wide-area efforts.

At the highest submillimeter frequencies, the CCAT-prime project will deploy the 6-meter [[fred-young-submillimeter-telescope|Fred Young Submillimeter Telescope]] (FYST) at an exceptional 5600-meter elevation on Cerro Chajnantor. FYST will host Prime-Cam, a first-generation instrument featuring multiple modules, including an 850 GHz (350 $\mu$m) camera equipped with roughly 40,000 kinetic inductance detectors (KIDs) [Chapman et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022SPIE12190E..05C/abstract), [Vavagiakis et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251101707V/abstract). This configuration will yield mapping speeds more than an order of magnitude faster than previous submillimeter observatories, providing unparalleled broadband intensity and polarization measurements to constrain the epoch of reionization, hierarchical structure formation, and galactic magnetic fields. Looking further ahead, space-based concepts like the proposed Submillimeter Probe of the Evolution of Cosmic Structure (SPECS) aim to provide kilometer-baseline interferometry from space, which would eventually resolve dusty galaxies at Hubble-like resolutions free from atmospheric interference [Mather et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph.12454M/abstract).
