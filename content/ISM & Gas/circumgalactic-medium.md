The **circumgalactic medium (CGM)** is the diffuse, multiphase envelope of gas and dust residing within the virial radius of a galaxy's dark matter halo. Acting as the critical boundary between a galaxy's internal [[neutral-interstellar-medium|Neutral Interstellar Medium]] and the broader cosmic web, the CGM is the primary arena for the galactic baryon cycle. It governs the continuous exchange of matter and energy that regulates galaxy evolution, simultaneously mediating the accretion of pristine intergalactic gas and the expulsion of metal-enriched [[galaxy-outflows|Galaxy outflows]] [Tumlinson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55..389T/abstract). Rather than a static halo, the CGM is a highly dynamic and structured reservoir that contains a substantial fraction of a galaxy's total baryonic mass and heavy elements.

The physical state of the [[multiphase-circumgalactic-medium|Multiphase circumgalactic medium]] is characterized by a complex coexistence of gas phases spanning several orders of magnitude in temperature and density. It consists of cool (${\sim}10^4$ K) photoionized clouds, a warm-hot ($10^{4.5-5.5}$ K) interface often traced by highly ionized species like O VI, and a volume-filling [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]] ($>10^6$ K) maintained near the host halo's virial temperature [Tumlinson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...334..948T/abstract). Historically, cosmological accounting at low redshift revealed a severe "missing baryons" problem. Advancements in ultraviolet absorption spectroscopy and recent detections of [[diffuse-x-ray-emitting-gas|Diffuse X-ray emitting gas]] have demonstrated that the extended hot coronae of individual galaxies are massive enough to potentially resolve this discrepancy, shifting the search for missing mass away from the more diffuse [[warm-hot-intergalactic-medium|Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium]].

The thermodynamic and chemical properties of the CGM are strictly coupled to the evolutionary state of its host galaxy. In actively [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]], powerful stellar winds and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] drive biconical [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]] that enrich the halo with heavy elements. Much of this material may eventually cool and cycle back onto the disk via [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]], fueling subsequent generations of stars. Conversely, the [[gaseous-halos|Gaseous halos]] of [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] show severe depletions in cool and warm-hot gas, suggesting that galaxy quenching is intimately linked to the disruption or exhaustion of the circumgalactic reservoir [Kacprzak et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.2289K/abstract), [Tchernyshyov et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...949...41T/abstract). Theoretical frameworks, such as the precipitation limit, suggest this delicate balance between heating and cooling dictates the phase structure across different halo masses [Voit et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...879L...1V/abstract).

While traditional quasar absorption-line spectroscopy has established the fundamental properties of the CGM, the field is rapidly advancing through modern integral field unit (IFU) emission mapping and X-ray surface brightness analyses. However, significant open questions remain. Astronomers are actively debating the precise mechanisms that maintain the stark dichotomy between star-forming and passive halos, as well as the efficiency of metal mixing between sub-kiloparsec gas clumps and the diffuse background. Resolving these tensions—and determining whether the hot CGM extends well beyond the virial radius to completely close the galactic baryon budget—awaits next-generation X-ray microcalorimeters and ultraviolet observatories capable of directly mapping these tenuous gas environments in emission.

## Defining the Circumgalactic Interface

Conventionally, the spatial extent of the circumgalactic medium is defined as the volume bounded by the galactic disk and the host dark matter halo's virial radius, $R_{\rm vir}$. However, recent observational metrics demonstrate that this boundary is permeable and strongly mass-dependent. Kinematic and absorption studies of neutral hydrogen show that the characteristic size of the cold CGM—defined as the impact parameter where the H I covering fraction for column densities $N_{\rm HI} > 10^{14}\ {\rm cm}^{-2}$ remains at 50%—can reach $1.2 R_{\rm vir}$ for [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] ($M_* > 10^{9.9}\ M_\odot$) and extend up to $2.4 R_{\rm vir}$ for intermediate-mass systems [Wilde et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912....9W/abstract). Furthermore, the DIISC survey demonstrates that the Ly$\alpha$ equivalent width is more strongly correlated with the impact parameter normalized by the H I radius ($\rho/R_{\rm HI}$) than by the traditional virial radius, suggesting the cool CGM is closely tied to the neutral gas disk [Borthakur et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240912554B/abstract). Indeed, the characteristic radial scale length for the circumgalactic medium varies by species, with Ly$\alpha$ extending to approximately $1 R_{\rm vir}$ and Si III limited to roughly $0.4 R_{\rm vir}$ [Borthakur et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..259B/abstract). Moving inward, detailed studies of the Milky Way's neighbor via Project AMIGA reveal a clear transition between Andromeda's thick disk and its inner CGM at $R < 30$ kpc, where corotating disk components dominate over halo gas for low- and intermediate-ionization species [Lehner et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250616573L/abstract). Closer to home, by using pairs of quasar and halo-star sightlines, the QuaStar survey has measured the low-velocity C IV content of the Milky Way's outer halo, finding a covering fraction of only 20% beyond 10 kpc [Bish et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912....8B/abstract). Indeed, the "sphere of influence" of a galaxy, where its feedback and accretion signatures significantly alter the Ly$\alpha$ absorption profile, may extend up to seven times the dark matter halo's virial radius [Sorini et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...859..125S/abstract).

As a galactic interface, the CGM is highly stratified, comprising cool ($T \sim 10^4$ K), warm-hot ($T \sim 10^{4.5-5.5}$ K), and hot ($T > 10^6$ K) components. The [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]], typically characterized by temperatures near the [[mean-galactic-virial-temperature|Mean galactic virial temperature]], is a volume-filling component in massive halos [Das et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...897...63D/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240117308Z/abstract). The exact extent and mass of this phase remains a critical metric for determining whether halos retain their full cosmological baryon allotment ($\Omega_b / \Omega_m$) within $R_{\rm vir}$, or if energetic feedback expels material deeper into the [[warm-hot-intergalactic-medium|Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium]] [Chadayammuri et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...936L..15C/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240117308Z/abstract).

The CGM is not merely a static halo, but a highly dynamic reservoir sculpted by [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]]. It supplies the pristine and recycled gas required to sustain star formation, while simultaneously acting as the repository for [[galaxy-outflows|Galaxy outflows]] driven by supernovae and [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] [Tumlinson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...334..948T/abstract), [Lan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...866...36L/abstract). This continuous baryon cycle enriches the halo with heavy elements, rendering the CGM a major repository of a galaxy's metals. Furthermore, ultraviolet reflection nebulae and absorption line studies confirm the presence of an [[extended-dust-component|Extended dust component]] within this interface, indicating that 5% to 15% of a galaxy's interstellar dust mass can be ejected into the inner CGM through radiation pressure and galactic winds [Aguirre et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...556L..11A/abstract), [Boettcher et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240811912B/abstract). Consequently, defining the circumgalactic interface requires characterizing it as an actively evolving, multiphase ecosystem whose physical state is fundamentally coupled to the evolutionary history of its central galaxy.

## Historical Development and the Missing Baryons Problem

Historically, efforts to conduct a complete cosmological census of baryons at high redshift ($z \sim 3$) proved highly successful. Observations of the Ly$\alpha$ forest and Damped Lyman-alpha systems (DLAs) in the spectra of background quasars revealed a [[cosmic-h-i-density|Cosmic H I density]] that could account for the vast majority of baryonic matter predicted by Big Bang nucleosynthesis and measurements of the cosmic microwave background. However, at low redshifts ($z \sim 0$), similar accounting efforts fell significantly short, giving rise to the "missing baryons" problem: roughly half of the expected baryonic mass could not be located in stars, cold interstellar gas, or dense intracluster gas.

Early theoretical and textbook models posited that these missing baryons were shock-heated into the [[warm-hot-intergalactic-medium|Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium]] (WHIM), a diffuse, filamentary network of intergalactic gas with temperatures of $T \sim 10^5 - 10^6$ K. While the WHIM remains a crucial component of the cosmic web, the prevailing paradigm shifted significantly as ultraviolet and X-ray observatories improved. Instead of assuming the space between galaxies was largely empty or populated only by minor high-velocity clouds, astronomers recognized that [[gaseous-halos|Gaseous halos]] represent a massive, multiphase reservoir tightly coupled to galaxy evolution.

A watershed moment in this historical development was the COS-Halos survey, which utilized the Cosmic Origins Spectrograph to probe the circumgalactic medium of low-redshift galaxies. The survey detected ubiquitous, large (${\sim}150$ kpc) halos of highly ionized oxygen (O VI) surrounding [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]]. This demonstrated that the CGM contains a substantial mass of heavy elements and gas that often rivals or exceeds the reservoirs of the galaxies themselves [Tumlinson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...334..948T/abstract).

Consequently, the search for the missing baryons pivoted from the large-scale WHIM to the [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] phase of the CGM ($T > 10^6$ K) belonging to individual dark matter halos. Because this volume-filling component exists as [[diffuse-x-ray-emitting-gas|Diffuse X-ray emitting gas]], characterizing its mass has proven observationally challenging. Deep X-ray observations of individual massive spiral galaxies successfully detected extended warm-hot halos at temperatures near the virial temperature (${\sim}2 \times 10^6$ K), with some analyses arguing that this phase alone contains enough mass to fully account for the missing galactic baryons [Das et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...897...63D/abstract). More recently, observations using the eROSITA all-sky survey have successfully detected the hot CGM around individual nearby ($< 50$ Mpc) $L^*$ galaxies out to approximately 50 kpc, confirming that non-thermal origins for this diffuse emission are unlikely [He et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260116499H/abstract).

Despite these observational breakthroughs, the literature remains explicitly conflicting regarding whether the CGM entirely resolves the missing baryons problem. Some high-resolution X-ray absorption studies have found a dearth of expected highly ionized species (such as O VII), placing strict upper limits on column densities (e.g., $\log(N_{\rm O VII}/{\rm cm}^{-2}) \leq 14.8$). These non-detections suggest that the putative $10^{5.5} - 10^{6.3}$ K gas cannot close the baryon budget unless its metallicity is unusually low (less than $10\%$ solar) [Yao et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...716.1514Y/abstract). Furthermore, recent X-ray stacking analyses using eROSITA data have mapped the surface brightness profiles of the hot CGM around galaxies of varying stellar mass. These studies find that the total baryon budget strictly within the virial radius ($R_{\rm vir}$) is frequently lower than $\Lambda$CDM cosmological predictions, implying significant gas depletion or expulsion. To fully account for the missing baryons, the hot CGM may need to extend well beyond standard halo boundaries, potentially out to $\approx 3 R_{\rm vir}$ [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240117308Z/abstract).

## The Multiphase Physical State: From Cool Clouds to Hot Coronae

The [[multiphase-circumgalactic-medium|Multiphase circumgalactic medium]] spans a vast range of densities and temperatures, broadly categorized into a cool phase ($T \sim 10^4$ K), a warm-hot phase ($T \sim 10^{4.5-5.5}$ K), and a volume-filling [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]] ($T > 10^6$ K) [Tumlinson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...334..948T/abstract), [Tumlinson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55..389T/abstract). The coexistence and interaction of these diverse thermal states govern the baryon cycle of the host galaxy.

The cool gas, frequently observed via low-ionization absorption lines such as Mg II, resides in dense, sub-kiloparsec structural clumps [Churchill et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...802...10C/abstract), [Augustin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993...52A/abstract). The physical state of this $\sim 10^4$ K gas is generally well-described by *photoionization equilibrium* driven by the metagalactic ultraviolet background and local galactic radiation fields [Cen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160903583C/abstract). This [[cold-halo-gas|Cold halo gas]] is tightly linked to the baryon cycle, exhibiting kinematic signatures consistent with both co-rotating accretion along the major axis and highly structured [[galaxy-outflows|Galaxy outflows]] along the minor axis [Lan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...795...31L/abstract), [Lan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...866...36L/abstract). Adding to this complex picture, recent DESI analysis of emission-line galaxies at $z \sim 1.5$ suggests that the cool (Mg II) and warm (C IV) gas phases are not co-spatial, with the higher-ionization gas possessing a more extended spatial distribution and distinct kinematics [Lan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251103195L/abstract).

Intermediate temperatures are classically traced by the highly ionized O VI $\lambda\lambda 1031, 1037$ doublet. The dominant ionization mechanism for this warm-hot gas remains a subject of active debate. Pure photoionization models relying solely on the extragalactic UV background are physically disfavored, as they require unphysically large path lengths ($\gtrsim 100$ kpc) to reproduce the observed column densities [Werk et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833...54W/abstract). Consequently, O VI is frequently attributed to *collisional ionization* in a $T \sim 10^{5.5}$ K phase. The literature remains divided on whether this gas traces radiatively cooling layers behind supersonic winds, turbulent mixing layers between cool clouds and the hot corona, or the ambient coronal medium of lower-mass halos [Werk et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833...54W/abstract), [Buie et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...890...33B/abstract). Recent models further suggest that O VI emission is theorized to trace the warm, collisionally ionized envelopes of cooler clumps accreting via metal-enriched [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]], rather than volume-filling outflows [Lochhaas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251025844L/abstract). Furthermore, observations with the Suzaku X-ray observatory suggest the potential widespread existence of a supervirial (${\sim}10^7$ K) gas component in the Milky Way's CGM, though this signal could alternatively be explained by an enhanced neon abundance within the standard warm-hot phase [Gupta et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909..164G/abstract).

The hot phase ($T \gtrsim 10^6$ K) is expected to be maintained near the [[mean-galactic-virial-temperature|Mean galactic virial temperature]] by accretion shocks and feedback. While historically elusive, deep stacking analyses and observations of [[diffuse-x-ray-emitting-gas|Diffuse X-ray emitting gas]] have successfully mapped this extended component via O VII, O VIII, and soft X-ray thermal emission out to the virial radius [Chadayammuri et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...936L..15C/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240117308Z/abstract). Although this massive reservoir is a leading candidate to account for the "missing baryons" of galaxy-scale halos, its measured X-ray surface brightness often implies baryon budgets lower than cosmological predictions within the virial radius, hinting at either significant gas depletion or extensive spatial profiles reaching beyond $3 R_{\rm vir}$ [Yao et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...716.1514Y/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240117308Z/abstract).

The dynamic coexistence of these distinct phases is heavily regulated by *thermal instability*. Analytical frameworks, such as the precipitation limit, suggest that when the ratio of the cooling time to the free-fall dynamical time ($t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm dyn}$) drops below a critical threshold (typically $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm dyn} \sim 4$), the hot corona becomes thermally unstable [Voit et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...879L...1V/abstract), [Faerman et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928...37F/abstract). This localized thermal instability triggers the condensation of cool clouds that "rain" down onto the galaxy. This precipitation fuels star formation and subsequently drives feedback that reheats the medium, naturally suspending the [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] near this equilibrium threshold [Voit et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...879L...1V/abstract).

## The Galactic Baryon Cycle: Accretion, Outflows, and Recycling

The circumgalactic medium serves as the primary reservoir mediating the galactic baryon cycle—the continuous, dynamic interplay of gas accretion, expulsion, and recycling that governs galaxy evolution. This cycle establishes a highly anisotropic kinematic and chemical structure within [[gaseous-halos|Gaseous halos]], driven by the delicate balance between the host halo's gravitational potential and energetic feedback processes.

**Gas Inflow and Cold Accretion**
Galaxies acquire their star-forming fuel from the intergalactic medium and the extended CGM. Theoretical models and observations indicate that a substantial portion of this inflowing material takes the form of cold accretion streams, which penetrate the shock-heated [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] halo to deliver un-enriched gas directly to the galactic disk. Direct observational evidence for these streams includes the detection of optically thick Lyman limit systems with exceptionally low metallicities ($Z \sim 0.01 Z_{\odot}$) near star-forming galaxies at high redshift [Crighton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...776L..18C/abstract). Recent high-resolution simulations of dwarf galaxies show that CGM accretion is a primary driver of disk mass, replenishing 40%-70% of the total mass in the galactic disk [Tung et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988..127T/abstract). 

Kinematic studies using cool gas tracers, such as Mg II, reveal that this accretion preferentially occurs along the major axis of the host galaxy [Nielsen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812...83N/abstract). Furthermore, a substantial fraction of the cool CGM ($f_{\text{EWcorot}} \sim 0.6$) is found to co-rotate with the galactic disk. This infalling, co-rotating gas exhibits inflow velocities of up to $\sim 50 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, closely linking the kinematics of the extended halo directly to the interstellar medium [Martin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...878...84M/abstract), [DeFelippis et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...923...56D/abstract), [Nateghi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.1321N/abstract).

**Metal-Enriched Outflows and Feedback**
In opposition to [[smooth-gas-infall|Smooth gas infall]], powerful galactic winds driven by supernova feedback and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] expel highly enriched material back into the CGM. These [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]] mechanisms are predominantly biconical and anisotropic, expanding preferentially along the path of least resistance—the galactic minor axis. Consequently, equivalent widths for low-ionization metal absorption lines (e.g., Mg II, Fe II) are observed to be enhanced and kinematically broader along the minor axis compared to the major axis in star-forming galaxies. Furthermore, their overall absorption is 5-10 times stronger around star-forming galaxies than passive galaxies at small scales [Lan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...795...31L/abstract), [Lan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...866...36L/abstract).

In massive halos, energetic feedback from [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] is essential for distributing heavy elements deep into the CGM, naturally elevating the column densities of highly ionized species like O VI and regulating the thermal state of the halo [Sanchez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...882....8S/abstract), [Truong et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508.1563T/abstract). Even in shallower potential wells governed by [[dwarf-galaxy-physical-processes|Dwarf Galaxy Physical Processes]], feedback remains highly efficient at transporting metals. Although dwarf galaxies lose a significant fraction of their metals to the broader intergalactic medium, roughly $50\%$ of their baryons and up to $10\%$ of their synthesized metals are retained in a warm ($10^{4.5}$--$10^{5.5} \text{ K}$) phase of the CGM [Zheng et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960...55Z/abstract), [Piacitelli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..230P/abstract). Counter-intuitively, theoretical modeling suggests that increasing the wind mass-loading parameter in such low-mass galaxies may actually lead to more star formation, as higher feedback energy is required to resist the contraction of the gaseous atmosphere and establish an equilibrium state [Voit et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240607631V/abstract).

**Gas Recycling and Galactic Fountains**
Not all outflowing gas possesses sufficient specific energy to reach the escape velocity of the dark matter halo. A significant fraction of the ejected material remains gravitationally bound, eventually cooling and returning to the disk via [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]]. Cosmological simulations suggest that recycled gas injected from the host galaxy typically accounts for one-third of the total CGM mass within the inner 50 kpc [Röttgers et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496..152R/abstract). This continuous recycling deeply influences [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|Galaxy chemical enrichment mechanisms]] by re-accreting previously processed metals. 

However, observational limitations make it challenging to unambiguously distinguish between pristine accretion, recycled fountain gas, and bound outflows based solely on kinematics [Nateghi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.1321N/abstract). This ambiguity highlights the complex, multiphase nature of the CGM, where high-metallicity recycled gas and pristine inflowing streams often remain poorly mixed on sub-kiloparsec scales. As a result, line-of-sight measurements frequently uncover extreme metallicity scatter and spatially distinct gas clumps hosting vastly different ionization states and thermal pressures within the same halo [Kacprzak et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...886...91K/abstract), [Augustin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993...52A/abstract).

## Observational Techniques: Absorption Spectroscopy and Emission Mapping

The observational study of the circumgalactic medium relies on a combination of highly sensitive techniques capable of detecting tenuous, multiphase gas extending out to a galaxy's virial radius. For decades, the field has been dominated by absorption-line techniques, but recent advancements in instrumentation have enabled direct mapping of the CGM in emission.

**Quasar Absorption Line Spectroscopy**
The traditional workhorse for probing the diffuse CGM is quasar absorption line spectroscopy. By utilizing the spectra of bright background [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] (quasars) that intersect the halos of foreground galaxies, researchers can detect absorption features imprinted by various gas phases. The physical properties of the gas are routinely extracted using Voigt profile fitting, which yields quantitative measurements of the ion column densities ($N$), Doppler broadening parameters ($b$), and line-of-sight velocity centroids [Werk et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833...54W/abstract), [Butsky et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935...69B/abstract). This technique has been vital for establishing the multiphase nature of the CGM, from cool ($T \sim 10^4$ K) gas traced by Mg II to the warm-hot component traced by ubiquitous O VI [Tumlinson et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...334..948T/abstract). However, because individual quasar sightlines provide only 1D "pencil-beam" probes, determining the global spatial coherence and morphology of the CGM requires either large statistical samples or the rare serendipity of extended background sources, such as gravitationally lensed arcs [Mortensen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...914...92M/abstract). Additionally, interpreting absorption lines from our own perspective inside the Milky Way introduces unique biases. The "Mocky Way" simulations demonstrate that observing the CGM from within a galactic disk yields a factor of 2 higher scatter in ion column densities and systematically biases detections toward high-velocity gas [Zheng et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...896..143Z/abstract).

**Emission Mapping with Integral Field Units**
To overcome the spatial limitations of absorption sightlines, the field is increasingly shifting toward direct faint-line emission mapping utilizing the Integral Field Unit (IFU). Instruments such as MUSE on the Very Large Telescope and KCWI on the Keck Observatory enable researchers to capture simultaneous 2D spatial and kinematic information. These IFU surveys have successfully detected extended [[giant-ly-nebulae|Giant Lyα nebulae]] and metal-line emission (e.g., He II and C IV) stretching tens to hundreds of kiloparsecs into the CGM [Zhang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952..124Z/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241005392Z/abstract). Complementarily, deep medium-band imaging with the JWST has discovered extended [O III] emission halos, directly highlighting extreme ion-emitting states [Peng et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241010993P/abstract). Additionally, the Condor Array Telescope has successfully mapped the "cosmic web" by stacking images of thousands of galaxies to reveal the faint Ly$\alpha$, C IV, and Mg II emission in the distant universe [Lanzetta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241210081L/abstract). By analyzing surface brightness profiles from IFU datacubes, observers can map the physical transition boundary between the interstellar medium and the CGM, separating gas ionized by local star formation from gas ionized by shocks or the extragalactic UV background [Nielsen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231100856N/abstract). 

**X-ray Surface Brightness and the Hot Phase**
Probing the [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] ($T > 10^6$ K) component of the CGM requires X-ray observations. Because the extended X-ray emission around individual [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] is exceptionally faint, observers frequently employ stacking techniques to derive average surface brightness profiles from surveys like the eROSITA All-Sky Survey [Chadayammuri et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...936L..15C/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240117308Z/abstract). These stacked X-ray surface brightness profiles are typically parameterized using $\beta$-models, which allow astronomers to estimate the hot baryon budget out to the virial radius and test the predictions of cosmological simulations [Das et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...897...63D/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240117308Z/abstract). Looking forward, future large-area X-ray microcalorimeters will also enable the detection of hot CGM absorption (such as O VII) by using the cumulative emission from cosmic X-ray background sources, providing a probe that extends beyond the virial radius [Bogdán et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953...42B/abstract).

Despite these advances, a significant tension exists between analytical models and observations regarding the degree of small-scale clumping and turbulence in the CGM, which strongly affects both absorption line widths and emission luminosities [Buie et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...890...33B/abstract), [Corlies et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...896..125C/abstract). Resolving these tensions and fully disentangling faint CGM emission from Milky Way foregrounds will require future X-ray microcalorimeter missions (e.g., LEM, XRISM, Athena). With $1-2$ eV spectral resolution, these next-generation instruments will be capable of mapping the kinematics and precise line ratios of the hot CGM in individual halos [Mathur et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908...69M/abstract), [Kraft et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221109827K/abstract).

## Chemical Enrichment and Metallicity Gradients

The distribution of heavy elements within the circumgalactic medium provides a vital fossil record of a galaxy's [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]] and the efficiency of its feedback mechanisms. Chemical enrichment of the CGM is primarily driven by supernova winds, [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|AGN]] feedback, and the tidal or ram-pressure stripping of satellite galaxies. Cosmological simulations indicate that while the main host galaxy supplies the majority of metals within the inner halo, satellite progenitors and nearby dwarfs contribute significantly to the enrichment of the outer halo, often dominating the metal budget beyond $3 R_{\rm vir}$ [Shen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...760...50S/abstract). Furthermore, simulations indicate that satellite galaxies often exhibit higher metallicities than centrals because ram-pressure stripping removes the metal-poor gas reservoir that usually dilutes the interstellar medium [De Rossi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452..486D/abstract).

The macroscopic spatial distribution of these metals is highly anisotropic, reflecting the ongoing baryon cycle. Observations of low-redshift star-forming galaxies reveal that metal-enriched [[galaxy-outflows|Galaxy outflows]], typically traced by low-ionization species like Mg II and Fe II, exhibit enhanced equivalent widths and larger velocity dispersions along the galaxy's minor axis [Lan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...795...31L/abstract), [Lan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...866...36L/abstract), [Nielsen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812...83N/abstract). In contrast, cold accretion streams can deliver metal-poor gas to galaxies [Crighton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...776L..18C/abstract), and kinematic observations show that a significant fraction of cool gas tends to align with the major axis, co-rotating with the galactic disk [Martin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...878...84M/abstract). 

A major active debate in the literature concerns the mixing scales of the CGM and the exact nature of its [[gas-phase-metallicity|Gas-Phase Metallicity]] distribution. While detections of extremely metal-poor, cool gas streams ($\sim 1\%$ solar) strongly suggest a bimodal distribution of pristine accretion and enriched outflows [Crighton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...776L..18C/abstract), other broad surveys find a more continuous, complex distribution characterized by a massive $\sim 2$ dex scatter [Pointon et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...883...78P/abstract), [Kacprzak et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...886...91K/abstract). Measurements of star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 0.3$ show that CGM metallicities are typically lower than those of the host galaxy's interstellar medium by an offset of $\log(dZ) = -1.17 \pm 0.11$, yet they show no statistically significant radial gradient out to the virial radius [Kacprzak et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...886...91K/abstract). High-resolution hydrodynamical simulations indicate that metals are poorly mixed on small scales; dense gas clumps ($R \sim 0.25$ kpc) can have metallicities $1.5-2$ times lower than the surrounding volume-filling diffuse gas, complicating line-of-sight abundance measurements [Augustin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993...52A/abstract).

Metal retention in the CGM depends strongly on halo mass. In [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], which possess shallow potential wells and are highly susceptible to stellar feedback, the cool CGM ($T \sim 10^4$ K) retains only a small fraction of the synthesized metals. However, observations and simulations demonstrate that a significant portion of the metals ever produced by a dwarf galaxy resides in the warm-hot CGM phase ($10^{4.5-5.5}$ K), which extends to multiple virial radii and dominates the total metal retention [Zheng et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960...55Z/abstract), [Piacitelli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..230P/abstract). Furthermore, observations at $z \sim 2.6$ show that while highly ionized C IV is preferentially found in high-mass, actively star-forming galaxies, low-mass galaxies harbor significant reservoirs of cool C II gas that may eventually fuel subsequent star formation [Méndez-Hernández et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...666A..56M/abstract). Conversely, in the extremely low-mass regime, observations of the local dwarf galaxy Sextans B reveal a highly metal-poor CGM ($[\mathrm{Si}/\mathrm{H}] \approx -1.7$), demonstrating that the circumgalactic reservoir remains the dominant baryonic component even in the smallest, most metal-poor galaxies [Fox et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260100437F/abstract). In massive halos, supermassive black hole feedback acts as a primary driver for transporting highly enriched material into the halo, significantly elevating the column densities of high-ionization species like O VI [Sanchez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...882....8S/abstract).

Detailed [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]], such as alpha-to-iron ratios ($[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$), offer clues to the specific nucleosynthetic origins of circumgalactic gas. At high redshifts ($z > 6$), absorption line studies of dense CGM environments have revealed gas with surprisingly low $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ ratios. This chemical signature provides potential observational evidence for the early enrichment of the intergalactic and circumgalactic environments by Population III stars at the end of the epoch of reionization [Zou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...963L..28Z/abstract). Further emphasizing the importance of earlier epochs, gamma-ray burst (GRB) afterglows at $z > 2$ serve as powerful probes of the early CGM, revealing ubiquitous warm outflows and indicating a fairly uniform ratio of C IV mass to stellar mass over cosmic time [Gatkine et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884...66G/abstract).

## Kinematics, Angular Momentum, and Spatial Coherence

The kinematic structure of the [[multiphase-circumgalactic-medium|Multiphase circumgalactic medium]] is governed by a complex interplay of gas accretion, [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]], and turbulence. Recent high-resolution spectroscopy and integral field observations have demonstrated that the CGM is not a static halo, but rather a dynamic environment highly correlated with the [[galactic-gas-dynamics-and-flows|Galactic Gas Dynamics and Flows]] of the host galaxy.

**Co-rotation and Angular Momentum**
A substantial fraction of the cool and warm CGM possesses angular momentum that is aligned with the host galaxy's rotating disk. Observations of Mg II absorption around star-forming galaxies reveal kinematic shifts consistent with disk rotation out to impact parameters of $50\text{--}70\text{ kpc}$, indicating that centrifugal forces provide partial pressure support for this low-ionization gas [Martin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...878...84M/abstract). The equivalent width co-rotation fraction, $f_{\rm EWcorot}$, is measured to be approximately $0.6$ within the virial radius ($R_{\rm vir}$) before dropping to $\sim 0.35$ beyond $R_{\rm vir}$ [Nateghi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.1321N/abstract). Cosmological simulations, such as EAGLE, reproduce this phenomenon, showing an axisymmetric distribution of cool gas that aligns with the rotation axis of the host galaxy [Ho et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904...76H/abstract).

**Azimuthal Dependence and Outflow Kinematics**
The kinematic properties of the CGM exhibit a strong azimuthal dependence, reflecting the anisotropic nature of the galactic baryon cycle. Gas detected along the projected minor axis of star-forming galaxies typically displays larger velocity dispersions and broader absorption profiles. This kinematic signature is characteristic of turbulent, entrained material driven outward in biconical outflows [Nielsen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812...83N/abstract). Conversely, gas observed along the major axis exhibits narrower line widths and smaller velocity dispersions, consistent with coherent, co-rotating accretion streams. However, distinguishing between pristine intergalactic accretion and the recycled, bound gas of [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]] remains observationally challenging. Note that unlike the low-ionization Mg II phase, the highly ionized O VI phase exhibits a kinematically uniform distribution around galaxies, suggesting it traces a more widespread component unaffected by recent star formation activity [Nielsen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...834..148N/abstract).

**Spatial Coherence Scale**
The physical geometry of these gas flows is heavily constrained by the spatial coherence scale of the absorbing clouds. By utilizing spatially extended background probes, such as giant gravitationally lensed arcs and close quasar pairs, observers can map the small-scale variation of absorption strengths. For cool gas traced by Mg II, the absorption structures exhibit a spatial coherence scale of $\ell_A \gtrsim 1.9\text{ kpc}$ [Rubin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...868..142R/abstract), with lensed-arc studies finding fractional variations consistent with a coherence length of $\approx 2.7\text{ kpc}$ [Shaban et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250117940S/abstract). Additionally, tomographic measurements of the Sunburst Arc have constrained the physical sizes of individual Lyman limit systems in the CGM to approximately 2 kpc at $z \sim 2$ [Berg et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250706443B/abstract). This indicates that the cool CGM is not a homogeneous fog, but rather consists of kiloparsec-scale coherent structures or spatially correlated cloud complexes. 

**Turbulence and Velocity Dispersion**
Turbulence plays a critical role in the energy balance and phase structure of the CGM. Photoionization models that incorporate an explicitly turbulent medium require 3D velocity dispersions of $\sigma_{\rm 3D} \ge 26 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ to simultaneously reproduce the observed column densities of low- and high-ionization species (e.g., Si IV and O VI) [Buie et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...890...33B/abstract). Tomographic studies of Mg II absorbers often reveal velocity dispersions of $\sigma \approx 50 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, indicating that the metal-enriched halo gas is largely dispersion-supported [Mortensen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...914...92M/abstract). At high redshifts during cosmic noon ($z \sim 2$), close galaxy pairs reveal a distinct kinematic transition near the virial radius (${\sim}80$ kpc), where the line-of-sight velocity dispersion reaches a minimum, beyond which the Ly$\alpha$ absorption depth flattens [Prusinski et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...994...85P/abstract). Furthermore, recent analyses comparing the Doppler line widths and column densities of H I and O VI absorbers reveal contrasting trends that map to a velocity-size relation consistent with a Kolmogorov-like turbulent cascade. This suggests that turbulence spans over five orders of magnitude in spatial scale, likely driven by large-scale accretion at the halo outskirts that continually injects energy to sustain the multiphase structure [Qu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251212958Q/abstract). Moreover, the hot CGM phase is characterized by complex kinematics including 200-500 km/s outflows and slower 50-100 km/s inflows, which will be distinguishable with future 1 eV resolution X-ray spectrometers [ZuHone et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967...49Z/abstract).

## Influence of Galaxy Properties and Environment

The physical state of the [[multiphase-circumgalactic-medium|Multiphase circumgalactic medium]] is tightly coupled to the evolutionary stage, mass, and environment of its host galaxy. A stark dichotomy exists between the [[gaseous-halos|Gaseous halos]] of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] and [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]]. Observations reveal a pronounced "O VI dichotomy", where the covering fraction of highly ionized oxygen is significantly higher around star-forming hosts compared to passive systems at similar impact parameters [Tchernyshyov et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...949...41T/abstract). A similar disparity is observed in C IV [Garza et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...978L..12G/abstract) and in cool gas traced by Mg II [Lan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...795...31L/abstract), [Lan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...897...97L/abstract). For star-forming systems, data from the DESI survey reveals that the strength of cool gas absorption (Mg II) correlates strongly with galaxy stellar mass ($\text{EW} \propto M_*^{0.26}$) [Chen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...981...81C/abstract), while earlier SDSS and GALEX studies found that the average excess Mg II equivalent width scales steeply with the star formation rate (SFR) as $\langle \delta W_0 \rangle \propto \text{SFR}^{1.2}$ [Lan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...795...31L/abstract), reflecting the chemical enrichment and kinematic stirring driven by [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]]. Furthermore, the COS-GASS survey found a strong correlation between a galaxy's interstellar H I gas fraction ($M_{\rm HI}/M_*$) and the Ly$\alpha$ equivalent width in its CGM, suggesting a direct physical link where the circumgalactic envelope continuously nourishes the H I disk [Borthakur et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...813...46B/abstract). In extreme cases, starburst galaxies exhibit exceptionally high (up to 80%) detection rates of C IV in their halos compared to normal star-forming galaxies, providing clear evidence that starburst-driven winds aggressively impact and enrich the CGM out to distances of 200 kpc [Borthakur et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...768...18B/abstract). Similarly, post-starburst galaxies exhibit significantly enhanced Mg II absorption out to 1 Mpc compared to normal star-forming or quiescent galaxies, likely due to high-velocity cool outflows that persist long after the starburst ends [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250622287H/abstract). 

Conversely, the severely depleted CGM around "green valley" galaxies suggests that galaxy quenching is intrinsically linked to the exhaustion or removal of the circumgalactic gas reservoir, leading to star formation suppression via starvation [Kacprzak et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.2289K/abstract). In massive Luminous Red Galaxies (LRGs), the covering fraction of cool gas increases with redshift, though it remains suppressed within the inner $0.3$ virial radii for the most massive systems [Chang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251203845C/abstract). More generally, the radial Mg II absorption profile around galaxies is found to follow a log-linear fit rather than a simple power law, with higher luminosity galaxies exhibiting more extended gas envelopes [Nielsen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...776..115N/abstract).

Host halo mass also fundamentally shapes the CGM. In the low-mass regime, [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] ($M_* \sim 10^{6.5-9.5} M_\odot$) retain surprisingly massive circumgalactic envelopes despite their shallow gravitational potential wells. High-resolution hydrodynamic simulations indicate that approximately $48\% \pm 11\%$ of the baryonic budget in dwarf halos resides in the CGM, producing column densities consistent with current UV absorption surveys [Piacitelli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..230P/abstract). Unlike $L^*$ galaxies, the dwarf galaxy CGM is overwhelmingly dominated by a warm phase ($T \sim 10^{4.5-5.5}$ K) that contains $\sim 70\%$ of the circumgalactic mass. Consequently, highly ionized species like O VI act as ubiquitous tracers, while low-ionization metal absorbers (e.g., C II, Si II) remain scarce beyond $0.5 R_{\rm 200c}$ [Zheng et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960...55Z/abstract), [Mishra et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976..149M/abstract), [Piacitelli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..230P/abstract). This warm reservoir retains a significant fraction of the metals ever produced by the dwarf galaxy (up to $\sim 10\%$ in both warm and cool phases), serving as a critical, long-lived metal sink before gas is permanently lost to the intergalactic medium [Piacitelli et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..230P/abstract). 

Beyond internal host properties, the broader cosmological environment drastically alters the CGM through [[galactic-environmental-stripping|Galactic Environmental Stripping]] and shock heating. In dense environments like galaxy clusters, the extended gaseous halos of satellite galaxies are often truncated. Ly$\alpha$ absorption surveys demonstrate that the CGM covering fraction is significantly suppressed for cluster members compared to isolated field galaxies [Yoon et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...772L..29Y/abstract). As gas-rich satellites fall into massive halos, they encounter cluster-scale accretion shocks and dense intracluster media; this transition rapidly boosts ram pressure, stripping both the interstellar medium and the CGM [Li et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942...44L/abstract). In intermediate environments, such as galaxy groups, the CGM can become a shared reservoir where gas stripped from dwarf satellites is transferred to the halos of massive neighbors, complicating the interpretation of individual galaxy feedback [Nateghi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.3987N/abstract).

## Theoretical Modeling and Cosmological Simulations

Modern hydrodynamic simulations are essential for interpreting the complex, multiphase nature of the circumgalactic medium. Because the CGM acts as a reservoir for both cosmological accretion and recycled gas, it provides a highly sensitive testbed for subgrid models of stellar and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] feedback. Large-volume cosmological simulations—such as IllustrisTNG, EAGLE, and SIMBA—have demonstrated that feedback injects turbulence and drives anisotropic metal enrichment, which significantly alters the density and thermodynamic structure of the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]] [Truong et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508.1563T/abstract). However, significant tensions remain between simulated outputs and empirical data. For example, comparisons of synthetic X-ray surface brightness profiles against eROSITA stacking analyses reveal that simulated quiescent galaxies are often underluminous in X-rays, suggesting their subgrid models rely too heavily on gas ejection to quench star formation [Chadayammuri et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...936L..15C/abstract). Conversely, simulated star-forming galaxies often fail to match the flat, extended profiles observed in reality, indicating underefficient [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]] prescriptions [Chadayammuri et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...936L..15C/abstract), [Grayson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...994...89G/abstract). A comparative analysis using the SMAUG framework suggests that while idealized simulations effectively model how feedback shapes the inner CGM, cosmological effects like non-spherical accretion are necessary to accurately describe the outer CGM phases [Fielding et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903...32F/abstract).

To understand the thermal stability and phase balance of the hot halo, theorists often rely on idealized analytic frameworks such as cooling flows and the precipitation limit. The precipitation limit posits that the ambient CGM pressure cannot exceed a critical threshold where the ratio of the cooling time to the dynamical time drops below a critical threshold of $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm dyn} \sim 4$. Beyond this limit, thermal instabilities cause cold clouds to condense out of the hot atmosphere and "rain" toward the central galaxy, fueling star formation and triggering feedback that subsequently re-heats the gas to restore equilibrium [Voit et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...879L...1V/abstract), [Voit et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190311212V/abstract). These analytic models predict specific entropy and density slopes that are actively being tested against the thermodynamic profiles generated by 3D simulations [Sultan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016359S/abstract), [Singh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.tmp.1670S/abstract). When inferring the thermodynamic profiles of the CGM using the thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect, modeling choices play a crucial role. Accurately modeling the two-halo term and carefully handling galaxy sample selection are essential for correctly recovering the underlying physical pressures of the extended gas from SZ cross-correlations [Moser et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919....2M/abstract).

While large-volume simulations capture global scaling relations, accurately capturing the CGM's small-scale kinematics and multiphase gas requires extreme spatial resolution. Zoom-in simulations, such as those from the FOGGIE project, demonstrate that resolving the CGM down to sub-kiloparsec scales (e.g., $R \sim 0.25$ kpc at $z=1$) is critical [Augustin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993...52A/abstract). Insufficient spatial resolution artificially smears out density peaks and shortens the mixing time between cold and hot phases, leading to inaccurate predictions for cold gas survival [Mohapatra et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251100229M/abstract). High-resolution models show that small-scale clumps are long-lived and feature internally varying ionization levels; properly resolving these structures can increase the predicted total luminosity of CGM emission lines by an order of magnitude [Corlies et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...896..125C/abstract), [Augustin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993...52A/abstract). 

Finally, forward-modeling simulations to match quasar absorption-line observations requires precise tracking of the gas ionization state, which introduces a major theoretical systematic: the assumed metagalactic ultraviolet background (UVB). Different UVB models significantly alter the predicted column densities of metal ions, particularly at lower gas densities and higher temperatures where photoionization dominates over collisional ionization [Taira et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991..221T/abstract), [Taira et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250311775T/abstract). Modifying the prescribed UVB can shift predicted column densities into better alignment with observed data without requiring any changes to the underlying hydrodynamic gas distribution [Corlies et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827..148C/abstract), highlighting a fundamental degeneracy in leveraging ion absorbers to constrain feedback physics.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite significant observational progress, the detailed physics governing the [[multiphase-circumgalactic-medium|Multiphase circumgalactic medium]] remains a subject of active debate. A primary unresolved issue is the efficiency of metal mixing between dense, small-scale clumps and the diffuse, volume-filling halo gas. High-resolution hydrodynamical simulations resolve small-scale clumps down to scales of $R \sim 0.25$ kpc, revealing that these long-lived structures exhibit internal density and ionization gradients [Augustin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993...52A/abstract). Simulated clumps frequently show lower metallicities than their immediate surroundings, suggesting inefficient mixing [Augustin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993...52A/abstract). Consequently, the literature remains conflicted on whether the CGM metallicity distribution is strictly bimodal—tracing distinct pristine accretion streams and enriched [[galaxy-outflows|Galaxy outflows]]—or if it represents a complex, poorly homogenized continuum [Crighton et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...776L..18C/abstract), [Pointon et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...883...78P/abstract).

The thermodynamic stability of the CGM also presents theoretical tensions. It is unclear whether analytic models based on cooling flows or the "precipitation limit" best describe the entropy and density profiles of the [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] across different halo masses [Voit et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...879L...1V/abstract), [Sultan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016359S/abstract). Furthermore, the role of non-thermal pressure support is only beginning to be quantified. Simulations indicate that an in situ turbulent dynamo can amplify magnetic fields in the CGM to strengths of $\sim 0.1\, \mu\mathrm{G}$ by $z=0$ [Pakmor et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.3125P/abstract). Furthermore, theoretical models predict that the escape of cosmic rays into the circumgalactic medium can trigger nonresonant instabilities that generate magnetic fields of ${\sim}0.02\, \mu\mathrm{G}$ on 10 kpc scales [Blasi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvL.122e1101B/abstract). Incorporating cosmic-ray pressure alongside these magnetic fields drastically alters the predicted kinematics of the cool and warm gas, yielding narrower high-ionization absorption lines that better match observations [Butsky et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935...69B/abstract). The thermal pressure profile of the CGM also poses a mystery; measurements of the thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect show that while quiescent galaxies follow self-similar gravitational scaling relations, star-forming galaxies deviate significantly from them. This suggests that the level of active star formation strongly dictates the degree of non-gravitational heating injected into the halo [Das et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991..205D/abstract).

Additionally, there is an ongoing debate regarding the total baryon and metal budgets. While some studies argue that the hot phase contains sufficient mass to account for a halo's "missing baryons," X-ray absorption and emission measurements often find lower-than-predicted column densities within the virial radius, suggesting significant gas depletion or extension to much larger scales [Yao et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...716.1514Y/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240117308Z/abstract). The exact mechanism—such as [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] versus supernova feedback—that maintains the sharp dichotomy in O VI covering fractions between star-forming and quiescent galaxies also remains an open question [Sanchez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...882....8S/abstract), [Tchernyshyov et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...949...41T/abstract).

Addressing these questions observationally requires moving beyond pencil-beam absorption surveys to map the CGM in emission. For the hot phase, future missions equipped with X-ray microcalorimeters (such as LEM, Athena, and XRISM) will provide the $\sim 1$--$2$ eV spectral resolution necessary to disentangle faint CGM emission lines (e.g., O VII and O VIII) from bright Milky Way foregrounds [Kraft et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221109827K/abstract), [Schellenberger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969...85S/abstract). These X-ray microcalorimeters will enable direct measurements of the temperature, velocity, and abundance gradients of individual galaxy halos. Concurrently, in the ultraviolet and optical regimes, the proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory aims to map $10^4$--$10^6$ K gas in emission. By employing multi-object and integral field spectroscopy, the Habitable Worlds Observatory will map macroscopic structures like galactic superwinds, while also unveiling the sub-kiloparsec thermal instabilities that govern the cool gas reservoirs [Burchett et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250703750B/abstract). Together, these next-generation facilities will determine whether the CGM is dominated by pristine accretion, bound outflows, or recycled [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]].
