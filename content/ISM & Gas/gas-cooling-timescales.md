The **gas cooling timescale**, often denoted $t_{\rm cool}$, is a fundamental parameter in astrophysics that quantifies the time required for a parcel of gas to radiate away its internal thermal energy. Defined mathematically as the ratio of the local thermal energy density to the volumetric cooling rate, this timescale is governed by a variety of [[astrophysical-cooling-processes|Astrophysical Cooling Processes]], including thermal bremsstrahlung at high temperatures, metal-line emission in enriched environments, and molecular transitions in cold, dense clouds. In the theoretical framework of structure formation, comparing the cooling timescale to the dynamical or [[free-fall-time|Free-fall time]] ($t_{\rm ff}$) of a system determines the thermodynamic fate of the [[hot-gas|Hot gas]]. If the cooling timescale is longer than the dynamical time, the gas can dynamically adjust to maintain hydrostatic equilibrium. Conversely, if the cooling timescale is shorter, the gas rapidly loses pressure support and undergoes runaway gravitational collapse, providing the primary reservoir of fuel for galactic star formation [Binney et al. (1977)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977ApJ...215..483B/abstract).

Historically, models of galaxy formation assumed that all infalling gas was shock-heated to the virial temperature of the dark matter halo before slowly cooling. However, a deeper understanding of local cooling timescales revealed a bimodal paradigm of galactic mass assembly. While massive halos exhibit this canonical "hot mode" accretion, high-redshift and lower-mass systems are dominated by prompt [[cold-gas-accretion|Cold gas accretion]], where gas streams along cosmic web filaments and radiates its energy so efficiently that it bypasses the hot phase entirely [Katz et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASSL..281..185K/abstract). Furthermore, when the cooling timescale drops below a critical threshold relative to the local free-fall time (typically $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff} \lesssim 10$), the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]] becomes thermally unstable. This local instability drives the fragmentation of the diffuse atmosphere, precipitating a [[multiphase-gas|Multiphase gas]] environment characterized by dense [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] clouds and warm filaments condensing out of the hotter ambient medium.

In the dense cores of massive galaxy clusters, central cooling timescales are often calculated to be significantly shorter than the Hubble time. Early idealized models predicted massive, continuous inward flows of cooling gas, proposing that the cooled mass was hidden in unobservable low-mass stars to explain the lack of optical light [Fabian et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984Natur.310..733F/abstract). However, high-resolution X-ray spectroscopy later revealed a severe observational deficit of X-ray emitting gas cooling below roughly one-third of the virial temperature—a discrepancy now known as the cooling flow paradox [Fabian et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007hvcg.conf...65F/abstract). Resolving this profound discrepancy necessitates the inclusion of potent [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|Heating mechanisms (astrophysics)]] and [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]]. It is now widely accepted that mechanical energy injected by supernovae and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] offsets [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]] losses, regulating star formation and preventing a catastrophic overcooling of the universe's baryons. 

This article explores the theoretical framework and historical development of gas cooling models, detailing the specific radiative channels that dictate the cooling function across varying temperature and metallicity regimes. It examines the physics of multiphase condensation and how cooling dictates empirical star formation scaling relations, such as the [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]], in the interstellar medium. Finally, the article reviews recent computational advances, including the use of artificial neural networks to model complex [[non-equilibrium-cooling|Non-equilibrium cooling]] and irradiated environments, and highlights active open questions—such as the thermodynamic influence of cosmic rays and the search for "hidden" cooling flows using new high-resolution JWST observations.

## Definitions and Theoretical Framework

The gas cooling timescale, $t_{\rm cool}$, serves as the primary metric for [[energy-dissipation|Energy dissipation]] via [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]]. Assuming an ideal monatomic gas, the cooling timescale is given by:

$$ t_{\rm cool} = \frac{\frac{3}{2} n k_{\rm B} T}{n_{\rm H}^2 \Lambda(T, Z)} $$

where $n$ is the total particle number density, $k_{\rm B}$ is the Boltzmann constant, $T$ is the [[kinetic-temperature|Kinetic temperature]] of the gas, $n_{\rm H}$ is the hydrogen number density, and $\Lambda(T, Z)$ is the temperature- and metallicity-dependent cooling function.

The cooling function, $\Lambda(T, Z)$, encodes the microphysics of the two-body radiative processes driving energy loss. For primordial gas at temperatures above $10^6$ K, or enriched gas above a few $\times 10^7$ K, the gas is fully collisionally ionized and cools primarily via bremsstrahlung, scaling as $\Lambda \propto T^{1/2}$. At intermediate temperatures ($10^4 \text{ K} < T < 10^6$ K), cooling is dominated by collisional excitation and subsequent radiative de-excitation. The presence of heavy elements significantly enhances the cooling rate in this regime; collisionally excited lines of ions such as oxygen, carbon, and iron create strong peaks in the cooling function, dramatically shortening the cooling timescale compared to pristine gas [Choi et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.393.1595C/abstract).

The theoretical framework of galaxy formation relies heavily on comparing $t_{\rm cool}$ to the dynamical or [[free-fall-time|Free-fall time]], $t_{\rm ff}$. This ratio dictates whether a gaseous halo can maintain hydrostatic equilibrium or must undergo gravitational collapse:
*   If $t_{\rm cool} > t_{\rm ff}$, radiative losses are slow enough that the gas can dynamically adjust, remaining in a hot, pressure-supported hydrostatic equilibrium.
*   Conversely, if $t_{\rm cool} < t_{\rm ff}$, the gas radiates its thermal energy faster than it can dynamically respond. It loses pressure support and undergoes runaway gravitational collapse, falling toward the center of the potential well. 

In recent years, the ratio $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff}$ has emerged as a critical diagnostic for thermal instability in [[multiphase-gas|Multiphase gas]] environments, such as cluster cores and the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]]. High-resolution hydrodynamic simulations and analytical models indicate that when $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff} \lesssim 10$, the hot gas becomes locally thermally unstable, leading to a "precipitation" of [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] clouds and filaments out of the hot ambient medium [Gaspari et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...746...94G/abstract), [Voit et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.519..203V/abstract). This multiphase condensation provides a mechanism for fueling central supermassive black holes and triggering feedback that reheats the gas. However, the exact universality of the $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff} \approx 10$ threshold remains an active debate in the literature, with some studies arguing that non-linear perturbations, rather than a strict linear instability threshold, govern the onset of condensation [Hogan et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...851...66H/abstract). 

Additionally, the classical thermodynamic assumptions underlying $t_{\rm cool}$ calculations are subject to modification in realistic halos. Recent hydrodynamical studies demonstrate that non-thermal kinetic energy from turbulence can roughly equal the thermal energy in virialized halos. This causes actual bulk gas temperatures to be lower than classically expected from pure thermal virial equilibrium, which directly alters the inferred cooling rates and pressure profiles [Lochhaas et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922..121L/abstract). These thermodynamic modifications also have crucial implications for observational cosmology; for example, radiative cooling and star formation are known to suppress the normalization of the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich Effect (SZE) observable-mass relations by roughly $30-40\%$ compared to pure adiabatic models [Nagai et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...650..538N/abstract).

## Historical Development of Cooling Models

The theoretical framework for gas cooling in galaxy formation originated with the realization that galaxies can effectively form only where the radiative cooling timescale is comparable to or shorter than the dynamical collapse time [Binney et al. (1977)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977ApJ...215..483B/abstract). In the seminal White-Rees model, dark matter halos provide the gravitational potential wells into which baryonic gas falls. Early models assumed a sequence of monolithic collapse: all accreting gas was thought to be shock-heated to the virial temperature of the dark matter halo, forming a hot, hydrostatic atmosphere before radiating its thermal energy and condensing to form stars.

As numerical simulations grew more sophisticated, this purely dissipational framework encountered the "cosmic cooling crisis." Standard hierarchical models with unimpeded radiative cooling predicted that the vast majority of baryons should cool and form stars, resulting in an overabundance of extremely massive galaxies and overly compact disks. Indeed, early semi-analytic models recognized the fundamental role of cooling flows in the hierarchical formation of galaxies, specifically as a mechanism to define the upper mass limit for massive spheroids [Nulsen et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.277..561N/abstract). However, observational constraints, such as the K-band luminosity function, indicate that only $\sim 5\%$ of the universe's baryons globally reside in a cooled state [Balogh et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.326.1228B/abstract). 

A parallel contradiction emerged in X-ray studies of the intracluster medium, known as the cooling flow problem. In the dense cores of many galaxy clusters, the radiative cooling time is significantly shorter than the Hubble time ($t_{\rm cool} \ll t_H$). Classical steady-state models predicted massive, continuous inflows depositing $100-1000 M_{\odot} \text{ yr}^{-1}$ of [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] into the central galaxies. Yet, high-resolution spectroscopy from Chandra and XMM-Newton revealed a severe deficit of gas cooling below roughly one-third of the virial temperature [Fabian et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007hvcg.conf...65F/abstract). While high-resolution spectroscopy generally revealed this deficit, intermediate-temperature cooling was not entirely absent. Direct observational evidence for gas cooling through intermediate temperatures was provided by FUSE detections of O VI emission in elliptical galaxies like NGC 4636 [Bregman et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...553L.125B/abstract). While rare exceptions exist—such as the Phoenix cluster, which exhibits a massive cooling flow of $\sim 3820 M_{\odot} \text{ yr}^{-1}$ that induces a central starburst of $\sim 740 M_{\odot} \text{ yr}^{-1}$ [McDonald et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.488..349M/abstract)—the vast reservoirs of cold gas and young stars expected in most cluster cores are observationally absent. In cooling flow environments, the lack of optical light from cooled gas has historically been interpreted as evidence for an initial mass function (IMF) biased toward low-mass stars [Fabian et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984Natur.310..733F/abstract), [Mathews et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...526..114M/abstract).

Further, the cooling flow problem is not restricted to extreme cluster environments; it also manifests in the Milky Way, whose gaseous halo is calculated to host a cooling flow with a mass inflow rate of $5 - 60 M_{\odot} \text{ yr}^{-1}$, significantly exceeding the galaxy's observed star formation rate [Fang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...894....1F/abstract). To resolve these discrepancies, the paradigm shifted to incorporate potent feedback mechanisms and [[multiphase-gas|Multiphase gas]] dynamics. Mechanical feedback from [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]], primarily through relativistic jets inflating buoyant bubbles, is now understood to offset radiative losses in massive halos [Voit et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...634..955V/abstract). This heating limits catastrophic cooling, though a tension remains regarding whether cooling is entirely quenched or if "hidden" cooling flows persist, obscured by intrinsic photoelectric absorption from cold dusty gas [Fabian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.535.2173F/abstract). Evidence for 'hidden' cooling flows has also been found in elliptical galaxies, with mass cooling rates ranging from 0.5 to $8 M_{\odot} \text{ yr}^{-1}$ [Ivey et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.535.2697I/abstract). Where cooling does occur, it operates as a multiphase process: cold clouds precipitate out of the hot atmosphere via thermal instability when the ratio of cooling time to [[free-fall-time|Free-fall time]] drops below the critical threshold of $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff} \lesssim 10$ [Gaspari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...746...94G/abstract), [Voit et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.519..203V/abstract).

Furthermore, the assumption of universal shock-heating during early monolithic collapse was revised. Hydrodynamic simulations revealed that gas accretion is strongly bimodal. In halos with masses below $\sim 10^{11.5} M_{\odot}$ and at high redshifts ($z > 2$), the dominant growth mechanism is [[cold-gas-accretion|Cold gas accretion]] (or "cold mode" accretion). In this regime, gas flows directly into the central galaxy along cosmic web filaments at temperatures $T \sim 10^4$ K, radiating its energy efficiently and bypassing the canonical hot-phase completely [Katz et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASSL..281..185K/abstract), [Brooks et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...694..396B/abstract), [van de Voort et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.2458V/abstract).

## Primary Radiative Cooling Channels

The volumetric [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]] rate of an [[optically-thin-gas|Optically thin gas]] is typically expressed as $C = n_H^2 \Lambda(T, Z)$, where $n_H$ is the hydrogen number density and $\Lambda(T, Z)$ is the temperature- and metallicity-dependent cooling function. The dominant physical channels for this energy loss depend sensitively on the gas temperature and its chemical composition.

### Bremsstrahlung Emission
In massive dark matter halos and galaxy clusters where the virial temperature exceeds $T \gtrsim 10^7$ K, the [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] is collisionally fully ionized. The primary radiative cooling channel is thermal bremsstrahlung (free-free emission), resulting from the acceleration of free electrons in the electrostatic fields of ions. In this high-temperature regime, the cooling function scales as $\Lambda \propto T^{1/2}$.

### Atomic Cooling and Metal-Line Emission
At intermediate temperatures, $10^4 \text{ K} < T < 10^6$ K, [[atomic-cooling|Atomic cooling]] dominates via the collisional excitation of bound electrons followed by radiative de-excitation. For a gas of primordial composition (hydrogen and helium), the cooling function exhibits distinct peaks at $T \approx 1.5 \times 10^4$ K and $T \approx 10^5$ K, corresponding to the excitation of neutral hydrogen (H$^0$) and singly ionized helium (He$^+$), respectively.

When the gas is chemically enriched, metal-line emission becomes the dominant cooling channel, drastically increasing the overall cooling efficiency [Choi et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.393.1595C/abstract). The cooling function for enriched gas features a prominent peak at $T \sim 10^5$ K driven by ions of carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen, with an additional enhancement at $T \sim 10^6$ K due to emission from neon, silicon, and iron. This metal-line cooling significantly shortens the cooling timescale, accelerating gas accretion and enhancing star formation efficiency at later cosmic epochs [Choi et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.393.1595C/abstract).

### Molecular Gas Chemistry and Fine Structure Cooling
Below $T \approx 10^4$ K, hydrogen recombines, and atomic collisional excitation becomes highly inefficient due to the lack of electrons with sufficient kinetic energy to overcome atomic excitation thresholds. In metal-free primordial gas, further cooling relies heavily on [[molecular-gas-chemistry|Molecular gas chemistry]], primarily the formation of molecular hydrogen (H$_2$) and hydrogen deuteride (HD). The ro-vibrational transitions of HD are particularly dominant for cooling primordial gas below $10^3$ K. Detailed models of its collisional transitions reveal that HD cooling efficiencies can be an order of magnitude higher at high densities than previously reported [Lipovka et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.361..850L/abstract).

In enriched gas, low-temperature cooling proceeds efficiently via collisional excitation of fine-structure lines from [[chemical-elements|Chemical elements]] (e.g., [CII], [OI], [OIII]) and rotational transitions of molecules like carbon monoxide (CO) and H$_2$. The presence of dust grains also facilitates gas-phase depletion and provides a surface for efficient H$_2$ formation, further altering the thermodynamic trajectory of the [[cold-gas|Cold gas]]. For instance, Herschel photometry of the brightest cluster galaxies in cooling flow clusters has revealed massive reservoirs of cold dust at $20-28$ K, whose dust mass and temperature align tightly with the cold molecular gas traced by CO emission [Edge et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518L..47E/abstract).

### Inverse Compton Cooling
In highly energetic, non-thermal environments, the interaction between relativistic electrons and ambient photon fields via inverse Compton scattering provides an additional, vital cooling channel. For example, during the plateau phase of Type IIP supernovae (such as SN 2004dj), inverse Compton cooling can act as the dominant process for electron energy loss at the supernova shocks, significantly shaping the long-term behavior of the observed radio band emissions [J et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180800283J/abstract).

### Influence of Radiation Fields
Standard cooling functions often assume collisional ionization equilibrium. However, the presence of a local ultraviolet radiation field—from young stars or [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]]—can substantially alter the net cooling rate. Photoionization modifies the equilibrium ion fractions, suppressing atomic cooling peaks, while photoelectric heating from dust grains provides a balancing heat source. In environments such as the circumgalactic medium, [[non-equilibrium-cooling|Non-equilibrium cooling]] and local irradiation effects must be accounted for; failing to include the local radiation field can lead to errors in the predicted cooling rates of more than an order of magnitude [Galligan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190101264G/abstract). Machine learning frameworks have recently been developed to accurately interpolate these complex, multi-dimensional irradiated cooling functions for use in modern hydrodynamic simulations [Galligan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190101264G/abstract).

## Accretion Modes: Hot vs. Cold Flow

The classical model of galactic gas infall assumed that all pristine gas entering a dark matter halo undergoes a strong accretion shock, heating to the halo's virial temperature before gradually radiating its thermal energy to condense into a rotationally supported disk. However, high-resolution cosmological hydrodynamic simulations have firmly established a bimodal paradigm of gas accretion, characterized by distinct hot and cold modes [Katz et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASSL..281..185K/abstract), [van de Voort et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.2458V/abstract). 

In "Hot mode accretion," infalling gas shock-heats to temperatures characteristic of the halo's virial temperature ($T_{\rm vir} \gtrsim 10^6$ K in massive systems) to form a quasi-spherical, pressure-supported [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] halo. The subsequent accretion rate onto the central galaxy is strictly regulated by the [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]] timescale of this [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]]. Hot mode accretion governs the growth of massive halos ($M_{\rm halo} \gtrsim 10^{12} M_\odot$) and becomes the primary accretion channel at lower redshifts ($z < 1$), primarily because the physical cross-section of expanding cosmic filaments eventually exceeds that of the central galaxies [Katz et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASSL..281..185K/abstract), [Dave et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..219D/abstract).

Conversely, [[cold-gas-accretion|Cold gas accretion]] (or "cold mode") occurs when gas streams along dense cosmic web filaments and penetrates deep into the halo without ever undergoing a virial shock. This gas remains comparatively cool ($T \lesssim 10^5$ K, and often $\sim 10^4$ K) and is delivered to the central galaxy on a prompt dynamical timescale rather than a protracted cooling timescale [Katz et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASSL..281..185K/abstract), [Brooks et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...694..396B/abstract). Cold mode accretion is the dominant mechanism for galactic mass assembly at high redshifts ($z > 2$) and in lower-mass halos ($M_{\rm halo} \lesssim 10^{11.5} M_\odot$) [van de Voort et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.2458V/abstract), [Dave et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..219D/abstract). Observational evidence for this prompt delivery of pristine gas is found in the "inverse" metallicity gradients of star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 3$, where central starburst regions exhibit lower metallicities than the outskirts due to dilution from metal-poor cold flows [Cresci et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.467..811C/abstract). Furthermore, because cold streams are highly anisotropic, they dictate a significantly different acquisition of angular momentum compared to the canonical shock-heated model [Stewart et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..430..249S/abstract). Tracking these dynamics in the EAGLE simulations reveals that cooling gas initially carries several times the specific angular momentum of the dark matter halo, but it subsequently loses approximately $60\%$ of this angular momentum during the condensation process as it accretes onto the central galaxy [Stevens et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467.2066S/abstract).

While cold streams efficiently fuel early star formation, their physical survival and interaction with the ambient [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]] remain subjects of active debate in the literature. As collimated streams of warm gas penetrate the hot halo, they are subjected to hydrodynamic instabilities (such as the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability) that threaten to disrupt the flow, induce a [[multiphase-gas|Multiphase gas]] medium, and mix the cold gas into the hot phase [Cornuault et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160904405C/abstract). Theoretical models and idealized simulations reveal a complex physical interplay where rapid [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]] in the turbulent mixing layers, alongside self-gravity and magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) effects, may stabilize the streams against these disruptive forces [Mandelker et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021gcf2.confE..63M/abstract). In the critical [[transition-halo-mass-scale|transition mass range]] of $M_{\rm halo} \sim 10^{11} - 10^{13} M_\odot$, where the bulk of cosmic star formation occurs, the phase separation and potential disruption of cold streams can significantly moderate the overall accretion efficiency onto the central galaxy, providing a natural mechanism to regulate early galaxy growth [Cornuault et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160904405C/abstract). These bimodal accretion processes highlight the multiphase nature of the circumgalactic medium (CGM), which acts as the ultimate regulator of a galaxy's gas supply. The complex physical properties and dynamic state of the CGM determine how efficiently hot or cold flows can reach the interstellar medium [Tumlinson et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55..389T/abstract).

## Thermal Instability and Multiphase Gas Formation

When gas in a hydrostatic halo loses energy through [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]], its thermodynamic trajectory depends critically on the relative rates of cooling and gravitational collapse. If the local cooling timescale becomes significantly shorter than the dynamical timescale—typically parameterized by the [[free-fall-time|Free-fall time]], $t_{\rm ff}$—the gas cannot maintain pressure support and becomes thermally unstable. This thermal instability causes the diffuse, [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] ($T \sim 10^6 - 10^7$ K) to fragment, precipitating a [[multiphase-gas|Multiphase gas]] environment characterized by dense [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] clouds and warm filaments ($T \sim 10^4$ K) embedded within the hot ambient medium [Maller et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.355..694M/abstract). 

Extensive hydrodynamic simulations and observational surveys of galaxy clusters have identified a critical threshold for the onset of this multiphase precipitation. Thermal instability ensues, and spatially extended cold filaments condense out of the hot intracluster medium, when the ratio of the cooling time to the free-fall time drops below $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff} \lesssim 10$ [Gaspari et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...746...94G/abstract), [Voit et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.519..203V/abstract), [Voit et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799L...1V/abstract). This threshold governs a self-regulating feedback loop: when $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff} \lesssim 10$, a "rain" of cold gas clouds falls toward the central supermassive black hole, triggering heating from [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] that drives the core back to a higher entropy state above the threshold. This precipitation-regulated framework operates across a wide range of cluster radii, halo masses, and cosmic times, and plays a key role in setting an upper mass limit of $\sim 10^{11} M_\odot$ for quiescent galaxies [Maller et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.355..694M/abstract), [Voit et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...808L..30V/abstract).

The physics of multiphase condensation also dictates the survival of cold clouds entrained in winds driven by [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]]. In a multiphase outflow, the persistence and potential mass growth of cold gas depend on the competition between radiative cooling and hydrodynamic destruction. Cold gas can successfully grow if the cooling time of the mixed warm gas ($t_{\rm cool, mix}$) at the cloud boundaries is shorter than the cloud-crushing time ($t_{\rm cc}$), satisfying $t_{\rm cool, mix}/t_{\rm cc} < 1$. Under these conditions, rapid cooling focuses stripped material into a tail where mixing leads to the net production of new cold gas, often exceeding the original cloud mass [Gronke et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480L.111G/abstract). Furthermore, theoretical models indicate that cold material within galactic outflows can condense directly from the initially hot phase at large radii. This in-situ condensation from the hot outflowing material provides a natural mechanism to explain the observed kinematic differences between absorption and emission line velocities in multiphase winds [Scannapieco et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837...28S/abstract).

The exact physical nature of the $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff}$ threshold remains a subject of active debate. While many models assume that linear perturbations trigger instability at $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff} \approx 10$, high-resolution X-ray observations of cluster cores find that $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff}$ rarely departs significantly below 10, suggesting this value may represent a selection effect or an observational floor rather than a strict trigger for linear instability [Hogan et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...851...66H/abstract), [Hogan et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837...51H/abstract). In such scenarios, cold gas condensation may instead be driven by non-linear perturbations, such as those seeded by the uplift of partially cooled material in the wakes of AGN-driven bubbles [Hogan et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...851...66H/abstract). Furthermore, turbulence is known to play a critical role in the condensation process; the region of active chaotic cold accretion can be defined by the cool-core condensation radius ($R_{\rm ccc}$), where the cooling time equals the turbulent eddy turnover time ($t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm eddy} \approx 1$), which typically peaks at $\sim 0.01 R_{500}$ in massive clusters [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A.102W/abstract).

## Regulation by AGN and Stellar Feedback

As introduced previously, massive halos frequently exhibit central [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]] timescales that are significantly shorter than the Hubble time. Absent any [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|Heating mechanisms (astrophysics)]], this rapid cooling would lead to catastrophic inward flows of gas and runaway star formation. However, observations universally lack the predicted massive reservoirs of [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] and young stars; the global star formation efficiency from cooling in cluster cores is constrained to just $\sim 1.4\%$ [McDonald et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...858...45M/abstract). To avert this cooling flow problem, galaxies employ self-regulating feedback mechanisms to heat the surrounding medium and maintain global thermal equilibrium.

In massive galaxies and galaxy clusters, [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] provides the primary regulatory mechanism. Observations from the SLUGGS survey indicate that while AGN heating is critical, the X-ray luminosity of early-type galaxies is primarily driven by the depth of the gravitational potential well, which shock-heats gas as it falls into the halo [Forbes et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464L..26F/abstract). Supermassive black holes inject mechanical energy into the [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] via relativistic jets, which inflate buoyant cavities and bubbles. The time-averaged mechanical power of these AGN outbursts frequently exceeds the total radiative cooling losses of the intracluster medium by orders of magnitude [Kraft et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...639..753K/abstract). While AGN feedback is generally highly effective at maintaining the gas in a hot phase, notable exceptions exist. The Phoenix cluster, for example, hosts an unsuppressed cooling flow of $\sim 3820\ M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$ that induces a central starburst of $\sim 740 M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$, suggesting that AGN feedback is not yet fully established in all systems [McDonald et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.488..349M/abstract). Recent studies of Abell 1885 suggest a temporal decoupling in feedback loops, where the timescale for energy dissipation in the large-scale intracluster medium is significantly longer than the short-term feeding of the central black hole [White et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603277W/abstract). Furthermore, the exact degree to which AGN quench cooling remains an active debate; recent studies propose the existence of "hidden" cooling flows, wherein the soft X-ray signatures of cooling gas are obscured by intrinsic photoelectric absorption from cold, dusty gas [Fabian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.535.2173F/abstract). While AGN and stellar feedback are the canonical solutions to overcooling, other exotic physical mechanisms have also been proposed. For instance, the theoretical possibility that dark matter neutralino annihilation in cluster cores could provide a steady, volume-filling heating source has been explored as an alternative method to offset cooling and solve the cooling flow problem [Totani et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PhRvL..92s1301T/abstract).

In lower-mass systems, stellar feedback is the dominant regulator of the [[multiphase-gas|Multiphase gas]] environment. Energy and momentum injected by supernovae, stellar winds, and radiation pressure drive [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]]. These processes reheat the interstellar medium, delay or disrupt [[cold-gas-accretion|Cold gas accretion]], and eject material back into the halo, effectively extending the [[depletion-time|Depletion time]] of the galaxy and preventing the rapid conversion of the gas reservoir into stars.

The distribution of this feedback energy throughout the halo relies heavily on microphysical transport mechanisms:

*   **Turbulent heating:** The dissipation of turbulent kinetic energy—often stirred by rising AGN bubbles or merging substructures—can locally balance radiative cooling at nearly all radii in a cluster core [Zhuravleva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.515...85Z/abstract). Mixing-length models for turbulent heat diffusion, parameterized by a dimensionless coefficient $\alpha \approx 0.01-0.03$, successfully reproduce the observed density and temperature profiles of hot halos [Kim et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...596L.139K/abstract).
*   **Thermal conduction:** Heat transport from the hotter outer halo inward to the cooler core has historically been invoked to offset radiative losses. However, the efficacy of thermal conduction is highly contested. While it can be significant in high-entropy cluster cores, anisotropic thermal conduction along magnetic field lines is susceptible to the heat-flux-driven buoyancy instability (HBI). The HBI reorients magnetic fields to be preferentially perpendicular to the temperature gradient, suppressing radial conduction to $\lesssim 10\%$ of the classical Spitzer value [Pope et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.364...13P/abstract), [Parrish et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...703...96P/abstract). 

Consequently, thermal conduction alone is generally considered insufficient to halt cooling catastrophes without the mechanical energy input from AGN or stars. Extensive theoretical models have corroborated this, explicitly demonstrating that the combination of stellar feedback (supernovae), magnetic fields, thermal conduction, and morphological quenching is consistently insufficient to halt cooling flows in Milky Way-mass to cluster-mass halos. The systemic failure of these alternative mechanisms definitively necessitates active AGN feedback to maintain quiescent, red galaxies over cosmic time [Su et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.4393S/abstract).

## Cooling in the ISM and Star Formation Scaling

Radiative cooling in the interstellar medium (ISM) drives the thermal instability that separates galactic gas into a [[multiphase-gas|Multiphase gas]] structure. As warm gas cools below $10^4$ K, atomic fine-structure and molecular line transitions dominate the cooling function, allowing the gas to reach temperatures of $10-100$ K. The rate at which this diffuse medium cools and condenses dictates the availability of fuel for star formation. When the local cooling time drops significantly below the dynamical time, the gas loses pressure support, becomes strongly self-gravitating, and fragments into dense molecular clouds. 

The conversion of this cold, dense gas into stars is empirically described by the [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]], a power-law scaling between the star formation rate surface density ($\dot{\Sigma}_\star$) and the cold gas surface density ($\Sigma_{\rm gas}$):

$$ \dot{\Sigma}_\star \propto \Sigma_{\rm gas}^{N} $$

where the index $N$ is typically observed to be near $1.4 - 1.5$. A fundamental parameter characterizing this process is the [[depletion-time|Depletion time]] (often referred to as the gas consumption timescale), defined as the ratio of the available gas mass to the star formation rate: $\tau_{\rm SF} \equiv M_{\rm gas} / \dot{M}_{\star}$. In normal spiral galaxies, $\tau_{\rm SF}$ is typically $1 - 5 \times 10^9$ yr. This is an order of magnitude or more longer than the relevant dynamical or free-fall timescales of the gas. In contrast, starburst galaxies exhibit much shorter depletion times of $\sim 10^7 - 10^8$ yr, which are closer to the actual dynamical timescales of their dense gas reservoirs.

The stark disparity between the short local [[free-fall-time|Free-fall time]], $t_{\rm ff}$, and the long global depletion time implies a remarkably low [[average-star-formation-efficiency|Average star formation efficiency]]. This efficiency is commonly parameterized per free-fall time as $\epsilon_{\rm ff} \equiv t_{\rm ff} / \tau_{\rm SF}$. High-resolution observations that resolve nearby galaxies into individual star-forming regions show that this efficiency is universally low, constrained to $\epsilon_{\rm ff} \approx 0.5_{-0.3}^{+0.7}\%$ across thousands of clouds [Schinnerer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ARA&A..62..369S/abstract). 

The physical origin of this inefficiency—and the resulting scaling relations—lies in the complex interplay of gas cooling, turbulence, and stellar feedback. Cloud collapse occurs on the order of the turbulent crossing or free-fall time, typically $10-30$ Myr. Following the onset of star formation, rapid pre-supernova stellar feedback unbinds and clears the remaining cold gas on timescales $\lesssim 5$ Myr [Schinnerer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ARA&A..62..369S/abstract). At the kiloparsec scale, the global depletion timescales ($\sim 200-2000$ Myr) are modulated by a continuous ISM cycle of formation, rapid dispersal, and turbulent transport [Kocjan et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260202657K/abstract). Although the ISM is locally capable of efficient cooling and shielding from the interstellar radiation field [Fumagalli et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...722..919F/abstract), the macroscopic [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]] emerges as a time- and space-averaged consequence of highly inefficient, feedback-regulated cooling and collapse.

## Recent Advances: Non-Equilibrium and Machine Learning Models

Standard calculations of [[radiative-cooling|Radiative cooling]] often assume collisional ionization equilibrium (CIE) and a uniform background radiation field. However, these assumptions break down in highly dynamic or strongly irradiated environments where the local cooling timescale ($t_{\rm cool}$) or hydrodynamical timescale becomes shorter than the ionic recombination timescale. In such regimes, the gas undergoes [[non-equilibrium-cooling|Non-equilibrium cooling]] and retains higher ionization states at lower temperatures than predicted by CIE. For example, in catastrophically cooling superwinds driven by starbursts, non-equilibrium ionization generates significantly enhanced ultraviolet line emission (e.g., C IV $\lambda 1549$ and O VI $\lambda 1037$), which cannot be reproduced by static equilibrium models [Gray et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887..161G/abstract). Similarly, the He II $\lambda 1640$ line serves as a robust diagnostic for gas accretion and cooling radiation, as its emissivity is less sensitive to the UV background and identifies gas at distinct temperature histories compared to Lyman-alpha [Yang et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...640..539Y/abstract). Capturing these effects requires explicitly solving time-dependent chemical networks for individual ions, which drastically modifies the effective cooling function $\Lambda(T, n)$.

### Irradiated Environments and Turbulent Dissipation
The volumetric cooling rate of [[multiphase-gas|Multiphase gas]] is also highly sensitive to local energy injection. The presence of a strong, local radiation field—such as from [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] or young stellar populations—introduces intense photoionization heating. Ignoring these local radiation fields and relying solely on a uniform cosmic background can lead to errors in the predicted cooling rate of more than an order of magnitude, particularly in the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]] [Galligan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190101264G/abstract).

Simultaneously, mechanical energy from AGN and galactic winds drives [[macroturbulence|Macroturbulence]] that further alters the thermal balance. In the cores of galaxy clusters, the dissipation of turbulent kinetic energy acts as a volume-filling heat source capable of locally offsetting radiative losses [Zhuravleva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.515...85Z/abstract). When the intracluster plasma is modeled as a combined cooling and heating flow—parameterized by a turbulent viscosity parameter $\alpha \approx 0.05$—the resulting models successfully reproduce observed X-ray spectra while maintaining realistic global cooling efficiencies [Zhoolideh Haghighi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884...47Z/abstract). Furthermore, high-resolution XRISM observations of Cygnus A have detected jet-induced velocity dispersions in multi-temperature gas, indicating that kinetic energy injection rates can significantly exceed the local cooling luminosity [Majumder et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251210167M/abstract).

### Machine Learning Approximations
Accurately computing gas cooling and [[heating-mechanisms-astrophysics|Heating mechanisms (astrophysics)]] on the fly in 3D hydrodynamic simulations using full photoionization codes (like CLOUDY) is computationally prohibitive. To bridge this gap, modern simulations are increasingly utilizing artificial neural networks. Data-driven frameworks such as `deepCool`, `deepHeat`, and `deepMetal` are trained on high-resolution radiation-hydrodynamics grids to predict total and metal-line cooling rates. These machine learning models predict the cooling rates of irradiated gas to within $\sim 5\%$ accuracy relative to full CLOUDY calculations, at a fraction of the computational cost [Galligan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190101264G/abstract).

Recent optimizations of these machine learning algorithms have demonstrated that the immense dimensionality of the incident radiation field can be vastly reduced. The local radiation field can be sufficiently characterized by as few as three broad energy bins (roughly corresponding to molecular hydrogen photodissociation, neutral hydrogen ionization, and fully ionized carbon) without sacrificing predictive accuracy [Robinson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240619446R/abstract). When applied to galaxy formation simulations, these machine learning approximations systematically alter the predicted temperature-density phase diagrams compared to standard polynomial interpolations, resulting in hotter low-density gas and improving the overall thermodynamic modeling of the interstellar and circumgalactic media [Robinson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241215324R/abstract).

### Advanced Subgrid and Integration Techniques
To manage the vast dynamic range required to simulate multiphase environments, modern simulations have developed sophisticated numerical techniques. For example, when the local cooling time drops significantly below the hydrodynamic timestep, standard explicit integration schemes fail or introduce massive errors. Implementing an exact time integration scheme for gas cooling allows hydrodynamic simulations to maintain strict thermodynamic accuracy under these extreme conditions without necessitating computationally prohibitive timestep constraints [Zhu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.1017Z/abstract). At the structural level, another major breakthrough is the Cold Gas Subgrid Model (CGSM), a two-fluid framework designed to actively track the formation, growth, and kinematics of unresolved cold clouds via thermal instability in large-scale galaxy simulations [Butsky et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240203419B/abstract). Additionally, advanced radiative transfer codes like COLT have implemented novel thermal equilibrium solvers that utilize Courant-limited cooling; this approach robustly preserves equilibrium temperatures in diffuse, extended halos while simultaneously resolving complex, multi-scale multiphase gas structures within the interstellar medium [McClymont et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251013952M/abstract). To optimize the modeling of these complex chemical networks without overwhelming computational resources, new approaches like the HYBRID-CHIMES model have been developed. This framework effectively combines on-the-fly non-equilibrium calculations with quasi-equilibrium cooling rates, improving computational efficiency while preserving accurate abundances of ions and molecules in simulations of galaxy formation [Ploeckinger et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543..891P/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite decades of observational and theoretical progress, the fate of gas in systems with short central cooling timescales remains a central puzzle in astrophysics. The classical cooling flow model predicts massive reservoirs of cold gas and extreme starbursts in cluster cores, yet these are rarely observed. This discrepancy is a localized manifestation of the broader "cosmic cooling crisis" or "overcooling problem", wherein the predicted fraction of cooled baryons in galaxies and clusters falls far short of observations without strong feedback mechanisms [Balogh et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.326.1228B/abstract), [Dave et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..219D/abstract). While [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] is widely accepted as the primary heating source offsetting radiative losses, the precise thermodynamics of this regulation remain actively debated.

One unresolved question is whether cooling is genuinely halted by feedback, or if it is simply obscured from soft X-ray observations. The "hidden cooling flow" hypothesis posits that cooling persists, but the signature soft X-ray emission is obscured. Earlier models proposed that this absorption was caused by warm, collisionally ionized gas with $T=10^5-10^6$ K [Buote et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...532L.113B/abstract), whereas more recent models and observations invoke absorption by intrinsic cold, dusty, [[optically-thick-gas|Optically thick gas]] [Fabian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.535.2173F/abstract). Novel JWST diagnostics are proving critical to testing this hypothesis. Recent JWST observations have successfully mapped the [Ne VI] $\lambda 7.65 \mu$m emission line in the Phoenix cluster, directly imaging the intermediate-temperature ($10^{5.5}$ K) cooling gas. This has revealed a massive, recent episode of rapid cooling with estimated rates of $5,000-23,000 \, M_{\odot} \text{ yr}^{-1}$ [Reefe et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025Natur.638..360R/abstract). Expanding these mid-infrared diagnostics to other [[cool-core-clusters|Cool core clusters]] and high-redshift environments will be vital for determining the true efficiency of AGN feedback. The search for hidden cooling flows has also recently expanded to spiral galaxies like the Sombrero and Whirlpool, which may host cooling rates of 0.3 to $1.1 M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$ in their circumgalactic media [Fabian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250814785F/abstract).

Another major theoretical frontier is the physics of multiphase condensation, commonly framed as precipitation. Analytical models and hydrodynamic simulations suggest that [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] clouds precipitate out of the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]] when the ratio of the cooling time to the free-fall time satisfies $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff} \lesssim 10$ [Gaspari et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...746...94G/abstract), [Voit et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.519..203V/abstract). It is currently an open question whether this threshold is a universal constant dictating thermal instability across all halo masses and redshifts, or if it is merely a selection effect arising from non-linear density perturbations and turbulent mixing [Hogan et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...851...66H/abstract). Understanding how this precipitating gas structurally couples to the central black hole is essential to finalizing models of self-regulated feedback.

Another area requiring further study is the impact of extreme local environments on gas cooling. For example, the [[central-molecular-zone|Central Molecular Zone]] of the Milky Way represents an extreme environment where cooling and star formation are potentially regulated by episodic matter transport and intense local dynamics rather than simple gravitational collapse [Morris et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ARA&A..34..645M/abstract). Moreover, the long-term chemical evolution driven by cooling gas is heavily modulated by galactic kinematics. Recent analyses using large stellar age catalogs highlight the critical role of radial gas flows (with velocities around $-1 \text{ km s}^{-1}$) in the Milky Way disk in shaping Galactic chemical evolution and metallicity gradients over cosmic time [Johnson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251005223J/abstract).

Finally, integrating microphysical transport and non-thermal pressure into cooling models is a critical future direction. Cosmic ray feedback has emerged as a particularly potent regulatory mechanism; because cosmic rays possess long cooling times and couple strongly to the thermal plasma, they can provide substantial non-thermal pressure and drive galactic winds [Ruszkowski et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&ARv..31....4R/abstract). It is not yet fully understood how cosmic rays and magnetic fields modify the classical $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff}$ collapse criterion. Similarly, the literature remains conflicted on the role of anisotropic thermal conduction. While conduction along magnetic field lines could theoretically transport heat inward to offset cooling in high-entropy clusters, instabilities such as the heat-flux-driven buoyancy instability (HBI) may reorient magnetic fields and suppress radial conduction to $\lesssim 10\%$ of the Spitzer value [Parrish et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...703...96P/abstract). Resolving the complex interplay between macroscopic AGN feedback and these microphysical [[astrophysical-cooling-processes|Astrophysical Cooling Processes]] will be necessary to develop a complete, predictive theory of galaxy evolution.
