The **hot circumgalactic medium** (hot CGM) is the vast, volume-filling plasma reservoir of [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] that surrounds a galaxy, extending well beyond its stellar disk to the dark matter halo's virial radius and beyond. As galaxies assemble hierarchically, primordial and recycled gas falling into the deepening [[galactic-potential|gravitational potential well]] undergoes supersonic accretion shocks. This process heats the infalling material to the [[mean-galactic-virial-temperature|Mean galactic virial temperature]], typically ranging from $T \sim 10^6$ K for Milky Way-mass halos to even higher temperatures in more [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] and [[clusters-of-galaxies|galaxy clusters]]. For decades, this million-degree corona was theorized as a simple, hydrostatic atmosphere. However, modern observations and hydrodynamic simulations have redefined the hot [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] as a highly dynamic ecosystem that continuously regulates galaxy evolution by governing the supply of fuel for star formation.

Within the broader context of cosmology, the hot CGM is the primary candidate for resolving the galactic "missing baryons" problem. While the stellar components and cold interstellar gas of a galaxy account for only a small fraction of the cosmologically expected [[total-baryon-fraction|Total baryon fraction]], the hot halo serves as a massive repository for the remaining material. The thermodynamic state and [[chemical-abundances|chemical composition]] of this reservoir are fundamentally shaped by continuous [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]]. Energy and [[chemical-elements|metals]] injected by core-collapse supernovae in [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] and by [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] drive powerful biconical winds that heat the halo and prevent catastrophic overcooling. Consequently, rather than acting as closed potential wells, galaxies exchange mass and angular momentum with their extended environments, enriching the hot CGM with heavy elements and often pushing a substantial portion of the baryon budget to super-virial scales.

Observing this highly rarefied plasma is technologically demanding, relying primarily on thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich (tSZ) mapping, large-scale stacking of [[diffuse-x-ray-emitting-gas|Diffuse X-ray emitting gas]], high-resolution ultraviolet absorption spectroscopy of highly ionized species like O VI against background quasars, alongside X-ray absorption studies tracing highly ionized transitions such as O VII and O VIII. These multimodal probes reveal that the hot CGM is not a monolithic structure, but rather a complex [[multiphase-circumgalactic-medium|Multiphase circumgalactic medium]]. It hosts intricate kinematics, including co-rotating gas disks, infalling cosmological streams, and a precipitation of condensed clouds that fuel [[cold-gas-accretion|Cold gas accretion]]. Notably, recent X-ray observations have uncovered a distinct [[super-virial-temperature-gas|Super-virial temperature gas]] phase exceeding $10^7$ K, challenging classical virialization models and highlighting the ongoing, energetic interplay between the galaxy and its halo.

This article explores the physical nature, formation, and evolutionary regulation of the hot circumgalactic medium. It details the observational methodologies used to map its mass budget and the scaling relations tying its X-ray luminosity to host galaxy properties. Finally, it outlines current theoretical tensions, particularly the struggle of modern cosmological simulations to accurately reproduce the observed thermodynamic profiles and the small-scale [[gas-clumping|Gas clumping]] of the halo. Resolving open questions regarding the true thermal structure, the origin of the super-virial phase, and the multiphase interfaces of the hot CGM remains a major frontier, driving the development of next-generation X-ray microcalorimeter missions.

## Physical Nature and Chemical Composition

The physical nature of the hot [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] (CGM) has traditionally been modeled as a volume-filling, quasi-hydrostatic plasma heated to the [[mean-galactic-virial-temperature|Mean galactic virial temperature]] (typically $T \sim 10^6$ K for $L^*$ galaxies) via accretion shocks. However, recent high-resolution X-ray and ultraviolet spectroscopy reveals a substantially more complex thermal landscape. Observations of the Milky Way and other nearby halos indicate the coexistence of multiple temperature phases, including a distinct "supervirial" component exceeding $10^7$ K (or $\sim 0.7$ keV). The origin of this [[super-virial-temperature-gas|Super-virial temperature gas]] remains a subject of active debate; while it may represent a genuinely hotter diffuse phase of the CGM, emerging research suggests that much of the $0.7$ keV X-ray surface brightness in the Milky Way could alternatively be dominated by unresolved low-mass stars in the galactic disk rather than a spatially extended halo. Furthermore, advanced hydrodynamical simulations indicate that the true [[x-ray-temperature|X-ray temperature]] distribution of the hot CGM is broad and log-normal, rendering simple single- or two-temperature models physically incomplete.

The extreme temperatures of the hot CGM govern its ionization state, stripping most atoms of their bound electrons. Consequently, the primary observational tracers of this medium are highly ionized [[chemical-elements|heavy elements]], particularly O VII and O VIII, which dominate the [[diffuse-x-ray-emitting-gas|Diffuse X-ray emitting gas]] and absorption spectra in the $10^6 - 10^7$ K regime. In contrast, intermediate ions such as O VI trace a cooler, transitionary phase ($T \sim 10^{5.5}$ K) that may represent gas radiatively cooling from the hot corona or localized interfaces between cold clouds and the ambient hot medium. Detailed studies such as the Cosmic Ultraviolet Baryon Survey (CUBS) find that this cool CGM component typically has a temperature of $\log T \sim 4.3$ and exhibits widespread non-thermal line widths driven by intra-clump turbulence, particularly in [[passive-galaxies|quenched galaxies]]. Moving to earlier epochs, recent James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) detections of [O III] in narrow, filamentary CGM structures at $z \sim 2.8$ suggest that mechanical heating dominates the energy budget of these higher-redshift systems.

The chemical composition of the hot CGM is fundamentally shaped by metal enrichment from the central galaxy. The plasma is enriched with heavy elements synthesized in stars and transported outward by [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]]. Detailed X-ray absorption studies reveal that the hot gas is frequently $\alpha$-enhanced, exhibiting super-solar [O/Fe] and [Ne/O] abundance ratios indicative of enrichment predominantly by core-collapse supernovae. Furthermore, the discovery of diffuse [C II] envelopes exceeding 20 kpc around merging systems at $z \sim 4.5$ points to early metal enrichment driven by gas stripping during [[gas-rich-galaxy-interactions|galactic interactions]]. Significant molecular components can also exist in the high-redshift CGM, as evidenced by ALMA detections of [C I] and CO forming an extended, cold molecular halo around merging proto-cluster galaxies like the Spiderweb Galaxy. Cosmological simulations suggest an "inside-out" [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|enrichment process]]: while the bulk of the CGM mass may consist of metal-poor gas accreted from the intergalactic medium, the X-ray luminosity-weighted metallicity is often biased toward near-solar values. This occurs because the soft X-ray emissivity is strongly dominated by the collisionally excited metal ions injected by central star formation rather than the pristine cosmological gas. This inside-out picture is supported by observations of the Milky Way halo, which suggest a [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|metallicity gradient]] where the chemical enrichment decreases to approximately 0.1 solar metallicity in the outer parts of the extended halo ($r \sim 250$ kpc).

The spatial distribution of these metals within the hot CGM is a topic of ongoing tension in the literature. Some observational and theoretical studies find strong azimuthal anisotropy, where metal-enriched gas and elevated temperatures are preferentially aligned with the galaxy's minor axis, reflecting the paths of bipolar outflows, while pristine accretion dominates the major axis. Conversely, other deep surveys find no significant azimuthal dependence for various ionization states, suggesting that the CGM is highly structured and clumpy, shaped predominantly by radial processes like thermal instabilities or isotropic precipitation rather than solely by axis-dependent outflows. This structural clumping suggests that [[gas-clumping|Gas clumping]] on sub-kiloparsec scales must be resolved to fully capture the multiphase nature of the halo.

## Formation and Evolutionary Regulation

The formation of the hot [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] (CGM) is fundamentally tied to the cosmological assembly of dark matter halos. As galaxies grow through hierarchical accretion, infalling primordial and recycled gas is accelerated into deepening gravitational potential wells. Upon entering the halo, this gas undergoes supersonic accretion shocks, heating the material to the system's [[mean-galactic-virial-temperature|virial temperature]], which is typically expressed as $T_{\rm vir} \simeq \mu m_p \sigma^2 / k_{\rm B}$, where $\sigma$ is the velocity dispersion of the dark matter halo and $\mu$ is the mean molecular weight.

If gravitational heating were the sole mechanism governing the halo, the dense central gas would radiate its thermal energy over short [[gas-cooling-timescales|Gas cooling timescales]]. This leads to the well-known overcooling problem: theoretical models lacking additional heat sources predict that the majority of halo baryons should catastrophically condense into cold gas and stars. Such a scenario results in a cosmic stellar mass density that violently contradicts observational constraints. To resolve this, the thermal state of the hot CGM must be maintained by continuous feedback regulation from the central galaxy.

In [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] and lower-mass halos, stellar feedback is the primary regulatory mechanism. Supernovae and winds from [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] drive large-scale [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]] that inject both thermal energy and metal-enriched material into the hot halo, with enriched gas detected at distances out to $\sim 200$ kpc in starbursts. Cosmic rays accelerated by supernovae may also provide critical non-thermal pressure support that helps drive these multi-phase winds. The efficiency of this regulation is often parameterized by the mass loading factor, $\eta_M = \dot{M}_{\rm wind} / \dot{M}_\star$. Recent analytic frameworks suggest that an equilibrium state is established where supernova energy exactly balances atmospheric contraction, and that in some regimes, increased mass loading paradoxically requires higher star formation rates to maintain this hydrostatic balance.

In more [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] (halo masses $\gtrsim 10^{12} M_\odot$), stellar feedback alone is energetically insufficient to prevent overcooling. Here, [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] becomes the dominant source of feedback regulation, heating the halo and successfully maintaining quiescent galaxies on the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] through hot halo starvation. Cosmological simulations predict that AGN-driven outflows leave distinct anisotropic imprints on the hot CGM, generally diluting the gas density while simultaneously enhancing the temperature and metallicity along the minor axis of the galaxy. Accurately modeling these feedback processes requires sophisticated numerical treatments. For instance, the recent ARKENSTONE framework introduces a novel method for robustly capturing high specific energy wind components in cosmological simulations, helping to prevent artificial burstiness and accurately resolve low-density, high-velocity flows.

The exact nature of the long-term evolutionary balance remains an area of active debate. One leading paradigm posits that the hot CGM is suspended near a "precipitation limit," where episodic drops in the central pressure trigger thermal instabilities. This causes cool clouds to condense out of the hot phase and rain down on the galaxy, fueling a new feedback cycle that subsequently reheats the halo. However, recent high-resolution simulations suggest that inflows at both $10^{12} M_\odot$ and $10^{13} M_\odot$ mass scales are typically dominated by the hot phase undergoing gradual radiative cooling, rather than by the multiphase precipitation of cold clouds. Furthermore, while simple one-dimensional cooling flow models accurately describe massive halos and the outer CGM of $10^{12} M_\odot$ halos, these 1D models can overpredict inner CGM densities in lower-mass halos due to the increased influence of angular momentum and cold [[gas-fraction|gas fractions]]. This complex interplay varies radially; findings from the SMAUG project demonstrate that while feedback largely dominates the multiphase structure of the inner CGM, cosmological effects such as [[satellite-galaxies|satellite accretion]] play the primary role in shaping the outer halo. In isolated IllustrisTNG halos, this structural shift is evident as large dynamical changes in mass and metal flows are found to frequently occur at a characteristic scale of 0.2 virial radii, effectively marking the physical transition from the inner galaxy to the extended, diffuse halo. Additionally, contrary to classical expectations, simulations suggest that this hot-accreted gas may form stars faster than cold-accreted gas at high redshifts, as the latter can become trapped in turbulent inner-CGM regions for several hundred million years.

## The Missing Baryon Problem and Global Mass Budget

According to standard cosmological models, the expected [[total-baryon-fraction|Total baryon fraction]] of a dark matter halo should reflect the cosmic mean, $f_b = \Omega_b / \Omega_m$, where $\Omega_b$ is the cosmological density parameter for baryons. However, at low redshifts, the sum of baryonic mass observed in stars and the cold interstellar medium accounts for only a small fraction of this expected value. This discrepancy constitutes the galactic "missing baryons" problem. The [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] of the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] (CGM), reaching temperatures of $T \gtrsim 10^6$ K, has long been proposed as the primary repository required to close this baryon budget.

Initial observational constraints provided conflicting views on the extent to which the hot CGM resolves this deficit. Some X-ray absorption studies of the Milky Way suggested that its warm-hot halo contains $\sim 10^{10} M_\odot$ of gas, an amount comparable to the total baryonic mass in the galactic disk, potentially resolving the local missing mass problem. Similarly, ultraviolet absorption measurements of highly ionized oxygen in $L^*$ galaxies suggested that circumgalactic gas accounts for at least half of the missing baryons within the virial radius, $R_{\rm vir}$. Contrasting these findings, earlier X-ray imaging from the Chandra legacy program had shown that this detectable hot gas is often heavily concentrated near the galactic bulge and disk, and that the column density on larger scales may be insufficient to account for the bulk of the missing baryons predicted by standard cosmology.

Indeed, modern large-scale X-ray stacking analyses have recently challenged the assumption that the entire baryon budget is contained within $R_{\rm vir}$. Stacking studies of local [[disk-galaxies|massive spiral galaxies]] reveal that the hot CGM within $R_{\rm vir}$ accounts for only $8 \pm 4\%$ of the expected cosmological baryons. When including the stellar mass, the total [[hot-gas-mass-fraction|gas mass fraction]] and stellar fraction reach only $\sim 27\%$ of the predicted halo budget. Recent data from the eROSITA All-Sky Survey confirm this significant gas depletion within the virial radius of $10^{11.5}-10^{14} M_\odot$ halos. These stacking measurements also demonstrate a steep scaling relation between the hot CGM X-ray luminosity and the central galaxy's stellar mass:
$$L_{X, \rm CGM} \propto M_*^{2.4 \pm 0.1}$$
This strong mass dependence indicates that non-gravitational processes, primarily [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]], strongly modulate the gas content at these mass scales by preventing overcooling and expelling gas into the broader halo environment.

To fully reconcile the baryon budget, observations suggest that the missing baryons have been pushed to scales significantly beyond the virial radius. Extrapolations of the hot CGM density profiles measured by eROSITA indicate that the predicted cosmological baryon fraction is only recovered when integrating out to $\approx 3 R_{\rm vir}$. This extended gas distribution is further corroborated by measurements of the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich (tSZ) effect, which detect high-pressure gas extending well into the circumgalactic environment of $10^{12}-10^{14} M_\odot$ halos. For galaxies residing in $10^{12.3}-10^{12.8} M_\odot$ halos, the tSZ signal confirms that this volume-filling hot phase is the largest single contributor to the galactic baryon budget. Consequently, the literature currently reflects a paradigm shift: rather than galaxies acting as closed potential wells that retain all their accreted gas, the hot CGM represents a highly extended, feedback-regulated reservoir that tracks the cosmic mean only on super-virial scales.

## Observational Probes: X-ray and Sunyaev-Zeldovich Methods

Observing the highly rarefied [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] that constitutes the majority of the volume in the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] (CGM) requires sensitive instrumentation. Current detection of this hot phase relies on three primary observational methods: Diffuse X-ray emission, X-ray absorption line spectroscopy, and the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich (tSZ) effect.

**Diffuse X-ray Emission**
Observations of [[diffuse-x-ray-emitting-gas|Diffuse X-ray emitting gas]] provide a direct probe of the thermal emission from the hot CGM, which radiates via thermal bremsstrahlung and collisionally excited metal lines. Because the surface brightness of the halo is exceptionally low, observers frequently utilize large-scale stacking techniques. Recently, the eROSITA All-Sky Survey has systematically mapped the X-ray surface brightness profiles of the hot CGM around [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], demonstrating that the emission extends out to the virial radius ($R_{\rm vir}$). 

By extrapolating these stacked emission profiles, observers have deduced that the hot CGM within $R_{\rm vir}$ accounts for only a small fraction of the expected [[total-baryon-fraction|Total baryon fraction]], implying that the remaining baryons are distributed out to much larger scales ($\sim 3 R_{\rm vir}$). Furthermore, there is significant tension between these observations and cosmological hydrodynamical simulations (such as EAGLE and IllustrisTNG). Current models frequently under-predict the X-ray luminosities of [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] while predicting steeper, less extended emission profiles for [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] than are observationally derived. Beyond X-rays, kiloparsec-scale integral field spectroscopy has identified a physical boundary between the interstellar medium and the CGM in starburst galaxies, characterized by a break in the surface brightness slope and a transition in ionization source from central star formation to the extragalactic background or shocks. Additionally, the Condor Array Telescope has recently achieved direct imaging of the extremely faint Lyman-alpha emission and dust absorption in the circumgalactic environments of tens of thousands of stacked galaxies. Complementing these emission techniques, tomographic mapping of gravitational arcs at $z \sim 1$ has revealed a clumpy, anisotropic CGM with unexpectedly low kinematic variation over large projected areas.

**X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy**
X-ray absorption against bright background sources, such as quasars, provides a complementary "pencil-beam" probe of the CGM. This method is highly sensitive to the column densities and kinematics of specific highly ionized species. Transitions of O VII and O VIII at soft X-ray wavelengths are the primary tracers of the virialized gas at $T \sim 10^6$ K. Across broader multiphase absorption surveys, the column density profiles of highly ionized species like O VI and lower-ionization species like Mg II are found to be well-described by simple exponential functions of projected distance. Notably, the scale height of these exponential profiles correlates tightly with the ionization energy of the respective ion, providing a direct mapping of the halo's ionization stratification.

Importantly, deep X-ray absorption studies have uncovered a multiphase temperature structure within the hot halo. The detection of highly ionized species like Si XIV and S XVI has provided direct evidence for a [[super-virial-temperature-gas|Super-virial temperature gas]] phase exceeding $10^7$ K (or $\sim 0.7$ keV). Broadly, the coexistence of this hotter phase alongside the standard virial component has been increasingly confirmed across multiple observations and diagnostic methods. As discussed previously, the origin of this $0.7$ keV component is heavily debated, with arguments spanning feedback-heated diffuse plasma to contamination from unresolved stellar emission.

**Thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich (tSZ) Effect**
The Thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich (tSZ) effect offers a redshift-independent measure of the integrated electron thermal pressure in the CGM. By cross-correlating cosmic microwave background maps with large galaxy catalogs, researchers have detected the tSZ signal in the CGM of halos down to masses of $M_{200} \approx 10^{12.3} M_\odot$. These measurements show that the thermal pressure of the CGM surrounding quiescent galaxies closely follows self-similar mass scalings. In contrast, [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] exhibit measurable deviations from self-similarity. This discrepancy indicates that non-gravitational energy injection—driven by stellar and AGN feedback—varies significantly among halos with different star formation histories, leaving a distinct thermodynamic imprint on the hot gas. As an emerging alternative probe of halo electrons, Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) provide an independent measure of the CGM density. Recent localizations of bursts piercing the Andromeda galaxy (M31) offer tentative evidence for its hot halo through dispersion measure contributions along multiple sightlines.

## Dynamics: Outflows, Inflows, and Rotation

The kinematics of the hot [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] (CGM) are defined by a complex velocity field that reflects the continuous interplay between cosmological accretion, co-rotation, and feedback-driven mass ejection. Rather than a static, hydrostatic atmosphere, the hot halo is a dynamic environment exhibiting systematic inflows, outflows, and rotational motions. Measuring the Doppler shifts and line broadening associated with these hot flows is a major target for next-generation high-resolution X-ray spectroscopy.

### Angular Momentum and Co-Rotation
The angular momentum of the hot CGM is a critical parameter governing galaxy evolution. Observational and theoretical studies indicate that the hot gas often co-rotates with the central stellar disk. In Milky Way-mass halos, the rotation velocity of the hot X-ray emitting phase is typically $v_{\phi} \sim 100-200\text{ km s}^{-1}$. This is generally slower than the rotation of colder gas phases but represents a significant reservoir of angular momentum. Extended halos with high specific angular momentum can prevent gas from flowing efficiently into the central star-forming reservoir, acting as a mechanism to maintain [[passive-galaxies|quenched, passive galaxies]]. Conversely, galaxies with centrally concentrated starbursts often feature a CGM with anomalously low angular momentum, facilitating rapid radial inflow. Observations connecting the kinematics of the cooler H I circumgalactic medium to galaxy rotation reveal that the H I co-rotation fraction is approximately 0.6 within the virial radius, but significantly decreases beyond it, particularly for gas located along the minor axis.

### Inflows and Accretion
To sustain star formation, galaxies must accrete gas from their halos. In the hot CGM, this process often manifests as slow, radial inflows near the galactic plane, with typical velocities of $v_{r} \sim 50-100\text{ km s}^{-1}$. The dominant [[gas-accretion-modes|Gas accretion modes]] depend strongly on the halo mass. In massive halos ($M_{\text{halo}} \gtrsim 10^{12} M_{\odot}$), cosmological simulations suggest that gas inflows are dominated by the hot phase undergoing gradual radiative cooling, which behaves as a classic cooling flow rather than precipitating as cold clouds. These hot inflows mix with the co-rotating disk material, supplying the necessary fuel for subsequent star formation. Interestingly, theoretical models suggest that coplanar accretion flows passing through the extended halo can generate large-scale Kelvin-Helmholtz turbulence exceeding a few kiloparsecs in scale; detecting this specific turbulent signature could serve as an indirect observational probe of active gas accretion from the CGM onto the central galaxy.

### Biconical Outflows and Anisotropy
Counteracting these inflows are powerful [[galaxy-outflows|Galaxy outflows]] driven by supernovae and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|active galactic nuclei]]. Feedback mechanisms inject mass, momentum, and thermal energy into the CGM, driving hot, metal-enriched winds predominantly along the minor axis of the galaxy, the path of least resistance. In typical star-forming disk galaxies, these hot outflows achieve velocities of $200-500\text{ km s}^{-1}$, while in extreme compact starbursts, multiphase winds can extend out to $100\text{ kpc}$ with components reaching velocities of $\sim 1500\text{ km s}^{-1}$.

This biconical outflow geometry is expected to introduce strong spatial anisotropy into the CGM. Simulations predict that along the minor axis, the hot gas is typically less dense but significantly hotter and more metal-rich than the gas along the major axis. However, the observational literature on this geometric separation remains conflicting. While some studies detect strong minor-axis metal enhancements, other multiphase absorption surveys find no significant azimuthal dependence for metallicities, suggesting that sightlines probe gas of highly mixed origins—incorporating both outflows and accretion—at all azimuthal angles. Even on macroscopic scales, the kinematic complexity of outflows can be highly asymmetric. Hydrodynamic modeling of the Milky Way's eROSITA bubbles, for instance, reveals morphological distortions consistent with a dynamic circumgalactic wind blowing across the halo at $\sim 200\text{ km s}^{-1}$, highlighting a turbulent and ongoing interaction between accreting material and galactic feedback.

## Multiphase Interactions and Thermal Stability

The [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] halo of a galaxy is a highly dynamic environment that constantly interacts with cooler gas phases, shaping a complex [[multiphase-circumgalactic-medium|Multiphase circumgalactic medium]]. These multiphase interactions—governed by thermal instability, magnetic fields, and thermal conduction—are critical for regulating the accretion of fuel into [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] and maintaining the global thermodynamic balance of the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]].

**Thermal Instability and the Precipitation Limit**
A fundamental mechanism driving multiphase interactions is thermal instability. In the hot halo, local density perturbations can cool rapidly, breaking the single-phase equilibrium and leading to the condensation of cool ($T \sim 10^4$ K) clouds. Theoretical frameworks propose that the physical state of the halo is governed by a "precipitation limit". According to this model, the ambient gas pressure cannot exceed the threshold at which cold clouds condense and precipitate toward the galactic center. If this precipitation fuels central active galactic nuclei or stellar feedback, the injected energy heats and expands the halo, lowering its pressure and halting further condensation. This self-regulating feedback loop tends to suspend the halo near the precipitation threshold. While cold gas condensation occurs frequently, its molecular content remains observationally constrained; CO absorption studies toward background quasars have successfully ruled out an extremely molecular-rich CGM in normal low-redshift galaxies.

Analytic models of precipitation predict that halos maintained in this state exhibit a characteristic radial entropy profile with a slope of $K(r) \propto r^{0.8}$. Furthermore, high-resolution cosmological simulations indicate that thermal instability in the hot medium is a primary formation channel for cool structures. For instance, approximately $62\%$ of Galactic high-velocity clouds may originate from circumgalactic gas cooling via thermal instabilities before acquiring anomalous velocities through interactions with the turbulent halo.

**Magnetic Fields and Thermal Conduction**
The survival and evolution of these condensed cool clouds depend heavily on their thermal interface with the surrounding million-degree plasma. In a purely hydrodynamic regime, steep temperature gradients would cause the rapid evaporation of cold clouds via thermal conduction. However, the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] is magnetized. Cosmological magnethydrodynamic simulations demonstrate that an *in situ* turbulent dynamo can amplify the circumgalactic magnetic field over gigayear timescales, reaching typical strengths of $\sim 0.1 \, \mu\mathrm{G}$ near the virial radius at $z=0$. This aligns with observational Faraday rotation measures, which place strict upper limits on the coherent line-of-sight magnetic field strength at $<2 \, \mu\mathrm{G}$.

Magnetic fields severely restrict the transport of electrons perpendicular to the field lines, rendering thermal conduction highly anisotropic. When parameterized as an effective isotropic conduction, the Spitzer thermal conduction efficiency factor $f$ is heavily suppressed to values of $f \approx 0.03 - 0.15$. Because conduction is largely quenched, cold clouds are shielded from rapid evaporation, allowing the continuous condensation of hot gas onto existing cold structures. 

This interface physics supports a morphological picture where the cool medium exists not as monolithic clouds, but as a fragmented "mist" of tiny cloudlets dispersed throughout the warm-hot medium. The blending of these individual cloudlets generates complex turbulent kinematics and localized mixing on the scale of cloud complexes. This structural complexity is further reinforced by high-resolution FOGGIE simulations, which resolve sub-kiloparsec clumps characterized by systematically lower metallicities than the ambient hot gas, an effect that heavily impacts observational metallicity estimates. Consequently, the interplay between suppressed conduction and thermal instability is essential for sustaining [[cold-gas-accretion|Cold gas accretion]] from the hot halo down to the galactic disk.

## Scaling Relations and Environmental Impact

Because the X-ray emission from the [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] in the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] (CGM) of $L^*$ galaxies is extraordinarily faint, statistical techniques such as stacking analysis have been critical for quantifying its global properties. Before the advent of these large stacking surveys, early detections of hot halos around individual giant spiral galaxies, such as NGC 1961, suggested a missing baryon fraction of up to 75% relative to the cosmic mean. Stacking large samples of galaxies from X-ray surveys (e.g., Chandra and eROSITA) has made it possible to map X-ray surface brightness profiles out to the virial radius and derive quantitative scaling relations between the CGM, the host galaxy's stellar mass ($M_*$), and the total halo mass ($M_{\rm 500c}$). Recent stacking results from the eROSITA All-Sky Survey targeting nearby $L^*$ galaxies have successfully detected diffuse X-ray emission extending out to 50 kpc, closely matching the predictions of the IllustrisTNG simulations.

The hot CGM X-ray luminosity ($L_X$) scales strongly with both stellar and halo mass. Observational constraints indicate that X-ray luminosity scales with stellar mass as:
$$L_X \propto M_*^{2.4 \pm 0.1}$$
for galaxies in the mass range $10^{10} < M_*/M_\odot < 10^{11.5}$. Similarly, the luminosity scales with the host halo mass as:
$$L_X \propto M_{\rm 500c}^{1.32 \pm 0.05}$$ 
spanning over two orders of magnitude in halo mass. Locally, spatially resolved studies of galaxies like M51 reveal a superlinear relationship between diffuse X-ray luminosity and the star formation rate in [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|galactic centers]], where core-collapse supernovae efficiently heat the central gas. These observed relations are highly sensitive to the underlying stellar-to-halo mass relation and indicate that non-gravitational processes dominate the thermodynamics of galaxy-scale halos. In cooler gas phases, Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) results show that the Mg II equivalent width strongly correlates with stellar mass below $10^{11} M_\odot$ and is significantly enhanced by outflows along the minor axis. Additionally, a direct connection between the halo and the interstellar medium is evident in the COS-GASS survey, which established a strong correlation between a galaxy's cold gas fraction and its impact-parameter-corrected Lyman-alpha equivalent width.

These scaling relations directly inform the "missing baryon" problem. As demonstrated by the emission profiles, the shallow density gradients imply that feedback processes have pushed a substantial portion of the baryonic mass to super-virial scales ($\approx 3 R_{\rm vir}$), leaving only a small fraction within the virial radius. 

Although cosmological hydrodynamical simulations (such as IllustrisTNG, EAGLE, and SIMBA) generally reproduce the presence of the hot CGM, significant tensions remain. The observed $L_X$ increases more slowly with stellar mass than predicted by many of these models. Furthermore, while massive galaxy clusters follow a well-defined [[l-t-relation|L-T relation]], the scaling at the galaxy mass scale demonstrates that feedback severely breaks self-similarity. Simulated CGM temperatures and densities often deviate from observations, reflecting uncertainties in the subgrid implementations of [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]] and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]].

The hot CGM is also inextricably linked to galaxy quenching mechanisms. A naive expectation of feedback-driven quenching is that the CGM must be completely expelled to starve the galaxy of fuel, a scenario that may apply to some transitional galaxies where the CGM is physically absent. However, stacking analyses reveal that massive [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] (quiescent galaxies with $\log(M_*/M_\odot) > 11.0$) actually host *brighter* and more luminous hot X-ray halos than [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] of the same stellar mass. In contrast, simulated quenched galaxies in models like IllustrisTNG are often much dimmer than observed, suggesting that simulations may rely too heavily on massive gas ejection to terminate star formation. This implies that for many massive galaxies, starvation is driven not by the physical absence of the CGM, but by the gas being maintained at high temperatures (e.g., via [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|AGN heating]]) or possessing too much angular momentum to accrete efficiently onto the central disk.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite significant observational and theoretical progress, fundamental questions regarding the thermodynamics, kinematics, and total mass of the [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] in the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] (CGM) remain unresolved. A primary tension lies in the galactic baryon budget. While early estimates suggested the hot CGM could entirely account for a galaxy's expected share of [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]], recent X-ray stacking analyses indicate that the hot gas within the virial radius ($R_{\rm vir}$) accounts for only $\sim 8-10\%$ of the cosmological baryon fraction, implying the remaining baryons may be pushed to larger scales up to $\sim 3 R_{\rm vir}$.

The thermal structure of the halo also presents active debates. High-resolution X-ray spectroscopy has revealed a component exceeding $10^7$ K along several galactic sightlines. The literature is currently conflicting on the origin of this [[super-virial-gas|Super-virial gas]]; it remains heavily debated whether it represents a distinct, feedback-heated physical phase in the CGM, or if the observed $0.7$ keV X-ray emission is substantially contaminated by unresolved low-mass stars in the galactic disk. Furthermore, the origin of the [[cold-gas|Cold gas]] ($\sim 10^4$ K) embedded within the hot halo is highly contested. While earlier models favored supernova-driven [[galactic-outflows-and-feedback|Galactic Outflows and Feedback]], recent studies specifically challenge the outflow scenario, instead providing evidence that this cool phase primarily arises from cosmological inflows and the thermal instability and condensation of the hot phase.

These observational ambiguities are compounded by modeling challenges. Current cosmological hydrodynamical simulations, such as IllustrisTNG, EAGLE, and SIMBA, struggle to consistently reproduce the observed X-ray luminosity and temperature scaling relations of the hot CGM. Simulated halos frequently predict X-ray luminosities that scale differently with stellar mass than observed, suggesting that current subgrid models for active galactic nuclei (AGN) and stellar feedback require significant refinement. Additionally, simulations often lack the spatial resolution necessary to capture the extreme [[gas-clumping|Gas clumping]] (the "mist") inherent to [[multi-gas-phase-components|Multi-gas phase components]], which drastically impacts predicted column densities and can lead to simulated gas that is artificially hotter than observations suggest. Incorporating non-thermal pressure support from cosmic rays and magnetic fields represents a critical frontier in resolving these thermodynamic discrepancies.

Addressing these open questions requires moving beyond one-dimensional absorption "pencil beams" to perform direct 2D mapping and 3D tomographic reconstructions of the diffuse plasma. This observational leap will be driven by the deployment of advanced X-ray microcalorimeters on upcoming space observatories. Future facilities, such as the HUBS mission (Hot Universe Baryon Surveyor) and Athena, are designed to provide unprecedented eV-level spectral resolution. These X-ray microcalorimeters will be capable of separating the faint, extended emission of the hot CGM from the bright Milky Way foreground. By mapping the emission and absorption of highly ionized species like O VII and O VIII, these missions will directly measure the velocity fields, abundance ratios, and radial temperature structure of the hot halo, providing the definitive empirical constraints needed to resolve current tensions in galaxy evolution models. In the ultraviolet regime, upcoming missions like Aspera will map diffuse O VI emission, which high-resolution FOGGIE simulations predict will primarily trace the warm ionized envelopes of clumpy structures accreting via a metal-enriched [[galactic-fountains|galactic fountain]].
