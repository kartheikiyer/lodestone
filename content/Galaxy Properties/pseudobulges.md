## Article

In galactic astrophysics, **pseudobulges** are central, dense stellar components of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] that form through slow, internal [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] rather than through violent major mergers. Historically, all central [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] were assumed to be "classical" bulges—dynamically hot, pressure-supported spheroids resembling scaled-down [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. However, modern kinematic and photometric surveys have established a fundamental two-family paradigm, revealing that many bulges retain a strong structural and dynamical "memory" of a disk origin [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). Driven by non-axisymmetries such as [[galactic-bars|Galactic Bars]], secular processes redistribute angular momentum and funnel cold gas into the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]]. Over gigayear timescales, this continuous or episodic gas transport fuels [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|Starburst Galaxy Evolution]] that builds a rotationally supported, disk-like central concentration [Fisher et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...642L..17F/abstract).

Pseudobulges are distinguished from their classical counterparts by several robust observational signatures. Photometrically, they exhibit flatter, nearly exponential surface brightness profiles, which contrasts with the steeper, highly concentrated profiles of classical spheroids. Kinematically, they are dominated by ordered rotation rather than random velocity dispersion. Furthermore, pseudobulges frequently host active star formation and planar [[morphological-inner-structures|Morphological inner structures]] that are indistinguishable from larger-scale disk phenomena, such as nuclear bars, nuclear spirals, and circumnuclear rings [Kormendy et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...642..765K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). The broader pseudobulge classification also encompasses boxy/peanut-shaped (B/P) bulges; rather than being true spheroids built by mergers, these are products of [[peanut-formation|Peanut formation]], representing the vertically thickened inner regions of bars that have been dynamically lofted out of the midplane by collisionless buckling instabilities [Athanassoula et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0802.0151A/abstract), [Gadotti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1208.2295G/abstract).

The ubiquity of pseudobulges has profound implications for theories of galaxy formation and supermassive black hole co-evolution. Unlike classical spheroids, pseudobulges do not strictly adhere to standard galactic scaling laws, frequently harboring undermassive central black holes that fall well below the traditional $M_{\rm BH}-M_{\rm bulge}$ and $M_{\rm BH}-L_{\rm bulge}$ relations, although the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ relation appears to continue to low masses. Furthermore, the existence of massive "pure disk" galaxies hosting only secularly evolved pseudobulges poses a significant theoretical challenge to the continuous hierarchical merging predicted by standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology. Current research actively explores these open questions, investigating how pristine secular structures survive hierarchical clustering and distinguishing them from pseudo-components formed by alternative external environmental interactions.

## Taxonomy and Historical Development

For decades, the central components of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] were interpreted through the lens of a monolithic Hubble sequence. In this classical framework, all galactic bulges were presumed to be scaled-down [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] embedded within a stellar disk—dynamically hot, pressure-supported spheroids formed through violent major mergers. 

This paradigm began to fray as kinematic and photometric anomalies emerged in the late 20th century. Observers noted that many bulges, particularly in late-type spirals, exhibited near-exponential surface brightness profiles and significant rotational support, contradicting the expectations for isotropic oblate rotators [Gadotti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1208.2295G/abstract). This growing body of observational evidence culminated in a major paradigm shift, synthesized most notably in the seminal reviews by Kormendy & Kennicutt (2004) and expanded upon in subsequent literature [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). This work firmly established a heterogeneous, two-family paradigm for galaxy centers. Cosmologically, this distinction is crucial to the overall stellar mass budget: disks account for approximately 58% of the local stellar mass density, while red spheroids (comprising ellipticals and classical bulges combined) make up roughly 39% [Driver et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...657L..85D/abstract).

The first family comprises traditional "classical bulges," which share the merger-built origins and tight supermassive black hole scaling relations of ellipticals. The second family, termed "pseudobulges," consists of structures that are the products of slow, internal [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]. The taxonomic distinction between the two classes relies on identifying whether a central component retains a structural and kinematic "memory" of its disk-like origin. Photometrically, classical bulges exhibit steep, highly concentrated surface brightness profiles, typically fit by a Sérsic index of $n \gtrsim 2$ to $n \approx 4$. In contrast, pseudobulges feature flatter, nearly exponential profiles with $n < 2$ [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). Kinematically, classical bulges are generally pressure-supported, whereas pseudobulges are rotationally dominated, exhibiting high values of $v/\sigma$ (the ratio of ordered rotation to random velocity dispersion) [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). 

Within the broad category of secularly evolved components, a further structural distinction is made between nuclear "disky" pseudobulges and boxy/peanut-shaped (B/P) bulges. Though historically grouped together observationally, simulations and kinematics confirm that B/P structures result from [[peanut-formation|Peanut formation]] in the vertically thickened inner regions of [[galactic-bars|Galactic Bars]] viewed edge-on, generated by buckling instabilities [Athanassoula et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0802.0151A/abstract). True disky pseudobulges and B/P structures frequently coexist within the same galaxy, operating on different spatial scales [Barentine et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...754..140B/abstract).

The formal recognition of pseudobulges has introduced a profound challenge to standard $\Lambda$CDM hierarchical clustering models. Because hierarchical theories predict frequent minor and major mergers over cosmic time, almost every giant galaxy is expected to host a classical, merger-built bulge. The widespread prevalence of massive, pure disk galaxies hosting only secularly evolved pseudobulges remains a significant tension in modern cosmology [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract).

## Mechanisms of Internal Secular Evolution

Internal [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] is the fundamental dynamical process by which isolated [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] slowly rearrange their energy, mass, and angular momentum over cosmic time [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). This evolution is primarily driven by non-axisymmetric structures, most notably [[galactic-bars|Galactic Bars]] and oval distortions, which exert strong gravitational torques on the galactic components. Because the interstellar medium is highly dissipative, gas clouds shock and lose their angular momentum to the bar, allowing gas to flow radially inward. The bar, in turn, efficiently transfers angular momentum outward to the broader stellar disk and surrounding dark matter halo [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract), [Kwak et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839...24K/abstract). However, sustaining this prolonged secular evolution and the cycle of bar reformation over cosmic timescales requires continuous external gas accretion from intergalactic filaments [Combes et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ASPC..419...31C/abstract). Indeed, models suggest that galaxies must roughly double their mass over $10\text{ Gyr}$ through the accretion of cold gas from these intergalactic filaments to sustain prolonged secular evolution [Combes et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319...57C/abstract). 

As gas is funneled into the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]], it reaches high surface densities $\Sigma_{\text{gas}}$ and triggers active star formation, often manifesting as [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|Starburst Galaxy Evolution]] or circumnuclear dust and gas rings [Kormendy et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...642..765K/abstract). Over timescales of several gigayears, this continuous or episodic central star formation builds a dense, rotationally supported stellar core known as a "discy" pseudobulge. Given the specific star formation rates measured in local late-type galaxies, it is estimated that a typical pseudobulge could assemble its present-day stellar mass in approximately $8\text{ Gyr}$ purely through this internal gas transport and in-situ star formation [Fisher et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697..630F/abstract).

Parallel to the gas-dynamical formation of discy pseudobulges, collisionless stellar dynamics drive [[peanut-formation|Peanut formation]]. As the bar redistributes angular momentum, the central stellar material is kinematically heated in the vertical direction, creating a thickened, peanut-like morphology when viewed edge-on [Athanassoula et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0802.0151A/abstract). Discy pseudobulges and B/P bulges frequently coexist, representing the respective gas-dissipative and collisionless outcomes of the same secular torque mechanisms. In addition to bar-driven dynamics, radial migration acts as another vital internal secular process. By extensively mixing stellar populations throughout the disk, radial migration contributes to the vertical thickening of disks into structures that resemble [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] or pseudobulges without destroying the ordered rotation of the system [Loebman et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...737....8L/abstract). The link between the Galactic thick disk and the broader secular assembly of disks and bulges is further emphasized by high-resolution spectroscopic findings [Bensby et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..298...17B/abstract). To contextualize the balance between internal and external growth drivers, a hybrid cosmological approach for modeling Milky Way assembly is often utilized, capturing the formation of the Galaxy, its stellar halo, and its satellites within a cosmological environment [De Lucia et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AN....333..460D/abstract).

Despite the canonical textbook view of internal bar-driven transport, the literature presents significant tension regarding whether these features are exclusively formed by isolated, internal processes. Cosmological simulations suggest that in some systems, the structures observationally classified as pseudobulges actually originate from rapid, high-redshift starbursts fed by early inflows of low-angular-momentum gas. In such models, late-time internal secular evolution accounts for only $\sim 13\% - 30\%$ of the final bulge mass [Okamoto et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..718O/abstract). Furthermore, external drivers, such as minor mergers and tidal interactions, can readily induce bar instability and drive identical gas inflows. For instance, even a 1:3 mass-ratio merger event has been shown capable of triggering a giant bar [Peirani et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...496...51P/abstract). During strongly interacting galaxy encounters and major mergers, the resulting tidally driven gas inflows can dilute central metallicities and significantly flatten the radial gas-phase metallicity gradients compared to isolated [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|Star-forming disk galaxies]] [Rupke et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...723.1255R/abstract). In this scenario, the "secular" central starbursts responsible for building a pseudobulge are ultimately externally triggered [Kannappan et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127.1371K/abstract). 

## Structural and Morphological Characteristics

A defining characteristic of pseudobulges is their structural similarity to [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] rather than to the dynamically hot spheroids typical of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. Photometrically, pseudobulges are characterized by nearly exponential light profiles. When fitted with a standard Sérsic surface brightness profile, $I(R) = I_e \exp\{-b_n [(R/R_e)^{1/n} - 1]\}$, pseudobulges typically exhibit a Sérsic index $n < 2$. In contrast, merger-built classical bulges display much steeper light profiles closer to the de Vaucouleurs law, where $n \sim 4$ [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). At the extreme morphological end of secular disk evolution are 'superthin' disk systems, which serve as crucial test cases for models of quiescent star formation and disk stability [Gallagher et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..285..303G/abstract).

Because they form via the slow, secular rearrangement of disk material—which is generally locally stable, as quantified by Toomre's $Q$ parameter, and whose formation was already well advanced by a redshift of $z \sim 2$ [van der Kruit et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ARA&A..49..301V/abstract)—pseudobulges retain a strong structural "memory" of their origins [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). They are generally flatter than classical bulges and host intricate, rotationally supported substructures that are hallmarks of continuous gas inflow and disk-like dynamics. High-resolution imaging reveals that pseudobulges frequently contain [[morphological-inner-structures|Morphological inner structures]] such as nuclear bars, nuclear spiral arms, and nuclear rings [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...642..765K/abstract). 

These features are dynamically distinct from the host galaxy's main disk and operate on much smaller spatial scales. Bulges in early-type spirals (S0-Sb) almost universally contain these central nuclear components, whose individual luminosities correlate strongly with the total bulge luminosity [Peletier et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..2286P/abstract). Central star clusters are also common across all [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] and are often intimately linked to this bar-regulated gas inflow [van der Marel et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001IAUS..205..154V/abstract). For instance, the galaxy NGC 4593 features a prominent nuclear ring of dust connected to radial dust lanes along its primary bar, explicitly tracing active gas transport into the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]] [Kormendy et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...642..765K/abstract). 

While these structures generally align closely with the host disk, rare configurations known as 'polar bulges' exist (for example, in NGC 4698), where a nuclear disk or bulge rotates perpendicularly to the main galaxy disk [Corsini et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423L..79C/abstract). The overwhelming presence of active, star-forming nuclear features firmly links the broader growth of pseudobulges to the internal secular evolution of galaxies, clearly differentiating them from quiescent classical bulges [Fisher et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...642L..17F/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015HiA....16..316K/abstract).

## Kinematics and Stellar Populations

Pseudobulges are fundamentally distinguished from classical bulges by their dynamical support and star formation histories. While classical bulges are primarily pressure-supported systems, pseudobulges exhibit rotationally supported kinematics and ongoing star formation [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). 

The dynamical support of a bulge is typically quantified by the ratio of ordered rotational velocity to random velocity dispersion ($V/\sigma$). Observational studies consistently show that pseudobulges possess $V/\sigma$ ratios that are significantly larger than expected for an isotropic oblate rotator [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract). Furthermore, their central velocity dispersions ($\sigma$) are typically smaller than the values predicted by the Faber-Jackson relation for classical bulges of equivalent luminosity [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). 

However, the literature cautions that kinematics alone can be deceptive. Hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate that gas-rich merger remnants can also exhibit significant rotation and elevated $V/\sigma$ ratios, complicating the purely kinematic distinction between secularly evolved pseudobulges and merger-built components [Graham et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015HiA....16..360G/abstract). Consequently, robust identification requires a combination of kinematic, structural, and photometric diagnostics [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015HiA....16..316K/abstract).

The stellar populations of pseudobulges further reflect their formation from disk material. Age patterns observed orthogonal to spiral arms, alongside detailed metallicity distribution functions in simulated cosmological disk galaxies, explicitly link star formation to the dynamics of the disk components from which pseudobulges emerge [Pilkington et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AAABS...4..112P/abstract). Unlike classical bulges, which are typically dominated by uniformly old stellar populations formed in rapid early bursts, pseudobulges frequently contain young stars and actively form stars at the present day [Fisher et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...642L..17F/abstract). Observations of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] reveal that the specific star formation rate (sSFR) in pseudobulges often exceeds that of the surrounding outer disk, driving an ongoing increase in the bulge-to-total mass ratio ($B/T$) through internal star formation [Fisher et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697..630F/abstract). 

This elevated sSFR is tightly linked to gas inflow driven by [[galactic-bars|Galactic Bars]] and oval distortions [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract). This behavior highlights a stark contrast between the efficient early star formation observed in massive galaxies (often referred to as downsizing) and the highly stochastic, ongoing star formation histories found in late-type and [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] [Grebel et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..270..335G/abstract). This phenomenon can be accelerated by [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|Starburst Galaxy Evolution]] triggered by external factors; for example, interacting galaxies with pseudobulges in close major-merger pairs show significantly broadened and elevated sSFR distributions compared to isolated controls [He et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24e5005H/abstract). 

While the standard secular model implies continuous, slow growth, detailed studies of the Milky Way's nuclear disk suggest that pseudobulge assembly can be highly episodic. The bulk of the Milky Way's nuclear disk stars formed over $8\text{ Gyr}$ ago, but following a long quiescent phase, the region underwent a massive starburst $\sim 1\text{ Gyr}$ ago that produced roughly $5\%$ of its stellar mass within just $\sim 100\text{ Myr}$ [Nogueras-Lara et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..377N/abstract). This indicates that while pseudobulges are built by secular processes over cosmic time, their actual star formation histories may be punctuated by intense, stochastic starburst events. Furthermore, chemical analyses of ancient stars provide constraints on the earliest stages of bulge assembly. For example, detections of lithium in metal-poor bulge dwarfs, such as MOA-2010-BLG-285S, suggest that the Spite plateau is universal and that there were no significant signs of lithium enrichment during the earliest phases of bulge formation [Bensby et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...521L..57B/abstract).

## Scaling Relations and Black Hole Co-evolution

Pseudobulges exhibit distinct deviations from the standard galactic scaling laws that tightly correlate the structural and kinematic properties of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] and classical [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]]. While classical spheroids occupy a well-defined locus in parameter space, pseudobulges consistently emerge as outliers.

Structurally, pseudobulges deviate from the Fundamental Plane of early-type galaxies [Ríos-López et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202546R/abstract). When compared to classical bulges and ellipticals, pseudobulges are typically larger and less dense at a given luminosity [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). In terms of central density, the central dynamical mass density in [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] scales almost linearly with the central baryonic density, suggesting a remarkably high central baryonic fraction ($f_{\rm bar} \approx 1$). This observation challenges standard $\Lambda$CDM predictions, which generally expect larger [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]] dark matter contributions in such systems [Lelli et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Galax...2..292L/abstract). The most striking divergence, however, occurs in the co-evolutionary scaling relations between the host galaxy and its central supermassive black hole (SMBH). 

In classical bulges, the black hole mass ($M_{\rm BH}$) tightly correlates with both the host's velocity dispersion (the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ relation) and the overall bulge mass and luminosity ($M_{\rm BH}-M_{\rm bulge}$ and $M_{\rm BH}-L_{\rm bulge}$). In contrast, pseudobulges typically harbor undermassive black holes that fall well below the $M_{\rm BH}-M_{\rm bulge}$ and $M_{\rm BH}-L_{\rm bulge}$ relations, although the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ relation appears to continue to low masses [Greene et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...688..159G/abstract), [Mathur et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...754..146M/abstract). Quantitatively, the ratio of black hole mass to bulge mass in pseudobulges is nearly an order of magnitude lower than that observed in classical bulges [Greene et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...688..159G/abstract). Furthermore, this secular evolution is in sharp contrast with some massive classical bulges, which can possess central nuclear 'mass deficits' created by the scouring action of binary black hole mergers [Milosavljević et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.331L..51M/abstract). Narrow-line Seyfert 1 (NLS1) galaxies, which frequently host pseudobulges, consistently fall below the $M_{\rm BH}-L_{\rm bulge}$ relation, indicating that their SMBH growth is primarily governed by secular processes rather than merger-driven accretion [Mathur et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...754..146M/abstract).

These deviations point to fundamentally different regimes of black hole feeding and feedback. In the standard paradigm for classical bulges, major mergers drive rapid, global gas inflows that trigger simultaneous starbursts and intense active galactic nucleus (AGN) activity, establishing a tight co-evolutionary feedback loop. Conversely, pseudobulge growth relies on local, stochastic black hole feeding driven by disk instabilities and bars. This secular feeding involves too little energy feedback to enforce the strict co-evolution seen in merger remnants [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract). The lack of correlation between black hole masses and large-scale disk properties further supports the conclusion that these structures do not co-evolve in the same manner as classical spheroids [Ríos-López et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202546R/abstract).

Debates remain over whether the deviations imply a strict dichotomy in formation mechanisms or if structural scaling relations, such as the Fundamental Plane, are inherently curved or continuous across different galaxy mass regimes. If these structural relations are continuous rather than strictly linear, the apparent outlier status of low-mass pseudobulges may be an artifact of fitting simple linear models to complex photometric variations [Graham et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011EAS....48..231G/abstract), [Graham et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ASPC..480..185G/abstract). Furthermore, while secular processes dominate the evolution of pseudobulges, hierarchical mechanisms still play a role in their host disks. For instance, minor galaxy mergers at $z < 1$ contribute significantly to the scatter in the stellar-mass Tully-Fisher relation, indicating a persistent hierarchical component to disk growth even within secularly dominated systems [Atkinson et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0712.1316A/abstract). On the other hand, the remarkably small scatter observed in the overall Tully-Fisher relation provides stringent constraints on supernova feedback and initial gas mass fractions during fundamental disk formation [van den Bosch et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..197..179V/abstract).

## Vertical Dynamics: The Boxy/Peanut Connection

When viewed edge-on, many [[galactic-bars|Galactic Bars]] display a characteristic boxy or peanut-shaped (B/P) morphology. A strong theoretical and observational consensus indicates that these B/P structures are not independent, merger-built spheroids, but result from [[peanut-formation|Peanut formation]] in the vertically thickened inner regions of the bars themselves [Gadotti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1208.2295G/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015HiA....16..316K/abstract). The Milky Way is a primary local example of this phenomenon, serving as a benchmark $L^\ast$ barred spiral that hosts a central boxy/peanut bulge within its dominant [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] structure [Bland-Hawthorn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..529B/abstract). Gas kinematics and near-infrared photometry confirm the Milky Way's boxy bulge to be a bar viewed nearly end-on.

The formation of B/P structures is dynamically driven by a vertical buckling instability. N-body simulations demonstrate that approximately $1\text{ Gyr}$ after a bar forms, the structure often undergoes a violent buckling out of the disk plane [Athanassoula et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0802.0151A/abstract). During this phase, rapid vertical heating alters the stellar kinematics, causing an increase in the ratio of the vertical to radial velocity dispersion ($\sigma_z^2/\sigma_r^2$) and lofting the inner stellar material into a thicker, boxy configuration. In systems with particularly strong bars or a low fraction of counter-rotating stars, recurrent buckling instabilities can occur. The efficient transfer of disk angular momentum to the dark matter halo enables the bar to regrow and buckle multiple times, eventually driving a morphological transition into pronounced X-shaped bulges [Kwak et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839...24K/abstract).

It is critical to distinguish B/P structures—which originate from the collisionless vertical heating of existing bar stars—from compact, nuclear "discy" pseudobulges. Discy pseudobulges form via the dissipative, bar-driven inflow of gas to the center of the galaxy followed by subsequent star formation, making them rotation-dominated and typically a factor of 5 smaller in radial extent than their larger B/P counterparts [Athanassoula et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0802.0151A/abstract). 

Despite their different physical origins, these two distinct manifestations of secular evolution frequently coexist. A prototypical example of this nested structure is found in the edge-on spiral NGC 5746. While the large-scale central morphology is dominated by a massive boxy pseudobulge (the buckled bar), precise photometric decompositions reveal an embedded, high-surface-brightness disky pseudobulge. This inner component exhibits a nearly exponential surface brightness profile characteristic of secular disk growth, with Sérsic indices of $n = 0.99 \pm 0.08$ and $n = 1.17 \pm 0.24$ along its minor and major axes, respectively [Barentine et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...754..140B/abstract). 

Because massive B/P structures can assemble entirely through internal disk dynamics, galaxies like NGC 5746 represent giant systems completely lacking a classical, merger-built bulge. Reproducing the survival and abundance of such pure disk galaxies remains a major challenge for hierarchical clustering models within a $\Lambda$CDM universe [Barentine et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...754..140B/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract).

## Environmental Secular Evolution and Galaxy Transformation

While internal [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] is primarily driven by non-axisymmetries, galaxies residing in rich cluster environments are subject to a parallel track of external, environmentally driven secular evolution. The morphological transformation of gas-rich, star-forming late-type disks into gas-poor, passive S0 and spheroidal galaxies is driven by a combination of ram-pressure stripping, galaxy harassment, and starvation [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015HiA....16..316K/abstract). 

Ram-pressure stripping effectively removes the cold interstellar medium when the hydrodynamic pressure of the intracluster medium (ICM) exceeds the gravitational restoring force of the galaxy disk. For a typical Milky Way-mass disk moving through a cluster at $v \sim 1000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, stripping becomes highly efficient at ICM densities of $\rho_{\text{ICM}} \gtrsim 4.6 \times 10^{-27} \text{ g cm}^{-3}$. Simultaneously, galaxy harassment—the cumulative effect of repeated, high-speed tidal encounters with other cluster members—impulsively heats the dynamically cold stellar disk, stripping mass and driving morphological [[disc-transformations|Disc transformations]].

These external processes fundamentally alter the structural scaling relations of the affected galaxies. Spheroidal galaxies, often found as cluster satellites, are not simply low-luminosity extensions of the fundamental plane of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. Instead, their structural parameters form a continuous sequence with the disks of S0 and late-type spiral galaxies. Similarly, star-forming dwarf galaxies occupy a fundamental plane that correlates their potential to line width and surface brightness, further supporting this structural sequence with disk galaxies [McCall et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...540A..49M/abstract). This indicates that they are "defunct" late-type systems or bulgeless S0s that have been transformed by environmental processes and internal supernova-driven feedback, retaining a structural memory of their disk origins [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). The local gas reservoirs necessary for future secular growth can also be heavily disrupted by these interactions; for example, observations of extragalactic H I clouds, such as those within the M81 filament, show that they are more likely generated through tidal stripping during galaxy interactions rather than existing as primordial dark matter minihalos [Chynoweth et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AJ....141....9C/abstract). Additionally, observations of certain blue compact dwarfs, such as NGC 2915, reveal that they may host dark or faded massive disks, providing evidence for previous epochs of star formation and structural evolution in low-surface-brightness systems [Quillen et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..1217Q/abstract).

The exact pathway from spiral to early-type cluster galaxy remains an active area of debate. If environmental gas removal simply quenches star formation, the disk will fade photometrically over several Gyr, naturally increasing the apparent central concentration (producing [[faded-spirals|Faded spirals]]). However, some studies argue that simple disk fading cannot fully account for the structural differences between early- and late-type galaxies in clusters. Because bulges in cluster environments are systematically brighter, physical bulge enhancement—driven by tidal interactions and minor mergers—is likely required alongside disk fading [Christlein et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...616..192C/abstract). 

Strong environmental interactions also leave distinct imprints on the surviving star cluster populations, providing a fossil record of these violent transformations. The specific frequencies of old, massive star clusters in the pseudobulges of late-type spirals are higher than those found in certain dwarf galaxies, supporting an evolutionary scenario where some members of [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] are the stripped, harassed remnants of spiral galaxies that originally hosted a pseudobulge [Di Nino et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AJ....138.1296D/abstract).

## Challenges to ΛCDM and Future Directions

The standard $\Lambda$CDM framework posits that galaxies assemble via hierarchical clustering and merging. In this paradigm, major mergers and repeated minor interactions inevitably randomize stellar orbits, predicting that almost every massive galaxy should possess a classical, merger-built bulge akin to [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005RMxAC..23..101K/abstract). However, observations present a stark contradiction to this theoretical expectation: a substantial fraction of local and intermediate-redshift [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] are "pure disk systems" completely devoid of classical bulges [Sachdeva et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...820L...4S/abstract).

The prevalence of bulgeless giant galaxies represents one of the most significant tensions in modern galaxy formation theory [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book....1K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). Statistical studies reveal that up to $15\%-18\%$ of disk galaxies have maintained single exponential surface brightness profiles from $z \sim 1$ to the present. Cosmological observations highlight a distinct evolutionary dichotomy: while massive "classical" bulges were largely in place by a redshift of $z \approx 1.5-2$, lower-mass pseudobulges continued to form and grow through [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] at much lower redshifts [Conselice et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..245..429C/abstract). Correspondingly, there is a remarkable lack of evolution in the disk stellar mass function since $z = 1$, which contrasts sharply with the rapid buildup of mass in spheroid-dominated galaxies over the same period [Bell et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..4407B/abstract). Observations indicate that disk galaxy morphologies begin to significantly deviate from local templates at redshifts as low as $z = 0.3$, with spiral structure becoming increasingly chaotic by $z = 0.5$ [Abraham et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..275...89A/abstract). This indicates that these pure disk systems have survived the last 8 billion years without experiencing major mergers or destructive minor mergers [Sachdeva et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...820L...4S/abstract). 

While the established view of pseudobulge formation relies heavily on late-time internal evolution driven by bar instabilities, cosmological simulations and high-redshift observations suggest that alternative pathways exist. Simulations by Okamoto (2013) indicate that rapid gas accretion and high-redshift starbursts can dominate the formation of disky central components, sometimes accounting for up to $\sim 70\%$ of the pseudobulge mass, with secular bar processes contributing only a minor fraction [Okamoto et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..718O/abstract). Furthermore, intense star formation and well-defined spiral morphology have been observed in galaxies as early as $z = 4.41$, demonstrating that compact central structures and organized rotating disks assembled long before the peak of cosmic star formation [Tsukui et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...372.1201T/abstract). 

It is important to note that the migration and coalescing of massive gas clumps in such violently unstable, gas-rich disks at high redshift provides an alternative channel for the formation of classical [[clump-origin-bulges|Clump-origin bulges]], rather than secular pseudobulges [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..431K/abstract). Additionally, there is a potential evolutionary link suggesting that compact, elliptical-like galaxies observed at high redshift ($z \sim 2$) may be the true progenitors of modern-day classical bulges, which later embedded themselves in massive extended disks [Graham et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013pss6.book...91G/abstract).

Resolving the "pure disk" problem remains an active frontier. Proposed solutions suggest that bulgeless galaxies might grow predominantly via smooth cold gas accretion along cosmic filaments, or that they are cushioned within uniquely massive dark matter halos that suppress extreme structural transformations and major merging [Sachdeva et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...820L...4S/abstract), [Corbelli et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396...19C/abstract). Moving forward, distinguishing truly secularly evolved pseudobulges from classical structures or early starburst remnants poses an observational challenge. Because simulated merger remnants can also exhibit significant rotation ($v/\sigma > 1$) and flattened isophotes, kinematics alone cannot definitively prove a history of isolated secular growth [Graham et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015HiA....16..360G/abstract). Future progress will require comprehensive, spatially resolved spectroscopic surveys to disentangle the kinematic and chemical memory of these central structures, clarifying how pristine [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] survive and evolve in a hierarchical universe.
