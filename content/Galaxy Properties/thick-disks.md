In galactic astronomy, a **thick disk** is a ubiquitous structural component of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] characterized by a geometrically extended vertical distribution of stars. First identified as an excess of surface brightness above and below the galactic mid-plane, thick disks typically exhibit vertical [[scaleheight-distribution|scale heights]] two to three times larger than those of their embedded [[thin-disks|Thin disks]]. Observations of [[nearby-edge-on-galaxies|Nearby edge-on galaxies]] have established that thick disks are a generic feature of [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] across the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] [Comerón et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741...28C/abstract). While they constitute a secondary mass component in massive, rapidly rotating spirals, their relative prominence scales inversely with host galaxy mass, frequently dominating the optical luminosity and stellar mass budget in [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] and dwarf systems [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..226Y/abstract).

Dynamically and chemically, thick disks are distinct from their thin counterparts, serving as crucial fossil records of early [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]]. The [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] of the thick disk are generally older, more metal-poor, and highly enriched in $\alpha$-elements compared to the mid-plane, indicating a rapid burst of [[galactic-star-formation-distribution|star formation]] prior to the delayed onset of Type Ia supernova enrichment. Kinematically, they are dynamically "hotter" systems. Because they possess higher [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|velocity dispersions]] in all three spatial coordinates, they require less centrifugal support to maintain equilibrium against the galaxy's gravitational potential. This results in a pronounced rotational lag—known as [[asymmetric-galaxy-morphologies-and-kinematics|asymmetric drift]]—relative to the thin disk. In rare, extreme cases, thick disks have even been observed to be counter-rotating, which places stringent constraints on their assembly histories [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...624..701Y/abstract).

The origins of thick disks remain a central topic in [[galactic-disk-structure-and-dynamics|Galactic Disk Structure and Dynamics]], with their assembly likely driven by a hybrid of physical processes. Historically, models emphasized "bottom-up" scenarios, such as the gradual radial migration of stars driven by the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]], or the vertical heating of a pre-existing thin disk via minor mergers with [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]]. However, recent high-redshift observations heavily support an "upside-down" or "born thick" formation pathway, wherein early gas-rich disks were highly turbulent and dynamically hot before subsequent cooling allowed a thin disk to settle within them [Lian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960L..10L/abstract). The direct accretion of extragalactic stellar debris also contributes significantly to the mass budget, meaning most thick disks contain a mixture of *in-situ* and *ex-situ* populations.

This article details the historical discovery and structural parameterization of thick disks before exploring their distinct chemical and kinematic signatures. It reviews the dominant theories of their formation—comparing heating, accretion, secular evolution, and *in-situ* mechanisms—and surveys their morphological prominence from local mass-scaling relations to the adolescent universe. Finally, the article outlines current open questions in the field, including the ongoing debate over "born thick" versus heated formation scenarios, the universality of $\alpha$-distinct thick disk sub-populations, and the precise physical drivers behind their varying mass fractions across the galaxy population.

## Historical Development and Discovery

Early structural models of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] typically assumed a single vertical stellar component, often described by an isothermal sheet model with a vertical [[stellar-density-profile|density profile]] proportional to $\mathrm{sech}^2(z/z_0)$, where $z$ is the distance from the galactic mid-plane. The first evidence challenging this single-disk paradigm emerged from deep photometry of five edge-on [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|S0 galaxies]] by Burstein (1979). During this analysis, Burstein observed a faint envelope of excess surface brightness at large vertical distances from the mid-plane. This vertical density excess could not be adequately fitted by the standard single $\mathrm{sech}^2$ law.

At the time of this discovery, it remained highly ambiguous whether this excess light represented a genuinely distinct physical structure. Skeptics argued that the excess might merely indicate that the $\mathrm{sech}^2$ formulation was an inappropriate empirical fit for the outer scaleheights of galactic disks, rather than proof of a secondary galactic component. 

This debate was conclusively settled by the seminal work of Gilmore & Reid (1983). They utilized photometric and spectroscopic observations of individual thick disk stars in the Milky Way. Their analysis demonstrated that the vertical distribution of stars could not be explained by a single population and explicitly required a two-component exponential fit, thereby confirming the existence of a geometrically distinct thick disk within our own Galaxy.

Subsequent spectroscopic and photometric studies of individual stars quickly established that the Milky Way's thick disk was not merely a geometric extension, but a distinct entity in terms of its [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]]. Stars belonging to the thick disk were found to be predominantly old ($\gtrsim 10-12$ Gyr), relatively metal-poor (spanning $-2.2 \lesssim [\mathrm{Fe}/\mathrm{H}] \lesssim -0.3$, with a mean around $-0.6$), and exhibiting elevated [$\alpha$/Fe] ratios compared to stars in the [[thin-disks|Thin disks]]. 

Following these foundational discoveries, the identification of thick disks expanded rapidly to external galaxies. Modern imaging surveys, such as the Spitzer Survey of Stellar Structure in Galaxies (S$^4$G), have demonstrated that thick disks are a generic by-product of galaxy formation, present in virtually all edge-on disk galaxies regardless of [[hubble-sequence|Hubble type]] [Comerón et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741...28C/abstract). Quantitatively, the vertical scale heights of these thick components are typically a factor of $\sim 3$ larger than those of the embedded thin disks. The structural decomposition of these systems often requires fitting two coupled stellar fluids in hydrostatic equilibrium to accurately capture the transition from the thin to the thick component [Comerón et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741...28C/abstract), [Comerón et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...610A...5C/abstract). 

The historical progression—from a subtle photometric anomaly in a handful of S0 galaxies to a ubiquitous feature of [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]]—has cemented the thick disk as a crucial fossil record for understanding [[galactic-disk-structure-and-dynamics|Galactic Disk Structure and Dynamics]].

## Structural and Geometric Properties

Thick disks are a nearly ubiquitous structural component of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], most readily identified and characterized through the surface photometry of [[nearby-edge-on-galaxies|Nearby edge-on galaxies]]. By definition, thick disks exhibit a geometrically extended vertical structure that represents an excess of surface brightness at large distances from the mid-plane. This extended emission cannot be adequately modeled by the single isothermal sheet (e.g., a $\mathrm{sech}^2$ distribution) or the exponential profile used to describe [[thin-disks|Thin disks]], necessitating a multi-component decomposition. Furthermore, when decomposing galaxy structures, care must be taken to distinguish macroscopic thick disks, which span kiloparsecs, from [[compact-circumnuclear-disks|Nuclear Stellar Disks]] (NSDs). Although NSDs are a common feature in approximately 20% of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|early-type galaxies]] and serve as tracers for the last major merger event, they are distinct, highly compact central structures only a few tens to hundreds of parsecs across [Ledo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1005.2524L/abstract). Similarly, [[galaxy-nuclei-and-complex-morphologies|Nuclear Star Clusters]] (NSCs) are found in nearly 80% of massive disk galaxies and frequently show an alignment with the host disk's broader position angle [Ashok et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958..100A/abstract).

The defining geometric metric of this component is its vertical [[scaleheight-distribution|scale height]] ($h_z$). Observations consistently show that the vertical scale height of a thick disk is typically a factor of two to three times larger than that of the embedded thin disk [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..226Y/abstract). Despite their pronounced vertical extent, these stellar populations remain highly flattened. Measurements of the intrinsic flattening ($c/a$) of galaxy disks—where $c$ and $a$ represent the vertical and long radial axes, respectively—demonstrate that total disk populations exhibit a mean intrinsic [[galaxy-shapes|flattening]] of $\langle c/a \rangle \approx 0.124$ [Favaro et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...978...63F/abstract). While geometrically thicker than the mid-plane populations, thick disks maintain moderately flattened axial ratios that confirm their rotationally supported, disk-like nature rather than a purely spheroidal geometry [Dalcanton et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..9700D/abstract). Detailed cosmological simulations, such as those from the NewHorizon project, demonstrate that mature thick disks typically contribute approximately 35% of the total [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass]], with their complex vertical [[stellar-density-profile|density profiles]] frequently requiring multiple coupled $\mathrm{sech}^2$ components rather than a single analytical fit [Yi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271....1Y/abstract).

In the radial direction, the spatial distribution of thick disks introduces a notable structural nuance. The radial scale length ($h_R$) of thick disks is generally more extended than that of their thin counterparts. On average, the relationship between the two scale lengths can be expressed as:
$$ \frac{h_{R,\mathrm{thick}}}{h_{R,\mathrm{thin}}} \approx 1.25 $$
with observed ratios typically spanning $1.0$ to $1.5$ [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..226Y/abstract). The extended nature of thick disks is also closely tied to the broader classification of outer disk surface-brightness profiles into Type I (exponential), [[type-ii-disk-break-radius|Type II]] (truncations), and [[type-iii-galaxy-profiles|Type III]] (anti-truncations), where Type III outer profiles are often associated with the superposition of an extended thick disk over a truncated thin disk [Erwin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..207E/abstract). However, an extended scale length does not necessarily imply that the physical edge of the thick disk extends further into the galactic outskirts. A study of edge-on systems in the Spitzer Survey of Stellar Structure in Galaxies (S$^4$G) found that when thick disks exhibit radial truncations, their [[type-ii-disk-break-radius|break radii]] are remarkably comparable to the truncation radii of the thin disks [Comerón et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...98C/abstract). Furthermore, outer truncations in both thin and thick disks can be linked to physical star formation gas density thresholds, as observed in the diffuse [[low-mass-galaxies|low-mass galaxy]] UGC 7321 [Díaz-García et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...667A.109D/abstract). Beyond the stellar disk, some isolated systems host unusually extended gas disks with HI-to-optical diameter ratios exceeding five, pointing to vast, complex baryonic reservoirs that surround the structured stellar components [Dowell et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhDT.......102D/abstract). In terms of central stellar density, analyses of massive SDSS samples show that the [[freeman-stellar-disk|Freeman Law]] effectively defines an upper limit for disk central surface brightness in bright disks [Fathi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1101.1339F/abstract).

The mass contribution of the thick disk to the total stellar mass budget scales strongly with the host galaxy's mass and rotation curve. Across the broader galaxy population, the total stellar mass residing in disks and spheroids is approximately equal, with a measured mass ratio of $1.3 \pm 0.2$ [Benson et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...574..104B/abstract). Within individual disk galaxies, in massive spiral systems characterized by circular velocities $V_c > 120 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, the thick disk is a secondary component, contributing $\sim 20\%$ to $70\%$ of the thin disk mass [Comerón et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...98C/abstract). Conversely, in lower-mass dwarf disk systems ($V_c < 120 \text{ km s}^{-1}$), the thick disk becomes increasingly dominant, frequently contributing nearly half of the total luminosity and dominating the overall stellar mass [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..226Y/abstract). Interestingly, this velocity threshold of $V_c = 120 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ also marks a mass-dependent transition in [[dust-lane-galaxies|dust lane structure]] across disk galaxies, driven by disk instabilities and changes in turbulent gas support [Dalcanton et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...608..189D/abstract). Furthermore, dynamical studies such as the [[diskmass|DiskMass]] Survey indicate that stellar disks typically contribute only 15% to 30% of the dynamical mass within 2.2 scale lengths, implying that galactic disks are generally [[submaximal-discs|submaximal]] and embedded in dominant dark matter halos [Bershady et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...739L..47B/abstract).

Historically, the structural reality of thick disks was challenged by the hypothesis that they might be observational artifacts, specifically diffuse scattered light from the thin disk mimicking an extended vertical structure. Advanced point spread function (PSF) modeling of ultra-deep imaging has definitively ruled this out. While scattered light does dominate the extreme low-surface-brightness tails (at $\mu \gtrsim 26 \text{ mag arcsec}^{-2}$), the underlying thick disks remain robust, physically distinct structural components [Comerón et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...610A...5C/abstract). Additionally, the "opaque disk" hypothesis suggests that substantial dust obscuration in the major parts of many spiral disks may complicate photometric profiles and invalidate some traditional mass-to-light ratio determinations [Valentijn et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990Natur.346..153V/abstract). Relatedly, extraplanar dust clouds and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) have been observed populating thick disks up to heights of $z \sim 2$ kpc, acting as critical tracers for galactic outflows that operate at the disk-halo interface [Howk et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012EAS....56..291H/abstract).

## Kinematics and Stellar Dynamics

Thick disks are distinguished from [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] not only by their vertical spatial extent but also by their distinct kinematic profiles. They are dynamically "hotter" systems, characterized by a higher [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|velocity dispersion]] ($\sigma$) in all three spatial coordinates and a pronounced rotational lag relative to the local standard of rest. The internal dynamics of these dynamically heated systems are particularly distinct in [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|early-type disk galaxies]]. For instance, in featureless S0 disks, the depletion of circular stellar orbits inherently inhibits the growth of local instabilities; this unique kinematic state is often reflected by a negative kurtosis in their radial velocity distributions, establishing a dynamically stable, hot configuration [Sellwood et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250102636S/abstract). Additionally, there is an established mass-velocity anisotropy relation in galactic stellar disks, where more luminous disks tend to have more radial stellar orbits, linking a galaxy's macroscopic mass to its detailed internal kinematics [Chemin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...618A.121C/abstract).

This rotational lag, physically manifested as [[asymmetric-galaxy-morphologies-and-kinematics|asymmetric drift]], arises because the increased random motions of thick disk stars provide partial pressure support against the galaxy's gravitational potential. As a result, the dynamically hotter thick disk requires less centrifugal support from bulk rotation and naturally exhibits slower net rotation. In typical [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], the thick disk rotates approximately 20% to 50% slower than the embedded thin disk [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...624..701Y/abstract). 

Within the Milky Way, kinematic data from the [[rave-survey|RAVE survey]] and other spectroscopic campaigns have firmly established the elevated velocity dispersions and rotational lag of the Galactic thick disk [Pasetto et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...547A..70P/abstract). The Milky Way's thick disk exhibits substantial internal kinematic complexity. Recent analyses have identified a distinct "Metal-Weak Thick Disk" (MWTD) population that exhibits an even more pronounced lag, rotating at $\bar{v}_\phi \approx 150 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ (a $\approx 30 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ lag relative to the canonical thick disk) with a velocity dispersion of $\approx 60 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Carollo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887...22C/abstract). Furthermore, thick disk kinematics are not purely axisymmetric; local velocity distributions show asymmetries ($v_R - v_\phi$) induced by resonances with the [[double-barred-galaxies|central bar]], indicating that thick disk orbits are actively perturbed by internal galactic structures [Antoja et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800L..32A/abstract). For example, the [[hercules-thick-disk-cloud|Hercules Thick Disk Cloud]] has been identified as a major substructure within the Galaxy's extended disk, stretching 1 to 6 kpc from the Sun and 0.5 to 4 kpc above the galactic plane, and is potentially associated directly with the disk bar [Larsen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AJ....141..130L/abstract).

Spectroscopic observations of external [[nearby-edge-on-galaxies|Nearby edge-on galaxies]] confirm that these kinematic signatures are ubiquitous, though they exhibit significant galaxy-to-galaxy variation. For instance, the thick disk in FGC 1415 rotates at only 30% to 40% of the thin disk speed, equating to a massive rotational lag of 60% to 70% relative to the thin disk midplane [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...624..701Y/abstract). However, the most stringent constraints on [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]] come from extreme kinematic outliers. In rare cases, such as FGC 227, the thick disk is observed to rotate extremely slowly ($<25\%$ of the midplane speed) or is entirely composed of counter-rotating stars with respect to the primary thin disk [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...624..701Y/abstract).

The existence of counter-rotating thick disks provides critical leverage in the ongoing debate over thick disk formation mechanisms. While radial migration and internal secular heating can pump the velocity dispersion of an existing stellar population over time, these internal processes inherently conserve the net angular momentum direction of the disk. Therefore, the detection of a counter-rotating thick disk conclusively rules out pure secular heating or monolithic collapse models for those specific systems [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...624..701Y/abstract). Instead, these extreme kinematic decoupling events strongly support scenarios involving the direct accretion of stars from disrupted [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] on retrograde orbits. Surveys of external galaxies have frequently identified significant numbers of such dynamically distinct systems; for example, the discovery of 120 counter-rotating disk galaxies in the MaNGA survey has allowed these systems to be classified as "inner" or "outer" based on the spatial concentration of the counter-rotating population [Gasymov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250402925G/abstract). In the broader population of disk galaxies, it is highly likely that thick disk kinematics reflect a hybrid assembly history, combining dynamically heated *in-situ* stars with an *ex-situ* accreted component that contributes significantly to the mass budget [Comerón et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741...28C/abstract), [Pinna et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.236P/abstract).

## Stellar Populations and Chemical Abundances

The stellar components of thick disks serve as a fossil record of the physical processes that occurred during the early stages of [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]]. Observations of the Milky Way and [[nearby-edge-on-galaxies|Nearby edge-on galaxies]] reveal that thick disks host [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] that are characteristically distinct from those of embedded [[thin-disks|Thin disks]]. Specifically, thick disk stars are predominantly older, more metal-poor, and exhibit higher levels of alpha-enrichment compared to their thin disk counterparts [Martig et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..139M/abstract), [Pinna et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.236P/abstract).

In the Milky Way, the canonical thick disk has a broad [[chemical-abundances|metallicity]] distribution typically spanning $-2.2 \lesssim [\mathrm{Fe/H}] \lesssim -0.3$, with a mean of approximately $-0.6$. A defining signature within the [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] of these stars is their elevated $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ ratios at a given [Fe/H] [Feltzing et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph.11118F/abstract). This alpha-enrichment indicates that the gas from which these stars formed was primarily enriched by core-collapse supernovae, implying a rapid, early star formation history before the delayed onset of Type Ia supernovae could contribute significant iron. Recent spatially resolved spectroscopy of the edge-on spiral UGC 10738 has confirmed the presence of distinct $\alpha$-rich and $\alpha$-poor disk components, demonstrating that this chemical dichotomy is not unique to the Milky Way and may be a generic feature of disk galaxies [Scott et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913L..11S/abstract).

While thick disks were historically viewed as chemically and temporally uniform, modern large-scale spectroscopic surveys have uncovered significant spatial variations. In the Milky Way, there are clear metallicity gradients. For example, dwarf stars exhibit a strong vertical metallicity gradient between heights of $1 < |Z| < 3$ kpc, measured as:
$$ \frac{\Delta [\mathrm{Fe/H}]}{\Delta |Z|} = -0.113 \pm 0.010 \text{ dex kpc}^{-1} $$
alongside a weak positive radial gradient of $+0.02$ to $+0.03 \text{ dex kpc}^{-1}$ [Carrell et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....144..185C/abstract). These observed metallicity gradients are commonly understood through "inside-out" growth models, where the radial dependence of star formation efficiency over time drives the evolution of the chemical profile across the disk [Pilkington et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...540A..56P/abstract). Environmental factors also play a key role; findings from the [[fornax-cluster-and-galaxy-studies|Fornax 3D project]] demonstrate that thick disks in cluster environments show evidence of later accretion components and dynamical heating from thin disks forming flares [Pinna et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...625A..95P/abstract).

Similarly, the stellar [[age-distribution|Age distribution]] is not strictly uniform. Analysis of red clump stars reveals a strong radial age gradient within the Milky Way's geometrically defined thick disk. The median age decreases from $\sim 9$ Gyr in the inner regions to $\sim 5$ Gyr in the outer disk [Martig et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..139M/abstract). This suggests that the outer thick disk may contain a younger flaring population, complicating models that rely on a purely monolithic collapse or a single heating event. Independent age estimates from white dwarf luminosity functions provide further constraints, suggesting thick disk ages of approximately 8.7 to 9.9 Gyr [Kilic et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837..162K/abstract). 

Furthermore, recent discoveries challenge the strict temporal separation of these components. Observations have uncovered evidence of an old thin disk population ($>11$ Gyr) that indicates a period of co-formation with the thick disk prior to significant merger events like [[accreted-dwarf-galaxies|Gaia-Sausage Enceladus]] [Borbolato et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...994..126B/abstract).

The thick disk may itself be a composite structure containing overlapping sub-populations. Recent kinematic and chemical analyses have identified a "Metal-Weak Thick Disk" (MWTD) overlapping with the canonical thick disk in the solar neighborhood. The MWTD has roughly half the metal content of the standard thick disk and exhibits a rotational lag of $\sim 30 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ relative to it, hinting at a distinct origin such as an early dwarf galaxy merger or the outward migration of a precursor inner disk [Carollo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887...22C/abstract). 

Cosmological simulations further support this composite nature. In the AURIGA simulations, thick disks at $z=0$ are on average $\sim 3$ Gyr older and $\sim 0.25$ dex more metal-poor than thin disks, with their overall age distribution and chemical properties driven by a combination of early *in-situ* star formation and the direct accretion of stars from satellite mergers [Pinna et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.236P/abstract).

## Formation Mechanisms

The origin of thick disks in [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] is a subject of active debate. Modern theories generally identify four primary pathways for their assembly: *in-situ* formation within early turbulent gas disks, [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|secular evolution]] driven by radial migration, dynamical heating of a pre-existing thin disk, and direct accretion of stars from [[satellite-galaxies|satellite mergers]]. The current consensus holds that mature thick disks are complex structures reflecting a hybrid of these mechanisms, rather than the product of a single monolithic process [Martig et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..139M/abstract), [Pinna et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.236P/abstract).

**In-Situ Formation and the 'Born Thick' Scenario**
Recent [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|high-redshift]] observations and cosmological simulations strongly support the "born thick" scenario, in which thick disks form *in-situ* during an early, gas-rich, and highly turbulent phase of [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]]. Deep imaging has identified highly regular edge-on stellar disks as early as $z = 5.289$ [Yan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975...44Y/abstract). At $z > 1.5$, early [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|star-forming disks]] exhibit significantly higher velocity dispersions than local thin disks, a turbulence likely driven by their own exceptionally high global star formation rates rather than cosmic accretion alone [Green et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.467..684G/abstract). This evidence points toward an "upside-down" formation model: galaxies initially form a dynamically hot, geometrically thick disk, while cooler, thinner components settle later as the gas fraction drops [Lian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960L..10L/abstract). Within this framework, some theories propose that [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|S0 galaxies]] may actually form as a primordial type directly from these turbulent, [[clumpy-disks|clumpy disks]] at $z > 2$, potentially evolving into spiral galaxies only later via subsequent cold gas accretion [Sil'chenko et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MSAIS..25...93S/abstract). However, tension remains in the literature, as some structural studies across $0.4 \leq z \leq 2.5$ argue that while early disks were born as thick as the Milky Way's current thick disk, they must continue to physically thicken over time to match the even larger [[scaleheight-distribution|scale heights]] of the local massive disk population [Hamilton-Campos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956..147H/abstract).

**Secular Evolution and Radial Migration**
Over gigayear timescales, the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] further reshapes vertical structure. Through secular disk heating, processes such as spiral scattering and interactions with giant molecular clouds efficiently redistribute random kinetic energy into vertical motion, steadily puffing up the disk over time [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). Radial migration—where stars scatter to larger galactocentric radii through resonant interactions with spiral arms and bars—can naturally reproduce the double-exponential vertical profile observed in the Milky Way without requiring violent dynamical heating [Loebman et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...737....8L/abstract). As stars migrate outward into regions with a lower gravitational restoring force, their vertical oscillations reach larger absolute heights, creating a thick-disk-like structure. Another internally driven model is the "levitation" theory, where stellar orbits are captured into resonance and drift to large vertical energies as the disk grows adiabatically [Sridhar et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996Sci...271..973S/abstract). While radial migration efficiently mixes older stellar populations, purely secular vertical heating via spiral arms is generally insufficient to produce the full thick disk in Milky Way-mass galaxies, although it is a significant factor in lower-mass, late-type disks [Martinez-Medina et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...802..109M/abstract).

**Vertical Heating via Minor Mergers**
The vertical heating scenario posits that a primordial thin disk is dynamically excited by gravitational encounters. As a galaxy undergoes hierarchical assembly, interactions with massive dark matter subhalos or infalling [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] perturb the stellar orbits of the primary disk [Navarro et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..655N/abstract). This process pumps kinetic energy into the vertical and radial motions of the stars, increasing their velocity dispersion ($\sigma_z$) and consequently expanding the disk's scale height. Simulations have demonstrated that such minor mergers can successfully replicate the structural and kinematic properties of observed thick disks. Interestingly, morpho-kinematic data suggests that the violent assembly history of disks is more complex than a monotonic growth pattern; as many as half of local thin disks may have been completely rebuilt after gas-rich major mergers within the last nine billion years, further complicating the origins of their associated thick disk components [Puech et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753..128P/abstract). In the case of the Milky Way, it has even been hypothesized that a close flyby of the Andromeda galaxy 7-11 Gyr ago could be responsible for pumping the observed velocity dispersions in the Galactic thick disk [Banik et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1406.4538B/abstract).

**Satellite Accretion and Tidal Debris**
Finally, gas-rich mergers and the direct accretion of shredded [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] play an essential role in the thick disk mass budget. As satellites are disrupted by tidal forces, their stars are deposited directly into the host galaxy's potential well, forming a diffuse, kinematically hot thick disk. Cosmological zoom-in simulations of Milky Way-mass halos indicate that accreted *ex-situ* stars contribute an average of $\sim 22\%$ to the thick disk mass, reaching up to $\sim 50\%$ in some systems [Pinna et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.236P/abstract). This mechanism may be even more dominant in [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] ($V_c < 120$ km s$^{-1}$), where the thick disk can dominate the total stellar mass. This implies that these low-mass systems were assembled from pregalactic fragments that suffered heavy baryonic loss due to supernova-driven winds prior to merging [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..226Y/abstract).

**Observational Constraints**
The necessity of the accretion and merger channel is conclusively demonstrated by the unique [[galaxy-kinematics|Galaxy kinematics]] of extreme kinematic outliers. For instance, the edge-on galaxy FGC 227 possesses a thick disk that rotates at less than $25\%$ of the midplane velocity, and may even be counter-rotating [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...624..701Y/abstract). Such distinct kinematic signatures firmly rule out pure secular heating or monolithic collapse for these systems, proving that the accretion of satellite debris on retrograde orbits is a mandatory component in the complete picture of thick disk assembly.

## Thick Disks Across the Hubble Sequence

Thick disks are a nearly ubiquitous structural component of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], found across essentially all morphological types along the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]]. While historically studied in detail within the Milky Way and other [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], systematic surveys of edge-on galaxies have demonstrated that the presence of a thick disk is a generic byproduct of galaxy assembly. However, the relative prominence of the thick disk—both in terms of scale height and stellar mass—varies systematically with the properties of the host galaxy, particularly its total mass and rotational velocity. For example, the ratio of thick-to-thin disk scale heights tends to be larger for early-type spirals compared to late-type systems [Comerón et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741...28C/abstract). The prominence and survival of these distinct disk structures are heavily influenced by the cosmic environment. This is consistent with the $\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}$ prediction that disk galaxies evolve mostly in isolation, whereas massive ellipticals congregate in clusters, defining large-scale structures like the local supergalactic plane [Sawala et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8..247S/abstract). During galactic evolution, thick disks may also play a crucial role as galaxies transition in morphology and star formation. Observations from the COSMOS survey reveal that up to 60% of galaxies transitioning to the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] may pass through a "passive disk" phase, characterized by large bulges, suppressed [[galactic-star-formation-distribution|star formation]], and preserved disk structures before eventually evolving into classic ellipticals [Bundy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...719.1969B/abstract).

The most striking trend across the galaxy population is the inverse relationship between the mass of the host galaxy and the mass dominance of its thick disk. In massive, rapidly rotating galaxies with circular velocities $V_c > 120\ \mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}$, the [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] contain the majority of the stellar mass. In these high-mass systems, the ratio of thick-to-thin disk stellar mass typically ranges from $M_{\mathrm{thick}}/M_{\mathrm{thin}} \approx 0.2$ to $0.7$ [Comerón et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...98C/abstract). Conversely, in [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] and [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf systems]] with $V_c < 120\ \mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}$, the thick disk becomes increasingly prominent. For the lowest-mass disks ($V_c \lesssim 75\ \mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}$), the thick component can contribute nearly half of the total optical luminosity and strictly dominate the galaxy's stellar mass budget [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..226Y/abstract). Utilizing improved, physically motivated fitting functions applied to ultra-deep imaging surveys suggests that thick disks may be even more massive than historically estimated, often approaching mass parity with the thin disk even in intermediate-mass systems [Comerón et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741...28C/abstract). At the extreme end of the structural spectrum are [[giant-low-surface-brightness-galaxies|Low-Surface-Brightness (LSB) galaxies]]. These LSB disks are strongly dark matter dominated at all radii and evolve at a significantly slower rate than their high-surface-brightness counterparts; their apparent youth suggests that galaxy formation is a greatly extended process [Bothun et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PASP..109..745B/abstract).

The physical origin of this mass-velocity relation remains an active area of investigation, but it likely reflects fundamental differences in the efficiency of star formation and feedback mechanisms across the mass spectrum. In high-mass galaxies, deep potential wells allow for the prolonged retention of accreted gas, which cools and settling to build a dominant thin disk over gigayears. In contrast, the shallower potential wells of low-mass galaxies make them highly susceptible to supernova-driven winds. It is hypothesized that intense supernova feedback in low-mass pre-galactic fragments drives significant gas outflows, effectively truncating the assembly of a massive thin disk and leaving the older, dynamically hotter thick disk as the dominant stellar component [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..226Y/abstract). This interpretation aligns with the observation that low-mass galaxies experience slower dynamical evolution and possess younger, less massive thin disks relative to their massive counterparts [Comerón et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...98C/abstract). Additionally, a structural separation between dwarfs and disks has been observed in the mass-scale length plane, where dwarfs are generally more diffuse and are supported by more disordered motion rather than structured rotation [Schombert et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..296S/abstract).

## Thick Disks in the High-Redshift Universe

A central debate in galactic archaeology is whether thick disks are formed "bottom-up"—whereby a pre-existing [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] is dynamically heated by minor mergers or [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|secular processes]]—or "upside-down," meaning they were born dynamically hot and geometrically thick at high redshift. Observations of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|high-redshift]] ($z > 1.5$) galaxies using integral field spectroscopy and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have provided critical constraints on these scenarios, largely favoring the latter. Despite the prominence of dark matter in the local universe, observations from the KMOS$^\mathrm{3D}$ survey demonstrate that at $z > 2$, baryons (stars and gas) make up the majority of the mass within the disk regions, making these early thick structures strongly baryon-dominated within their effective radii [Wuyts et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..149W/abstract). However, the true kinematic nature of these early structures remains complex. Recent analyses of high-redshift apparent disks and their local low-mass counterparts suggest they may undergo a prolate-to-[[oblate-galaxies|oblate]] shape transition, indicating that high-redshift flattened systems might be driven by tangentially anisotropic random motion rather than ordered rotation [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...973L..29W/abstract).

Kinematic studies demonstrate that early [[star-forming-disk-galaxies|star-forming disks]] are highly turbulent and dynamically hotter than their local counterparts. They are characterized by high [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|vertical velocity dispersions]] ($\sigma$) relative to their rotational velocities ($V_\mathrm{rot}$) [Law et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.487..338L/abstract), [Simons et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874...59S/abstract). This elevated turbulence is generally attributed to high [[dense-gas-fraction|gas fractions]], rapid cosmological accretion, and intense stellar feedback [Green et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.467..684G/abstract). In these gas-rich, turbulent environments, early disks are prone to [[violent-disk-instability|violent disk instabilities]]. Unlike the continuous, uniform star formation typically seen in local spiral arms, star formation in high-redshift disks often occurs in massive, kiloparsec-scale clumps [Elmegreen et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...688...67E/abstract). This [[clumpy-star-formation|Clumpy star formation]] is a hallmark of the thick-disk formation epoch, where gravitational fragmentation in the self-gravitating gas rapidly converts accreted material into distinct, hot stellar populations before the gas can settle into a cooler, thinner configuration [Fisher et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...839L...5F/abstract).

Recent deep imaging surveys have directly measured the vertical [[scaleheight-distribution|scale heights]] of edge-on galaxies across cosmic time, lending strong empirical support to an upside-down formation model. In this paradigm, galaxies are "born thick," and only later—as the cosmic gas accretion rate declines and the interstellar medium cools—does an embedded thin disk settle and form. JWST NIRCam observations of edge-on galaxies up to $z = 5$ reveal that early disks possess scale heights comparable to the thick disk of the Milky Way and exhibit no significant dependence on redshift at $z > 1.5$ [Lian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960L..10L/abstract). Independent HST analyses spanning $0.4 \leq z \leq 2.5$ corroborate this, finding a median intrinsic scale height of $0.74 \pm 0.03$ kpc for high-redshift disks, meaning they are born as thick as the Milky Way's current thick disk [Hamilton-Campos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956..147H/abstract). The implication is that local thick disks were already geometrically thick at the time of their formation, rather than requiring billions of years of gradual dynamical heating to reach their current state [Lian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960L..10L/abstract). However, the literature is not entirely free of tension; some studies argue that while early disks were already geometrically thick, they must continue to physically thicken over time to match the even larger scale heights ($\sim 1.5$ kpc) of massive local descendant disks [Hamilton-Campos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956..147H/abstract).

The timeline for the assembly of these thick structures is surprisingly rapid, with mature disk features appearing much earlier than once theoretically expected. Highly regular, edge-on stellar disks have been spectroscopically confirmed as early as $z = 5.289$, demonstrating that organized [[galaxy-kinematics|Galaxy kinematics]] and stable disk morphologies can establish themselves within the first billion years of the Universe [Yan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975...44Y/abstract). Furthermore, the discovery of a giant spiral disk at $z = 3.25$ with a [[dust-continuum-half-light-radius|half-light radius]] $R_e \approx 9.6$ kpc and a [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass]] of $3.7 \times 10^{11} M_\odot$ challenges the long-held assumption that all early disks were strictly compact [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..710W/abstract). Together, these findings indicate that the thick components of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] assemble rapidly in the adolescent Universe, establishing the massive structural foundation upon which modern thin disks later grow.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The study of thick disks remains a highly active field, with current research focusing on reconciling the differences between simulations and observations, establishing the universality of alpha-distinct populations, and understanding the role of environment and galaxy mass in [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]]. 

**Formation Mechanisms and Simulation Discrepancies**
A primary unresolved debate is whether thick disks form "upside-down" (i.e., they are born thick at high redshift in gas-rich, turbulent environments) or "bottom-up" (i.e., they originate as [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] that physically thicken over time via dynamical heating). The literature currently presents conflicting evidence on this front. Recent [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|high-redshift]] observations indicate that the scale heights of young galaxies at $z > 1.5$ are already comparable to the Milky Way's present-day thick disk, strongly supporting the upside-down formation scenario [Lian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960L..10L/abstract). Conversely, other studies tracking the physical thickness of stellar disks up to $z \sim 2$ suggest that while early disks are born thick, they must continue to physically thicken after formation to explain the even more extended scale heights of the most massive local populations [Hamilton-Campos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956..147H/abstract). 

Resolving these tensions requires addressing significant simulation discrepancies. For instance, theoretical models relying heavily on radial stellar migration can successfully reproduce the double-exponential vertical structure of the Milky Way without invoking violent merger events [Loebman et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...737....8L/abstract). However, migration models alone cannot explain the existence of counter-rotating thick disks—which definitively require the direct accretion of [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...624..701Y/abstract). Determining the exact relative contributions of radial migration, *in-situ* high-redshift formation, and satellite accretion across different [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] remains a major open question.

**Universality of Alpha-Distinct Populations**
Within the Milky Way, the thick disk is defined by distinct chemical signatures, specifically an enhancement in $\alpha$-elements and lower metallicities compared to the thin disk. A major future direction is determining whether this chemical dichotomy is a universal feature of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]. Recent integral-field observations using MUSE spectroscopy have detected an $\alpha$-rich thick disk in the edge-on galaxy UGC 10738, proving that the Milky Way's distinct chemical structure is not a unique anomaly [Scott et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913L..11S/abstract). Internally, the Milky Way's thick disk itself may not be a single monolithic population; the recent identification of a Metal-Weak Thick Disk (MWTD) exhibiting a rotational lag of $\sim 30\text{ km s}^{-1}$ relative to the canonical thick disk suggests the presence of overlapping components [Carollo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887...22C/abstract). Future analyses combining high-precision Gaia data with extensive ground-based spectroscopy will be essential to untangle these complex overlapping [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] and their kinematics.

**The Role of Environment, Mass, and the Baryon Cycle**
Finally, the scaling relations of thick disks present lingering theoretical challenges. Observations of [[nearby-edge-on-galaxies|Nearby edge-on galaxies]] reveal that the prominence of the thick disk is strongly inversely correlated with galaxy mass. In [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] ($V_c > 120\text{ km s}^{-1}$), the thick disk is a minority component by mass, but in [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|low-mass dwarf galaxies]], the thick disk often dominates the total stellar mass budget [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..226Y/abstract), [Comerón et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...98C/abstract). Understanding why low-mass galaxies form or retain proportionally more massive thick disks requires a better understanding of the global baryon cycle, specifically how supernova-driven winds and galactic outflows operate in shallower potential wells. Because thick disks are more massive than previously assumed in some photometric fits, they are increasingly being investigated as a potential reservoir for the "missing" local baryons in the universe [Comerón et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741...28C/abstract).
