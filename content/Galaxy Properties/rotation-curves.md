In astrophysics, a galaxy's rotation curve is a fundamental kinematic profile that plots the orbital [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]] of stars and interstellar gas as a function of their [[galactocentric-distance|Galactocentric distance]]. Under the assumption of circular orbits within an axisymmetric potential, the centrifugal force experienced by these kinematic tracers is balanced by the local gravitational acceleration. This physical relationship allows the rotation curve to act as a direct empirical probe of a galaxy's enclosed mass distribution. Historically, based on the luminous distribution of stars and gas in [[thin-disks|Thin disks]], the orbital velocities in the outer regions of spiral galaxies were expected to exhibit a Keplerian decline ($V \propto r^{-1/2}$). However, foundational optical and 21-cm radio observations in the 1970s and 1980s revealed that most disk galaxies instead possess [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] that remain elevated well beyond their optical extent [van der Kruit et al. (1978)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1978ARA&A..16..103V/abstract), [Rubin et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985ApJ...289...81R/abstract). The failure of the [[asymptotic-circular-velocity|Asymptotic circular velocity]] to decline implies that the enclosed mass continues to grow linearly with radius, providing the primary dynamical evidence for massive, non-luminous dark matter halos enveloping visible galaxies [Begeman et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987PhDT.......199B/abstract).

Disentangling the underlying mass components of a galaxy—typically a stellar bulge, a baryonic disk, and a dark matter halo—requires careful structural decomposition and has yielded profound empirical scaling relations. Statistical analyses of rotation curves across diverse galaxy populations demonstrate that [[galactic-kinematics-and-rotation|galactic kinematics]] are tightly coupled to their baryonic mass. This coupling manifests in global scaling laws such as the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]], which links a galaxy's total luminosity to its [[maximum-rotational-velocity|Maximum rotational velocity]], and the framework of [[universal-rotation-curves|Universal Rotation Curves]], which parameterizes the entire shape of the velocity profile based on luminosity [Persic et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...368...60P/abstract). More recently, high-quality kinematic databases have established the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] (RAR), a spatially resolved correlation between the observed dynamical acceleration and the acceleration predicted solely from luminous baryons [Lelli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..157L/abstract). The incredibly low scatter of these relations places stringent constraints on the physics of galaxy formation, forcing models to explain a highly tuned "disk-halo conspiracy."

Despite the broad success of the standard Cold Dark Matter ($\Lambda$CDM) paradigm, detailed mass modeling of rotation curves reveals significant ongoing tensions at galactic scales. Chief among these is the core-cusp problem: while collisionless dark matter simulations predict steeply rising central density cusps [Navarro et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..7084N/abstract), the inner [[halo-circular-velocity-curve|Halo circular velocity curve]] of many dwarf and low-surface-brightness galaxies is often better described by a shallow, constant-density [[core-radius|core]]. This discrepancy, along with the sheer diversity of rotation curve shapes at fixed halo masses, drives active research into baryonic feedback mechanisms, self-interacting dark matter, and alternative modified gravity theories like MOND. Furthermore, the observational frontier of [[galactic-kinematics-and-rotation|Galactic Kinematics and Rotation]] has recently expanded to "cosmic noon" ($z \sim 1-2$). Spectroscopic observations suggest that massive, star-forming galaxies at these high redshifts frequently exhibit declining outer rotation curves. This has sparked intense debate over whether early galaxies are genuinely baryon-dominated and deficient in dark matter, or if these declining profiles are merely artifacts of severe pressure support and observational beam-smearing in highly turbulent primordial disks.

## Fundamental Principles and Circular Velocity

The kinematics of a rotationally supported galaxy are fundamentally defined by its circular velocity profile, $V_c(R)$, which traces the orbital speed of stars and cold gas as a function of [[galactocentric-distance|Galactocentric distance]], $R$. In a stable, circular orbit, the centripetal acceleration required to maintain the motion of a test particle is exactly provided by the local gravitational acceleration. Assuming a spherically symmetric mass distribution for simplicity, the balance between centripetal acceleration and the gravitational field yields a direct measurement of the enclosed mass, $M(R)$:

$$ \frac{V_c^2(R)}{R} = \frac{G M(R)}{R^2} $$

which rearranges to $M(R) = \frac{R V_c^2(R)}{G}$. While real galaxies are flattened systems requiring axisymmetric potential theory for their [[thin-disks|Thin disks]], this spherical approximation illustrates the core principle: the rotation curve acts as a direct probe of the cumulative mass profile of the galaxy. 

If the mass of a galaxy were entirely represented by its luminous stellar and gaseous components, the enclosed mass $M(R)$ would rapidly converge to a constant total mass just beyond the optical extent of the disk. Under standard Newtonian dynamics, the circular velocity should then exhibit a Keplerian decline at large radii, characterized by $V_c(R) \propto R^{-1/2}$. However, decades of high-resolution 21-cm H I and optical spectroscopy have demonstrated that the rotation curves of most spiral galaxies do not follow this expectation. Instead, the velocities remain elevated in their outer regions, typically transitioning into [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] [Rubin et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985ApJ...289...81R/abstract), [Sofue et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ARA&A..39..137S/abstract). In the asymptotic regime where $V_c(R)$ is roughly constant, the enclosed mass must scale linearly with radius ($M(R) \propto R$). Because the enclosed mass does not converge where the luminous matter drops off, these kinematics require the presence of a massive, non-luminous dark matter halo extending far beyond the visible stellar disk [Kent et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987AJ.....93..816K/abstract). Recent statistical analyses of the SPARC database show no significant evidence for a Keplerian decline at the largest measured radii, instead favoring flat or rising profiles over the standard Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) taper [Bariego-Quintana et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250712120B/abstract). In fact, some recent observations indicate that the rotation curves of isolated galaxies may remain flat out to nearly $1 \text{ Mpc}$, suggesting that dark matter halos might form in extended isothermal states [Hoeneisen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241217869H/abstract).

Extracting an accurate mass profile from observational data requires deriving the true circular velocity from the measured line-of-sight kinematics. It is generally assumed that the observed tangential velocity, $V_{\rm obs}$, corrected for geometric inclination, is a close proxy for $V_c$. However, observed tracers such as ionized and neutral gas can experience additional radial forces due to pressure gradients. This pressure support, driven by interstellar turbulence, causes the measured rotation speed to lag the true circular velocity—a phenomenon akin to stellar asymmetric drift. While this correction is often negligible in massive spirals, turbulent gas pressure provides substantial radial support in low-mass dwarf galaxies. Failing to account for this pressure support can lead to significant underestimates of the underlying central dark matter density and the total enclosed mass [Dalcanton et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...721..547D/abstract). Therefore, precise mass modeling requires decoupling the measured rotation into discrete structural components—typically a stellar bulge, a baryonic disk, and a dark halo—while rigorously accounting for non-circular motions and pressure gradients in the interstellar medium.

## Observational Techniques and Tracers

The kinematics of galaxy disks are predominantly mapped using cold and ionized gas, which typically settle into [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] on nearly circular orbits. For extended mappings, the H I 21-cm line is the preferred observational tracer. Because neutral hydrogen often extends far beyond the optical stellar disk, radio interferometry can probe rotation curves out to 10-20 effective radii, revealing the flat [[asymptotic-circular-velocity|Asymptotic circular velocity]] that signifies the presence of massive dark matter halos [Begeman et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987PhDT.......199B/abstract), [de Blok et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....136.2648D/abstract). However, the extended nature of these gas disks makes them susceptible to environmental effects; for instance, H I-deficient galaxies in dense cluster environments often exhibit truncated disk scale lengths compared to their field counterparts [Vogt et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127.3325V/abstract). In the innermost regions, or in galaxies lacking abundant atomic gas, millimeter interferometry of molecular gas (such as CO) is used to map the deep central potential well [Sofue et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PASJ...65..118S/abstract), [Shelest et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...641A..31S/abstract).

For higher spatial resolution within the baryon-dominated optical disk, researchers rely on H$\alpha$ spectroscopy of HII regions. Historically, this was performed using optical long-slit spectrographs aligned with the galaxy's major axis [Dale et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AJ....121.1886D/abstract). Modern observational campaigns increasingly extract kinematics from 3D emission-line datacubes obtained via Integral Field Unit (IFU) spectrographs (e.g., MUSE, KMOS, SINFONI) and submillimeter arrays (e.g., ALMA) [Lelli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230518224L/abstract). These datacubes have been particularly transformative for studying high-redshift star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 1-2$, allowing astronomers to robustly separate [[ordered-rotation|Ordered rotation]] from turbulent gas dispersion and non-circular motions across the entire galactic face [Tiley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485..934T/abstract), [Nestor Shachar et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...78N/abstract). To synthesize such vast amounts of modern observational data, researchers have developed large-scale resources like the PROBES compendium, which provides thousands of deep rotation curves and matched multiband photometry for late-type spirals [Stone et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..262...33S/abstract).

In early-type galaxies (ellipticals and lenticulars) where cold gas is sparse, [[stellar-and-galactic-kinematics|Stellar and Galactic Kinematics]] must be measured using stellar absorption lines. Deriving the underlying gravitational potential from stellar tracers is more complex than for gas disks, as stars possess significant random motions (pressure support) that counteract gravity. Modeling these systems requires measuring the full line-of-sight velocity distribution, which is often parameterized by the [[fourth-order-gauss-hermite-moment|Fourth-order Gauss-Hermite moment]] $h_4$ alongside the mean velocity and velocity dispersion. Furthermore, detailed kinematic studies reveal that elliptical galaxies often show significant [[minor-axis-rotation|Minor axis rotation]] and kinematically distinct cores, supporting a triaxial structure rather than simple axisymmetry [Franx et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ApJ...344..613F/abstract).

Measuring the rotation curve of the Milky Way requires specialized techniques due to our embedded vantage point. Galactic kinematics are reconstructed using a combination of H I tangent-point analysis, molecular gas observations, and individual tracer objects such as masers, open clusters, and classical Cepheids [Frinchaboy et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..8666F/abstract), [Sofue et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PASJ...65..118S/abstract). Recently, precise proper motions and radial velocities from the *Gaia* mission have been combined with mid-infrared Cepheid distances to map the Galactic rotation curve to high precision up to a [[galactocentric-distance|Galactocentric distance]] of approximately $20$ kpc [Mróz et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...870L..10M/abstract).

To convert observed 2D velocity fields into a 1D rotation curve $V(R)$, astronomers typically use the [[tilted-ring-method|Tilted-ring method]]. This geometric modeling technique divides the disk into concentric annuli, fitting the [[galaxy-inclination|Galaxy inclination]], position angle, and systemic velocity independently at each radius. For low-mass galaxies or high-redshift systems where turbulent gas motions are significant, the measured tangential speeds must be mathematically corrected for asymmetric drift (pressure support) to accurately reflect the true circular velocity dictated by the gravitational potential [Dalcanton et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...721..547D/abstract), [Nestor Shachar et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...78N/abstract). When utilizing these derived velocity profiles for mass modeling, the information gained depends heavily on the observational characteristics; notably, precise velocity measurements are significantly more critical for robustly constraining inner halo density slopes than simply observing an extended spatial extent of H I surface density [Yasin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.5066Y/abstract).

## Historical Development: The Discovery of Missing Mass

Prior to the 1970s, the kinematics of spiral galaxies were generally expected to trace their luminous matter distributions. Because the surface brightness of galactic disks decays exponentially, it was assumed that the total enclosed mass $M(r)$ would converge to a finite value at large [[galactocentric-distance|galactocentric distances]]. Consequently, the circular velocity $V(r)$ was predicted to exhibit a Keplerian decline in the outer regions, following $V(r) \propto r^{-1/2}$.

The first major observational challenges to this paradigm emerged from extensive optical spectroscopic surveys of spiral galaxies. Pioneering observations by Vera Rubin initially mapped the H$\alpha$ and [N II] emission lines across the optical disks of a sample of Sc galaxies [Rubin et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980ApJ...242L.149R/abstract). She subsequently expanded these studies to systematically map and compare [[rotation-velocity|rotation velocities]] across Sa, Sb, and Sc galaxies, finding that, virtually independent of Hubble type, the rotation velocities did not fall off but instead remained constant, or even rose slightly, out to the limits of the visible disk [Rubin et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985ApJ...289...81R/abstract). However, it is notable that some massive, high-surface-brightness early-type spirals (such as S0 to Sab galaxies) frequently exhibit declining rotation curves in their outer regions, specifically where the stellar disk continues to dominate the gravitational potential [Noordermeer et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004IAUS..220..287N/abstract). The definitive realization that the vast majority of late-type galaxies possess [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] firmly established the galactic missing mass problem, transitioning it from a curiosity of galaxy clusters to a ubiquitous property of individual disk galaxies.

While optical emission lines effectively probed the inner star-forming disk, 21-cm radio observations of neutral hydrogen (H I) were required to trace kinematics far beyond the optical radius. A foundational milestone in this regime was the 21-cm synthesis mapping of the Sc spiral galaxy NGC 3198 [Begeman et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987PhDT.......199B/abstract). Its H I rotation curve was shown to be remarkably flat at approximately $150 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ out to 11 disk scale lengths, extending well into a regime where the luminous mass contribution is entirely negligible. At the last measured point, the inferred enclosed mass was $1.5 \times 10^{11} M_\odot$, roughly four times larger than the stellar mass estimated from a maximum disk model.

The asymptotic flatness of these extended H I profiles has profound gravitational implications. Assuming spherical symmetry, the balance of centrifugal and gravitational forces dictates that:
$$ V(r) = \sqrt{\frac{G M(r)}{r}} $$
An asymptotically constant $V(r)$ requires that the enclosed mass scales linearly with radius, $M(r) \propto r$, rather than converging to a limiting visible mass. This implies that the local mass density must fall off as $\rho(r) \propto r^{-2}$ at large distances [Begeman et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991MNRAS.249..523B/abstract). 

To reconcile the rapidly truncating luminous mass distribution with these observed kinematics, dynamicists introduced multi-component mass models comprising a stellar bulge, a luminous disk, and an extended, invisible dark matter halo [Kent et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987AJ.....93..816K/abstract). The necessity of a dark matter halo to fit the outer rotation curves of both high-mass spirals and low-surface-brightness dwarfs became the cornerstone of the modern cosmological paradigm. This shift ultimately paved the way for discovering tight scaling relations between total dynamical mass and baryonic luminosity, such as the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]], and the broader phenomenological framework of [[universal-rotation-curves|Universal Rotation Curves]] [Persic et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...368...60P/abstract).

## Mass Modeling and Component Decomposition

Mass modeling is the analytical process of disentangling the individual gravitational contributions of a galaxy's luminous and dark components. Assuming circular orbits in an axisymmetric potential, the total circular velocity $V_{\rm tot}(R)$ of a galaxy is modeled as the quadrature sum of its dynamically distinct constituents:
$$V_{\rm tot}^2(R) = V_{\rm bulge}^2(R) + V_{\rm disk}^2(R) + V_{\rm gas}^2(R) + V_{\rm halo}^2(R)$$

To perform a proper bulge-disk decomposition, 3D luminosity densities $\nu(r)$ are derived from surface photometry and converted to mass profiles using the stellar mass-to-light ratio, $\Upsilon_\star$. Because $\Upsilon_\star$ is often treated as a radially constant free parameter, it represents the largest source of degeneracy in baryonic mass modeling. Independent constraints on $\Upsilon_\star$ from stellar population synthesis models or vertical velocity dispersions often yield conflicting results; for example, the DiskMass survey suggests low values ($\Upsilon_\star \simeq 0.2\, M_\odot/L_\odot$ at $3.6\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), whereas kinematic fits to near-infrared photometry consistently favor higher values ($\Upsilon_\star \simeq 0.5$--$0.7\, M_\odot/L_\odot$) [Lelli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..157L/abstract). 

The interstellar gas fraction must also be accounted for, typically mapped via 21-cm H I and molecular CO emission. While gas mass is negligible in the inner regions of early-type spirals, it frequently dominates the baryonic mass budget in dwarf irregulars and low-surface-brightness (LSB) galaxies, necessitating highly accurate H I mapping [Begeman et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987PhDT.......199B/abstract).

A historically prominent approach to resolving the $\Upsilon_\star$ degeneracy is the maximum disk hypothesis. This framework scales $\Upsilon_\star$ such that the stellar components account for the entirety of the inner rotation curve, requiring no dark matter at small radii [Kent et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987AJ.....93..816K/abstract). A prominent alternative constraint is the Bottema criterion, which suggests that the stellar disk's maximum rotation velocity is typically only about 63% of the observed [[maximum-rotational-velocity|maximum circular velocity]], implying that even large spiral galaxies may not be strictly maximal in their stellar components [Bottema et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997A&A...328..517B/abstract). Modern analyses indicate that high-surface-brightness spiral galaxies are often consistent with maximum-disk configurations, operating as self-gravitating, baryon-dominated systems in their inner regions. Conversely, LSB and dwarf galaxies robustly fail the maximum-disk test; they are submaximal and require massive dark matter components even at small [[galactocentric-distance|galactocentric radii]] [de Blok et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..117...39D/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..157L/abstract).

Subtracting the baryonic contributions from the observed kinematics yields the [[halo-circular-velocity-curve|Halo circular velocity curve]], $V_{\rm halo}(R)$. The residuals are typically fitted with parametrized dark matter density profiles. This introduces a major tension in modern astrophysics: while standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology predicts "cuspy" Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) halos with steeply rising central densities ($\rho \propto r^{-1}$) [Navarro et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..7084N/abstract), the mass models of LSB and dwarf galaxies consistently favor "[[core-radius|cored]]" profiles with constant central densities (e.g., Burkert or pseudo-isothermal models) [Swaters et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...583..732S/abstract), [de Blok et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....136.2648D/abstract). However, this tension may be partially mitigated by statistical techniques; accounting for radial correlations in rotation curve data as "nuisance parameters" during fitting can significantly reduce the statistical preference for cored profiles over cuspy NFW models [Chase et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251123106C/abstract). Alternatively, mass models that incorporate dark matter-gas interactions to create disky dark matter components have been shown to significantly improve rotation curve fits compared to traditional NFW profiles or MOND formulations [Loizeau et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...920L..10L/abstract). Historically, some alternative mass models have also considered the hypothesis of 'dark baryons'—a non-luminous spheroidal baryonic component—as an additional inner mass component that works alongside Cold Dark Matter to solve the core-cusp discrepancy in dwarf galaxies like DDO 154, still requiring a standard dark matter halo in their outer regions [Burkert et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...488L..55B/abstract).

Recent mass modeling efforts emphasize the necessity of rigorous statistical treatments. Neighboring data points in rotation curves are often highly correlated due to beam smearing and non-circular motions; neglecting these spatial covariances during decomposition can significantly bias the resulting dark matter halo parameters [Posti et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RNAAS...6..233P/abstract). Furthermore, comprehensive mass models have established tight dynamical laws, such as a central density relation linking the central surface density of stars to the central dynamical mass [Lelli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..19L/abstract). On a spatially resolved scale, this coupling manifests as the tight [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] (RAR) between the observed dynamical acceleration and the acceleration predicted from baryons alone [Lelli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..157L/abstract), [Stiskalek et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.6130S/abstract). The surprisingly low scatter in this relation places stringent constraints on galaxy formation models, as it requires a precise, non-stochastic coupling between the baryonic disk and the dark matter halo.

## Scaling Relations and the Universal Rotation Curve

While individual galaxy rotation curves exhibit a wide range of morphologies, statistical analyses across broad populations reveal a highly constrained set of dynamical scaling relations. These empirical laws demonstrate that the kinematics of disk galaxies are not stochastically determined, but are instead intimately coupled to the distribution of their baryonic mass.

The most prominent of these global scaling laws is the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] (TFR), which establishes a tight power-law connection between a galaxy's luminosity $L$ and its characteristic [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]] $V_{\rm max}$. The classical TFR takes the form $L = A V_{\rm max}^\alpha$, where the slope $\alpha$ typically ranges from 2.5 to 4 depending on the photometric band. When re-cast in terms of total baryonic mass (stars plus gas), the Baryonic Tully-Fisher Relation (BTFR) emerges as an even more fundamental correlation. Using high-quality extended rotation curves, analyses find that the BTFR is tightest when measured using the mean velocity along the flat portion of the rotation curve ($V_{\rm flat}$). This yields an orthogonal intrinsic scatter of merely $\sim 6\%$ and a best-fitting slope of $M_{\rm bar} \propto V_{\rm flat}^{3.85 \pm 0.09}$ [Lelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3267L/abstract). Indeed, Bayesian characterization of intrinsic scatter across various galaxy scaling relations reveals that the stellar mass-velocity ($M_*$-$V$) and luminosity-dynamical specific angular momentum ($L$-$j_{\rm dyn}$) relations are among the tightest identified in large kinematic surveys [Stone et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...912...41S/abstract).

The predictability of galaxy kinematics extends beyond a single characteristic velocity parameter. The [[universal-rotation-curves|Universal Rotation Curves]] (URC) paradigm demonstrated that the entire shape of a rotation curve varies systematically as a function of galaxy luminosity and surface brightness [Persic et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...368...60P/abstract). Rather than requiring independent structural modeling for every halo, the URC shows that the circular velocity profile, $V(r/R_{\rm opt})$, follows a universal functional form. Massive, high-surface-brightness galaxies typically feature rapidly rising curves that flatten or decline slightly at large radii, while low-mass, low-surface-brightness (LSB) and dwarf galaxies exhibit shallow, continuously rising curves. This universality persists down to the dwarf galaxy regime, where the URC demonstrates that these systems are completely dark matter-dominated but exhibit tightly coupled structural parameters between the luminous disk and the dark halo [Karukes et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4703K/abstract).

Another critical global scaling law is the Fall relation, which links the specific stellar angular momentum $j_*$ to the stellar mass $M_*$. For a wide morphological range of disk galaxies, this follows an unbroken power law $j_* \propto M_*^\alpha$ with $\alpha = 0.55 \pm 0.02$ [Posti et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...612L...6P/abstract). However, the BTFR appears to be the more foundational scaling law. The scatter in the Fall relation is roughly seven times larger than that of the BTFR, a variance driven predominantly by the underlying scatter in the mass-size relation of galaxies [Lelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3267L/abstract). Further analysis reveals no significant correlation between Tully-Fisher velocity residuals and mass-size residuals across galactic radii. This decoupling implies a highly tuned "disk-halo conspiracy" in which the baryonic and dark matter components must balance to maintain the observed scaling relations without inducing large scatter [Desmond et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484..239D/abstract). A further manifestation of this conspiracy is the Bosma effect, which demonstrates that the spatial distributions of dark matter and cold gas are tightly correlated; this proportionality has recently been utilized to establish a new 'Bosma Tully-Fisher relation' that further constrains the interplay between baryonic and dark components [Sylos Labini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.2697S/abstract).

Extending these global regularities to local, radially resolved scales yields the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] (RAR). The RAR demonstrates a tight correlation between the observed total dynamical acceleration and the Newtonian acceleration predicted solely from the baryonic distribution, a relationship that holds at every measured radius over several orders of magnitude in mass [Lelli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..157L/abstract). Whether these incredibly tight scaling relations are an emergent property of baryonic feedback operating within cold dark matter halos [Keller et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835L..17K/abstract), or whether they constitute evidence for modified theories of gravity [Gentile et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...527A..76G/abstract), remains a subject of active and unresolved debate in the literature.

## The Radial Acceleration Relation (RAR)

The [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] (RAR) constitutes a tight empirical coupling between the observed dynamical acceleration in galaxies and the gravitational acceleration predicted solely by their luminous baryonic mass [McGaugh et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Galax...2..601M/abstract), [Lelli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230518224L/abstract). Historically rooted in studies of the mass discrepancy-acceleration relation, the RAR demonstrates that the requirement for dark matter—or the effective departure from Newtonian dynamics—is strictly correlated with the spatial distribution of visible baryons.

At any given galactocentric radius, the observed centripetal acceleration is given by $g_{\rm obs} = V_{\rm rot}^2 / R$, where $V_{\rm rot}$ is the local [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]]. The expected baryonic acceleration, $g_{\rm bar}$, is computed by solving the Poisson equation for the observed distribution of stars and gas. In the high-acceleration regime ($g_{\rm bar} \gtrsim 10^{-10} \text{ m s}^{-2}$), galaxies are largely baryon-dominated and $g_{\rm obs} \approx g_{\rm bar}$. However, in the low-acceleration outskirts of galaxies, a mass discrepancy emerges, and the relation smoothly transitions to a regime where $g_{\rm obs} \propto \sqrt{g_{\rm bar}}$ [Navia et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180405914N/abstract). The transition between these two dynamical regimes is marked by a universal acceleration scale, $a_0 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m s}^{-2}$ [Gentile et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...527A..76G/abstract), [Desmond et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.3342D/abstract).

The robust nature of the RAR was firmly established using the Spitzer Photometry and Accurate Rotation Curves (SPARC) database, a large compilation of high-quality rotation curves and near-infrared photometry spanning several orders of magnitude in galaxy mass and surface brightness [Lelli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..157L/abstract). Statistical analyses of the SPARC sample reveal that the RAR is a fundamental one-dimensional correlation governing the radial dynamics of late-type galaxies [Stiskalek et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.6130S/abstract). In this sense, the RAR acts as the spatially resolved, local counterpart to the global [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] [Swaters et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.2299S/abstract).

One of the most remarkable features of the RAR is its extraordinarily small intrinsic scatter. Detailed Bayesian modeling indicates an intrinsic scatter of just $\sim 0.034$ dex, suggesting that the correlation is driven by deterministic underlying physics rather than coincidental observational scatter [Desmond et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.3342D/abstract). Furthermore, while initially established for late-type [[thin-disks|Thin disks]], recent observations suggest the relation also holds for early-type lenticular galaxies, reinforcing its universality across morphological types [Shelest et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...641A..31S/abstract).

The tightness of the RAR presents a significant challenge to cosmological models, fueling an active debate in the literature. On one hand, the near-zero scatter and the universality of $a_0$ are explicitly predicted by Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) and other modified gravity frameworks, which interpret the RAR as evidence of a fundamental modification to gravitational or inertial laws at low accelerations [McGaugh et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Galax...2..601M/abstract), [Chae et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...55C/abstract). Conversely, within the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm, the RAR must be understood as an emergent property of galaxy formation. Several studies using cosmological hydrodynamical simulations (e.g., MassiveBlack-II and MUGS2) have demonstrated that an RAR-like correlation can arise naturally as a consequence of the dissipative collapse of baryons, coupled with star formation and stellar feedback within cold dark matter halos [Keller et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835L..17K/abstract), [Tenneti et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.3125T/abstract). Whether the RAR reflects new fundamental physics or complex baryonic feedback mechanisms remains a highly active area of research in galactic dynamics.

## The Core-Cusp Problem and Rotation Curve Diversity

Cosmological $N$-body simulations of Cold Dark Matter (CDM) predict a universal dark matter density profile characterized by a steep central "cusp." The widely adopted Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile predicts that the inner dark matter density scales as $\rho \propto r^{-1}$ [Navarro et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..7084N/abstract). While high-mass, high-surface-brightness disk galaxies generally have inner kinematics consistent with NFW predictions, testing this is often complicated by the strong gravitational dominance of their baryonic bulges and disks.

The kinematics of dwarf and Low Surface Brightness (LSB) galaxies, however, present a stark contrast to CDM predictions. In these systems, where dark matter dynamically dominates down to small radii, the observed [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]] tends to rise slowly with [[galactocentric-distance|galactocentric distance]]. These slowly rising curves are typically better fit by models featuring a constant-density central region, such as the Burkert profile or the pseudo-isothermal sphere, rather than a cuspy NFW profile [Swaters et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...583..732S/abstract), [de Blok et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....136.2648D/abstract). The discrepancy between the predicted $\rho \propto r^{-1}$ scaling and the observationally preferred flat inner density profiles is known as the core-cusp problem. Moreover, detailed mass modeling often reveals that the inferred dark matter density at intermediate radii is systematically lower than predicted by standard $\Lambda$CDM structure formation simulations [McGaugh et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...659..149M/abstract).

Closely related to this tension is the "diversity problem" of dwarf galaxy rotation curves. Observations reveal that galaxies with similar maximum rotational velocities ($V_{\rm max}$) exhibits a remarkably wide variance in the shapes of their inner rotation curves. This diversity can be parameterized by the fractional velocity at a fixed radius, such as $V_{\rm rot}(1{\rm~kpc})/V_{\rm max}$ [Brook et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.1719B/abstract). Dark-matter-only $\Lambda$CDM simulations predict a tight relationship between halo mass and concentration, which fails to reproduce the wide scatter observed in the inner mass distributions of these galaxies [Brook et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.1719B/abstract), [Santos-Santos et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.495...58S/abstract). 

Resolving the core-cusp and diversity problems remains an active area of debate, with literature divided between observational systematics and necessary modifications to galaxy formation physics. On the observational side, gas in low-mass galaxies may be significantly supported by turbulent pressure rather than pure rotation. If uncorrected, this pressure support causes the measured tangential velocity to lag the true circular velocity, artificially flattening the inner rotation curve and mimicking a dark matter [[core-radius|core]] [Dalcanton et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...721..547D/abstract). Other systematic effects, such as non-circular motions, slit misalignment, and beam smearing, further complicate the precise inversion of rotation curves to infer inner density slopes [Swaters et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...583..732S/abstract).

If the inferred cores are physical, they may point to missing baryonic physics in standard collisionless dark matter models. Baryonic feedback—particularly cyclic gas blowouts driven by supernovae—can cause rapid fluctuations in the central gravitational potential. These fluctuations transfer energy to dark matter particles, gradually expanding a primordial cusp into a [[core-radius|Core radius]] and introducing significant scatter in the inner density profiles based on a galaxy's specific star formation history [Brook et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.1719B/abstract), [Del Popolo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ARep...66..102D/abstract). In cases where baryonic feedback models are deemed insufficient to explain the full range of observed profiles, the core-cusp and diversity problems are often cited as motivation for alternative dark matter models. For instance, self-interacting dark matter (SIDM) models allow particle collisions to thermalize the inner halo, naturally generating cored density profiles while preserving the large-scale successes of $\Lambda$CDM [Brook et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.1719B/abstract), [Zentner et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...07..031Z/abstract).

## Alternative Theories: Modified Gravity and Modified Inertia

The discrepancy between the mass inferred from luminous matter and the dynamical mass required to produce [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] has motivated theories that modify the laws of gravity or inertia, eliminating the need for a dark matter halo.

**Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND)**
The most prominent alternative is Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), which postulates a departure from Newtonian dynamics at a critical acceleration scale, $a_0$. Empirical fits to high-quality rotation curves consistently find $a_0 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-8}$ cm/s$^2$ [Milgrom et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ApJ...333..689M/abstract), [Gentile et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...527A..76G/abstract). MOND naturally reproduces scaling laws like the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] and the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] directly from the observable baryonic distribution [McGaugh et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Galax...2..601M/abstract). Tests using the THINGS survey show that MOND provides highly competitive zero-parameter predictions for individual galaxy rotation curves, with the "simple" interpolating function often yielding better fits than the "standard" formulation [Gentile et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...527A..76G/abstract). However, MOND faces challenges in capturing the full diversity of rotation curve shapes in dwarf galaxies without invoking variable interpolating functions or external field effects [Rodrigues et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PDU....4101230R/abstract), [Desmond et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.3342D/abstract). Furthermore, statistical comparisons of the inner and outer regions of rotation curves strongly disfavor modified inertia formulations of MOND, pointing instead toward modified gravity if dark matter is to be excluded [Chae et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...55C/abstract).

**f(R) Gravity and Conformal Gravity**
Extended theories of gravity, such as f(R) gravity, introduce a fifth force that enhances gravitational attraction at galactic scales. While specific $R^n$ models can successfully fit the rotation curves of low-luminosity and dwarf galaxies [Salucci et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IJMPD..2342005S/abstract), comprehensive tests using the SPARC sample heavily constrain chameleon f(R) models. Assuming a single global background scalar field, f(R) models with $\log_{10}|\bar{f}_{R0}| > -6.1$ are excluded. The apparent preference for f(R) gravity at lower field values is largely degenerate with the core-cusp problem of cold dark matter, providing no unambiguous evidence for f(R) modifications [Naik et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489..771N/abstract). In addition to f(R) gravity, recent studies have tested f(R,T) theories—incorporating the trace of the energy-momentum tensor—showing they can effectively reproduce flat rotation curves without dark matter [Mohan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024IJGMM..2150082M/abstract).

Alternatively, conformal gravity provides exact fits to a wide variety of [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] without dark matter by introducing a linear potential term associated with the global Hubble flow. This framework identifies a universal constant of spatial curvature, $k = -(\gamma_0 / 2)^2 \approx -2.3 \times 10^{-60}$ cm$^{-2}$, that dictates non-Keplerian behavior at large [[galactocentric-distance|galactocentric distances]] [Mannheim et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...479..659M/abstract), [O'Brien et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421.1273O/abstract).

**Scalar-Vector-Tensor Gravity (STVG)**
Another theoretical avenue is Scalar-Vector-Tensor Gravity (STVG), which modifies gravity via massive vector fields and varying gravitational constants. STVG has been shown to successfully reproduce the Milky Way's rotation curve without requiring a dark matter halo, potentially outperforming MOND and other modified Newtonian frameworks in specific local kinematic tests [Moffat et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvD..91d3004M/abstract).

**Emergent Gravity**
Verlinde's theory of emergent gravity derives the gravitational field strictly from the baryonic distribution and the entanglement entropy of the vacuum. When tested against SPARC data, emergent gravity successfully predicts the observed rotation curve accelerations with a mean offset of $-0.060 \pm 0.004$ dex and a scatter of $0.137 \pm 0.004$ dex (assuming a de Sitter universe), demonstrating performance competitive with standard dark matter models [Yoon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40bLT01Y/abstract).

**Relativistic and Retardation Effects**
A subset of the literature explores whether the mass discrepancy is an illusion caused by non-inertial reference frames [Gomel et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Galax...9...34G/abstract) or general relativistic retardation effects over galactic scales [Yahalom et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JPhCS1239a2006Y/abstract), [Yahalom et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022IJMPD..3142018Y/abstract). In principle, exploring relativistic Doppler and gravitational shifts could potentially distinguish between theories modifying inertia versus those modifying the underlying field equations of gravity [Cooney et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1202.2853C/abstract). However, the broad consensus in the field remains that standard post-Newtonian corrections and retardation effects are orders of magnitude too small to significantly alter galactic rotation curves. Critics note that models relying on these effects to replace dark matter often suffer from improper relativistic scaling or imply infinite enclosed masses when strictly evaluated within General Relativity [Menzies et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006gr.qc.....4092M/abstract), [Glampedakis et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023CQGra..40n7001G/abstract).

**Empirical and Alternative Frameworks**
Beyond the most established models, several other novel theoretical frameworks have emerged. The Luminous Convolution Model (LCM) has been proposed as a zero-parameter alternative that utilizes Lorentz-type transformations of frequency shifts in curved space-times to predict rotation curves [Cisneros et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160808316C/abstract). Other emerging frameworks include entropic force models derived from the Fokker-Planck equation, which have demonstrated a strong agreement with rotation curve data from the SPARC database [Morales-Salgado et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220105594M/abstract). Similarly, a recent empirical model suggests that spacetime may store additional energy proportional to the square of the baryonic gravitational potential, which could explain the flat outer regions of rotation curves solely from observed baryonic mass profiles [Kamada et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251223008K/abstract). Another phenomenological approach introduces an empirical velocity correction term, $\omega$, anchored to Keplerian baselines, which has been shown to consistently align with observed rotation profiles across various galaxies without altering Newtonian gravity [Flynn et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260100522F/abstract).

## Kinematics at Cosmic Noon: Declining Curves at High Redshift

In the local Universe, spiral galaxies typically exhibit [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] at large radii, a cornerstone piece of evidence for the presence of massive dark matter halos. However, recent spatially resolved kinematic surveys of massive star-forming galaxies at "cosmic noon" ($z \approx 1-2$) have revealed a surprising deviation from this paradigm. Observations utilizing Integral Field Unit (IFU) spectroscopy have found that a significant fraction of these high-redshift galaxies possess declining rotation curves, suggesting that their inner kinematics are strongly baryon-dominated with comparatively low dark matter fractions [Nestor Shachar et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...78N/abstract).

Dynamical modeling of H$\alpha$ and CO kinematics in these systems indicates a marked evolution in the dark matter fraction within the effective radius, $R_e$. At $z \approx 2$, the median dark matter fraction is found to be $f_{\rm DM}(R_e) = V_{\rm DM}^2(R_e) / V_{\rm circ}^2(R_e) \approx 0.27 \pm 0.18$, which increases to $f_{\rm DM}(R_e) \approx 0.38 \pm 0.23$ by $z \approx 1$ [Nestor Shachar et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...78N/abstract). Nearly half of the massive disk galaxies at $z \approx 2$ can be characterized as "maximal disks" with $f_{\rm DM}(R_e) < 0.28$, exhibiting a deficit in dark matter mass of approximately 40% compared to expectations from standard cosmological stellar-to-halo mass relations [Nestor Shachar et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...78N/abstract). This conclusion is consistent with earlier models based on density wave theory, which suggested that the inner regions of massive disk galaxies at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 0.7-1$) are already maximal and heavily dominated by baryons [Fuchs et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004A&A...427...95F/abstract).

The physical interpretation of these declining curves remains a subject of active debate. On one hand, hydrodynamical $\Lambda$CDM simulations demonstrate that declining rotation curves and low central dark matter fractions can arise naturally at $z \approx 2$ as a consequence of complex baryonic physics [Teklu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...854L..28T/abstract). In this scenario, rapid baryonic inflows produce highly concentrated stellar and gaseous disks, while feedback from active galactic nuclei or supernovae—coupled with dynamical friction—may heat the dark matter, driving the central halo profile from a steep cusp toward a shallower [[core-radius|Core radius]] [Nestor Shachar et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...78N/abstract).

Conversely, a substantial portion of the literature cautions that the observed declines may be influenced by observational biases and systematic effects in data analysis. High-redshift disks are typically highly turbulent, meaning that pressure support (asymmetric drift) contributes significantly to their equilibrium. While uncorrected pressure support is well known to cause the measured [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]] to lag the true circular velocity in local low-mass dwarf galaxies [Dalcanton et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...721..547D/abstract), modern high-redshift studies explicitly incorporate these corrections into their dynamical models and still observe a substantial dark matter deficit in massive galaxies [Nestor Shachar et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...78N/abstract). Furthermore, cosmological surface brightness dimming and beam-smearing (loss of spatial resolution) can obscure non-circular motions and merger-induced asymmetries, artificially smoothing the velocity fields and biasing the derived kinematic profiles [Araujo-Carvalho et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991....3A/abstract), [de Araujo-Carvalho et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250710544D/abstract). 

The methodology used to extract average kinematics also plays a critical role. While individual galaxy fits often show declining outer velocities, some studies employing stacked position-velocity diagrams—normalized by the disk scale length ($R_d$) and corrected for seeing—recover average rotation curves that remain flat or rising out to $6 R_d$ ($\approx 13$ kpc) [Tiley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485..934T/abstract). These stacked profiles imply dark matter fractions of at least 60% at large radii, consistent with standard $\Lambda$CDM expectations. Therefore, while declining rotation curves are a well-documented observational feature at cosmic noon, the literature remains explicitly conflicted on whether they represent a fundamental difference in early halo assembly or are an artifact of observational limitations and turbulent gas dynamics. New initiatives like the KMOS Ultradeep Rotational Velocity Survey (KURVS) are employing 120-hour integrations of $z \approx 1.5$ star-forming galaxies to provide high-precision individual rotation curve decompositions [Dudzeviciute et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021essp.confE..47D/abstract). Despite these ongoing debates regarding the exact dark matter fractions, deep spectroscopic integrations demonstrate that the stellar mass [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] is already well-established by $z \approx 1$, exhibiting a scatter comparable to local relations [Miller et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741..115M/abstract).

## The Milky Way Rotation Curve and Local Kinematics

Measuring the [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]] of the Milky Way is inherently challenging due to our position within the galactic disk. Unlike external galaxies, where inclination and line-of-sight kinematics can be mapped globally, mapping the Milky Way requires decoupling non-circular motions from galactic rotation across varying lines of sight. Despite these observational hurdles, combining multi-wavelength data—including CO and CS emission lines in the inner disk, 21-cm H I in the outer disk, and stellar tracers—allows for the construction of a "grand rotation curve" extending from the central supermassive black hole out to the dark matter halo [Sofue et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PASJ...65..118S/abstract). This curve exhibits pronounced localized structures, such as dips at $R \approx 3 \text{ kpc}$ and $R \approx 9 \text{ kpc}$, which are dynamically associated with the central bar and the Perseus spiral arm, respectively [Sofue et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PASJ...61..227S/abstract).

The fundamental parameters defining [[galactic-kinematics-and-rotation|Galactic Kinematics and Rotation]] in our solar neighborhood are the [[galactocentric-distance|Galactocentric distance]] of the Sun, $R_0$, and the LSR circular speed, $\Theta_0$. High-precision interferometry from the GRAVITY collaboration strongly constrains $R_0 \approx 8.122 \text{ kpc}$. Anchored to this distance, kinematic modeling of classical Cepheids yields an LSR circular speed of $\Theta_0 \approx 233.6 \pm 2.8 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Mróz et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...870L..10M/abstract), consistent with other modern mass models built upon this framework [McGaugh et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018RNAAS...2..156M/abstract).

Successive data releases from the Gaia space observatory have revolutionized our mapping of the local velocity field. By utilizing high-precision astrometric samples from Gaia DR2, researchers have placed stringent constraints on the Oort constants, which parameterize local shear and rotation. Recent analyses in the solar vicinity measure these constants to be $A = 15.1 \pm 0.1 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ kpc}^{-1}$ and $B = -13.4 \pm 0.1 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ kpc}^{-1}$. This corresponds to a local angular velocity of $\Omega = |A - B| \approx 28.5 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ kpc}^{-1}$ [Li et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872..205L/abstract).

Between Galactocentric radii of 4 and 20 kpc, the Milky Way's rotation curve is largely flat, exhibiting only a mild negative gradient of approximately $-1.34 \pm 0.21 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ kpc}^{-1}$ [Mróz et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...870L..10M/abstract). Spectroscopic measurements of N-type carbon (C) stars have extended the Galactic rotation curve to approximately 24-25 kpc, suggesting a slight decline beyond the solar circle [Battinelli et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Ap.....56...68B/abstract). Early investigations into the outer declines of the Milky Way's rotation curve, such as Honma's mass estimates, inferred a total Galactic mass of approximately $2.0 \times 10^{11} M_\odot$, noting a preference for a local circular speed ($\Theta_0$) near $200 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ to explain these declining outer profiles under standard dark matter assumptions [Honma et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996PASJ...48L.103H/abstract). More recent mass models fit to the grand rotation curve estimate the local dark matter density at the solar neighborhood to be $\rho_{\text{DM}} \approx 0.24 \text{--} 0.26 \text{ GeV cm}^{-3}$ (or roughly $6.1 \text{--} 6.8 \times 10^{-3} M_\odot \text{ pc}^{-3}$) [Sofue et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PASJ...64...75S/abstract), [McGaugh et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018RNAAS...2..156M/abstract).

While the consensus strongly supports a flat or very mildly declining outer rotation curve out to $\sim 20 \text{ kpc}$, the literature does contain some conflicting interpretations. Measurements tracking certain interstellar clouds and Cepheids in the outer Galaxy have occasionally suggested a steeper, Keplerian-like decline, implying a dark matter density up to 60% lower than standard models predict [Gnaciński et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PASP..129d4101G/abstract), [Gnaciński et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AN....340..787G/abstract). However, some researchers argue that flat rotation curves themselves might be an artifact of incorrectly modeling elliptical stellar orbits as circular, while the Keplerian curves derived from interstellar clouds reflect true circular motion and accurately trace the underlying potential [Gnaciński et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PASP..129d4101G/abstract). Furthermore, analysis of off-plane stellar velocity components from Gaia DR3 has enabled the construction of 'generalized rotation curves,' which suggest that mass models where dark matter is confined to the galactic plane (DMD model) may provide superior fits to large-radius data compared to standard NFW halos [Sylos Labini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976..185S/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the ubiquity of [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] firmly establishes a mass discrepancy in the outer regions of galaxies, the exact physical mechanisms governing galactic kinematics remain a subject of active research. The incredibly tight empirical coupling between the baryonic mass distribution and total dynamical acceleration, encapsulated by the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]], poses a persistent challenge to stochastic galaxy formation models and motivates ongoing theoretical debate [Lelli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..157L/abstract), [Stiskalek et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.6130S/abstract). However, interpreting these kinematics must be done with caution; recent studies highlight how dynamical perturbations—such as mergers, gas inflows, and disk warps—can cause the measured rotation curves in low-mass galaxies to systematically fail as accurate tracers of the underlying equilibrium circular velocity [Downing et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.3318D/abstract). For example, tidal interactions during the first pass of galaxy encounters can induce transient features such as central bumps and falling outer profiles, further complicating the extraction of true mass distributions [Barton et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...511L..25B/abstract).

A primary tension in modern astrophysics is the "core-cusp" and associated "diversity" problem. Standard $\Lambda$CDM simulations predict that dark matter halos should possess steep central density cusps ($\rho \propto r^{-1}$), yet the inner rotation curves of dwarf and low-surface-brightness galaxies frequently favor shallow, constant-density [[core-radius|cores]] [Navarro et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..7084N/abstract), [de Blok et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....136.2648D/abstract). Furthermore, the inner shapes of rotation curves exhibit a wide diversity at fixed maximum velocity that collisionless dark matter struggles to reproduce natively. Two leading mechanisms are debated to reconcile this tension. The first relies on baryonic feedback, wherein supernova-driven gas outflows repeatedly alter the gravitational potential, progressively flattening the central dark matter profile over time [Brook et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.1719B/abstract). The second posits self-interacting dark matter (SIDM), which naturally produces isothermal cores via dark sector scattering. Detailed model comparisons using SPARC data demonstrate that both SIDM and feedback-modified CDM can successfully reproduce the observed kinematic diversity, with current statistical analyses showing no definitive preference between the two frameworks [Zentner et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...07..031Z/abstract).

Beyond particulate CDM, alternative theoretical formalisms are actively being explored. Phenomenological models have begun treating the dark sector as a macroscopic continuum; for instance, modeling it as a dark matter fluid allows researchers to constrain the fluid's barotropic equation of state and its effective speed of sound directly from empirical rotation curve data [Perković et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023EPJC...83..306P/abstract). Additionally, the standard assumption of spherical symmetry in mass modeling is increasingly being questioned. Analytical studies indicate that non-spherical halos—such as highly prolate or cylindrical structures—could mathematically yield flat asymptotic velocities without the precise radial density tuning required by spherical NFW models [Llanes-Estrada et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Univ....7..346L/abstract). Observational efforts are currently underway to detect the subtle kinematic signatures of such non-spherical geometries in high-precision datasets [Loizeau et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...03..049L/abstract).

Another major unresolved question concerns the kinematic evolution of galaxies at "cosmic noon" ($z \sim 1-2$). Integral Field Unit (IFU) spectroscopy reveals that massive star-forming galaxies at these epochs frequently exhibit declining rotation curves at large radii, yielding median dark matter fractions of $\sim 0.27$ within the effective radius, which is significantly lower than local counterparts [Teklu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...854L..28T/abstract), [Nestor Shachar et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...78N/abstract). The literature, however, is highly conflicting regarding the physical reality of this decline. Several studies argue that these apparent drops in rotation velocity are actually artifacts of observational biases—specifically cosmological surface brightness dimming, resolution loss (beam smearing), and the failure to adequately account for pressure support (asymmetric drift) in highly turbulent, gas-rich disks [Tiley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485..934T/abstract), [de Araujo-Carvalho et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250710544D/abstract). Fully decoupling genuine evolutionary trends in halo assembly from these observational limitations remains a critical frontier for next-generation kinematic surveys.
