In extragalactic astronomy, **galaxy properties** encompass the intrinsic physical, structural, and chemical characteristics that define a dynamically bound system of stars, gas, dust, and dark matter. These attributes range from global integrated metrics—such as [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|total stellar mass]], the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]], and the overall [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]]—to localized features like [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]], gas-phase [[metallicity|Metallicity]], and internal kinematics [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract). Historically, the systematic study of these properties has been anchored by the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]], which broadly distinguishes rotationally supported [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] from dispersion-supported [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. In modern astrophysics, however, these observable properties are understood not merely as a taxonomic classification system, but as a detailed fossil record of a galaxy's assembly. They reflect the complex interplay between cosmological initial conditions, hierarchical merging, and internal baryonic physics operating over billions of years [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

Despite the immense diversity of the cosmic galaxy population, empirical observations reveal that galactic properties do not populate parameter space randomly; rather, they are tightly coupled through [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]]. Dynamical correlations, such as the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] for spirals and the Fundamental Plane for ellipticals, demonstrate a profound link between a galaxy's observable baryonic components and the potential well of its host dark matter halo. Similarly, the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] strictly correlates stellar mass with instantaneous star formation, mapping the steady growth of galaxies across cosmic time. A galaxy's evolutionary trajectory is also heavily modulated by the local [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]]. This is classically codified in the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]], which illustrates how dense cluster environments act to suppress star formation and drive morphological transformations. By tracing how these properties evolve from the compact, [[arp-atlas-of-peculiar-galaxies|peculiar systems]] prevalent in the early Universe to the mature structures seen locally, astronomers can rigorously constrain the physical drivers of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] and mass assembly [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract).

This article surveys the structural, integrated, and chemical properties of galaxies, while detailing the modern observational methodologies used to measure them across large [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Galaxy Surveys and Catalogs]]. As the field moves beyond single-fiber spectra toward spatially resolved measurements and [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]], several critical open questions and theoretical tensions have emerged that challenge standard evolutionary models [Mihalas et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981gask.book.....M/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982SAAS...12..115K/abstract). Foremost among these are ongoing debates regarding the exact physical origin of the "tilt" in the Fundamental Plane, the true causal mechanisms underlying the morphology-density relation, and stark discrepancies between cosmological simulations and the observed abundances of massive galaxies at high redshift. Furthermore, the historically foundational assumption of a universal [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] is increasingly being challenged by recent property measurements in massive early-type systems, pointing toward a highly dynamic, environmentally dependent parameter space that remains an active frontier of research.

## Morphological Classification and the Hubble Sequence

The [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] provides the foundational framework for [[galaxy-morphological-classification|Galaxy morphological classification]], dividing the local galaxy population into two broad categories based on their visual appearance: [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|early-type galaxies]] (ellipticals and lenticulars) and [[late-type-galaxies-and-stellar-populations|late-type galaxies]] (spirals and irregulars). This classification is not merely taxonomic; it reflects systematic variations in fundamental physical properties, including [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]], gas content, and [[galaxy-subcomponents|internal kinematics]]. Modern classifications increasingly refine this classical framework by accounting for internal perturbations, the morphological influence of active galactic nuclei (AGN), and the underlying role of dark matter-driven morphology in shaping the galaxy structure [Buta et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1102.0550B/abstract).

**Early-Type and Late-Type Systems**
Early-type galaxies, encompassing ellipticals (E) and lenticulars (S0), are characterized by smooth, ellipsoidal surface brightness distributions. They are predominantly supported by the random motions of their stars and contain largely old, red stellar populations with minimal cold gas or dust. Despite their structural simplicity, early-type galaxies adhere to remarkably tight scaling relations linking their size, surface brightness, and [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|velocity dispersion]].

Conversely, late-type galaxies possess a more complex [[primary-morphology|Primary morphology]]. They typically feature a rotationally-supported [[thin-disks|thin stellar disk]] containing spiral arms, alongside a central [[galaxy-subcomponents|spheroidal bulge]]. The spiral arms are active sites of star formation, highlighted by young blue stars, [[h-ii-galaxies|H II regions]], and dense molecular gas. Late-type systems obey their own dynamical scaling laws, most notably the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]], which establishes a tight correlation between a galaxy's luminosity $L$ and its maximum rotation velocity $V_{\rm max}$, commonly expressed as $L = A V_{\rm max}^{\alpha}$ where $\alpha \approx 2.5 - 4$ depending on the photometric band. Lenticular (S0) galaxies serve as a transitional class within this framework, exhibiting a smooth disk and central bulge but lacking prominent spiral arms or significant ongoing star formation. The relative abundance of these types is strongly governed by the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]], with dense cluster environments hosting predominantly early-type systems.

**Dwarf and Peculiar Galaxies**
While the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] effectively describes the majority of luminous local galaxies, it does not perfectly capture the extremes of the galaxy mass function or galaxies undergoing violent evolutionary phases. At the faint end, [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxies]] generally lack the regular structure of spirals or the smooth profiles of giant ellipticals, instead appearing as diffuse dwarf spheroidals or chaotic dwarf irregulars. Dedicated efforts, such as the Local Volume dwarf survey, systematically measure the structural parameters of these faint systems to test the universality of scaling laws, such as the [[z-l-relation|metallicity-luminosity relation]], in extreme low-density environments [Grebel et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..221..147G/abstract).

Furthermore, a subset of systems known as [[arp-atlas-of-peculiar-galaxies|peculiar galaxies]] defies standard classification. These galaxies exhibit highly asymmetric or distorted structures, such as tidal tails, shells, or multiple nuclei, and are typically the result of recent or ongoing major mergers. Observational evidence indicates that the morphological mix of galaxies is strongly redshift-dependent. At $z > 2$, an increasingly large fraction of massive galaxies are morphologically peculiar, undergoing rapid structural assembly before smoothly transforming into the modern [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]], a transition that primarily occurs between $1 < z < 2$ [Conselice et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..489C/abstract).

**Quantitative Structural Classification**
In the era of large-scale digital surveys, the subjective nature of visual [[galaxy-morphological-classification|Galaxy morphological classification]] has increasingly been supplemented by quantitative, automated structural indices [Wadadekar et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1201.2252W/abstract). Parametric approaches fit the surface brightness profiles using analytical forms like the [[structural-concentration-and-profiles|Sérsic profile]], objectively characterizing the [[logarithmic-slope|Logarithmic slope]] and effective radius. Meanwhile, non-parametric statistics, such as the [[cas-parameters|CAS parameters]] (concentration, asymmetry, clumpiness) and the Gini/$M_{20}$ coefficients, provide robust methodologies for quantifying structural disturbances and merger activity [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). These objective metrics are especially critical for classifying irregular high-redshift systems that predate the established [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]], providing a necessary link between a galaxy's visual form and its physical assembly history.

## Structural Quantifiers and Component Analysis

The transition from subjective visual classification to quantitative, automated morphology has been a major paradigm shift in modern extragalactic astronomy. To resolve the internal architecture of galaxies across large datasets, researchers rely on a combination of parametric modeling and non-parametric structural indices.

### Parametric Light-Profile Fitting

The most prevalent method for quantifying galaxy structure is parametric surface brightness modeling, commonly referred to as [[structural-concentration-and-profiles|Sérsic fitting]]. The Sérsic profile describes the projected surface brightness as a function of radius using an effective (half-light) radius and a Sérsic index ($n$) that governs the concentration of the profile. 

The Sérsic index varies systematically with galaxy mass and morphological type. Faint dwarf ellipticals typically exhibit shallow profiles with $n \sim 0.5$, whereas classical exponential disks have $n \approx 1$. Massive elliptical galaxies are highly concentrated, often approximating the $n = 4$ [[de-vaucouleurs-law|De Vaucouleurs law]], with the brightest ellipticals sometimes exceeding $n > 10$ [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

Because most galaxies are not simple monolithic structures, accurate modeling requires disentangling the light into distinct [[galaxy-subcomponents|Galaxy subcomponents]], such as a central bulge, a thin and [[thick-disks|thick disk]], and occasionally bars or stellar halos. The resulting bulge-to-disk ratio is a fundamental metric that correlates smoothly with the classical Hubble sequence. To robustly separate these components and break parameter degeneracies, modern approaches (such as the MegaMorph project) employ simultaneous multi-band 2D image fitting, which can also map radial color gradients within individual subcomponents [Bamford et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..442..479B/abstract), [Kennedy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...593A..84K/abstract).

### Non-Parametric Structural Indices

Parametric models often fail to capture the morphologies of irregular, clumpy, or merging galaxies, particularly at high redshifts where peculiar morphologies dominate the population. For these systems, model-independent non-parametric quantifiers are necessary [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). 

The most widely adopted framework is the [[cas-parameters|CAS parameters]] (Concentration, Asymmetry, and Clumpiness/Smoothness) [Bershady et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.269..183B/abstract). Together, these metrics act as high-level conceptual proxies; for instance, asymmetry strongly indicates ongoing major mergers or severe tidal disruptions, while clumpiness traces unmixed, patchy regions of active star formation. Additionally, the Gini coefficient ($G$) and the $M_{20}$ parameter are routinely used for automated classification to serve as structural proxies for ongoing merger activity and concentration without relying on assumed symmetric spatial profiles. The $G-M_{20}$ parameter space reliably separates normal Hubble types from peculiar and merging systems [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract).

### Evolutionary Context

These structural quantifiers are deeply connected to a galaxy's physical state and evolutionary history. For instance, a galaxy's structural parameters strongly correlate with its position relative to the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]]. As galaxies quench and evolve towards the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]], they exhibit a nearly monotonic trend toward higher Sérsic indices, smaller effective radii, and higher central stellar densities [Brennan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..619B/abstract). This structural transformation highlights the intertwined nature of mass assembly, morphological evolution, and the cessation of star formation.

## Integrated Stellar and Gaseous Properties

The integrated properties of galaxies collapse their complex internal kinematics and spatially resolved details into global metrics, such as total stellar mass, luminosity, and gas content. These integrated attributes form the basis for statistical studies of galaxy evolution and cosmology. The global distribution of optical, UV, and atomic gas [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|mass functions]] in the nearby Universe has been extensively mapped, providing a comprehensive baseline for these integrated metrics across a wide range of environments [Blanton et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ARA&A..47..159B/abstract).

A fundamental statistic characterizing the galaxy population is the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]], which describes the comoving number density of galaxies as a function of their luminosity. This distribution is most commonly modeled using a [[schechter-function|Schechter function]]:
$$ \phi(L) dL = \phi^* \left( \frac{L}{L^*} \right)^\alpha \exp\left( - \frac{L}{L^*} \right) \frac{dL}{L^*} $$
where $L^*$ is the characteristic luminosity corresponding to the "knee" of the function, $\phi^*$ is the normalization, and $\alpha$ is the faint-end logarithmic slope. The bright end of the luminosity function is strongly dominated by early-type ellipticals, while the faint end is heavily populated by dwarf irregulars in the field, as well as dwarf ellipticals in dense clusters [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Converting these integrated luminosities into total [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass]] typically requires resolving degeneracies between stellar age, metallicity, and dust extinction. Extinction laws can vary significantly across different environments; for example, interstellar dust properties in the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC) differ from those in the Milky Way, notably exhibiting a weaker 2200 Å bump and higher far-ultraviolet extinction [Clayton et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985ApJ...288..558C/abstract). Correcting for these complex effects is increasingly achieved via hierarchical Bayesian SED fitting applied to multi-wavelength survey data [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...16H/abstract).

Large-scale surveys have established a profound [[galactic-bimodality|Galactic Bimodality]] in both the [[color-magnitude-relation|Color-magnitude relation]] and specific star formation rate distributions [Lahav et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASA...21..404L/abstract). The global population broadly splits into a star-forming "blue cloud" and a quiescent [[red-sequence|Red sequence]], separated by a sparsely populated transition region known as the "green valley" [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). Notably, recent evidence suggests that the Milky Way and M31 may currently reside in this transitional green valley, undergoing a critical phase of evolution where their star formation is slowly extinguishing. This challenges the historical assumption that our galaxy is a perfectly typical spiral [Mutch et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...736...84M/abstract). The [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy (M31)]] acts as a premier high-resolution template for understanding external galaxy star formation histories and complex gas streams [Leahy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Univ....9..349L/abstract). In this context, the Milky Way serves as a benchmark luminous barred spiral with a distinct box/peanut bulge, acting as an essential template for near-field cosmology and the broader interpretation of integrated galaxy properties [Bland-Hawthorn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..529B/abstract).

For star-forming galaxies, the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] (SFR) scales tightly with total stellar mass, an empirical scaling relation formalized as the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] (SFMS). Normal disk galaxies on the SFMS exhibit specific star formation rates ($\mathrm{sSFR} \equiv \mathrm{SFR}/M_*$) of $\sim 0.1 \text{ Gyr}^{-1}$, corresponding to gas consumption timescales on the order of the Hubble time. Conversely, extreme starbursts can display sSFRs 10 to 100 times higher. As galaxies fall below the sequence and quench, their global SFRs plummet, a shift structurally accompanied by increasing central densities and more concentrated light profiles [Brennan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..619B/abstract). Additionally, compact remnants provide independent evolutionary constraints; simple methods to compute the [[white-dwarf-luminosity-function|White dwarf luminosity function]] have long been utilized as a powerful tool to probe overall Galactic evolution and reconstruct the historical star formation rate [Garcia-Berro et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996A&AS..117...13G/abstract).

The physical mechanisms driving galaxies off the SFMS—often termed "quenching"—remain a subject of active debate. While some models emphasize internal drivers like AGN feedback and secular bulge growth [Brennan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..619B/abstract), others argue for environmentally driven gas depletion followed by wind-driven mass loss, pointing to an unexpectedly large population of quiescent galaxies with intermediate star formation rates [Oemler et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844...45O/abstract).

Finally, the integrated gaseous properties of a galaxy serve as a fossil record of its accretion and evolutionary history. Total gas-phase [[metallicity|Metallicity]] tightly scales with stellar mass. The mass-metallicity relation serves as a fundamental constraint on the cosmic cycles of gas inflow, star formation, and subsequent supernova-driven outflows [Erb et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..265..147E/abstract). The interstellar medium itself structures star formation on varying scales; for example, observations of Galactic Planck cold cores reveal that high-mass star formation is often concentrated in clumps that exhibit systematically higher peak column densities toward the Galactic center compared to the outer Galaxy [Zahorecz et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...591A.105Z/abstract).

## Chemical Abundances and Stellar Populations

The chemical properties of a galaxy reflect the integrated history of gas that has been reprocessed by stars and exchanged with the intergalactic medium over cosmic time [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). This makes the determination of [[galaxy-stellar-age|Galaxy stellar age]] and [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] critical for reconstructing a system's [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and mass assembly. Spectroscopically, two distinct metallicities can be measured for a given galaxy: the gas-phase metallicity, derived from emission lines, which snapshots the current enrichment of the interstellar medium; and the stellar metallicity, derived from atmospheric absorption lines, which represents the time-averaged fossil record of prior stellar generations [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). In high-redshift star-forming galaxies, the mass-metallicity relation serves as a sensitive probe of the ongoing balance between gas accretion, star formation, and feedback-driven outflows [Erb et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..265..147E/abstract).

Spatially resolved distributions of [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] reveal systematic internal structures. [[negative-metallicity-gradients|Negative metallicity gradients]] and [[color-gradients|Color gradients]] are ubiquitous, though their physical origins depend on galaxy morphology and mass. In blue, low-Sérsic index ($n$) galaxies, these negative color gradients strongly support [[galactic-disk-evolution|inside-out disk growth]] models driven by continuous gas accretion [Kennedy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...593A..84K/abstract). In contrast, red, high-$n$ galaxies exhibit gradients consistent with late-time envelope accretion driven by dry minor mergers [Kennedy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...593A..84K/abstract). These [[abundance-gradients|Abundance gradients]] are typically steepest in the inner regions of the galaxy [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Deep mapping of the faint outer envelopes of [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] is frequently used to disentangle the fraction of stars formed *in situ* from those acquired via accretion [McDermid et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..290M/abstract). Within the Milky Way, these spatially resolved distributions correspond to at least six distinct 'types' of stellar populations: the [[thin-disks|thin disk]], [[thick-disks|thick disk]], bulge, stellar halo, accreted components, and theoretical traces of ancient [[massive-population-ii-galaxies|Population III stars]] [Gilmore et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASPC..374..209G/abstract). Recent results from the Pristine Inner Galaxy Survey (PIGS) have further characterized the orbital properties of the ancient, very metal-poor inner Milky Way, revealing a kinematic transition between a concentrated, rotating component and an extended, slow-rotating spheroidal population [Ardern-Arentsen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.3391A/abstract). 

Stellar chemical properties also deeply affect the production of interstellar dust. Comparing the infrared properties of asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stars across the Milky Way and the Magellanic Clouds shows distinct variations between O-rich and C-rich stars, notably highlighting a deficiency of thick dust shells around AGB stars in the metal-poor environment of the Small Magellanic Cloud (SMC) [Suh et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...891...43S/abstract).

Translating integrated luminosities and colors into physical stellar masses relies heavily on the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. Historically, the IMF has been treated as a universal function across different environments and redshifts [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). However, this assumption is now a subject of active debate. Recent dynamical models and detailed population synthesis analyses of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] present strong evidence for a non-universal IMF, indicating systematic variations linked to local physical conditions such as metallicity or central velocity dispersion [Zaritsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ISRAA2012E..12Z/abstract), [McDermid et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..290M/abstract). 

Ancient, discrete stellar systems like [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] provide a complementary fossil record extending back to the epoch of cosmic reionization [Bekki et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...626L..93B/abstract). Across a wide range of galaxy types, the total mass of a galactic globular cluster system is observed to scale linearly with the mass of its [[galaxy-halo-scales|host dark matter halo]] [Beasley et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020rfma.book..245B/abstract). Moreover, there is a strong observed correlation between the total globular cluster population ($N_{\rm GC}$) and the overall bulge mass (or dynamical mass) of host galaxies. However, the growth of the central supermassive black hole ($M_{\rm bh}$) over time is less similar from galaxy to galaxy and thus less predictable, arguing against a direct evolutionary link tightly coupling GC populations specifically to central nuclear growth [Harris et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.2117H/abstract). While these global scaling relations are robust, the specific enrichment histories of individual galaxies show significant diversity; for instance, the stellar halo of the Andromeda Galaxy is approximately $1 \text{ dex}$ more metal-rich than that of the Milky Way [Freeman et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..165..167F/abstract). Despite these large-scale variations in chemical evolution, localized stellar physics appears highly uniform. X-ray observations of young clusters in the outer Milky Way show coronal properties identical to those in the solar neighborhood, suggesting that small-scale stellar formation and coronal physics operate independently of the broader galactic environment [Caramazza et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...539A..74C/abstract).

## Fundamental Scaling Relations

Galaxies exhibit a variety of [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] that connect their structural, kinematic, and physical properties. Rather than populating parameter space randomly, galaxies adhere to tight empirical scaling laws that reflect the underlying physics of galaxy formation. These relations emerge from the virial theorem and the co-evolution of baryonic matter within dark matter halos [Navarro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ASSP...56..103N/abstract), [Zaritsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ISRAA2012E..12Z/abstract). Beyond these purely mass-driven or environment-driven models, secular evolution—an [[internal-galactic-structure|internal dynamical paradigm]] involving the slow, steady rearrangement of a galaxy's structure—plays a key role and contributes significantly to the emergence of observed galaxy scaling laws over a galaxy's lifetime [Zhang et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004IAUS..220..417Z/abstract).

For rotationally supported [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], the most prominent kinematic scaling law is the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]], which links a galaxy's total luminosity $L$ to its maximum rotation velocity $V_{\max}$. This relationship is typically parameterized as:
$$ L = A V_{\max}^\alpha $$
where the slope $\alpha$ varies between 2.5 and 4 depending on the observational waveband [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). At a fixed $V_{\max}$, the scatter in luminosity is remarkably tight, on the order of 20%. Extending this framework to account for gas mass yields the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]], which tightly connects the total baryonic mass to the rotation velocity. Interestingly, while these relations hold for the general population, individual galaxies can deviate significantly; the Milky Way, for instance, appears to be a $\sim 9.5$-sigma outlier in the related luminosity-velocity-radius (LVR) relation, possessing an unusually compact disk scale length compared to other spiral galaxies of similar mass [Licquia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..220L/abstract).

In contrast, dispersion-supported [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] populate a two-dimensional "Fundamental Plane" within the three-dimensional parameter space of effective radius $R_e$, central velocity dispersion $\sigma_e$, and surface brightness $\mu_e$ [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). While the existence of the Fundamental Plane is a natural consequence of virialized systems, there remains an active debate regarding the physical origin of its "tilt"—its measured deviation from pure virial expectations. The literature is currently divided on whether this tilt is primarily driven by systematic variations in the stellar initial mass function and mass-to-light ratios, or by structural non-homology among early-type galaxies, with no clear consensus yet established [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract), [Zaritsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ISRAA2012E..12Z/abstract).

Recent studies emphasize that these seemingly distinct kinematic relations can be unified. The [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] tightly correlates the observed radial acceleration traced by baryons with the total dynamical acceleration inferred from rotation curves or velocity dispersions. Combining galaxy data with Solar System data, this relation covers approximately 16 orders of magnitude in Newtonian baryonic acceleration [Lelli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102499L/abstract). This relation is remarkably consistent across both late-type and early-type galaxies. While these scaling laws emerge naturally in $\Lambda$CDM cosmological simulations due to the self-similar mass profiles of cold dark matter halos [Navarro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ASSP...56..103N/abstract), they were predicted *a priori* by Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), presenting an ongoing theoretical tension in interpreting the fundamental physical origins of these dynamics [Lelli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102499L/abstract).

Beyond kinematics, the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] dictates that the physical extent of a galaxy scales tightly with its stellar mass ($M_*$). Observations show a distinct break in the logarithmic slope of this relation: high-mass galaxies follow a steep scaling of $R_{1/2} \propto M_*^{0.54}$, whereas dwarf systems exhibit a shallower slope of $R_{1/2} \propto M_*^{0.3}$ [Chiosi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1206.2532C/abstract). Furthermore, the origin of dispersion in early-type galaxy sizes reveals distinct evolutionary pathways: the size variations in high-mass systems are heavily influenced by minor mergers and rotational dynamics, while low-mass systems follow fundamentally different structural mechanisms [Lee et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...762L...4L/abstract). 

The [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] links the instantaneous star formation rate (SFR) to $M_*$ in star-forming galaxies. A galaxy's position relative to this sequence correlates strongly with its structural parameters. As galaxies quench and evolve off the sequence—dropping to lower SFRs—they exhibit a monotonic trend towards higher Sérsic indices, smaller effective radii, and higher stellar mass densities, reflecting the intertwined nature of structural transformation and the cessation of star formation [Brennan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..619B/abstract).

Finally, fundamental scaling relations extend beyond individual galaxies to the scales of entire galaxy clusters. These macroscopic scaling laws intimately link a cluster's total mass to specific multi-wavelength observables, such as X-ray luminosity and temperature, as well as the amplitude of the Sunyaev-Zeldovich (SZ) effect [Giodini et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013SSRv..177..247G/abstract).

## Environmental Influences and Spatial Clustering

The physical properties of a galaxy are deeply intertwined with its spatial distribution and local [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]]. This connection manifests most prominently in the classical [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]], which demonstrates that high-density environments, such as rich clusters, are overwhelmingly populated by early-type (elliptical and lenticular) galaxies with suppressed star formation, whereas low-density field regions are dominated by gas-rich, late-type spirals. For instance, galaxies residing in [[mass-assembly-galaxy-group-catalogue|groups]] also exhibit a measurable decrement in gas-phase metallicity compared to field galaxies of similar mass, reflecting the complex effects of environmental gas processing [Lara-López et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AN....334..466L/abstract). Historically, surveys spanning diverse density regimes have established that galaxies in high-density environments exhibit notable H I deficiency, alongside a higher fraction of barred spirals and concentrated starburst activity [Maia et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJS...93..425M/abstract).

While this relation is well established observationally, there is ongoing debate regarding the true causal drivers of environmental transformation. Some research argues that a galaxy's [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] is the primary property sensitive to environmental density, suggesting that morphological changes may be a secondary consequence of quenching rather than the fundamental environmentally driven variable [Hogg et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf...41H/abstract). Supporting this view, spatial clustering measurements indicate that optical colors (e.g., $u-r$ and $g-r$) are the strongest indicators of local environment, correlating more tightly with density than stellar mass or specific star formation rate [Joy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250510429J/abstract). Beyond simple two-point correlation functions, high-order clustering statistics such as the void probability function (VPF) provide deeper insights. These metrics reveal that bright galaxies are more strongly clustered than faint ones at high order, even when their two-point correlation functions appear similar [Maurogordato et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...369...30M/abstract). Furthermore, while environment dictates broad trends, localized interactions are potent star formation triggers. Analyses of starburst galaxies show that these events frequently involve near neighbors within 20 kpc, although a significant fraction of starbursts can still be internally driven without obvious environmental triggers [Owers et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.381..494O/abstract).

To understand environmental influences, it is necessary to distinguish between [[central-galaxy-properties-and-evolution|Central Galaxy Properties and Evolution]] and the properties of [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]]. Central galaxies reside at the bottom of the host dark matter halo's potential well, growing primarily through continuous gas accretion and mergers. Satellite galaxies, conversely, are subjected to harsh external forces upon falling into a larger halo. Mechanisms such as ram-pressure stripping, tidal stripping, and strangulation systematically remove or heat the satellite's cold gas reservoir. This accelerates quenching, effectively driving the satellite off the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] and onto the quiescent [[red-sequence|Red sequence]]. The Milky Way's own satellite system provides a crucial laboratory for these dynamics; for example, the fossil information preserved in the stellar halo of the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC) strictly constrains the timing of its accretion and its complex interaction history with the Milky Way [Bekki et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2359B/abstract). The broader spatial distribution of these satellite galaxies provides critical cosmological tests; for instance, the existence of a 'Vast Polar Structure' (VPOS) of satellite galaxies orbiting the Milky Way has sparked debate over whether such anisotropic arrangements constrain the concentration or formation time of host dark matter halos, or if they pose a deeper challenge to standard galaxy formation models [Pawlowski et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017sf2a.conf..189P/abstract).

Beyond simple environmental density, galaxy evolution is subject to [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|assembly bias]]—the dependence of galaxy properties on the assembly history of their dark matter halos. For central galaxies, simulations show that stellar mass $M_*$ exhibits a tighter correlation with the halo's peak maximum circular velocity, $V_{\rm peak}$, than with its present-day mass, $M_h$. While theoretical models explore these connections, applying such models to actual local clustering measurements reveals that when controlled for $V_{\rm peak}$, the correlation between a galaxy's star formation history (or specific star formation rate) and its halo formation time is actually consistent with being weak [Xu et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.2739X/abstract).

The extreme ends of the density spectrum provide robust tests for these evolutionary models. In [[galaxy-density-fields|cosmic voids]], galaxies are predominantly disk-dominated and star-forming, yet their absolute star formation rates remain low (typically $<1\ M_\odot\,\rm{yr}^{-1}$), reflecting the lack of major accretion events [Beygu et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..308..600B/abstract). In contrast, within massive clusters, the [[galaxy-cluster-dynamical-state|Galaxy Cluster Dynamical State]] leaves a subtle but measurable imprint on the member galaxies. While fundamental scaling relations—such as the color-magnitude and mass-size relations—are resilient and remain unchanged between relaxed and disturbed clusters, dynamical activity significantly alters the higher-order statistical moments of galaxy properties. This introduces asymmetric, heavier tails into the property distributions of low-mass and red sequence galaxies, reflecting recent pre-processing and dynamic interactions [Véliz Astudillo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250413337V/abstract). 

While environment dictates broad morphological trends, certain extreme phenomena appear more isolated from local density. For example, Type I quasars at low redshift have been found to possess similar neighbor properties—in terms of mass, morphology, and star formation—to inactive galaxies, suggesting that such nuclear activity is largely driven by internal secular processes rather than immediate environmental interactions [Stone et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946..116S/abstract). Furthermore, environmental dependencies are not strictly a late-time phenomenon; they are established early in cosmic history. Analyses utilizing marked correlation functions at high redshift ($z \sim 3-5$) reveal that the UV magnitude and color of Lyman-break galaxies correlate strongly with their environment, extending to physical scales well beyond typical halo radii [Mons et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241212573M/abstract). Interestingly, at these early epochs, stellar mass and star formation rate exhibit a comparatively weak environmental dependence, indicating that the mechanisms driving the morphology-density relation evolve substantially over cosmic time [Mons et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241212573M/abstract).

## Cosmic Evolution and Assembly History

The transformation of galaxy properties across cosmic time reflects the complex interplay of hierarchical mass assembly, gas accretion, and environmental quenching. The [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]] of the Universe is most commonly characterized by the global star formation rate density, $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$. Observational constraints utilizing deep multi-wavelength surveys indicate that $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$ peaked during "cosmic noon" between $1 < z < 2$ and has subsequently declined by roughly an order of magnitude down to $z \sim 0$ [Lacey et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997seim.proc..307L/abstract). 

During these early epochs of peak star formation, the familiar [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] was largely absent. Studies of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] show that galaxies at $z > 3$ were predominantly compact, dynamically unrelaxed, and morphologically peculiar systems [Conselice et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..489C/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). The most rapid period of structural transformation occurred between $1 < z < 2$, an era that marks the definitive assembly of mature [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] and the emergence of massive [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] [Conselice et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..319..489C/abstract).

A defining feature of this [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|Galaxy and Universe evolution]] is the profound size growth of galaxies over time. At a fixed stellar mass, early galaxies at $z \sim 2-3$ are observed to be a factor of $2$ to $5$ smaller in effective radius than their local counterparts [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). For massive galaxies, this physical size evolution and mass assembly from $z=3$ to $z=1$ is understood to be driven roughly equally by hierarchical merging and continuous [[gas-fraction|cold gas accretion]] [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract). In this process, hierarchical merging plays a pivotal role, characterized by distinct evolutionary impacts depending on the gas content of the progenitors. The dominance epochs of 'wet' (gas-rich) mergers, which trigger intense starbursts, versus 'dry' (gas-poor) and mixed mergers directly sculpt the evolving mass function and structural properties of galaxies across cosmic time [Lin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..423..329L/abstract). However, there remains an ongoing tension in the literature: current $\Lambda$CDM cosmological simulations still exhibit significant discrepancies with observations regarding the exact abundances of massive galaxies and their specific merger histories at high redshifts [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract). 

As the Universe expanded and large-scale structures virialized, the [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]] began to exert a dominating influence on galaxy populations, accelerating the transition of galaxies from the active star-forming main sequence to the quiescent [[red-sequence|Red sequence]]. A classic hallmark of this [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]] in dense environments is the [[butcher-oemler-effect|Butcher-Oemler effect]]. Observations of rich clusters at intermediate redshifts ($0.3 \lesssim z \lesssim 0.5$) reveal a dramatically higher fraction of blue, actively star-forming, and morphologically disturbed spiral galaxies than is observed in modern, early-type-dominated local clusters [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). 

While the [[butcher-oemler-effect|Butcher-Oemler effect]] demonstrates clear evidence of environmental quenching over the last several gigayears, the broader mechanism for shutting down star formation is still contested. The precise physical balance between internal, secular processes (such as AGN feedback) and external, environmentally driven mechanisms (such as ram-pressure stripping and minor mergers) responsible for transitioning galaxies into permanent quiescence remains an unresolved debate [Brennan et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..619B/abstract).

## Advanced Methodologies and Future Directions

The study of galaxy evolution is undergoing a fundamental paradigm shift, moving away from treating galaxies as unresolved point sources toward leveraging massive data streams from modern [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Galaxy Surveys and Catalogs]]. This transition is heavily driven by the deployment of [[spatially-resolved-galaxy-analysis|integral field spectroscopy (IFS)]], the adoption of [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]], and the application of machine learning to disentangle complex physical processes.

Historically, global magnitudes and single-fiber spectra dominated the field, but they often obscured critical internal structures. The advent of large-scale IFS surveys has catalyzed a shift toward the analysis of [[spatially-resolved-stellar-populations|Spatially resolved stellar populations]] and kinematics [Sánchez et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021RMxAA..57....3S/abstract). For example, spatially and spectrally resolved observations of massive star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 2$ can now map kinematics and star formation on scales of $1$ to $5$ kpc [Förster Schreiber et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Msngr.145...39F/abstract). In the local Universe, these resolved measurements have revealed the ubiquity of strong negative color gradients—supporting inside-out growth models for blue, low-Sérsic index galaxies [Kennedy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...593A..84K/abstract). Spatially resolving distinct galactic components is also proving crucial for understanding mass assembly histories and testing the universality of the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] across different galactic environments [McDermid et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..290M/abstract).

As observational datasets grow, deriving physical parameters such as stellar mass ($M_*$), star formation rate (SFR), stellar age, and metallicity ($Z$) requires navigating highly degenerate parameter spaces. [[traditional-sed-fitting-techniques|Traditional spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting]] is increasingly being replaced by [[hierarchical-bayesian-inference|Hierarchical Bayesian inference]]. For example, the PROVABGS framework for the [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI)]] utilizes Bayesian SED modeling to infer full posterior distributions of galaxy properties, demonstrating that the joint analysis of spectra and photometry significantly mitigates model priors and improves parameter constraints [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...16H/abstract). Similar hierarchical Bayesian techniques have been successfully applied to 3D extinction mapping to simultaneously determine distance-extinction relationships and individual stellar properties with high precision [Sale et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.427.2119S/abstract). Theoretical frameworks are simultaneously advancing alongside these observational techniques; phenomenological modeling utilizing continuity equations is increasingly employed to self-consistently link star formation quenching, merging histories, and chemical evolution, offering a powerful complementary approach to hydrodynamical simulations [Lilly et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..141L/abstract).

To process the unprecedented volume of imaging and spectroscopic data, automated methodologies and machine learning models are becoming indispensable. Historically, multiparametric virtual instruments like the MIGALE project were developed to systematically analyze morphological and kinematic evolution across large cosmological fields [Theureau et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph..9831T/abstract). [[galaxy-manifold|Principal Component Analysis (PCA)]] has long been used to derive continuous, robust metrics of spectral type—such as the $\eta$ parameter in the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]]—which tightly correlate with physical properties [Madgwick et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10220M/abstract). More recently, deep neural networks have been developed to predict physical properties directly from pixel-level image cutouts. A historic challenge in astronomical machine learning has been the "black box" nature of deep learning architectures; however, novel models like the Sparse Feature Network (SFNet) address this by producing interpretable features. SFNets can linearly combine these extracted features to accurately estimate gas-phase metallicities and optical emission line ratios without sacrificing performance [Wu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250100089W/abstract).

Looking forward, the synthesis of spatially resolved data, robust Bayesian modeling, and interpretable machine learning will be vital for addressing unresolved tensions in galaxy formation. Current debates—such as discrepancies between cosmological simulations and the observed abundances of massive galaxies at high redshift [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract), or whether the IMF systematically varies in massive early-type systems [McDermid et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..290M/abstract)—will rely heavily on these advanced analytical frameworks to extract subtle physical truths from next-generation surveys. Significant progress is also being made in bridging the gap between theoretical frameworks and these nuanced observations. For instance, modern cosmological hydrodynamical models, such as the Magneticum Pathfinder simulation suite, have successfully and self-consistently reproduced 28 distinct galaxy scaling relations spanning from redshift $z=4$ to the present day [Dolag et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250401061D/abstract).
