**Galactic bars** are elongated, [[non-axisymmetric-structures|Non-axisymmetric structures]] comprised of stars, gas, and dust that extend across the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|central regions]] of a majority of local [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]. They arise primarily from spontaneous global $m=2$ dynamical instabilities within rotationally supported stellar disks. While classic textbook models, such as the Ostriker-Peebles criterion, posited that massive dark matter halos and central [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] naturally stabilize disks against these modes, modern [[galactic-disk-structure-and-dynamics|dynamic models]] reveal a more complex interaction. Rather than strictly suppressing instability, dynamically responsive ("live") dark matter halos actively absorb angular momentum emitted by the bar at orbital resonances, a process that can drive the instability and strengthen the bar over time [Athanassoula et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book..305A/abstract). Furthermore, while isolated disks will inevitably form bars once they accrue sufficient stellar mass, external environmental perturbations, such as galaxy flybys or minor mergers, can also prematurely catalyze their formation in the form of [[tidally-induced-bars|Tidally induced bars]].

Once established, bars act as the primary engines of the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]. By exerting strong [[galactic-torques|Galactic torques]], bars systematically redistribute angular momentum between the disk and the halo. This process drives cold interstellar medium (ISM) gas inward along characteristic shocks and leading dust lanes, a dynamic accurately supported by modern observations of non-axisymmetric torques [Yu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...666A.175Y/abstract). This massive influx of material toward the Nuclear Regions of Galaxies triggers intense nuclear starbursts and is theorized to aid in fueling active galactic nuclei, although overcoming the final parsec-scale angular momentum barrier is highly complex and debated. Bars are structurally robust features; while early models suggested they could be easily destroyed by the growth of central supermassive black holes, high-resolution simulations demonstrate that primary bars can survive massive central mass concentrations [Shen et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...604..614S/abstract). Over time, however, thin bars frequently succumb to a rapid vertical buckling instability [Raha et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991Natur.352..411R/abstract), a violent dynamical phase that thickens their inner regions into the [[x-p-instability|boxy, peanut-shaped, or X-shaped]] [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] ubiquitous in massive galaxies.

Despite their prevalence, the exact evolutionary trajectory of galactic bars remains the subject of intense contemporary research, particularly regarding their interaction with dark matter and their timeline in early cosmic environments. A major open question in galactic dynamics is the "pattern speed problem." Standard $\Lambda$CDM models predict that bars should continuously lose angular momentum to the dark matter halo via dynamical friction, rapidly slowing their rotation over cosmic time. However, empirical measurements of the [[dimensionless-bar-rotation-parameter|Dimensionless bar rotation parameter]] reveal that observed bars are overwhelmingly "fast," creating a massive statistical tension with predictions from cosmological hydrodynamical simulations. Additionally, recent observations by the JWST have unexpectedly identified mature, strongly barred galaxies at high redshifts ($z > 3$). The existence of dynamically cold, barred disks so early in the Universe directly challenges previous theoretical assumptions about early cosmological turbulence and [[galactic-disk-evolution|disk settling]], suggesting that the non-linear processes governing bar formation operate on much faster timescales than previously understood.

## Dynamical Stability and Formation Mechanisms

The formation of galactic bars is fundamentally driven by the susceptibility of a rotationally supported disk to global, [[non-axisymmetric-structures|non-axisymmetric]] $m=2$ instabilities. The theoretical understanding of these instabilities has evolved significantly, particularly regarding the competing roles of stellar mass, gas fraction, and the larger galactic environment. 

Historically, simple applications of the Ostriker-Peebles criterion suggested that the presence of massive dark matter halos and [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] should stabilize a disk against bar-forming modes. However, early cosmological hydrodynamical simulations successfully demonstrated that both strong, bulge-influenced bars and shorter, bulge-less bars can form naturally within a $\Lambda$CDM framework [Scannapieco et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425L..10S/abstract). Modern $N$-body simulations have further demonstrated that a static view of stability is incomplete. A "live," dynamically responsive dark matter halo does not suppress bar formation; rather, it actively participates in the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] by absorbing angular momentum at orbital resonances, thereby strengthening the bar over time [Athanassoula et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005NYASA1045..168A/abstract), [Athanassoula et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013seg..book..305A/abstract). Furthermore, while large stellar masses are typically required, bars can also form in dark-matter-dominated dwarf galaxies, where the stellar structure dynamically reflects the underlying triaxial potential of the dark matter halo [Marasco et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.2168M/abstract).

Internally, bar formation is driven by repeated swing amplification of density perturbations, a process regulated by the Toomre stability parameter ($Q_T$) and the dimensionless wavelength of the perturbation [Jang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250907353J/abstract). Morphologically, the concentration, asymmetry, and clumpiness (CAS) parameters serve as indicators of a disk's initial Toomre $Q$ state, with $Q < 1$ disks developing clumps that can eventually lead to lopsided barred morphologies [Chantavat et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...965...77C/abstract). The onset of the instability is highly sensitive to both the disk's kinematics and its [[stellar-and-galactic-core-structure|central mass concentration]] (CMC). Recent numerical models propose a unified empirical condition for bar formation in isolated disks:
$$ \left(\frac{Q_{T,\min }}{1.2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\mathrm{CMC}}{0.05}\right)^2 \lesssim 1 $$
where $Q_{T,\min}$ is the minimum Toomre parameter of the disk [Jang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942..106J/abstract). Additionally, massive classical bulges can suppress bar modes if the bulge-to-disk radial force ratio, evaluated at the disk scale length, exceeds $F_b/F_d > 0.35$ [Kataria et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180100181K/abstract). This dynamical threshold is supported by observational surveys demonstrating that bar fractions are significantly higher ($\approx 70\%$) in disk-dominated galaxies compared to those with prominent bulges ($\approx 40\%$) [Barazza et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...675.1194B/abstract). Observationally, bar prevalence heavily depends on the observed wavelength: at $z \approx 0$, the deprojected bar fraction is significantly higher in the near-infrared ($\approx 60\%$) compared to the optical ($\approx 44\%$), with most bars having a size-to-disk ratio ($a_{\rm bar}/R_{25}$) between $0.1$ and $0.5$ [Marinova et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...659.1176M/abstract). The baryonic composition of the disk also dictates its stability; high gas fractions and elevated turbulence tend to dynamically heat the disk, delaying or entirely inhibiting bar formation [Cheung et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...779..162C/abstract). Consequently, the fraction of low surface brightness (LSB) galaxies hosting strong bars is significantly lower than in high surface brightness counterparts, likely due to correlations between surface brightness, gas richness, and halo spin [Cervantes Sodi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...847...37C/abstract).

Once formed, large-scale stellar bars are structurally robust. Observational surveys such as S$^4$G have found that bars in early-type galaxies are typically longer and exhibit larger density amplitudes than those in intermediate-type galaxies, supporting the concept of secular bar growth over a Hubble time [Díaz-García et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...587A.160D/abstract). Furthermore, numerical models distinguish between bars in early-type galaxies, which exhibit flat density profiles and high pattern speeds, and those in late-type galaxies, which show exponential profiles and rotate more slowly [Combes et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993A&A...271..391C/abstract). Early theoretical work proposed that the secular accumulation of gas or the growth of a supermassive black hole could quickly destroy a bar by scattering its supporting orbits. However, high-resolution studies have shown that a central mass must reach several percent of the total disk mass to completely dissolve a primary bar—a threshold rarely met in nature [Shen et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...604..614S/abstract). In contrast, short, secondary inner bars (see [[nested-bars|Nested bars]]) are considerably more fragile and can be destroyed by central black holes comprising just $\sim 0.1\%$ to $0.2\%$ of the host galaxy's stellar mass [Du et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844L..15D/abstract).

While spontaneous internal instability is considered inevitable once a dynamically cold disk acquires sufficient stellar mass [Zana et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.2608Z/abstract), bars can also be triggered externally by tidal perturbations. [[tidally-induced-bars|Tidally induced bars]] are effectively generated during prograde flybys and mergers, whereas retrograde encounters generally fail to excite strong bar modes [Łokas et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...857....6L/abstract). For Milky Way-like target galaxies, an interacting satellite must typically exceed a mass of $3 \times 10^9 M_\odot$ to efficiently trigger a bar [Zhou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988..205Z/abstract). The importance of external triggers is corroborated by observations showing that the fraction of barred disk galaxies is significantly enhanced in interacting cluster environments compared to isolated field populations [Yoon et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NatAs...3..844Y/abstract). Ultimately, the literature suggests a complex interplay: while interactions can accelerate bar formation, internal secular processes ensure that most massive disk galaxies will eventually form bars independently.

## Tidally Induced Bars and Environmental Triggers

While galactic bars frequently form spontaneously through internal global disk instabilities during the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]], external environmental perturbations act as a secondary, powerful catalyst. The formation of [[tidally-induced-bars|Tidally induced bars]] occurs when interactions—such as galaxy flybys, minor mergers, or cluster-cluster collisions—exert a non-axisymmetric tidal torque on an otherwise stable stellar disk.

High-resolution $N$-body simulations demonstrate that the efficacy of flyby interactions depends strictly on the orbital geometry. Encounters on prograde orbits strongly couple with the rotational kinematics of the target disk, efficiently transferring angular momentum to the dark matter halo and rapidly inducing strong bars [Łokas et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...857....6L/abstract). Conversely, retrograde encounters produce negligible morphological changes [Łokas et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...857....6L/abstract). On larger scales, the host environment heavily influences the likelihood of harboring a bar. Observations of galaxy clusters at $z \approx 0.015-0.060$ reveal that large-scale cluster-cluster interactions dramatically increase the barred disk-dominated galaxy fraction from $\approx 27\%$ in relaxed clusters to $\approx 42\%$ in interacting systems [Yoon et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NatAs...3..844Y/abstract). Within individual relaxed clusters, simulated bar fractions and strengths are mildly decreasing functions of the clustercentric radius, indicating that repeated tidal forces near the dense cluster core trigger bar formation more frequently than in the cluster outskirts [Łokas et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...826..227L/abstract).

Bars triggered in dense environments often exhibit distinct physical properties compared to their isolated counterparts. Observational surveys indicate that bars in high-density regions are significantly shorter than those in the field [Cuomo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250923460C/abstract) and tend to rotate more slowly. Statistically, bars in high-density environments exhibit a median [[dimensionless-bar-rotation-parameter|Dimensionless bar rotation parameter]] of $\mathcal{R} = 1.67$, placing them firmly in the "slow" regime ($\mathcal{R} > 1.4$), compared to $\mathcal{R} = 1.37$ for bars in low-density environments [Puczek et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251102054P/abstract). Interestingly, statistical surveys of field galaxies find that bar properties—such as length and strength—show no significant dependence on local galaxy density [Aguerri et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...495..491A/abstract), emphasizing that intrinsic processes likely dominate once isolated from extreme cluster environments. Conversely, while interactions can form bars, they can also disrupt them; simulations from TNG50 indicate that while most bars are stable once formed, approximately $5\%$ of barred galaxies at $z=1$ lose their structure by $z=0$ due to tidal interactions in crowded environments [Rosas-Guevara et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...684A.179R/abstract).

Despite these general trends, the literature contains notable ambiguities regarding whether tidal interactions are strictly necessary to form bars in certain disks, or if they merely modulate an inevitable internal process. Some cosmological zoom-in simulations suggest that once a disk has accreted sufficient mass, bar formation is inevitable, and external perturbations like late minor mergers may actually heat the disk and delay the bar's emergence rather than trigger it [Zana et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.2608Z/abstract). The role of interactions also appears highly dependent on the host galaxy's luminosity and dynamical state. In lower-mass regimes, bars in cluster dwarf galaxies are generally shorter and weaker than those in massive galaxies, potentially due to tidal formation mechanisms or high central dark matter fractions [Cuomo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.52711218C/abstract). Furthermore, in tidally perturbed gas-rich dwarf galaxies, the gaseous component can remain distinctly axisymmetric and decoupled from the potential of the newly formed stellar bar [Gajda et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...868..100G/abstract). While massive, dynamically cold disks can efficiently channel tidal perturbations into a bar mode, fainter disks may undergo severe tidal heating that inhibits bar formation entirely [Méndez-Abreu et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761L...6M/abstract). Recent controlled simulations suggest that the comparatively slow rotation of tidally induced bars might be a byproduct of the initial kinematic "hotness" required to keep the host disk stable in isolation, rather than a direct consequence of the tidal formation mechanism itself [Zheng et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...979...60Z/abstract).

## Bar Kinematics and the Pattern Speed Problem

The kinematic state of a galactic bar is fundamentally characterized by its pattern speed, $\Omega_p$, and the resulting corotation resonance radius, $R_{\rm CR}$, where the stars in the disk co-rotate with the bar pattern. Observationally pinpointing these resonances can be complex; for example, while photometric "dark gaps" in the surface brightness of barred galaxies were once assumed to mark corotation, they are actually morphological indicators of the 4:1 ultraharmonic resonance [Krishnarao et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929..112K/abstract). To quantify how rapidly a bar rotates relative to its size, astronomers rely on the [[dimensionless-bar-rotation-parameter|Dimensionless bar rotation parameter]], defined as:
$$ \mathcal{R} = \frac{R_{\rm CR}}{R_{\rm bar}} $$
where $R_{\rm bar}$ is the semi-major axis of the bar. Dynamically, bars are classified into two regimes: "fast" bars have $\mathcal{R} \le 1.4$, placing the corotation resonance just outside the ends of the bar, while "slow" bars have $\mathcal{R} > 1.4$ [Font et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835..279F/abstract). 

Observational determinations of $\Omega_p$ are most robustly carried out using the Tremaine-Weinberg method, a model-independent technique tracking the surface brightness and line-of-sight velocity of the stellar component, frequently applied to integral field spectroscopy data. These observations reveal that the vast majority of bars in the local Universe are dynamically fast [Gerssen et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..275..197G/abstract), [Lee et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926...58L/abstract). The BaLROG project utilizes integral field spectroscopy to show that stronger bars correlate with distinct angular momentum 'dips' at roughly $0.2$ bar lengths, reflecting enhanced orbit mixing [Seidel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451..936S/abstract). Additionally, higher-order moments of the line-of-sight velocity distribution, such as skewness and kurtosis, provide robust kinematic signatures that confirm the presence and orientation of the [[alard-bar|Milky Way's bar]] [Palicio et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.1231P/abstract).

This empirical consensus stands in stark contrast to the theoretical predictions of standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology, creating a major challenge for models of the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]. In a dark matter-dominated universe, a massive, rotating stellar bar should experience significant dynamical friction against the surrounding dark matter halo. Over time, the bar transfers angular momentum outward to the halo via [[galactic-torques|Galactic torques]]. Theory dictates that this angular momentum loss must systematically decrease the pattern speed, inevitably driving mature bars into the slow regime [Algorry et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.1054A/abstract). The rate of this angular momentum exchange is heavily modulated by the disk's vertical structure; specifically, vertical disc thickness ($h_z/R_d$) plays a dominant role in bar evolution. Thinner discs produce bars that rotate more quickly and lose significantly less angular momentum to dynamical friction [Klypin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1027K/abstract). High-resolution cosmological hydrodynamical simulations, such as IllustrisTNG and EAGLE, overwhelmingly produce slow bars. This creates a severe statistical tension with observations, measured as high as a $13.56\sigma$ discrepancy in the case of the TNG100 simulation [Roshan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508..926R/abstract).

The severity of this "fast bar" tension depends on the precise coupling between the baryonic disk and the dark matter halo. Simulations indicate that baryon-dominated galaxies host faster bars because the relative dearth of central dark matter reduces the efficacy of dynamical friction [Merrow et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260120941M/abstract). Furthermore, the angular momentum exchange is highly complex; stellar bars can induce the formation of co-rotating "dark bars" (triaxial overdensities in the dark matter halo) that shadow the baryonic structure, absorbing angular momentum and altering the expected torque [Ash et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976..189A/abstract). In extreme theoretical scenarios, a stellar bar interacting with a counter-rotating dark matter halo could slow to zero rotation and even 'flip' its rotation direction entirely [Collier et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230506383C/abstract).

Ultimately, the ambiguity in resolving the pattern speed problem has led to competing interpretations. While many researchers look to refine the subgrid physics and baryonic feedback in cosmological simulations to produce more realistic, baryon-dominated galactic centers, others cite the fast bar tension as direct evidence against cold dark matter. Under alternative paradigms such as Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), galaxies lack the massive dark matter halos responsible for dynamical friction, naturally bypassing the theoretical expectation of slow bars [Banik et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Symm...14.1331B/abstract).

## Drivers of Secular Evolution: Gas Inflow and Feedback

Galactic bars are fundamental drivers of the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]], systematically reorganizing the distribution of mass and angular momentum within galactic disks over cosmological timescales. Through non-axisymmetric [[galactic-torques|Galactic torques]], bars induce strong shocks in the interstellar medium, causing gas to lose angular momentum along characteristic leading dust lanes. This material is then funneled inward toward the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]] [Sellwood et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract), [Yu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...666A.175Y/abstract). This inflow process can be highly efficient; for instance, observations of the barred spiral NGC 1097 indicate a bar-driven mass inflow rate of $\dot{M} \approx 3.0 \pm 2.1\ M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$, which is sufficient to sustain its active [[ring-galaxies|circumnuclear starburst ring]] [Sormani et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.2918S/abstract). Statistically, this connection is robust: observational correlations reveal that roughly $71\%$ of starburst galaxies host a strong bar with an axis ratio less than $0.6$ [Martin et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995AJ....109.2428M/abstract). However, while bars successfully transport gas to the central regions, strong bar-driven shear and turbulence simultaneously inhibit *in situ* star formation along the bar arms themselves [Fraser-McKelvie et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.495.4158F/abstract), [Kim et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968...87K/abstract), [Géron et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...973..129G/abstract). Moreover, this transport efficiency is highly dependent on the structure's prominence; while strong bars drive efficient inward gas funneling as seen in equivalent width EW(H$\alpha$) structures, weakly barred systems often lack this pattern, suggesting a limited dynamical influence on gas transport [Magnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251210164M/abstract). 

A major unresolved question in galactic dynamics is the extent to which this bar-driven gas inflow contributes to AGN fueling. While extensive historical observational surveys suggested that the average nuclear accretion rates and X-ray luminosities in barred galaxies are statistically indistinguishable from those in unbarred systems [Ho et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...487..591H/abstract), [Cisternas et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...802..137C/abstract), [Goulding et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...843..135G/abstract), recent studies introduce critical nuance. Strong bars are, in fact, correlated with enhanced AGN activity and higher fractions of powerful AGNs in certain environmental densities [Marels et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250523958M/abstract). Furthermore, the relationship may be regulatory; rather than a strictly one-way fueling mechanism, the growth of the central supermassive black hole can alter the central dynamical potential and modify the bar's shape over time [Zee et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...949...91Z/abstract). Overcoming the angular momentum barrier to drive gas to the final parsec scales remains widely debated. Some theories propose it requires dynamically decoupled [[nested-bars|Nested bars]] [Shlosman et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..275..231S/abstract); however, direct observational studies on nuclear dust morphology have explicitly ruled out strong nuclear bars as the primary Seyfert fueling mechanism, suggesting the necessity of alternative structures like nuclear spirals [Regan et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999AJ....117.2676R/abstract). Simulations demonstrate that central mass concentrations—such as a supermassive black hole growing to $\sim 0.1\%-0.2\%$ of the host galaxy's stellar mass—can dynamically disrupt and destroy short inner bars, potentially severing the inner fueling mechanism entirely [Du et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844L..15D/abstract).

Beyond their hydrodynamic effects, bars drive the radial migration of both stars and gas, profoundly impacting the chemical structure of the disk. Structurally, statistical evidence shows that stellar bars actively drive spiral density waves locally within approximately $1.6$ bar radii, tightly coupling the bar's kinematics to the surrounding spiral arms [Salo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...715L..56S/abstract). By trapping stars in resonant orbits and interacting with these spiral arms, bars efficiently mix stellar populations across large galactocentric radii. On a global scale, barred galaxies exhibit older, more metal-rich stellar populations and lower H I gas fractions compared to unbarred galaxies, suggesting a link between bar presence and the early cessation of star formation [Fraser-McKelvie et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.1116F/abstract). Spatially resolved integral field spectroscopy reveals that stellar populations within the bars themselves exhibit flatter metallicity gradients [Fraser-McKelvie et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488L...6F/abstract) and are approximately $0.03$ dex more metal-rich than the surrounding disk at equivalent radii [Neumann et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.534.2438N/abstract), though their relative stellar ages show a variety of contrasts with no consistent trend [Neumann et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.534.2438N/abstract). 

Despite clear evidence of radial migration, the net effect of bars on gas-phase metallicity gradients is complex. Early theoretical models and foundational observations indicate that bars can significantly flatten global stellar and gaseous abundance gradients within just a few dynamical timescales [Friedli et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...430L.105F/abstract). However, subsequent observations reveal a diversity of fine-scale profiles. Galaxies exhibit varied "shallow-steep" and "steep-shallow" metallicity gradient breaks near the bar ends, indicating that the final chemical profile depends sensitively on the interplay between the bar's evolutionary stage, local star formation efficiency, and overlapping spiral arm resonances [Chen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4801C/abstract), [Marques et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202651M/abstract).

## Vertical Structure and the Buckling Instability

Following their initial formation, stellar bars frequently undergo a violent vertical bending known as the buckling instability [Raha et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991Natur.352..411R/abstract), representing a dramatic phase in the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]. During this rapid dynamical event, which typically lasts $<1$ Gyr, the inner portion of the vertically thin bar buckles out of the disk plane, ultimately thickening into a three-dimensional structure [Xiang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909..125X/abstract). Depending on the observer's viewing angle, this thickened region manifests differently: at high inclinations (edge-on), it appears as a boxy, peanut, or X-shaped (BPX) structure, often observationally classified among [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] or [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]]; at low inclinations (face-on), the same structure projects as a rounded central barlens [Li et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...845...87L/abstract).

The buckling process leaves a distinct kinematic imprint on the host galaxy. In nearly face-on systems, an active buckling event can be identified through a characteristic quadrupolar pattern in the out-of-plane stellar velocity field [Xiang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909..125X/abstract). Because the buckling timescale is so short, capturing bars in the act of buckling is observationally rare, though the structural remnants (BPX features) are ubiquitous in the local universe. For instance, $N$-body models of peanut-shaped bars seen edge-on closely replicate the COBE/DIRBE infrared imagery of the Milky Way bulge, suggesting the Galaxy's central bulge is a vertically thickened bar [Sellwood et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993AIPC..278..133S/abstract).

The formation of these thickened features is fundamentally driven by the evolution of bar-supporting stellar orbits interacting with horizontal and vertical resonances. As the bar evolves, planar orbits can become vertically extended. Recent numerical studies indicate that while sudden resonance overlap can drive this process, a pre-existing classical bulge can stabilize the fraction of vertically extended orbits, allowing a BPX feature to grow steadily without requiring sudden resonance-induced instabilities [McClure et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008277M/abstract).

The likelihood and severity of the buckling instability depend strongly on the host galaxy's structural properties. Buckling and subsequent BPX formation are highly prevalent in massive ($M_* > 10^{10.5}\ M_\odot$), early-type disks [Li et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...845...87L/abstract). In these systems, the deeper gravitational potential is more capable of sustaining the vertical resonant orbits necessary to maintain the thickened bar structure. Conversely, bars in low-mass, bulgeless galaxies undergo only slight vertical thickening over time, rather than a rapid buckling phase [Jang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250907353J/abstract). 

The exact role of the interstellar medium in this process remains debated, though observations and simulations generally suggest that high gas mass fractions tend to inhibit buckling [Li et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...845...87L/abstract). Additionally, the shape of the surrounding dark matter halo strongly influences the instability: $N$-body simulations demonstrate that prolate halos promote earlier buckling and can even induce multiple distinct buckling events, whereas oblate halos tend to delay the onset of the instability [Kumar et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.1262K/abstract). Finally, buckling is not restricted to bars formed via internal secular processes; [[tidally-induced-bars|Tidally induced bars]], such as those formed during prograde cluster flybys, also undergo extended periods of buckling that thicken their vertical structure [Łokas et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...826..227L/abstract), [Łokas et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...857....6L/abstract).

## Nested Bars and Nuclear Substructures

While primary galactic bars are highly efficient at driving gas inward from the outer disk, this radial inflow typically stalls at the central kiloparsec. Gravitational torques tend to accumulate gas near the inner Lindblad resonances (ILRs), leading to the formation of star-forming [[ring-galaxies|nuclear rings]] rather than funneling material directly into the galactic nucleus [Maciejewski et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999arse.conf..267M/abstract), [Sellwood et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004cbhg.symp..203S/abstract). 

To bypass this dynamical bottleneck, many galaxies rely on [[morphological-inner-structures|secondary stellar and gaseous substructures]]. Observations indicate that approximately $28\%$ of barred galaxies host nested bars, placing them in the category of [[double-barred-galaxies|Double-barred galaxies]] [Laine et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...567...97L/abstract). A defining feature of these systems is their dynamical decoupling: the secondary (inner) bar rotates independently of the [[outer-bar|primary bar]], typically exhibiting a significantly higher pattern speed ($\Omega_p$) [Friedli et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993A&A...277...27F/abstract), [Shlosman et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..275..231S/abstract). 

The orbital support for these inner structures strongly depends on this pattern speed; if the inner bar rotates too slowly or too quickly relative to the primary bar, the supporting families of periodic orbits dissolve, leading to a loss of coherence [Maciejewski et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...719..622M/abstract). Observationally, the lengths of primary and secondary bars exhibit a stark bimodal distribution. While primary bar lengths scale with the overall size of the host galaxy, secondary bars do not, further indicating that their spatial extent is strictly governed by the resonance structures (specifically the ILRs) of the primary bar [Laine et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...567...97L/abstract).

In the presence of a dynamically decoupled [[nested-bars|secondary bar]], gas transport can resume. Hydrodynamic simulations demonstrate that nested bars excite straight shocks that drive substantial mass inflows further inward, effectively crossing the ILR barrier. This process can produce highly compact, self-gravitating [[gaseous-nuclear-bars|nuclear gas disks]] with radii on the order of $\sim 15\text{ pc}$ [Namekata et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...691.1525N/abstract). This nested transport is considered a robust mechanism to fuel central activity and supermassive black hole (SMBH) accretion in the context of the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]], bypassing the need for hierarchical mergers.

Despite their crucial role in nuclear fueling, secondary bars are dynamically fragile. While large-scale primary bars are robust against central mass concentrations, numerical models show that the secular growth of a SMBH profoundly impacts the inner bar. Once the SMBH reaches a mass of roughly $0.1\%$ to $0.2\%$ of the galaxy's total stellar mass ($M_*$), it scatters the phase-space orbits supporting the secondary bar, leading to its rapid dissolution [Du et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844L..15D/abstract), [Guo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888...65G/abstract). 

The destruction of the short inner bar has two profound consequences. First, it chokes off the central gas supply, potentially terminating the active galactic nucleus phase [Du et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844L..15D/abstract). Second, it redistributes the stellar mass into a compact, dispersion-dominated central structure. The resulting aggregate of the initial [[pseudobulges|pseudobulge]] and the dissolved short bar bears the kinematic and morphological hallmarks of a classical bulge (Sérsic index $n \approx 2$). This provides a purely secular channel for building classical [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] in disk galaxies that have otherwise avoided significant mergers [Guo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888...65G/abstract).

## Cosmic Evolution and High-Redshift Observations

The prevalence and structural properties of barred galaxies evolve significantly over cosmic time, tracing the dynamical maturity of their host halos. In the local Universe, bars are ubiquitous; the large-scale DESI survey results find a total bar fraction of $42.9\%$, rising to $62\%$ specifically in [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]-dominated systems [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982..129W/abstract). Tracking the **bar fraction** to higher redshifts reveals a complex history of disk formation. To contextualize this evolution locally, new methodologies utilizing MUSE integral field spectroscopy now allow for the derivation of precise bar ages, revealing that bars in nearby galaxies span a remarkably wide range of ages from $1$ to $13$ Gyr [de Sá-Freitas et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...698A...5D/abstract). 

At intermediate epochs ($0.2 < z < 1.0$), optical surveys indicate that the fraction of strong bars remains roughly constant at $\sim 30\%$ [Jogee et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...615L.105J/abstract). However, this evolution is highly mass-dependent. For the most massive Disk Galaxies ($M_* > 10^{11} M_\odot$), the bar fraction and morphological demographics have remained largely stable since $z \sim 0.6$, suggesting that Hubble sequence convergence occurred relatively early for high-mass systems . In contrast, intermediate-mass disks ($10^{10.5} < M_* < 10^{11} M_\odot$) continued converging toward the local Hubble sequence until at least $z \sim 0.2$ . A general decline in the overall bar fraction at higher redshifts reflects an era when dynamically unstable disks limited the formation and persistence of [[non-axisymmetric-structures|non-axisymmetric]] structures [Chacón et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260100106C/abstract).

Prior to recent observational leaps, theoretical consensus held that the high gas turbulence and frequent mergers typical of the early Universe would suppress the [[galactic-disk-evolution|disk settling]] required to trigger global bar instabilities. Cosmological simulations generally predicted that stellar bars should be exceedingly rare at $z > 1.5$ [Costantin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Natur.623..499C/abstract). 

However, recent JWST observations and high-resolution ALMA millimeter data have fundamentally disrupted this paradigm by identifying mature, strongly barred galaxies during and prior to 'Cosmic Noon' ($z \sim 2-3$). A landmark discovery is ceers-2112, a Milky Way-like barred spiral observed at $z \approx 3$, when the Universe was only 2 Gyr old [Costantin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Natur.623..499C/abstract). Its existence implies that dynamically cold stellar disks could have been in place as early as $z \sim 4-5$, requiring baryons to dominate over dark matter in the inner galaxy much earlier than previously thought [Costantin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Natur.623..499C/abstract). 

These early bars were not merely passive structures; they actively drove the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] in the young Universe. ALMA observations of the barred galaxy J0107a at $z = 2.467$ reveal a mature bar driving massive non-circular motions, funneling molecular gas inward at a staggering rate of $\sim 600 \ M_\odot \ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ [Huang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025Natur.641..861H/abstract). Furthermore, the recent discovery of a bar-built, star-forming [[galaxy-nuclei-and-complex-morphologies|nuclear disk]] at $z = 1.5$ provides direct evidence that bars were already constructing [[galaxy-nuclei-and-complex-morphologies|complex central architectures]] at early epochs [Le Conte et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260118871L/abstract).

The existence of these high-redshift bars introduces a significant tension between early disk settling and the turbulent conditions expected in the young Universe. To reconcile this, updated magneto-hydrodynamical simulations suggest that high-redshift bars may follow unique evolutionary pathways. Unlike local bars that grow gradually in length and strength, early bars may be "born saturated" in length, rapidly triggered by the intense merger and cold accretion activity characteristic of the early Universe [Fragkoudi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.538.1587F/abstract). Simulations also suggest that high-$z$ bars are exceptionally gas-rich and may be destroyed and reformed multiple times as oval distortions before permanently settling [Bi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934...52B/abstract). The precise mechanisms that allow for such rapid disk stabilization and sustained bar survival amidst early cosmological turbulence remain an active and heavily debated frontier in astrophysics.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite the established role of galactic bars in driving the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]], several major questions remain regarding their kinematics, formation timelines, and their role in central AGN fueling.

Foremost is the severe "fast bar" tension between empirical observations and $\Lambda$CDM cosmological models. As discussed previously, the persistent statistical discrepancy between the overwhelmingly "fast" bars observed in the local Universe and the "slow" bars produced by dynamical friction in hydrodynamical simulations (such as IllustrisTNG) continues to challenge standard dark matter paradigms, prompting explorations into refined subgrid physics or alternative gravity models like MOND [Roshan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508..926R/abstract), [Banik et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Symm...14.1331B/abstract). 

Similarly, the timeline of bar formation in the early Universe is undergoing rapid revision. The unexpected discovery of mature, strongly barred galaxies at $z > 3$ by JWST and ALMA [Costantin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Natur.623..499C/abstract), [Espejo Salcedo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250321738E/abstract) contradicts earlier predictions that cosmological turbulence would suppress early [[non-axisymmetric-structures|Non-axisymmetric structures]]. Reconciling these observations requires refined models of early disk settling, subgrid feedback physics, and merger-induced bar triggering [Fragkoudi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240609453F/abstract).

Furthermore, the precise statistical link between bars and Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) remains highly ambiguous. While broad, large-scale statistical studies initially found no significant enhancement in average AGN accretion rates in barred versus unbarred galaxies [Ho et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...487..591H/abstract), [Cisternas et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...802..137C/abstract), recent nuanced findings show strong correlations under specific environmental and bar strength conditions [Marels et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250523958M/abstract). Bridging the final parsec-scale gap to the supermassive black hole likely requires intermediate structures. However, the dynamical fragility of intermediate structures like [[nested-bars|Nested bars]] complicates a direct link to continuous AGN fueling, as they can be rapidly destroyed by the growing central black hole [Du et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844L..15D/abstract). Additionally, the mass accumulation and growth of the supermassive black hole can dynamically react and alter the bar's structure [Zee et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...949...91Z/abstract), making the cause-and-effect relationship deeply entangled.

Finally, the broader role of magnetic fields in galactic evolution represents a critical and rapidly developing frontier. High-resolution radio observations indicate that magnetic fields strongly align with gas streamlines and shock fronts within the bar, actively exerting magnetic stress that can drive mass inward to fuel central activity [Beck et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Natur.397..324B/abstract). While these central processes are significant, recent studies analyzing large-scale galactic outflows, such as eROSITA bubbles, have found that massive non-thermal magnetized structures extending kiloparsecs into the galactic halo are not driven by central bar phenomena; rather, they are sustained by active star-forming regions located further out in the galactic disk, typically $3-5$ kpc from the center [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1416Z/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240806312Z/abstract). This paradigm of disk-driven magnetized outflows is further supported by pulsar measurements in globular clusters, such as 47 Tucanae, which provide independent evidence for magnetic fields extending directly from the galactic disk into the surrounding halo [Abbate et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..704A/abstract).
