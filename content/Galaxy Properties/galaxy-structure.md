## Article

Galaxy structure encompasses the spatial distribution, physical organization, and kinematic state of the baryonic and dark matter components that constitute a galaxy. At a fundamental level, the [[galaxy-properties|observable structure]] of a galaxy is a fossil record of its underlying gravitational potential and its multi-phase [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|assembly history]] [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Galaxies are broadly organized into distinct morphological subcomponents, most notably rotationally supported [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] and [[thick-disks|Thick disks]], central pressure-supported [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]], and extended, metal-poor [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]] [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract). These luminous baryonic components are embedded within massive, invisible dark matter halos that dictate the system's global dynamics and are critical for stabilizing large-scale galactic architecture [Benson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhR...495...33B/abstract). By mapping these features through photometric surface brightness profiles and [[galaxy-kinematics|Galaxy kinematics]], astrophysicists can decode the complex interplay of hierarchical merging, gas accretion, and feedback-regulated star formation.

Historically, the study of galaxy structure relied heavily on visual [[galaxy-morphological-classification|Galaxy morphological classification]] frameworks, most famously the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]]. However, modern extragalactic astronomy increasingly utilizes robust, mass-correlated mathematical frameworks—such as the parametric [[sersic-profile|Sersic profile]] and non-parametric [[cas-parameters|CAS parameters]] (Concentration, Asymmetry, Clumpiness)—to provide automated, quantitative descriptions of galactic light distributions [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). These metrics reveal that galaxy structure undergoes profound transformation over cosmic time. Under the hierarchical Galaxy Formation and Assembly paradigm, galaxies grow "inside-out," evolving from highly compact, asymmetric, and gas-rich progenitors at high redshift into the mature, extended systems observed in the local Universe. This structural evolution is simultaneously shaped by external environmental drivers, evidenced by the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]], and by internal [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] driven by bar instabilities and spiral density waves [Mihalas et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981gask.book.....M/abstract). 

This article explores the taxonomy, quantitative analysis, and physical mechanisms governing galaxy structure. It details the properties of individual galactic subcomponents and examines the dynamic scaling relations that link luminous stellar populations to their encompassing dark matter halos. It also uses [[milky-way-structure-and-components|Milky Way Structure and Components]] as a foundational, albeit actively debated, benchmark for understanding typical spiral galaxies. Finally, the article outlines major open questions and theoretical tensions in the field of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. Chief among these challenges are the unexpectedly early structural maturity of massive quiescent galaxies at $z > 1.5$, and the controversial empirical evidence suggesting that global galaxy structure may be tightly regulated by a single, yet-unidentified parameter—a finding that directly challenges the multi-parameter, highly stochastic predictions of standard $\Lambda$CDM models.

## Foundations of Galaxy Morphology and Taxonomy

The systematic study of galaxies necessitates a robust framework for [[galaxy-morphological-classification|Galaxy morphological classification]]. The foundation of this taxonomy was established by Edwin Hubble in the 1920s and 30s, culminating in the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] (often referred to as the "tuning fork" diagram). This system categorizes galaxies into two primary branches based on their visual appearance: [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|early-type galaxies]] (ellipticals and lenticulars) and [[late-type-galaxies-and-stellar-populations|late-type galaxies]] (spirals and irregulars). It is important to note that the "early" and "late" nomenclature is entirely historical and does not imply an evolutionary sequence.

Elliptical galaxies are denoted by E$n$, where the integer $n$ is the closest integer to $10(1 - b/a)$, which characterizes the projected [[ellipticity-function|ellipticity]] ($a$ and $b$ being the semi-major and semi-minor axes). Spiral galaxies are divided into unbarred (Sa, Sb, Sc) and barred (SBa, SBb, SBc) branches, ordered by the decreasing prominence of the central [[galactic-bulges|bulge]] relative to the disk, and the increasing openness and clumpiness of the spiral arms.

In 1974, Gérard de Vaucouleurs expanded the Hubble sequence to capture more subtle structural variations. He introduced intermediate classes (e.g., Sab, Sbc) and formally integrated [[irregular-morphology|irregular galaxies]] by appending Magellanic-type extensions (Sdm, Sm, Im) to the late-type sequence. Furthermore, de Vaucouleurs assigned numerical $T$-types ranging from $T = -6$ for early ellipticals to $T = 10$ for irregulars, enabling quantitative correlations between morphology and physical [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]]. Traversing the sequence from early to late types strongly correlates with decreasing stellar mass, bluer [[galaxy-color-distributions|colors]], increasing fractional gas content, and higher specific [[global-star-formation-rate|star formation rates]]. This phenomenological approach has been further refined in modern reviews of Galaxy morphological classification, which introduce new galaxy types and comprehensive classification frameworks based on the explosion of multi-wavelength morphological data [Buta et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1102.0550B/abstract).

Despite its ubiquity, the classical Hubble sequence does not encapsulate the full diversity of the galaxy population. Notably, the faintest systems—comprising the vast majority of the Universe's [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]]—do not cleanly map onto the sequence. Dwarf irregulars and diffuse dwarf spheroidals occupy distinct structural parameter spaces. Similarly, galaxies undergoing massive tidal interactions or [[structural-transformation|Structural transformation]] exhibit highly asymmetric, disturbed morphologies. These peculiar galaxies, historically cataloged in collections like the [[arp-atlas-of-peculiar-galaxies|Arp Atlas of Peculiar Galaxies]], are often excluded from standard tuning-fork classifications but provide critical insight into merger-driven assembly.

In the era of [[large-galaxy-catalogs|Large galaxy catalogs]] and massive digital sky surveys—pioneered by foundational projects like the [[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey]], which provided the first statistically representative map of large-scale structure in the local Universe [Colless et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004mmu..symp..196C/abstract)—pure visual classification is increasingly being supplemented or superseded by automated, quantitative techniques [Wadadekar et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1201.2252W/abstract). Modern taxonomy relies on measuring internal light distributions using parametric models, such as the [[sersic-profile|Sersic profile]], or non-parametric indices like the [[cas-parameters|CAS parameters]] (Concentration, Asymmetry, Clumpiness) and the Gini/$$M_{20}$$ system [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). These quantitative metrics reveal that "structure" and "morphology" are physically distinct properties: a galaxy's fundamental structural profile (e.g., its Sérsic index) is primarily driven by its stellar mass, whereas its visual morphology (e.g., clumpiness or star-forming bumps) is highly sensitive to the local [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]] [van der Wel et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...675L..13V/abstract). Consequently, modern taxonomic frameworks evaluate galaxies not merely by their visual class, but through a continuous, multidimensional parameter space connecting structural kinematics to their hierarchical assembly history.

## Quantitative Structural Analysis and Non-parametric Indices

The advent of large [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]] has necessitated a shift from subjective visual [[galaxy-morphological-classification|Galaxy morphological classification]] (such as the traditional Hubble sequence) to automated, quantitative structural measurements [Wadadekar et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1201.2252W/abstract). This transition enables the reproducible processing of billions of images and provides a framework to physically link galaxy structure to formation processes and merger histories [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract).

Traditionally, galaxy light distributions are quantified using surface brightness photometry to fit parameterized radial profiles. The most ubiquitous model is the [[sersic-profile|Sersic profile]], which is frequently used to measure galaxy sizes and surface brightness profile shapes [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). However, to capture irregular structures that deviate from smooth symmetric profiles, non-parametric indices have become standard. The most widely employed systems are the [[cas-parameters|CAS parameters]] (Concentration $C$, Asymmetry $A$, and Clumpiness $S$) and the Gini/M20 indices [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). These structural parameters enable the establishment of modern [[galaxy-scaling-relations|scaling relations]]—connecting physical sizes, intrinsic luminosities, and stellar concentrations—that serve as essential benchmarks for evolutionary studies and allow for the robust distinction between bulges, disks, and elliptical structures [Graham et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013pss6.book...91G/abstract). These model-independent descriptors also serve as empirical proxies for a galaxy's dynamical state. For example, the asymmetry parameter is highly sensitive to major mergers; quantitative CAS studies show that the median asymmetry merger timescale is $0.50_{-0.18}^{+0.33}$ Gyr, yielding merger rates that scale with redshift as $\sim (1+z)^{1.87 \pm 0.04}$ [Whitney et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919..139W/abstract). 

For [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], automated structural analysis also extends to spiral arms. To eliminate the subjectivity historically associated with manual spiral arm tracing, researchers have developed automated systems that translate survey images into quantitative data by modeling spiral arm arrangements as sets of discrete arcs [Davis et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1301.5484D/abstract). Furthermore, one-dimensional and two-dimensional Fourier decompositions are used to extract pitch angle and arm strength, demonstrating that grand-design spirals possess a systematically higher relative amplitude in their $m=2$ Fourier mode [Yu et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862...13Y/abstract).

Despite the power of these indices, there is an active tension in using structural parameters (such as the Sersic index) as direct proxies for morphology, particularly in studies of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. Research demonstrates that structure and morphology are distinct properties driven by different physical mechanisms: structural concentration depends primarily on total stellar mass, whereas morphological appearance (e.g., "bumpiness" and color-traced star formation) is overwhelmingly dictated by the local environment [van der Wel et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...675L..13V/abstract). Confusing the two can lead to biased interpretations of how galaxy populations evolve.

To circumvent the systematic errors introduced by dust and [[galaxy-inclination|Galaxy inclination]], modern structural analyses have developed newer non-parametric metrics. One such approach is the "linear brightness profile," which collapses a galaxy's light distribution onto its major axis to define inclination-independent metrics, such as $x_{50}$ (the linear distance containing 50% of the total luminosity) and the concentration ratio $c_x = x_{90}/x_{50}$ [Devour et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..244....3D/abstract). Furthermore, at the faint limits of observational data, extracting reliable surface brightness profiles is increasingly reliant on Bayesian deconvolution using score-based likelihoods and diffusion model priors, which can mathematically recover detailed structures previously blurred by instrumental noise and the point-spread function [Adam et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....169..254A/abstract).

## Constituent Components: Disks, Bulges, and Spheroids

[[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] are complex, multicomponent systems generally consisting of a rotationally supported disk comprising cold gas, dust, and stars, alongside a central bulge and an extended dark and stellar halo [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Edge-on observations reveal that the stellar disk often separates into two distinct kinematic and chemical populations: the [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] and [[thick-disks|Thick disks]]. The vertical density distribution of these components is commonly parameterized by an exponential profile, $\rho(z) \propto \exp(-|z|/z_0)$ [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). While the Milky Way exhibits fundamental structural parameters (such as scale length, scale height, and bulge-to-total luminosity ratio) that are largely typical of local spiral galaxies, its thick disk appears appreciably thinner and less extended than is predicted by typical $\Lambda$CDM zoom-in simulations of merger-driven growth [Guerrette et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24c5002G/abstract).

Within the thin disk, spiral arms trace regions of enhanced gas density and active star formation [Hart et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461.3663H/abstract). Morphological classifications traditionally distinguish between "grand-design" spirals featuring continuous, well-defined arms and [[flocculent-spiral-galaxies|flocculent]] systems with patchy, multi-armed structures. There is an active debate in the literature regarding both the physical origin and the continuity of spiral arms. While some studies model spiral arms as long-lived density waves [Simonson et al. (1970)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1970A&A.....9..163S/abstract), [Mishurov et al. (1979)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1979AZh....56..268M/abstract), others argue that many-armed and flocculent structures represent a short-lived phase driven by stochastic star formation [Hart et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461.3663H/abstract). Furthermore, while the Milky Way is commonly modeled with four major continuous arms, recent statistical analyses show that 82% of spiral galaxies possess a two-armed structure in their inner regions [Wei et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..264W/abstract). If the Milky Way genuinely possesses four continuous arms extending from the inner to the outer disk, it would be highly atypical; it is increasingly suggested that the Galaxy may instead have a standard two-armed inner structure that branches outward [Wei et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..264W/abstract). 

The central regions of disk galaxies are often dominated by [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]], whose prominence varies systematically along the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). In the Milky Way, near-infrared photometry and maser kinematics confirm the presence of a prominent triaxial or box/peanut-shaped bar, though its precise geometry remains a subject of active debate. While some near-infrared surveys suggest an inclination of $22^\circ \pm 5.5^\circ$ relative to the Sun-Galactic Center line and a radius of approximately $2.5$ kpc terminating near an inferred inner pseudo-ring [Babusiaux et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.358.1309B/abstract), other kinematic models and maser mapping support a long, thin bar with a $45^\circ$ orientation that connects to the 3 kpc arms [Green et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...733...27G/abstract). Furthermore, it is increasingly recognized that these central structures do not form exclusively via mergers; galactic bulges can also form through internal secular evolution from clumpy, gas-rich disks, leading to disk-like, alpha-rich stellar populations [Gerhard et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006EAS....20...89G/abstract).

Surrounding the disk and bulge is a diffuse, metal-poor spheroidal component known as the [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]], which is assembled primarily through the accretion and disruption of [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] over cosmic time [Bland-Hawthorn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..529B/abstract). The hierarchical assembly of these spheroids leaves persistent chemo-kinematic signatures. For example, recent surveys of the Milky Way's inner halo have identified a distinct metal-poor stellar structure—termed the Inner Galaxy Structure (IGS)—which is chemically detached from the more metal-rich bulge populations. The elemental abundances of the IGS suggest it represents a major early accretion event from a progenitor system with a stellar mass of $\sim 5 \times 10^8 M_\odot$ [Horta et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.1385H/abstract). The overall assembly of these halos across the universe can also be traced by resolving the faint stellar outskirts of neighboring galaxies, such as M31 and M33, using deep star counts [Ferguson et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASSP....3..239F/abstract). Within our own Galaxy, the systematic tabulation of structure parameters—such as core radii and density profiles—for galactic [[globular-clusters|globular clusters]] provides complementary indicators of the early spheroidal component's fundamental characteristics [Webbink et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985IAUS..113..541W/abstract).

## Dark Matter Distribution and Galactic Kinematics

The structural and dynamic properties of galaxies are intimately linked to their invisible dark matter components. In the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological paradigm, galaxies form and evolve within extended, dynamically dominant dark matter halos [Benson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhR...495...33B/abstract). While baryonic matter cools, condenses, and forms the visible luminous structures, the massive dark matter halo dictates the overall depth and geometry of the global gravitational potential well [Nulsen et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999PASA...16....3N/abstract).

The distribution of dark matter is primarily mapped through [[galaxy-kinematics|Galaxy kinematics]]. In rotationally supported [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], cold atomic gas (H I) and stars serve as kinematic tracers. Observations consistently show that their [[universal-rotation-curves|rotation curves]] remain flat out to large galactocentric radii, extending well beyond the visible stellar disk. This lack of Keplerian decline is the classical signature of an extended dark matter halo [Sancisi et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.269...59S/abstract). Conversely, [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] are supported primarily by the random motions of their stellar populations. For these systems, dynamical masses are inferred from measurements of the stellar velocity dispersion profile [Courteau et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86...47C/abstract). 

To quantify the relationship between luminous and dark matter, kinematic data are typically fit using multi-component [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]]. To statistically specify how galaxies of different types populate [[dark-matter-halo-scales|dark matter halos]] within a broader cosmological model, researchers frequently employ the Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD) framework [Weinberg et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..283....3W/abstract). The underlying mass distribution is often parameterized by generalized density functions, such as:
$$ \rho(r) \propto \frac{1}{r^\gamma (r+r_s)^{3-\gamma}} $$
where $r_s$ is the scale radius and $\gamma$ is the inner logarithmic slope. This functional form is structurally similar to models used to describe the radial number density distribution of galaxies in clusters, illustrating dynamic scaling symmetries across distinct astrophysical environments [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Decoupling the baryonic and dark matter contributions to the total potential allows for precise estimators of the underlying dark mass [Courteau et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86...47C/abstract). Beyond internal kinematics, the macroscopic distribution of dark matter imprints itself on the orientation of galaxies. Observations reveal that galaxy orientations are not random but exhibit distinct alignments with their underlying dark matter structures and the broader cosmic web [Joachimi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015SSRv..193....1J/abstract).

Despite the overarching success of hierarchical assembly models, significant tensions remain in the literature regarding the exact interplay between baryons and dark matter on galactic scales. The standard hierarchical framework predicts that the structural properties of a galaxy should be governed by stochastic merger histories and at least six independent parameters, including mass, angular momentum, age, and baryon fraction. However, blind H I surveys reveal that gas-rich galaxies exhibit tight correlations across multiple independent observables, leading to the controversial claim that galaxy structure might be regulated by a single, currently unidentified parameter [Disney et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455.1082D/abstract). 

Furthermore, discrepancies persist regarding the shape of dark matter halos and the evolutionary timeline of rotation curves. Some authors have noted that standard hierarchical models struggle to reproduce the early development of flat rotation curves, hypothesizing that dark matter might be redistributed into highly elliptical orbits during periods of active galactic nuclei feedback [Friedmann et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0912.1668F/abstract). On sub-galactic scales, the mismatch between the predicted number of low-mass dark matter halos and the observed satellite population has led to the hypothesis that High Velocity Clouds (HVCs) might be the elusive, dark-matter-dominated building blocks of the Local Group [Blitz et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph..1307B/abstract). These issues highlight an active, ongoing debate concerning how baryonic feedback and dark matter halo dynamics couple over cosmic time.

## Hierarchical Assembly and Structural Evolution

The structural evolution of galaxies is fundamentally driven by the [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] paradigm of hierarchical cold dark matter ($\Lambda$CDM) cosmology. Within this framework, the [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|Galaxy assembly process]] is governed by the gravitational collapse of dark matter halos, followed by the condensation of baryons, ongoing star formation, and stochastic merging [Benson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhR...495...33B/abstract). The large-scale environment in which this assembly occurs has been mapped by early redshift surveys, which revealed "cellular" features and large-scale bubbles in the galaxy distribution on 50-100 Mpc scales [Ratcliffe et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.281L..47R/abstract). Furthermore, quantitative measures of this large-scale structure, such as the genus statistic, confirm that the connectedness of the galaxy distribution is consistent with the Gaussian random density fields predicted by inflationary cosmology [James et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375..128J/abstract). Consequently, the morphologies and structural parameters of galaxies exhibit profound redshift evolution, transitioning from compact, peculiar systems to the well-defined [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] observed in the local Universe.

Observations from deep-field surveys reveal that galaxies at high redshift ($z > 1$) differ drastically from their modern counterparts. Rather than presenting as smooth, rotation-supported disks or relaxed ellipsoids, early [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]] are frequently characterized by irregular, clumpy, and highly asymmetric structures [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). Detailed kinematic mapping of this epoch has been revolutionized by programs such as the SINS and zC-SINF surveys, which utilized the SINFONI integral field spectrometer to resolve the kinematics and star formation of massive galaxies at $z \sim 2$ on kiloparsec scales [Förster Schreiber et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Msngr.145...39F/abstract). These high-redshift, dynamically distinct populations drove an era of intense galaxy growth; indeed, the cosmic star formation rate peaked between $z \sim 1$ and $z \sim 2$ at a rate roughly ten times higher than in the present day [Madau et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997AIPC..393..481M/abstract). Furthermore, for a given stellar mass, high-redshift galaxies are significantly more compact, measuring roughly 2 to 5 times smaller than local galaxies [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). Quantitative structural metrics, such as the [[cas-parameters|CAS parameters]] (Concentration, Asymmetry, Clumpiness), empirically demonstrate that galaxies become progressively more asymmetric at higher redshifts. 

The transition from these compact progenitors to extended, mature galaxies is largely characterized by inside-out growth. In this model, galaxies form from dense initial cores, while their outer extended disks and halos are subsequently built up over time through a combination of sustained gas accretion and minor mergers [Whitney et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919..139W/abstract). Major mergers also play a critical role in this structural transformation; asymmetry measurements yield a median merger timescale of $0.50_{-0.18}^{+0.33}$ Gyr, from which derived merger rates are found to scale rapidly with redshift as approximately $(1+z)^{1.87 \pm 0.04}$ Gyr$^{-1}$ [Whitney et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919..139W/abstract). For the most [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], observational evidence suggests that mergers and cold gas accretion contribute roughly equally to mass assembly between $z=1$ and $z=3$ [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract).

Despite the broad successes of the hierarchical model, significant tensions remain in the literature regarding the precise timing of structural maturity. A pure bottom-up $\Lambda$CDM framework predicts that the most massive systems should be the last to assemble. However, near-infrared and spectroscopic observations indicate a high abundance of massive, [[passive-galaxies|quiescent galaxies]] that were already structurally in place just 3 Gyr after the Big Bang ($z \sim 1.5-2$) [Glazebrook et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Natur.430..181G/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract). This rapid early assembly poses a persistent challenge to conventional models of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. 

Additionally, there is an unresolved theoretical debate regarding the complexity of galaxy formation. While hierarchical theory implies that [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] are dictated by at least six independent parameters alongside a highly stochastic merger history, empirical studies of H I-selected galaxies suggest that global structural properties correlate tightly enough to be governed by a single dominant, yet unidentified, parameter [Disney et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455.1082D/abstract). Resolving these discrepancies between theoretical predictions of hierarchical assembly and the observational constraints of high-redshift galaxy structures remains an active frontier in extragalactic astrophysics.

## Internal Secular Evolution and Dynamics

The [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] encompasses the slow, internally driven physical processes that alter a galaxy's morphology, kinematics, and star formation rate over cosmic time. In contrast to rapid structural transformations driven by major mergers, secular evolution dominates the long-term development of isolated disk systems. 

A primary mechanism of internal secular evolution is the development of non-axisymmetric structures, particularly bar instabilities. Central bars form via global gravitational instabilities in cold, rotationally supported disks. Once established, they redistribute angular momentum and efficiently channel cold gas toward the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]]. This gas inflow frequently triggers nuclear starbursts and feeds active galactic nuclei (AGN). Over billions of years, the vertical buckling of these bars can lead to the formation of box- or peanut-shaped pseudobulges. Within the context of [[milky-way-structure-and-components|Milky Way Structure and Components]], near-infrared surveys confirm that our Galaxy harbors a triaxial central bar. While some studies suggest it extends approximately $2.5$ kpc and is inclined at $22^\circ \pm 5.5^\circ$ relative to the Sun-Galactic Center line terminating near a pseudo-ring [Babusiaux et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.358.1309B/abstract), competing models argue for a longer, thinner bar oriented at $45^\circ$ [Green et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...733...27G/abstract). The interaction of this central bar with the inner resonant structures, such as the 3 kpc arms, is thought to dictate the gas dynamics and star formation of the inner Galaxy [Green et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...733...27G/abstract).

Beyond the barred core, [[spiral-galaxy-dynamics|Spiral Galaxy Dynamics]] govern the large-scale disk architecture, though the exact physical nature of spiral arms remains an active debate. Classical density wave theory posits that spiral arms are long-lived, quasi-stationary density perturbations. However, alternative models suggest that many-armed or [[flocculent-spiral-galaxies|Flocculent spiral galaxies]] represent a short-lived, stochastic phase associated with local star formation activity [Hart et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461.3663H/abstract). Observational demographics indicate that $82\%$ of spiral galaxies exhibit a two-armed structure in their inner regions, which typically branch into multiple outer arms rather than forming a continuous grand-design pattern [Wei et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..264W/abstract). The relationship between spiral structure and star formation efficiency is currently ambiguous in the literature; while spiral arms are associated with enhanced gas density, recent statistical studies suggest that galaxies with a higher multiplicity of spiral arms may actually exhibit lower specific star formation rates [Porter-Temple et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515.3875P/abstract). Irrespective of global morphology, "star complexes" serve as a fundamental structural unit of star formation within these systems, frequently aligning with and highlighting the underlying spiral arm architecture [Efremov et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ssfg.book.....E/abstract).

The conversion of cold gas into stars within these dynamical structures is globally parameterized by the empirical Schmidt-Kennicutt law, which relates the star formation surface density ($\Sigma_{\dot{\star}}$) to the total gas surface density ($\Sigma_{\rm gas}$) as:
$$ \Sigma_{\dot{\star}} \propto \Sigma_{\rm gas}^N $$
where the exponent is typically $N \approx 1-2$. This process is tightly controlled by feedback-regulated star formation. Evolving stars, supernovae, and AGN inject thermal, kinetic, and radiative energy into the interstellar medium. This feedback maintains the multiphase composition of the gas, regulates the vertical scale height of [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] and [[thick-disks|Thick disks]], and produces macroscopic features such as large-scale H I shells and supershells in spiral disks, which act as observable indicators of the ISM energy balance and stellar feedback structure [Brinks et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ASSL..161...39B/abstract). It can also drive galactic winds that suppress further star formation. Recent surveys have also identified unique transitional populations, such as BreakBRD galaxies—spiral galaxies characterized by truncated disk star formation but robust, centrally concentrated star formation within otherwise red disks. These systems are highly useful for studying the internal mechanisms that drive the secular quenching of star formation [Tuttle et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889..188T/abstract).

The degree to which the Milky Way serves as a typical template for secular evolution is an open question. Its notably quiescent merger history over the past 10 Gyr has allowed internal dynamical processes to dominate its recent structural development, leading some to argue it is anomalously undisturbed compared to standard $\Lambda$CDM predictions [Wyse et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph..2636W/abstract), [Wyse et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IAUS..254..179W/abstract). While many of its structural parameters are completely typical of local spirals [Guerrette et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24c5002G/abstract), there is conflicting evidence suggesting the Milky Way may currently be a "green valley" transition object undergoing a mid-life quenching of its star formation [Mutch et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...736...84M/abstract). Furthermore, literature is divided on whether the Milky Way possesses an anomalous four-arm continuous spiral structure or a more typical two-armed inner structure that branches into multiple outer arms [Wei et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..264W/abstract).

## Environmental Drivers of Structural Transformation

The large-scale [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]] plays a fundamental role in shaping the observable properties of galaxies. As galaxies assemble within the cosmic web, their evolutionary trajectories diverge significantly depending on whether they reside in low-density field regions or high-density [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]]. This environmental divergence is most famously parameterized by the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]], which demonstrates that the fraction of spiral galaxies decreases from approximately $60\%$ in the lowest-density field environments to less than $10\%$ in the dense cores of rich clusters, with the [[early-type-fraction|early-type fraction]] exhibiting the inverse behavior.

While traditional [[galaxy-morphological-classification|Galaxy morphological classification]] often intertwines a galaxy's dynamical structure with its recent star formation history, quantitative analyses reveal that these properties exhibit distinct environmental dependencies. Studies of local SDSS samples complete down to a stellar mass of $M = 10^{10} M_\odot$ demonstrate that a galaxy's underlying mass distribution—parameterized by the [[sersic-profile|Sersic profile]] index—is predominantly determined by its stellar mass. In contrast, morphological features driven by star formation, such as color and visual "bumpiness," are highly sensitive to the local environmental density [van der Wel et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...675L..13V/abstract). This indicates that the Morphology-density relation is primarily driven by environmentally induced quenching of star-formation activity, which alters a galaxy's visual appearance even if its deeper structural scaffolding remains largely intact. Consequently, studies that use structural indices as direct proxies for morphology can suffer from significant biases, particularly when attempting to infer the morphological mix of high-redshift populations [van der Wel et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...675L..13V/abstract).

The physical drivers of this transformation are particularly severe in massive clusters, which can possess total masses of $10^{14}\text{--}10^{15} M_\odot$ and velocity dispersions of $\sim 1000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$. The structural scaling relations and internal dynamics of these massive galaxy clusters profoundly impact their constituent populations [Biviano et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0811.3535B/abstract). To robustly identify and study these dense environments over cosmic time, researchers increasingly utilize [[planck-sz-cluster-catalog|Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect surveys]] to select massive galaxy clusters at high redshifts ($z > 1$) independently of optical selection biases [Bartlett et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..6241B/abstract). When [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] fall into these massive halos, they are subjected to a suite of hydrodynamical and gravitational forces. Ram-pressure stripping and "strangulation" effectively remove or cut off the galaxy's cold gas reservoir, leading to a rapid cessation of star formation. Simultaneously, tidal interactions with both the global cluster potential and other cluster members can strip loosely bound outer stars and alter internal kinematics. These tidal forces gradually transform extended star-forming disks into more compact, passive early-type systems.

As a result of these environmental drivers, the structural evolution of a galaxy is strongly dictated by its hierarchical status. [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]] typically reside at the bottom of the host dark matter halo's potential well, where they can continue to grow through gas accretion and the consumption of smaller companions whose orbits decay via dynamical friction [Contini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240401560C/abstract). The photometric structure of these central [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest Cluster Galaxies]] (BCGs) provides strong observational support for dynamical friction acting as a dominant driver of galaxy evolution in dense cluster environments [Hoessel et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980ApJ...241..493H/abstract). Conversely, Satellite galaxies are systematically stripped of their gas and stellar mass, inhibiting further inside-out growth and relegating them to a passive evolutionary track.

## The Milky Way as a Structural Benchmark

Our Galaxy, the Milky Way, serves as a fundamental benchmark for understanding [[internal-galactic-structure|Internal Galactic Structure]] because it is the only system where the full spatial and kinematic distribution of stars—from faint dwarfs to supergiants—can be resolved and studied in detail . Broadly classified as a luminous ($L_\star$) barred spiral galaxy, the Milky Way possesses structural parameters, such as its radial scale length, vertical scale height, and bulge-to-total luminosity ratio, that are largely typical of local disk galaxies [Guerrette et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24c5002G/abstract). However, its global colors suggest it may lie in the sparsely populated "green valley," indicating it is undergoing a secular transition between the actively star-forming blue cloud and the passive [[red-sequence|red sequence]] [Mutch et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...736...84M/abstract), [Bland-Hawthorn et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..529B/abstract).

The [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]] in the Milky Way are dominated by a triaxial structure, with substantial evidence indicating that both the Galactic bulge and the surrounding stellar spheroid possess significant triaxiality [Spergel et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ASSL..180...77S/abstract). Near-infrared photometry and kinematic models confirm the presence of a central bar, though tension remains over its precise geometry. Some models favor an inclination of $22^\circ \pm 5.5^\circ$ relative to the Sun-Galactic Center line and a length of $\sim 2.5 \text{ kpc}$ circumscribed by an inner pseudo-ring [Babusiaux et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.358.1309B/abstract), while others strongly support a $45^\circ$ orientation interacting with the 3 kpc arms [Green et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...733...27G/abstract). Within these central regions, spectroscopic surveys have also identified a chemically and dynamically distinct metal-poor stellar population. This is interpreted as a major early-assembly building block of $\sim 5 \times 10^8 M_\odot$ that was accreted during the Galaxy's infancy [Horta et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.1385H/abstract).

The [[milky-way-structure-and-components|Milky Way Structure and Components]] are classically partitioned into [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] and [[thick-disks|Thick disks]]. The vertical density distribution of these stellar components is typically parameterized by an exponential decay from the midplane, 
$$ \rho(z) = \rho_0 \exp\left(-\frac{|z|}{h_z}\right) $$
where $h_z$ denotes the scale height. Interestingly, the Milky Way's thick disk appears physically thinner and less extended than is predicted by $\Lambda$CDM zoom-in simulations for galaxies with significant satellite accretion histories [Guerrette et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24c5002G/abstract). To unravel the detailed evolutionary timeline and spatial geometry of the Galaxy's disk, astrophysicists frequently utilize [[galactic-open-cluster-population|open clusters]]. Due to their wide range of ages and Galactocentric distances, open clusters serve as critical probes of the Galaxy's disk structure and its historical star formation timeline [Friel et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.265..271F/abstract).

Furthermore, the precise morphology of the Galaxy's spiral arms remains a subject of active debate. While historical kinematic models and maser mapping strongly argue for a four-armed continuous spiral pattern extending from the inner to outer regions [Blitz et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983Sci...220.1233B/abstract), [Green et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...733...27G/abstract), recent statistical analyses of analogous galaxies suggest the Milky Way may instead feature a two-armed inner structure that branches into multiple outer arms—a configuration far more common among local barred spirals [Wei et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..264W/abstract). The large-scale magnetic field follows this spiral structure, exhibiting a bisymmetric pattern where field directions reverse from arm to arm [Han et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004mim..proc....3H/abstract). Additionally, Faraday rotation measures indicate that the magnetic field in the central kiloparsec of the Galaxy is organized on $300 \text{ pc}$ scales, likely exhibiting a poloidal geometry that is shifted west of the dynamical center [Law et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...731...36L/abstract).

The [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] of the Milky Way record a surprisingly quiescent merger history over the past $\sim 10 \text{ Gyr}$. This lack of recent major mergers is somewhat anomalous when compared to standard hierarchical structure formation models, prompting debate over whether our Galaxy is truly a representative $\Lambda$CDM system [Wyse et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph..2636W/abstract), [Wyse et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IAUS..254..179W/abstract). Nevertheless, the outer disk continues to exhibit signs of minor dynamical interactions. For instance, a kinematically cold structure of young OB stars located $12\text{--}15 \text{ kpc}$ from the Galactic center displays vertical and radial motions indicative of a disk "wobble." This perturbation was likely excited by a massive satellite interacting with the Galaxy's disk approximately $200 \text{ Myr}$ ago [Casetti-Dinescu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...883....8C/abstract).

## Current Challenges and Future Directions

Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations and semi-analytic models have become cornerstone tools for interpreting the structural assembly of galaxies over cosmic time. By modeling the complex interplay of dark matter, gas accretion, and stellar feedback, these simulations provide a theoretical counterpart to observational data. For example, recent simulations like IllustrisTNG have been used to calibrate the timescale of structural asymmetries during mergers, finding a median rest-frame optical asymmetry duration of $0.50_{-0.18}^{+0.33}$ Gyr [Whitney et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919..139W/abstract). 

Despite these theoretical successes, [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] presents significant challenges. Observations reveal that galaxies at $z > 1$ are often compact, star-forming, and structurally peculiar, with sizes 2 to 5 times smaller than their local counterparts at a fixed stellar mass [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). Quantitative structural analyses indicate that merger rates increase with redshift as approximately $(1+z)^{1.87 \pm 0.04}$ Gyr$^{-1}$ [Whitney et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...919..139W/abstract). However, there is a pronounced tension between observations and standard hierarchical $\Lambda$CDM models. While hierarchical clustering predicts that the most massive galaxies should assemble last, spectroscopic surveys have identified a high abundance of massive, quiescent galaxies already in place just 3 Gyr after the Big Bang [Glazebrook et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Natur.430..181G/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract). Reconciling the early appearance of these massive systems with "inside-out" [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] remains an active area of debate.

Another major unresolved frontier concerns [[galaxy-halo-scales|Galaxy-halo scales]] and the precise mapping of baryons to dark matter profiles. While the standard paradigm relies on extended dark matter halos to produce flat rotation curves and govern large-scale structure, observational discrepancies persist. For instance, the expected number of low-velocity-dispersion dark halos predicted by simulations greatly exceeds the observed dwarf galaxy population, prompting debates over whether High Velocity Clouds might represent these "missing" halos [Blitz et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph..1307B/abstract). Furthermore, the structural complexity of galaxies is fundamentally challenged by observations of optically unselected [[h-i-rich-galaxies|H I-rich galaxies]], which exhibit tight correlations suggesting their global structure may be governed by a single parameter. This apparent simplicity directly contradicts hierarchical formation theories, which model galaxy structure as the product of at least six independent parameters and a highly stochastic merger history [Disney et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455.1082D/abstract). Discrepancies also remain regarding the precise shape of dark matter halos and the evolutionary development of galactic rotation curves [Friedmann et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0912.1668F/abstract).

Resolving these tensions requires pushing the limits of [[spatially-resolved-galaxy-analysis|Spatially Resolved Galaxy Analysis]]. The field is rapidly transitioning from subjective [[galaxy-morphological-classification|Galaxy morphological classification]] to automated, non-parametric structural indices (such as Concentration, Asymmetry, and Clumpiness) to process the billions of galaxies expected from next-generation surveys [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract), [Wadadekar et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1201.2252W/abstract). Novel methodologies, such as the use of inclination-independent "linear brightness profiles" [Devour et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..244....3D/abstract) and Bayesian deconvolution incorporating diffusion model priors, are now enabling the extraction of faint structural features in galaxy populations [Adam et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....169..254A/abstract). Combining these advanced automated pipelines with future deep-field observations will be critical for identifying the physical drivers of galaxy structure and resolving the standing tensions in cosmological assembly models.
