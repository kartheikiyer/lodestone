## Article

**Galaxy property distributions** describe the statistical frequency and multi-dimensional correlations of physical characteristics—such as luminosity, stellar mass, color, and morphology—across observed [[galaxy-populations|Galaxy populations]]. Because galaxies are complex systems shaped by highly non-linear baryonic physics and dark matter halo assembly, individual objects exhibit vast diversity. However, when analyzed as an aggregated population, galaxies occupy tightly constrained regions of parameter space. Projecting these multidimensional distributions onto single observable axes yields fundamental demographic measures like the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and the [[mass-distribution-and-functions|stellar mass function]], which are often parameterized by the [[schechter-function|Schechter function]] to identify characteristic scales of galaxy assembly. Another prominent statistical feature of the local universe is [[galactic-bimodality|Galactic Bimodality]]; the [[color-magnitude-relation|Color-magnitude relation]] cleanly divides the population into a star-forming "blue cloud" and a quiescent [[red-sequence|Red sequence]], reflecting distinct evolutionary pathways and star formation histories [Lahav et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASA...21..404L/abstract). 

Historically anchored by visual classifications like the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]], the study of [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] has increasingly relied on quantitative, non-parametric morphometrics to trace structural evolution over cosmic time [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). Extensive observations demonstrate that a galaxy's structure, kinematics, and chemical abundances (e.g., [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]], [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]) are not strictly independent. Instead, they form tight [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]], such as the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] and the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]], which suggest that populations are confined to a highly organized, low-dimensional [[galaxy-manifold|Galaxy Manifold]]. Furthermore, these intrinsic distributions are strongly modulated by external factors. The [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]] illustrates that dense cluster environments preferentially host early-type, passive systems, highlighting the crucial role that environmental quenching plays in truncating star formation [Oemler et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844...45O/abstract).

Beyond evaluating their internal properties, astronomers map the spatial distributions of these populations to trace the underlying dark matter density field. Through [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|Galaxy Bias and Clustering Statistics]], spatial correlations can trace the underlying density field, a foundational concept established in early reviews [Fall et al. (1979)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1979RvMP...51...21F/abstract). In modern analyses, variations in clustering strength as a function of luminosity and color are linked directly to theoretical dark matter halo models. This article reviews the historical development of large-scale [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]] and modern [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]], exploring how key statistical measures of the galaxy population have evolved from the early universe to the present day. Finally, it outlines current frontiers in the field, including the application of machine learning to disentangle cosmology from astrophysical feedback, and the ongoing theoretical debates surrounding the precise physical mechanisms of galaxy quenching and [[anisotropic-galaxy-assembly-bias|Anisotropic galaxy assembly bias]].

## Fundamental Statistical Measures: Luminosity and Mass Functions

The statistical distribution of galaxies with respect to their intrinsic properties can be formally described by a multivariate [[probability-density-function-cosmology|probability density function]]. Projecting this parameter space onto individual observable properties yields fundamental one-dimensional demographic measures, most notably the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] and the [[mass-distribution-and-functions|stellar mass function]]. These core statistics quantify the comoving [[number-density|Number density]] of galaxies per unit volume as a function of their total light output or stellar mass, serving as foundational constraints for theories of structure formation and galaxy assembly.

Observationally, constructing these distributions from flux-limited [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]] requires correcting for Malmquist bias—the preferential detection of intrinsically brighter objects at larger cosmological distances. A standard non-parametric approach to compute the true volume-weighted number density is the [[v-vmax-test|V/Vmax test]], where each observed galaxy is weighted by $1/V_{\rm max}$, the inverse of the maximum comoving volume within which it could have been detected given the survey limits. Alternatively, when relying on purely photometric surveys, researchers can employ spatial [[cross-correlation-analysis|cross-correlation methods]] to recover accurate redshift distributions by exploiting the clustering information shared with overlapping spectroscopic catalogs [Schmidt et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431.3307S/abstract). Expanding upon this technique, cross-correlation methods can also simultaneously yield [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|accurate redshift-dependent luminosity functions]] and [[redshift-distribution|redshift distributions]] using only clustering and apparent magnitudes, effectively bypassing the need for individual photometric redshifts [van Daalen et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.4649V/abstract). Additionally, angular two-point correlation functions can be used to reconstruct the redshift distributions of galaxies binned by photometric redshift, although this approach introduces significant parameter-dependent uncertainties [Schneider et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...651...14S/abstract).

The resulting distributions are typically modeled using the [[schechter-function|Schechter function]], an analytical approximation that describes the number density of galaxies per unit luminosity. The shape of this distribution reveals a fundamental characteristic scale in the galaxy assembly process. While faint galaxies numerically dominate the total cosmic number density, galaxies near the characteristic "break" luminosity (comparable in brightness to the Milky Way) contribute the majority of the integrated luminosity density in the local Universe. 

The shape of the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] varies significantly with waveband, [[galaxy-morphological-classification|Galaxy morphological classification]], and environment. For example, local environmental overdensities can significantly alter the luminosity function, such as causing a distinct deficiency of faint red galaxies in underdense regions, creating structural "holes" in the sky [Phleps et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASPC..379..327P/abstract). The bright end is overwhelmingly populated by early-type, [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] galaxies, while the faint end is dominated by irregulars in the field and dwarf ellipticals in clusters. The origins of these ubiquitous low-mass systems reflect diverse formation pathways, ranging from early cosmological collapse in low-mass halos at high redshift to "nurture"-driven formation at later epochs due to environmental tidal interactions [Roukema et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..6111R/abstract).

Because a galaxy's total luminosity evolves as its [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] age—younger populations exhibit a higher luminosity per unit stellar mass—the [[mass-distribution-and-functions|stellar mass function]] provides a more direct physical probe of baryonic assembly. However, converting observed spectral energy distributions into stellar masses introduces systematic uncertainties, particularly regarding the universality of the assumed [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. 

Despite these complexities, theoretical frameworks closely tie the stellar mass function to the underlying dark matter distribution. At the halo level, the median stellar mass of a galaxy displays a strong, monotonic dependence on the mass of its host dark matter subhalo [Contreras et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1861C/abstract). Phenomenological models linking baryonic growth rates and star formation efficiencies directly to halo formation histories have successfully reproduced both the red and blue stellar mass functions in the local Universe, as well as the global stellar mass function out to $z = 3$ [Mutch et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.2445M/abstract).

## The Bimodal Color-Magnitude Relation

The distribution of galaxy colors as a function of their absolute magnitude reveals a pronounced [[galactic-bimodality|Galactic Bimodality]]. Large-scale redshift surveys have demonstrated that the galaxy population naturally divides into two distinct loci in the [[color-magnitude-relation|Color-magnitude relation]] [Lahav et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASA...21..404L/abstract). To properly quantify this bimodality, volume corrections such as $1/V_{\rm max}$ weighting (see [[v-vmax-test|V/Vmax test]]) must be applied to account for selection biases in flux-limited samples. The resulting distribution exhibits a broad "blue cloud," predominantly populated by actively star-forming galaxies, and a tightly correlated [[red-sequence|Red sequence]], consisting largely of massive, [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] with older stellar populations. Furthermore, detailed structural studies have revealed that diffuse dust concentrations in the disks of disky E-type galaxies create specific signatures in major-axis reddening and minor-axis color asymmetry, modulating their observed light [Michard et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&A...335..479M/abstract). Notably, the relative dominance of these populations varies with mass and luminosity: the faint end of the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] is heavily dominated by blue galaxies, whereas the [[red-sequence|Red sequence]] strongly dominates at the bright end.

Separating these two primary populations is a more sparsely populated transitional zone known as the "green valley." Galaxies within this region are generally undergoing quenching—the cessation of star formation that causes a galaxy to migrate from the blue cloud to the red sequence. Recent analyses indicate the presence of an unexpectedly large population of quiescent galaxies residing between the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] and fully passive systems [Oemler et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844...45O/abstract). The evolution away from the main sequence can largely be attributed to the progressive depletion of cold gas reservoirs following a cutoff of gas inflow. In this framework, galaxies enter a quiescent state when their disk gas density drops below a critical stability threshold, though complete gas exhaustion and subsequent reddening likely require additional physical processes, such as wind-driven mass loss [Oemler et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844...45O/abstract).

A galaxy's position on the [[color-magnitude-relation|Color-magnitude relation]] is strongly coupled to its local environment. Denser environments, such as galaxy clusters, host a significantly higher [[early-type-fraction|Early-type fraction]] and larger proportions of massive, gas-poor red sequence galaxies compared to the field. This environmental dependence demonstrates that dense regions accelerate quenching through external mechanisms like ram-pressure stripping or tidal interactions. For example, the blue, low-mass galaxies observed in clusters are almost exclusively recent infallers that have not yet been quenched by the cluster environment [Martínez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250204446M/abstract). Conversely, "backsplash" galaxies—those that have passed through the cluster core and are temporarily outside the virial radius—are found to be systematically redder than expected, underscoring the efficiency of environmental quenching [Martínez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250204446M/abstract).

While environmental effects dominate the quenching of satellite galaxies, the truncation of star formation in massive central galaxies is frequently attributed to feedback from active galactic nuclei (AGN). However, the exact role of AGN feedback in rapidly terminating star formation remains a subject of active debate in the literature. Furthermore, assessing the exact impact of active nuclei on host colors requires distinguishing between different accretion modes; for instance, low-luminosity AGNs often lack the "big blue bump" (ultraviolet excess) characteristic of highly luminous quasars, which significantly alters their ionizing spectrum and net contribution to the host galaxy's composite color [Ho et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...516..672H/abstract). Studies of global molecular gas properties reveal that galaxies hosting AGNs actually exhibit systematically higher molecular gas masses across various quenching stages compared to non-active galaxies [Bazzi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A.149B/abstract). Furthermore, the star formation properties and scaling relations of AGN hosts are largely consistent with those of non-active quenching galaxies, showing no strong signatures of instantaneous AGN feedback on the global molecular gas reservoirs [Bazzi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A.149B/abstract). This suggests that while AGNs may be present during the transition across the green valley, their role may be more complex than simply instantaneously expelling the star-forming gas.

## Structural Parameters and Morphological Classification

Historically, the study of galaxy structure has relied on qualitative visual categorization based on the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]]. This system effectively separates the local galaxy population into rotation-supported disks (spirals), pressure-supported spheroids (ellipticals), and lenticulars (S0 galaxies). However, the classical Hubble scheme is widely considered inadequate for characterizing the complex, heavily disturbed, and low-surface-brightness systems frequently observed at higher redshifts [van den Bergh et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Natur.445..265V/abstract). Early attempts to visually categorize anomalous systems often fell prey to observational biases. For example, Zwicky's famously cataloged "compact galaxies" were eventually shown not to constitute a distinct physical class; rather, they are largely conventional galaxies whose outer regions were tidally stripped, or objects subject to specific photographic selection effects [Fairall et al. (1978)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1978Obs....98....1F/abstract). With the advent of massive digital sky surveys, [[galaxy-morphological-classification|Galaxy morphological classification]] has largely transitioned from visual inspection to quantitative, automated morphometrics [Wadadekar et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1201.2252W/abstract), [Holwerda et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021gamo.book.....H/abstract). Despite the transition to automated metrics, comprehensive reviews of morphological phenomenology demonstrate that refined classification types remain essential; these detailed visual and descriptive categories robustly link a galaxy's structure to internal perturbations, active galactic nuclei (AGN), and its overarching star formation history [Buta et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1102.0550B/abstract).

A foundational parametric approach utilizes the [[sersic-profile|Sersic profile]] to measure galaxy sizes and surface brightness profile shapes [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). Such measurements reveal that the spatial distribution of light is intimately tied to other global parameters; for example, as demonstrated by Shen et al. (2003) using SDSS data, the size distribution of local galaxies at a fixed luminosity is well approximated by a log-normal function. Over cosmic time, parametric fitting demonstrates significant structural evolution: at a given stellar mass, galaxies at high redshift were a factor of 2 to 5 smaller than their local counterparts [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). 

In addition to photometric profiles, the structural modeling of early-type galaxies heavily relies on their kinematics. To characterize the internal dynamics of pressure-supported ellipticals, researchers frequently use generalized Osipkov-Merritt inversions to model the two-parameter distribution functions of anisotropic spherical galaxies, allowing for exact analytical models of various density profiles [Cuddeford et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991MNRAS.253..414C/abstract). Observationally, such studies reveal that constant velocity dispersions and a lack of rotation along the minor axis are common properties in ellipticals, signaling significant anisotropy variations in their velocity fields [Bertola et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988A&A...192...24B/abstract).

Because parametric models often fail to capture complex, irregular structures, non-parametric structural indices have become standard tools for evaluating galaxy morphology. Prominent among these are the [[cas-parameters|CAS parameters]] (Concentration, Asymmetry, and Clumpiness) and the [[gini-coefficient|Gini coefficient]] combined with $M_{20}$ (the second-order moment of the brightest 20% of the galaxy's pixels) [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). These indices measure fundamental physical properties without requiring a predefined morphological center. Concentration and the Gini coefficient broadly track the central mass distribution and bulge fraction, while Asymmetry and $M_{20}$ are highly sensitive to morphological disturbances and tidal features. Consequently, the parameter spaces defined by these non-parametric indices are widely used to accurately identify galaxies undergoing active mergers and to measure the merger history of the universe out to $z \sim 3$ [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract).

Applying these quantitative structural metrics across cosmic time demonstrates that galaxy structures have evolved significantly. At $z \sim 3$, the population was heavily dominated by small, highly asymmetric, and peculiar systems. The classical [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] is thus a relatively late-forming phenomenon, emerging dynamically as these chaotic progenitors underwent structural quenching and settled into stable disks and spheroids [Ellis et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.395A...3E/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). 

Concurrently, the need for extensive morphological catalogs in the local universe has been met by combining these automated metrics with massive citizen science initiatives. Projects such as Galaxy Zoo have provided quantitative visual classifications for over a million galaxies, proving invaluable for disentangling the many variables that affect galaxy assembly and confirming that morphological transformation is strongly correlated with both local density and internal dynamics [Wadadekar et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1201.2252W/abstract), [Masters et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..353..205M/abstract).

## Scaling Relations and the Galaxy Manifold

Although galaxies exhibit an enormous diversity in their [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]], their global structural and kinematic parameters are not independent. Instead, they are bound by tight empirical [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] that represent stable equilibrium states governed by the virial theorem and the regulated physics of star formation and feedback. 

For rotation-supported disk galaxies, the fundamental correlation is the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]], which links a galaxy's total luminosity or stellar mass to its asymptotic rotation velocity. Detailed mapping of velocity fields and velocity dispersions allows researchers to study both the local and global stability of these disks, as well as their underlying mass distributions [van der Kruit et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990mwg..conf..185V/abstract). Conversely, dispersion-supported elliptical galaxies populate the Fundamental Plane, a tight logarithmic relation connecting their effective radius, central velocity dispersion, and mean surface brightness. Similarly, the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] dictates that galaxy sizes—which measure specific angular momentum in disks and the degree of dissipation in ellipticals—scale monotonically with stellar mass. Although historically treated as distinct phenomenological rules, these kinematic scaling relations can be unified. By explicitly accounting for systematic variations in the total mass-to-light ratio within the half-light radius, both the Fundamental Plane and the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] can be shown to naturally emerge from the virial theorem, forming a continuous sequence that successfully extends to low-mass spheroidal galaxies and stellar clusters [Zaritsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ISRAA2012E..12Z/abstract). Complementing these baryonic scaling laws, observations have revealed a universal relation for matter distributions that holds across all observed scales, from dwarf spheroidals to massive galaxy clusters; this relation provides powerful new evidence for dark matter and aligns tightly with numerical simulations [Boyarsky et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0911.1774B/abstract).

The tightness and ubiquity of these scaling relations point to a fundamental simplicity in galaxy formation that is somewhat in tension with the stochastic nature of hierarchical assembly. According to standard cold dark matter ($\Lambda$CDM) models, a galaxy's [[global-observables-of-galaxies|Global observables of galaxies]] should depend on a complex, multi-parameter interplay of mass, angular momentum, merger history, and baryonic feedback. However, empirical studies have shown that H I-selected galaxies exhibit up to five independent correlations among six observables, suggesting that their structure is effectively controlled by a single parameter [Disney et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455.1082D/abstract). Consequently, galaxies do not uniformly populate the available parameter space; rather, they are tightly confined to a low-dimensional "[[galaxy-manifold|Galaxy Manifold]]".

Recent analyses of cosmological hydrodynamical simulations have demonstrated that the structure of this galaxy manifold encodes not only local baryonic physics but also global cosmological parameters. Autoencoder-based dimensionality reduction reveals that parameters such as the total matter density $\Omega_m$ and supernova feedback efficiency directly shift the manifold into new regions of the high-dimensional property space [Lue et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986..133L/abstract). Because these parameters define the absolute position of the manifold rather than just scattering galaxies along it (as is the case for the clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$), it is theoretically possible to infer cosmological parameters like $\Omega_m$ from the internal properties of even a single simulated galaxy [Lue et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250217568L/abstract). The existence of this low-dimensional manifold highlights a profound, underlying self-organization in galaxy evolution that remains an active area of theoretical investigation through [[mock-galaxy-surveys-and-numerical-modeling|Mock Galaxy Surveys and Numerical Modeling]].

## Environmental Dependence and the Morphology-Density Relation

The statistical distribution of galaxy morphologies exhibits a profound dependence on local environmental density, a phenomenon classically formalized as the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]]. Observations establish that denser environments, particularly the cores of massive galaxy clusters, host a substantially higher fraction of early-type, [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] compared to the general field. Specifically, the fraction of spiral galaxies decreases from $\sim 60\%$ in low-density regions to less than $10\%$ in the highest-density cluster cores, while the combined fraction of elliptical and lenticular (S0) galaxies correspondingly rises from $\sim 30\%$ in the field to $\sim 80\%$ in regular, relaxed clusters. Notably, while the S0 fraction is significantly elevated in clusters relative to the field, it does not show a strong density gradient within the clusters themselves.

The evolutionary nature of this relationship is highlighted by the [[butcher-oemler-effect|Butcher-Oemler effect]], which describes a dramatic increase in the fraction of blue, star-forming galaxies in intermediate-redshift clusters ($0.3 \lesssim z \lesssim 0.5$) compared to their present-day counterparts. High-resolution morphological studies confirm that many of these blue cluster galaxies are spirals exhibiting disturbed morphologies, suggesting that the established local [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] in dense environments is the result of relatively recent dynamical and morphological transformations. 

The physical mechanisms driving these transformations are intrinsically tied to [[galaxy-cluster-properties-and-scaling-relations|Galaxy Cluster Properties and Scaling Relations]]. Clusters are the largest virialized structures in the Universe, typically characterized by total masses of $10^{14}$--$10^{15} M_\odot$ and velocity dispersions of $\sim 1000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$. However, determining these cluster masses requires explicit corrections for environmental substructure, as the presence of substructure significantly influences the mean galaxy separation and dynamical mass estimates [Bird et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...445L..81B/abstract). Galaxies entering these dense environments are subjected to tidal interactions and ram pressure stripping by the hot Intracluster medium. These processes efficiently truncate cold gas inflows, leading to the depletion of molecular gas and the subsequent quenching of star formation [Oemler et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844...45O/abstract). Consequently, the disk brightness of spirals is systematically reduced in high-density regions [Kent et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981ApJ...245..805K/abstract). Phase-space analyses of cluster populations reveal that the blue, low-mass galaxies found in clusters are almost exclusively recent infallers that have not yet been quenched, whereas "backsplash" galaxies—those that have passed through the cluster core and exited—are systematically redder than expected [Martínez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250204446M/abstract).

Despite clear observational signatures, the literature remains ambiguous regarding which environmental variable is the fundamental causal driver of quenching. Because local galaxy density, host halo mass, and position within the cosmic web are heavily correlated, distinguishing the precise mechanism is complex. Furthermore, while active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback is often invoked as a primary quenching agent in dense environments, its role is contested. Recent integral-field spectroscopic surveys of local galaxies indicate a lack of prominent "instantaneous" AGN feedback signatures on global molecular gas reservoirs; in fact, AGN hosts often exhibit systematically higher molecular gas masses than non-active galaxies at similar evolutionary stages [Bazzi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A.149B/abstract). Thus, while the environment undeniably acts to accelerate morphological evolution and the transition to a quiescent state [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract), isolating the precise interplay between internal feedback processes and external environmental triggers remains an active area of debate.

## Galaxy Clustering and the Dark Matter Halo Connection

The spatial distribution of galaxies provides a critical probe of both the underlying cosmic matter density field and the physics of galaxy formation. Galaxies are not randomly distributed; they trace a complex cosmic web of filaments, sheets, and nodes dictated by dark matter. These structures exhibit complex irregular patterns with fractal properties, organized predominantly in dense filaments surrounding vast, empty voids [Pietronero et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005cmn..conf...91P/abstract).

To characterize this structure, [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|Galaxy Bias and Clustering Statistics]] frequently employ the two-point correlation function, $\xi(r)$, which measures the excess probability over a random distribution of finding a galaxy pair separated by a comoving distance $r$. For optically selected galaxies with luminosities near the characteristic scale $L^*$, the real-space correlation function is well-approximated by a power law on scales $r \lesssim 20\ h^{-1}\ \text{Mpc}$:
$$ \xi(r) = \left( \frac{r}{r_0} \right)^{-\gamma} $$
where the logarithmic slope is typically $\gamma \approx 1.8$ and the correlation length is $r_0 \approx 5\ h^{-1}\ \text{Mpc}$. Beyond the basic two-point function, researchers also examine higher-order statistics to capture the full complexity of the cosmic web. By applying generating functions and factorial moments, astronomers can formalize the hierarchy of [[n-point-correlation-function|N-point correlation functions]] and systematically quantify the non-Gaussian nature of the galaxy distribution [Szapudi et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...408...43S/abstract). On larger scales, where density fluctuations remain in the linear regime, it is often more advantageous to analyze the power spectrum—the Fourier transform of the two-point correlation function—because different Fourier modes evolve independently. Studying the evolution of this large-scale structure with time acts as a profound theoretical probe, allowing astronomers to tightly constrain cosmological parameters such as the universal mass density and the nature of dark matter itself [Strauss et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.395A...9S/abstract).

Galaxies are biased tracers of the underlying mass distribution. The clustering strength, parameterized by $r_0$, depends robustly on internal galaxy properties, with more luminous and redder galaxies exhibiting stronger clustering than their fainter, bluer counterparts. To rigorously quantify how internal galaxy properties (such as luminosity or color) correlate with this spatial environment without arbitrarily splitting the sample into discrete sub-populations, astronomers frequently employ "[[mark-correlation-function|marked correlations]]"—a statistical technique that weights the traditional correlation function by the property of interest [Sheth et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.11773S/abstract). Furthermore, the earliest galaxies in the high-redshift universe are observed to cluster significantly more strongly than the underlying mass density [Bagla et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998lsst.conf..253B/abstract). The relationship between the galaxy density field and the total matter density field is typically defined by a linear bias parameter $b$, where $\xi_{gg}(r) \approx b^2 \xi_{mm}(r)$. To analytically capture the multiscale nature of this clustering, theorists employ threshold functions to describe how the absorption of smaller scale structures within larger ones systematically affects expected galaxy number densities in biased models [Borgani et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989A&A...215...17B/abstract).

Modern theoretical frameworks link galaxy properties to the dark matter web via the galaxy-halo connection, often parameterized using a halo occupancy distribution or subhalo abundance matching. While median properties like stellar mass and central black hole mass show a monotonic dependence on the host dark matter subhalo mass, simple subhalo abundance matching models frequently over-predict the amplitude of the observed clustering signal [Contreras et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1861C/abstract). 

This discrepancy highlights the role of "assembly bias"—the phenomenon where halo clustering depends on secondary parameters independent of halo mass. For massive cluster-size halos, secondary biases are strongly driven by halo concentration, spin, and subhalo abundance, rather than solely by the halo's mass accretion history [Mao et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5143M/abstract). The evolution of these dark matter halo properties is highly dynamic; while major mergers are dramatic, minor mergers also play a critically important role in driving halo relaxation and property evolution (such as altering spin and shape), often showing effects similar in magnitude to those of major mergers [Wu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv171109308W/abstract). Moreover, the host halo's "dynamical age"—its degree of virial relaxation since its last major merger—serves as a crucial secondary parameter that largely determines the evolutionary state of its baryonic contents and influences observable distributions [Taylor et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AdAst2011E...6T/abstract). In [[mock-galaxy-surveys-and-numerical-modeling|Mock Galaxy Surveys and Numerical Modeling]], the manifestation of this galaxy assembly bias is highly complex, altering clustering amplitudes by up to 25% depending on the specific selection criteria, number density, and redshift of the galaxy sample [García-Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250406770G/abstract). No single secondary halo property fully captures this effect across all galaxy populations, necessitating multivariate approaches to faithfully model clustering [García-Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250406770G/abstract).

Finally, the scale at which the universe transitions to true statistical homogeneity remains a point of active debate. While analyses of large empirical surveys indicate that galaxy distributions reach homogeneity at scales of roughly $140\ h^{-1}\ \text{Mpc}$ [Pandey et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.2647P/abstract), some researchers identify persistent large-scale inhomogeneities up to $\sim 100\ h^{-1}\ \text{Mpc}$ that are in tension with the "super-homogeneity" expected in standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmologies [Sylos Labini et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0912.1191S/abstract).

## Chemical Evolution and Stellar Population Distributions

The distributions of stellar ages and metallicities within and across galaxies act as a fundamental fossil record, preserving the history of gas accretion, star formation, and environmental interactions over cosmic time [Wyse et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.10025W/abstract). For resolved systems like the Milky Way, the kinematics, ages, and chemical abundances of individual long-lived stars can be measured directly; consequently, large photometric and spectroscopic surveys of stars serve as essential keystones for investigating the detailed merging history of the Galaxy [Wyse et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995PASP..107..785W/abstract). However, for unresolved extragalactic populations, astronomers must infer these properties from integrated light.

To extract these physical parameters from broadband photometry and spectroscopy, researchers rely on [[traditional-sed-fitting-techniques|Traditional SED fitting techniques]] and Stellar Population Synthesis. By modeling the panchromatic spectral energy distribution (SED) of a galaxy, variables such as the star-formation history, dust mass, and [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|Metallicity Distributions and Scaling Relations]] can be constrained [Conroy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ARA&A..51..393C/abstract), [da Cunha et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ascl.soft06010D/abstract). The treatment of dust is particularly crucial; for example, applying supernova-origin dust extinction curves to infrared galaxies at $z \sim 1$ demonstrates how the specific geometric distribution of dust strongly dictates the overall attenuation of starlight [Kawara et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412.1070K/abstract). A cornerstone of these synthesis models is the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], which defines the birth mass distribution of a stellar population. The exact nature of the IMF is currently the greatest source of systematic uncertainty in galaxy evolution models. The literature remains in active conflict over whether the IMF is strictly universal, or whether it varies systematically with local environment, star formation rate, or metallicity [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...39H/abstract), [Conroy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ARA&A..51..393C/abstract).

Detailed chemical modeling tracks the time-dependent injection of heavy elements from stellar winds and supernovae. Analyzing the Age-metallicity relation and detailed abundance patterns places strict constraints on the astrophysical sources of specific elements. For instance, high-performance analytical models of galactic chemical evolution demonstrate that neutron star mergers, rather than collapsars, must be the dominant source of the modern $r$-process element budget in the Milky Way [Fraser et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.6008F/abstract). Furthermore, comparing the integrated properties of host galaxies to the observed rates of explosive transients provides insights into stellar endpoints. Studies of Type Ia supernova hosts suggest a characteristic delay time lower limit of $\sim 2.0$ Gyr, with evidence indicating that progenitor age, rather than the host galaxy's metallicity, is the primary driver of variability in Type Ia peak luminosities [Gallagher et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...634..210G/abstract). Furthermore, accurately assessing the global star formation rates and total chemical budgets requires considering the chemical composition of dark baryonic matter alongside the visible matter within these systems [Pagel et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001PASP..113..137P/abstract).

Connecting these empirically derived stellar population distributions to hierarchical assembly frameworks requires sophisticated forward modeling, such as semi-analytic models (SAMs). A persistent tension in theoretical models is the inability to simultaneously reproduce the observed number densities of high-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies and massive [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] (quiescent populations). Reconciling the disparate distributions of these extreme early populations often requires robust Markov Chain Monte Carlo calibration, invoking highly efficient star formation during galaxy mergers and decoupling the cold gas accretion of supermassive black holes from host halo mass [Araya-Araya et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250415283A/abstract), [Araya-Araya et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.542.2808A/abstract). 

To better disentangle the complex degeneracies between assembly history, mass, and chemistry, recent efforts have focused on extracting 2D and 3D maps of galactic attributes. Comprehensive datasets capturing stellar age, metallicity, and [[mass-distribution-and-functions|stellar mass function]] distributions across thousands of galaxies are increasingly being paired with machine learning algorithms to isolate the fundamental components controlling galaxy evolution [Çakır et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231206016C/abstract).

## Historical Development of Population Surveys

The study of galaxy populations has undergone a radical transformation, evolving from the manual compilation of photographic plates to the automated processing of massive digital datasets. In the context of early [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]], pioneering efforts to quantify the spatial and morphological distributions of galaxies relied exclusively on visual inspections of the sky. Pioneering [[large-galaxy-catalogs|Large galaxy catalogs]] such as the [[zwicky-catalogue|Zwicky Catalogue]], which provided early samples of 14th magnitude galaxies for statistical clustering analyses [Barrow et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984MNRAS.210P..19B/abstract), and the [[abell-catalog-of-clusters|Abell Catalog of Clusters]] provided the first systemic maps of the local universe. These early visual catalogs were instrumental in identifying the large-scale clustering of galaxies and establishing foundational demographic trends, such as the [[morphology-density-relation|Morphology-density relation]]. Parallel to these galaxy-centric catalogs, the REFLEX cluster survey emerged as a major historical effort dedicated to weighing the cluster population and probing large-scale cosmological motions through X-ray selected samples [Böhringer et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001Msngr.106...24B/abstract).

Even before the era of massive digital datasets, early theoretical work established that collective galaxy properties like counts and color-redshift relations are highly sensitive to galactic evolution, famously predicting bluer populations at redshifts of 0.5 [Tinsley et al. (1977)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977ApJ...211..621T/abstract). The transition toward modern [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]] began with automated plate-measuring machines, which digitized photographic surveys to produce datasets like the [[apm-galaxy-catalogue|APM galaxy catalogue]]. This catalog served as the astrometric and photometric bedrock for the first generation of massive spectroscopic surveys. At the turn of the 21st century, observational cosmology entered the era of mega-surveys, dominated by two major campaigns. The 2dFGRS ([[2dfgrs-galaxy-catalogue|2dFGRS galaxy catalogue]]) utilized multi-object fiber spectroscopy to obtain redshifts for approximately $220,000$ galaxies down to a limiting magnitude of $b_J \sim 19.45$. Covering an effective volume of $\sim 10^8 h^{-3} \text{ Mpc}^3$ at a median redshift of $z \sim 0.1$, the 2dFGRS enabled unprecedented constraints on the large-scale galaxy distribution and power spectrum [Peacock et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001defi.conf..221P/abstract), [Couch et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004cgpc.symp...42C/abstract). Contemporaneously, deep spectroscopic campaigns like the K20 survey became early key projects for probing the evolution of massive galaxies and directly testing competing galaxy formation scenarios at higher redshifts [Cimatti et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003mglh.conf..124C/abstract).

Concurrently, the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) revolutionized the field by combining a dedicated photometric imaging survey in five custom broadbands ($u, g, r, i, z$) with a massive spectroscopic campaign. The initial SDSS targeted $\sim 10^6$ galaxies brighter than $r < 17.77$ and $\sim 10^5$ quasars. This provided the immense statistical power necessary to rigorously define the local [[color-magnitude-relation|Color-magnitude relation]] and robustly characterize [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]]. 

As sample sizes grew from thousands to millions, the methodology for classifying [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] also shifted. There is ongoing debate regarding the adequacy of the traditional [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] classification scheme for modern digital surveys; visual typing often proves inadequate for capturing the complexity of distant, low-surface-brightness, or irregular systems [van den Bergh et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Natur.445..265V/abstract). This tension prompted a paradigm shift toward quantitative, non-parametric morphometrics, utilizing structural indices such as the [[cas-parameters|CAS parameters]] (concentration, asymmetry, and clumpiness), alongside the [[gini-coefficient|Gini coefficient]] and $M_{20}$ indices [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). At the same time, citizen science initiatives like Galaxy Zoo harnessed crowd-sourced visual classifications for nearly $900,000$ SDSS galaxies, mitigating individual classifier bias and providing robust morphological labels for subsequent machine-learning applications [Lintott et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410..166L/abstract), [Masters et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..353..205M/abstract).

Modern population surveys now actively probe the [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] frontier. Expanding upon the local statistical benchmarks set by the SDSS and 2dFGRS, deep multi-wavelength campaigns have mapped galaxies out to $z \sim 3$ and beyond. These surveys allow astronomers to track the [[number-density-evolution|Number density evolution]] and structural assembly of galaxies across cosmic time, revealing a clear evolutionary sequence: the early universe was dominated by compact, heavily clustered, and morphologically peculiar star-forming systems that only later settled into the stable Hubble sequence observed locally [Ellis et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.395A...3E/abstract), [Steidel et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RSPTA.357..153S/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). Looking forward, future very large, high-density spectroscopic surveys at high redshifts are recognized as essential for definitively understanding the complex links between galaxies and their large-scale cosmological environments [Dickinson et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019BAAS...51c.538D/abstract).

## Redshift Evolution and Cosmic Assembly History

Tracking the property distributions of galaxies across cosmic time provides critical constraints on the cosmological assembly history of the universe. Observations spanning from $z > 6$ to the local volume reveal a profound [[morphological-transformation|Morphological transformation]] within the galaxy population. At $z > 1$, galaxies exhibit a high frequency of compact, irregular, and merger-driven shapes, which transition only at later epochs into the stable spirals and ellipticals characteristic of the local [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] [Ellis et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.395A...3E/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). 

A defining feature of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] is the strong redshift dependence of galaxy sizes. For a fixed stellar mass, high-redshift galaxies are observed to be a factor of $2$ to $5$ times smaller than their local counterparts [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). To interpret these distant observations, astronomers leverage the integrated spectral energy distributions of distant galaxies, which provide direct information on the change of stellar population properties with redshift [Oemler et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986stpo.meet..197O/abstract). Furthermore, morphological study at high redshift often involves directly comparing faint galaxy counts and color distributions with the precise predictions of detailed evolutionary models [Bruzual et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ASSL..141..161B/abstract). To quantify this structural evolution objectively in large digital surveys, modern morphometric analyses rely heavily on nonparametric spatial indices—such as the [[cas-parameters|CAS parameters]] (concentration, asymmetry, clumpiness), Gini coefficient, and $M_{20}$. These metrics effectively identify interacting and merging systems without assuming structural symmetry, allowing researchers to trace the [[merger-rate-density|Merger rate density]] and structural quenching over cosmic time [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract).

The structural maturation of galaxies is tightly coupled to the evolution of the global star formation rate density, $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$. Measurements of [[number-density-evolution|Number density evolution]] as a function of luminosity demonstrate that $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$ peaked between $z \approx 1$ and $3$ and has systematically declined since. This decline is strongly modulated by environment. Studies of rich clusters at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 0.3 - 0.5$) reveal the [[butcher-oemler-effect|Butcher-Oemler effect]], marked by a dramatically higher fraction of blue, star-forming galaxies—many exhibiting disturbed morphologies—compared to the passive, early-type populations dominating present-day clusters. To trace these evolutionary pathways and robustly link early progenitors to their local [[descendant-galaxies|Descendant galaxies]], observers often select samples using a constant cumulative [[number-density|Number density]] threshold (e.g., $n \approx 10^{-4} \, h^{3} \mathrm{Mpc}^{-3}$), which is statistically more reliable than tracking a constant stellar mass limit over large redshift baselines [Mundy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.3696M/abstract).

The physical drivers of mass assembly during these active epochs remain a subject of active research. To comprehensively model this evolution, researchers often employ a "continuity equation" approach. This phenomenological framework provides a self-consistent method for linking observationally derived star formation quenching rates, steady gas accretion, and discrete merging events across different cosmic epochs [Lilly et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..141L/abstract). For the most massive galaxies at $z \sim 1-3$, major mergers and *in situ* gas accretion appear to contribute roughly equally to stellar mass growth [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract). However, linking progenitor and descendant populations in theoretical models reveals significant tensions. Notably, semi-analytic models struggle to simultaneously reproduce the observed number densities of high-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies (DSFGs) and massive [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] (quiescent populations). Resolving this tension often requires invoking specific phenomenological calibrations, such as exceptionally high star formation efficiencies during mergers and a null dependence of supermassive black hole cold-gas accretion on halo mass [Araya-Araya et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250415283A/abstract), [Araya-Araya et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.542.2808A/abstract). 

Finally, the spatial clustering of early populations provides a fundamental test of the hierarchical assembly paradigm. High-redshift populations, such as Lyman-break galaxies at $z \sim 3$, are highly clustered [Steidel et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999RSPTA.357..153S/abstract). This indicates that early galaxy formation was strongly biased, with the first galaxies collapsing in the highest-density peaks of the underlying dark matter distribution, before the correlation amplitude evolved to match the local large-scale structure [Bagla et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998lsst.conf..253B/abstract).

## New Frontiers: Machine Learning and Manifold Learning

The hierarchical model of galaxy formation suggests that [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] are shaped by a complex interplay of mass, angular momentum, accretion, and stochastic merger histories. However, empirical studies of [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] have long indicated a surprising degree of organization, suggesting that the global observable properties of galaxies are controlled by a much smaller number of independent parameters [Disney et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.455.1082D/abstract). This implies that within the high-dimensional parameter space of possible galaxy configurations, real galaxies are tightly constrained to a low-dimensional [[galaxy-manifold|Galaxy Manifold]].

Recent advancements in computational astrophysics leverage machine learning to explicitly map this manifold and isolate the physical drivers of galaxy evolution. By applying dimensionality reduction techniques to highly resolved datasets, researchers can identify the most essential axes of variance in the galaxy population. For example, applying Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to the GAMMA dataset—which contains spatially resolved maps of [[galaxy-stellar-age|Galaxy stellar age]], stellar mass, and metallicity—demonstrates that the dimensionality of galaxy data can be reduced by a factor of $\sim 200$ for two-dimensional images and $3650$ for three-dimensional data cubes, all while maintaining a reconstruction error below $5\%$ [Çakır et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231206016C/abstract).

Beyond linear techniques like PCA, non-linear deep learning architectures such as autoencoders are uniquely suited for capturing the intrinsic topology of the galaxy manifold. Specifically, autoencoders utilizing an Information-Ordered Bottleneck (IOB) can compress the complex internal properties of simulated galaxies into a minimal latent representation [Lue et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986..133L/abstract). Remarkably, this architecture has demonstrated that global cosmological parameters, such as the total matter density $\Omega_m$, can be inferred from the internal properties of a single randomly selected galaxy drawn from hydrodynamical simulations like [[mock-galaxy-surveys-and-numerical-modeling|CAMELS or IllustrisTNG]] [Lue et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250217568L/abstract), [Lue et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986..133L/abstract). 

The geometric behavior of the learned manifold provides physical insight into why such single-galaxy cosmology is possible. When evaluating simulated populations with varying initial conditions, the neural networks reveal that changes in certain cosmological parameters, such as the amplitude of matter fluctuations ($\sigma_8$), produce variations that resemble natural astrophysical scatter. Consequently, altering $\sigma_8$ shifts galaxies *along* the established manifold. In contrast, altering $\Omega_m$ or strong astrophysical feedback parameters—such as the efficiency of supernova feedback ($A_{\text{SN1}}$)—shifts galaxies *off* the fiducial manifold [Lue et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986..133L/abstract). These latter parameters generate fundamentally unsampled combinations of properties that extend beyond the standard astrophysical scatter of the fiducial model. By successfully disentangling these orthogonal geometric shifts, autoencoders provide a robust framework to separate fundamental cosmological signatures from the complex baryonic feedback governing galaxy evolution.

## Open Questions: Quenching Mechanisms and Assembly Bias

Understanding how galaxies transition from active star formation to quiescent states—a process broadly termed quenching—remains a major open question in extragalactic astrophysics. While it is well established that denser environments host a higher [[early-type-fraction|Early-type fraction]] and more [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] [Kent et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981ApJ...245..805K/abstract), isolating the causal physical drivers from secondary statistical correlations is challenging. Observations have identified a substantial population of galaxies residing between the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] and the fully quenched sequence. This intermediate population suggests that initial departures from the main sequence are driven by the depletion of cold gas following a cutoff in cosmological inflows, whereas permanent quenching likely requires additional wind-driven mass loss [Oemler et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844...45O/abstract). Furthermore, kinematic classifications of cluster environments reveal that blue, low-mass galaxies are almost exclusively recent infallers that have yet to be quenched by the ambient intra-cluster medium [Martínez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250204446M/abstract).

Theoretical models frequently invoke AGN feedback as the primary mechanism for rapid gas expulsion and massive galaxy quenching. However, observational literature reveals significant tensions regarding its instantaneous efficacy. The exact nature of the central power source also remains open to exploration; for example, alternative theoretical models have proposed that active galactic nuclei may be powered by the energy released in relativistic stellar collisions within highly dense central star clusters, rather than solely by classical black hole accretion [Courvoisier et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996A&A...308L..17C/abstract). For instance, recent analyses using the CALIFA survey indicate that AGN hosts actually possess systematically higher molecular gas masses than non-active galaxies at comparable evolutionary stages. This result challenges the paradigm of rapid, AGN-driven molecular gas clearing, suggesting that the signatures of instantaneous feedback on global gas reservoirs are not prominent [Bazzi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A.149B/abstract). Similarly, determining the precise physical origins of [[galaxy-conformity|Galaxy conformity]]—the observed correlation between the star-forming states of central galaxies and their surrounding satellites—remains an active area of debate, as models must disentangle direct physical interplay from correlated environmental histories.

A second major theoretical frontier involves the mapping between [[galaxy-properties|Galaxy properties]] and their host dark matter halos. The standard halo occupation framework assumes that galaxy clustering is dictated almost entirely by total halo mass. However, precision cosmology requires accounting for secondary halo dependencies, a phenomenon generally referred to as assembly bias. Recent studies have clarified that for cluster-sized halos, this secondary clustering bias is driven primarily by internal halo properties such as concentration, spin, and subhalo abundance, rather than strictly by the halo's formation time or assembly history [Mao et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.5143M/abstract). 

The manifestation of galaxy assembly bias is highly sensitive to the observational selection function. Studies utilizing hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate that selecting galaxies by stellar mass, $r$-band magnitude, or broad-band color can alter the predicted small-scale clustering amplitude by up to $25\%$ [García-Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250406770G/abstract). Because no single secondary halo property fully captures the strength of this effect, simple subhalo abundance matching often fails to capture these nuances, typically over-predicting the clustering signal [Contreras et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1861C/abstract). This tension is also starkly apparent when evaluating spatial configurations within sub-structures; comparisons between semi-analytical models (like those from the Millennium simulation) and SDSS data reveal discrepancies in the spatial distribution of galaxies within small groups, with models often predicting groups that are too compact and luminous [Nurmi et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436..380N/abstract). Furthermore, the spatial distribution of satellites around host galaxies is not perfectly isotropic; it exhibits significant lopsidedness toward neighboring structures in $\Lambda$CDM simulations, introducing [[anisotropic-galaxy-assembly-bias|Anisotropic galaxy assembly bias]]. Ultimately, reconciling these dependencies indicates that future frameworks for [[galaxy-bias-and-clustering-statistics|Galaxy Bias and Clustering Statistics]] must move beyond simple mass-only prescriptions to incorporate complex, multivariate correlations between baryons and dark matter.
