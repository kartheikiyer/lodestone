## Article

**Galaxy kinematics** is the branch of astrophysics dedicated to the study of the motion of stellar and gaseous components within galaxies. As a fundamental pillar of [[internal-stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|Internal Stellar and Galactic Dynamics]], it provides the primary empirical constraints on the underlying gravitational potential and mass distribution of galactic systems. The kinematic state of a galaxy is broadly characterized by the balance between [[ordered-rotation|Ordered rotation]]—the dominant form of dynamical support in [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]—and random velocity dispersion, which supports pressure-dominated spheroids [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract). By mapping these velocity fields and extracting the full line-of-sight velocity distribution (LOSVD) from various galactic tracers, astrophysicists can disentangle luminous baryonic matter from invisible structural components. This process is essential for constraining the mass of central supermassive black holes and provides critical evidence for extended dark matter halos via the observation of [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]].

Over the past two decades, the field has been revolutionized by the transition from one-dimensional long-slit observations to large-scale, two-dimensional integral field spectroscopy (IFS) surveys. By providing comprehensive, spatially [[resolved-spectroscopic-observations|Resolved spectroscopic observations]], these surveys have demonstrated that traditional visual morphologies often mask a galaxy's true dynamical state [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract). Consequently, early-type galaxies are now fundamentally reclassified by their internal kinematics: the vast majority are [[fast-rotators|Fast rotators]] that retain significant [[specific-angular-momentum|Specific Angular Momentum]] and disk-like rotation, while a minority are massive [[slow-rotator|Slow rotator]]s formed through successive gas-poor [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]] [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract), [Graham et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191106103G/abstract). Furthermore, the wealth of 2D kinematic data has enabled the formulation of unified dynamical scaling relations that combine both rotational and dispersion support, seamlessly connecting diverse galaxy types across relationships like the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] [Romanowsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJS..203...17R/abstract), [Cortese et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...795L..37C/abstract). In the local universe, high-precision astrometry serves as a critical local benchmark, providing a highly detailed [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]] view of the Milky Way’s intricate kinematic substructures, including its thin and [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] and accreted stellar streams.

Looking beyond the local volume, observing galaxy kinematics over cosmic time is crucial for understanding the [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|galaxy assembly process]] and [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. Observations of early star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 2$ reveal systems that were highly turbulent and gas-rich, lacking the dynamically settled structures seen today. Surprisingly, massive quiescent galaxies at these epochs often exhibit fast-rotating kinematics, requiring significant subsequent evolution to transform into the local population of slow rotators. Moving forward, the field faces several critical open questions. These include resolving the tension between theoretical dark matter profiles and observations at the low-mass end of the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]], testing alternative gravity models, and determining the relative roles of stellar mass versus environmental pre-processing in driving kinematic transformations. Addressing these tensions, alongside mapping the complex [[non-circular-motions|Non-circular motions]] of the first galaxies using next-generation observatories, remains at the forefront of modern extragalactic research.

## Fundamental Principles and Tracers

Galaxy kinematics categorizes systems based on the balance between [[ordered-rotation|Ordered rotation]] and random velocity dispersion. This balance dictates the physical structure and dynamical support of a galaxy. Observationally, the relative importance of these motions is characterized by the $V/\sigma$ ratio, where $V$ is the maximum or characteristic line-of-sight [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]] and $\sigma$ is the central or integrated velocity dispersion. Recent large-scale integral field spectroscopy surveys have demonstrated that combining rotation and dispersion provides a unified measure of dynamical support across the entire Hubble sequence. Specifically, generalized parameters tightly correlate with stellar mass for both early- and late-type galaxies, providing a single dynamical scaling relation that is agnostic to visual morphology [Cortese et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...795L..37C/abstract). Furthermore, mapping small-scale velocity patches reveals an inverse loglinear relation between kinematical fluctuation degrees and galaxy stellar surface mass densities, offering deeper insights into internal dynamical heating [Zhong et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957L..12Z/abstract).

The [[stellar-component|Stellar component]] of a galaxy behaves as a collisionless fluid. Stellar kinematics are primarily extracted from optical and near-infrared absorption lines (e.g., the Ca II triplet, Mg $b$ lines, or CO band-heads) by recovering the line-of-sight velocity distribution (LOSVD). Because the LOSVD frequently deviates from a pure Gaussian, it is parameterized using higher-order moments, including skewness and kurtosis. Modern kinematic extraction methods deconvolve the LOSVD using either parameterized template-fitting algorithms or non-parametric Bayesian approaches [de Zeeuw et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.329..513D/abstract), [Reiter et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A..12R/abstract). Due to the rapid decline in stellar surface brightness, continuous stellar kinematics are typically limited to $\sim 1-2 R_e$. To map the gravitational potential at larger radii (up to $\sim 5 R_e$ and beyond), discrete [[tracers|Tracers]] such as planetary nebulae and globular clusters must be employed [Coccato et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUFM..29B..20C/abstract).

Unlike stars, the interstellar medium is collisional and highly dissipative, causing gas clouds to predominantly settle into dynamically cold, thin rotating disks. Different gas phases serve as complementary kinematic tracers. Ionized gas (traced by optical emission lines like H$\alpha$ and [O II]) is widely used to map the internal kinematics of star-forming disks [Yang et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...477..789Y/abstract). Cold molecular gas (traced by CO) provides a high-resolution view of the dense, inner disk [Leung et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477..254L/abstract), while extended neutral hydrogen (21-cm HI emission) traces [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] well into the dark matter-dominated halo [Sackett et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PASA...14...11S/abstract). Because gas is dynamically cold, its observed rotation velocity closely approximates the circular velocity of the gravitational potential, minimizing the large asymmetric drift corrections required for stellar tracers.

When used to derive fundamental quantities like dynamical mass, gas and stellar tracers generally show strong agreement in equilibrium systems, once appropriate corrections for asymmetric drift or pressure support are applied to the stellar kinematics [Leung et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477..254L/abstract). However, active tensions remain regarding whether gas and stars always trace the same underlying potential. Because gas is highly responsive to non-gravitational forces, [[non-circular-motions|Non-circular motions]] driven by bar potentials, active galactic nuclei outflows, or stellar feedback can cause significant deviations between gas and stellar kinematics, particularly in nuclear regions and compact starburst galaxies [Allen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451.2780A/abstract), [Bik et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...666A.161B/abstract).

## Observational Techniques: From Long-Slit to Integral Field Spectroscopy

Historically, kinematic studies of galaxies relied heavily on 1D long-slit spectroscopy. While effective for extracting global [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]] and dispersion profiles along a chosen axis, long-slit observations were highly sensitive to the slit's position angle and often failed to capture non-axisymmetric kinematic features. The advent of 2D Integral Field Spectroscopy (IFS) has revolutionized the study of [[internal-stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|Internal Stellar and Galactic Dynamics]] by enabling the simultaneous mapping of stellar and gas kinematics across the entire spatial extent of a galaxy [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract). IFS has demonstrated that purely visual morphological classification can be misleading; for instance, many galaxies photometrically classified as ellipticals are revealed by IFS to be misclassified face-on disks with significant rotational support [Graham et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191005136G/abstract).

Extracting kinematic maps from IFS data requires deconvolving the integrated galaxy spectrum to recover the LOSVD for each spatial bin, or using novel approaches like spaxel cross-correlation and iterative smoothing to calculate galaxy rotation curves robustly even at very low signal-to-noise ratios (S/N $\sim 1$) [Bag et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.2278B/abstract). Because galaxies support rich, multi-component orbital structures, their LOSVDs often deviate significantly from a simple Gaussian profile [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract). Foundational work utilizing a hybrid deconvolution method first demonstrated how recovering the full shape of the LOSVD allows astronomers to identify subtle line-profile asymmetries driven by either ordered rotation or velocity anisotropy [Bender et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990A&A...229..441B/abstract). To quantify these deviations, the LOSVD is traditionally parameterized using higher-order Gauss-Hermite moments. The third moment, $h_3$, measures asymmetric skewness—frequently indicating a rotating stellar disk embedded within a hot, dispersion-dominated bulge—while the [[fourth-order-gauss-hermite-moment|Fourth-order Gauss-Hermite moment]], $h_4$, measures symmetric kurtosis and is critical for breaking the mass-anisotropy degeneracy in dynamical models [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract), [de Zeeuw et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.329..513D/abstract).

Currently, there is active debate in the literature regarding the optimal extraction of the LOSVD. Standard parametric methods, such as the widely used `pPXF` code, rely on the Gauss-Hermite expansion. However, recent analyses suggest that parametric assumptions can bias the recovered mass profiles and orbit superpositions, particularly in complex systems like [[multi-spin-galaxies|Multi-spin galaxies]]. Non-parametric approaches, such as `Bayes-LOSVD`, offer a more robust representation of the LOSVD, allowing for the reliable recovery of dynamically cold counter-rotating disks and accurate [[galaxy-velocity-anisotropy-profiles|Galaxy velocity anisotropy profiles]] [Reiter et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A..12R/abstract).

The transition to 2D kinematics has been driven by several massive, statistically complete surveys. Following pioneering instruments and projects like SAURON [de Zeeuw et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.329..513D/abstract) and CALIFA [Falcón-Barroso et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...597A..48F/abstract), multi-object IFS campaigns such as the SAMI Galaxy Survey [Foster et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.3105F/abstract), [Croom et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505..991C/abstract) and the SDSS Mapping Nearby Galaxies at Apache Point Observatory (MaNGA) survey [Graham et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191005136G/abstract) now map thousands of targets. Further bridging local and extragalactic regimes, the GECKOS survey—a large MUSE program—connects Galactic archaeology with deep IFS observations of [[nearby-edge-on-galaxies|Nearby edge-on galaxies]] to detail the kinematics of thick disks and outflows [van de Sande et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024IAUS..377...27V/abstract). By providing comprehensive 2D maps of both the stellar and gaseous velocity fields, these surveys allow astronomers to construct unified dynamical scaling relations—such as the $S_{0.5}$ parameter linking rotational velocity and dispersion—that are valid across the entire Hubble sequence [Cortese et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...795L..37C/abstract).

Furthermore, the application of IFS is no longer restricted to the local universe. Instruments on 8-10m class telescopes, such as SINFONI, and advanced space observatories like JWST equipped with NIRSpec, have extended IFS techniques to high-redshift star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 1$ to $z \sim 9$ [Förster Schreiber et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Msngr.145...39F/abstract), [Perna et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IAUS..373...60P/abstract). Because stellar continuum emission is often too faint at these epochs, high-$z$ IFS studies frequently utilize nebular emission lines as dynamical tracers. These observations have fundamentally reshaped our understanding of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]], revealing that the peculiar, irregular morphologies of early galaxies often mask underlying, highly turbulent, gas-rich rotating disks rather than chaotic major mergers [Shapiro et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...682..231S/abstract), [Rhoades et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250322039R/abstract).

## Kinematics of Disk-Dominated Systems

The dynamics of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], encompassing spirals and lenticulars, are predominantly governed by [[ordered-rotation|Ordered rotation]]. In these systems, cold gas and stars move in the disk plane on roughly circular orbits, making their bulk kinematics largely definable by their rotation curves, $V_{\rm rot}(R)$. 

Observations of HI 21-cm and optical emission lines reveal that rotation curves typically rise rapidly at small galactocentric radii before transitioning into an extended, nearly constant velocity plateau. These [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] imply that the enclosed dynamical mass scales as $M(r) \propto r$ in the outer regions. Because the luminous baryonic matter converges at much smaller radii, this kinematic signature robustly indicates that disk galaxies are embedded within massive dark matter halos.

The connection between the total dynamical mass and the baryonic content is formalized by the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]], an empirical scaling law taking the form:
$$ L = A V_{\rm max}^\alpha $$
where $L$ is the luminosity, $V_{\rm max}$ is the [[maximum-rotational-velocity|Maximum rotational velocity]], $A$ is the zero-point, and $\alpha \approx 2.5 - 4$ depending on the photometric band. Recent integral field spectroscopy (IFS) surveys have generalized this relationship across the entire Hubble sequence by incorporating the stellar velocity dispersion, $\sigma$. This yields a unified dynamical scaling parameter:
$$ S_{0.5} = \sqrt{0.5 V_{\rm rot}^2 + \sigma^2} $$
which provides a tight correlation with stellar mass for both rotation-supported disks and dispersion-supported ellipticals, offering a morphology-independent measure of dynamical support [Cortese et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...795L..37C/abstract).

While cold molecular and ionized gas efficiently trace the circular velocity of the underlying gravitational potential, stars act as a collisionless fluid and exhibit significant random motions. Consequently, the observed rotational velocity of the stellar component systematically lags behind that of the gas. To derive true circular velocities and dynamical masses from stellar kinematics, an asymmetric drift correction must be applied to account for the pressure support provided by the velocity dispersion [Leung et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477..254L/abstract).

Despite the dominance of circular orbits, high-resolution 2D kinematic mapping reveals that [[non-circular-motions|Non-circular motions]] are ubiquitous in [[galactic-disk-structure-and-dynamics|Galactic Disk Structure and Dynamics]]. These deviations are primarily driven by a [[non-axisymmetric-pattern|Non-axisymmetric pattern]] in the gravitational potential, most notably bars and spiral arms. In barred galaxies, strong non-circular streaming motions funnel gas into the nuclear regions, driving the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] and facilitating the formation of inner disks and pseudobulges [Erroz-Ferrer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.427.2938E/abstract), [Gadotti et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..353..135G/abstract), [DiGiorgio Zanger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240711908D/abstract). Furthermore, minor mergers or gas accretion events can trigger substantial kinematic disturbances, occasionally resulting in significant counter-rotating stellar or gaseous populations coexisting within an otherwise unperturbed host disk [Kuijken et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PASP..105.1016K/abstract), [Knapen et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004A&A...423..481K/abstract).

## Fast and Slow Rotators in Early-Type Galaxies

Historically, early-type galaxies (ETGs) were assumed to be homogeneous, pressure-supported systems. However, the advent of large-scale integral field spectroscopy (IFS) surveys—such as SAURON, the [[atlas3d-survey|Atlas3D survey]], and MaNGA—has demonstrated that the traditional photometric Hubble classification masks a fundamental kinematic dichotomy. ETGs are now broadly separated into [[fast-rotators|Fast rotators]] and [[slow-rotator|Slow rotator]]s based on their internal stellar kinematics [de Zeeuw et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.329..513D/abstract), [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract).

**Kinematic Distinction and Angular Momentum**
The division between fast and slow rotators is primarily quantified by the ratio of ordered rotation to random motions, $V/\sigma$, or more robustly by $\lambda_R$, an empirical proxy for [[specific-angular-momentum|Specific Angular Momentum]]. Fast rotators constitute the vast majority of ETGs. They exhibit regular, disk-like rotation fields and are often oblate systems flattened by centrifugal forces. In these systems, the maximum line-of-sight streaming velocity $v_m$ relative to the central velocity dispersion $\sigma$ follows the baseline prediction for an isotropic oblate rotator:
$$ \frac{v_m}{\sigma} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}} $$
where $\epsilon$ is the observed ellipticity. Detailed modeling reveals that while isotropic models provide a baseline, fast-rotating ellipticals frequently possess substantial velocity anisotropy, reaching values as high as $\delta \approx 0.5$.

In contrast, slow rotators lack significant ordered rotation, exhibit low $\lambda_R$, and are supported primarily by random motions. They are generally weakly triaxial, highly massive systems ($M_\star \gtrsim 2 \times 10^{11} M_\odot$) and often feature complex central kinematics, such as kinematically decoupled cores [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract). Analysis of the stellar angular momentum shows that ellipticals and spirals follow parallel scaling tracks in the $j_\star - M_\star$ plane; however, ellipticals retain on average only $\sim 10\%$ of their initial halo angular momentum, compared to $\sim 60\%$ for spirals [Romanowsky et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJS..203...17R/abstract).

**Evolutionary Pathways**
The bifurcation in kinematic properties reflects distinct assembly histories. Fast rotators are thought to begin as star-forming disks, evolving via gas accretion, bulge growth, and subsequent quenching. Because visual morphology can be misleading, up to two-thirds of galaxies visually classified as "spheroidal" are actually face-on fast-rotating disks when mapped kinematically [Graham et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191005136G/abstract).

Slow rotators, meanwhile, are established as the relics of violent hierarchical assembly, primarily forming through a sequence of gas-poor ("dry") [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]] [Graham et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191106103G/abstract). Observations of massive quiescent galaxies at $z \sim 2$ indicate that they are predominantly fast rotators with $(V/\sigma)_{R_e} = 1.7 - 2.3$ [Newman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862..126N/abstract). To match the local population of slow rotators, these high-redshift ancestors must reduce their specific angular momentum by a factor of 5 to 10 via subsequent merging that simultaneously increases their mass and erodes their rotational support [Newman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862..126N/abstract). Furthermore, deep imaging links specific tidal features to these kinematic states and formation histories: shells are preferentially found around slowly rotating galaxies formed via radial mergers, while tidal streams are common in galaxies hosting kinematically distinct cores [Valenzuela et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A.182V/abstract).

**Environmental Dependence**
The role of environment in this kinematic dichotomy remains an active subject of debate. Observations robustly place the highest fraction of massive slow rotators in the dense cores of galaxy clusters, tracing the local density peaks [Graham et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191106103G/abstract). The kinematics of galaxies within these clusters also display marked spatial anisotropy; for example, the line-of-sight velocity dispersion of galaxies is higher along the major axis of the central galaxy compared to its minor axis [Skielboe et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...758L..16S/abstract). Furthermore, merging cluster environments contain an abundance of kinematically active elliptical galaxies, suggesting that significant dynamical pre-processing of galaxies occurs within infalling substructures [Bilton et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.1507B/abstract). The complex environmental gas dynamics further complicate this picture; findings from simulations such as TNG-Cluster show that gas motions in cluster cores are intricately balanced between inflows and outflows, while the cluster outskirts exhibit peaks of cosmic accretion [Ayromlou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A..20A/abstract). However, the literature is conflicting on whether the environment actively and independently drives the depletion of angular momentum (e.g., via pre-processing and repeated interactions), or if stellar mass is the sole primary driver of the kinematic state, with environmental correlations arising merely as a secondary effect of mass assembly in dense regions [Rutherford et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...918...84R/abstract).

## Dynamical Mass Modeling and Dark Matter Constraints

Kinematic data provide the primary empirical constraint on the gravitational potential of galaxies, allowing astrophysicists to determine the total dynamical mass and disentangle the baryonic mass from the underlying dark matter halo. 

In [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], the [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]] profile $V_{\text{rot}}(R)$ derived from cold gas tracers (such as HI or CO) offers a relatively direct probe of the potential. Where stellar kinematics are used instead, an asymmetric drift correction must be applied to recover the true circular velocity. Comparisons between stellar models and CO kinematics show that asymmetric drift corrections, Jeans anisotropic modeling (JAM), and Schwarzschild models can all successfully recover the dynamical mass at one effective radius ($1R_e$) to within $\sim 10-20\%$, though scatter increases in the inner regions due to variations in the mass-to-light ratio ($\Upsilon$) or departures from thin-disk geometries [Leung et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477..254L/abstract). At larger radii, the presence of [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] implies an enclosed mass that scales as $M(R) \propto R$, providing the classical evidence for extended dark matter halos. To bridge the kinematic diversity across the Hubble sequence, recent integral-field spectroscopy (IFS) surveys have shown that galaxies of all morphological types obey a unified dynamical scaling relation when characterized by the parameter $S_{0.5}$, which robustly combines both rotational and dispersion support into a single proxy for dynamical mass [Cortese et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...795L..37C/abstract).

For pressure-supported early-type galaxies, mass modeling is complicated by the mass-anisotropy degeneracy inherent in the Jeans equation. A flat or rising projected velocity dispersion profile, $\sigma_p(R)$, can indicate either an outward-increasing mass-to-light ratio (a dark matter halo) or a tangentially anisotropic velocity ellipsoid. To break this degeneracy, models must incorporate higher-order moments of the line-of-sight velocity distribution (LOSVD), particularly the third and [[fourth-order-gauss-hermite-moment|Fourth-order Gauss-Hermite moment]]s ($h_3$ and $h_4$). 

The most robust technique for analyzing such complex systems is Schwarzschild orbit superposition, which constructs a model by weighting a vast library of orbits to simultaneously fit the observed photometry and LOSVD, thereby reconstructing the internal [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]]. However, the inferred dynamical mass and orbital structure are highly sensitive to the methodologies used to extract the LOSVD from spectra. Utilizing non-parametric Bayesian methods (e.g., Bayes-LOSVD) rather than standard parametric Gauss-Hermite expansions (e.g., pPXF) can yield significantly different mass estimates and velocity anisotropies, particularly when modeling complex structures like [[multi-spin-galaxies|Multi-spin galaxies]] [Reiter et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A..12R/abstract).

Because the stellar surface brightness of early-type galaxies fades rapidly, characterizing the dark matter halo beyond $\sim 2 R_e$ requires discrete tracers such as planetary nebulae and globular clusters [Kissler-Patig et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000RvMA...13...13K/abstract), [Coccato et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUFM..29B..20C/abstract). At even larger scales, satellite kinematics provide a powerful statistical tool; by measuring satellite velocity dispersions as a function of central galaxy luminosity, astronomers can tightly constrain the mean and scatter of the halo mass-luminosity relation [More et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.392..801M/abstract). Dynamical modeling of these outer-halo tracers has revealed unexpected tensions. While bright, boxy elliptical galaxies exhibit the strong kinematic signatures expected from massive dark matter halos, faint, disky ellipticals often show surprisingly low signatures of dark matter within their inner regions [Romanowsky et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006EAS....20..119R/abstract). This observed disparity in dark matter fractions remains an unresolved challenge in galaxy formation theory. It has also motivated the testing of alternative gravity formulations, such as MOND or the $\kappa$-model, which attempt to mimic dark matter effects through modified geometry or dynamics [Pascoli et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Ap&SS.367...62P/abstract). However, alternative models like MOND currently struggle to reproduce the strong halo signatures found in massive boxy ellipticals [Romanowsky et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006EAS....20..119R/abstract).

## Internal Substructures: Decoupled Cores and Outflows

High-resolution mapping of [[nuclear-gas-kinematics|Nuclear gas kinematics]] and stellar motions reveals that galaxy centers frequently host substructures that are dynamically distinct from their host galaxies. These features, including kinematically decoupled cores (KDC), counter-rotating disks, and non-gravitational flows, provide fossil records of assembly and feedback processes.

**Kinematically Decoupled Cores (KDCs)**
A kinematically decoupled core (KDC) is characterized by a central region where the mean line-of-sight velocity suddenly changes in amplitude, axis, or sign relative to the main body of the galaxy. Such features are prevalent in early-type galaxies and serve as signatures of major or minor accretion events. For example, integral-field mapping of NGC 1407 reveals a triaxial KDC with a radius of $\sim 0.6$ kpc and a kinematic twist of $\sim 148^{\circ}$ relative to the outer galaxy [Johnston et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.3215J/abstract). Despite this stark kinematic misalignment, the stellar population within the KDC exhibits a uniform age and super-solar $\alpha$-enhancement, suggesting that the core formed early in the galaxy's history through [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]] [Johnston et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.3215J/abstract). 

Extreme decoupling can manifest as counter-rotating stellar populations, which can account for up to $\sim 30\%$ of the stars in otherwise normal [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] like NGC 7217 [Kuijken et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PASP..105.1016K/abstract). Beyond central cores, tracer kinematics are also highly effective at mapping extended stellar substructures; for instance, the reconstruction of the Umbrella Galaxy's stream orbit and progenitor properties powerfully demonstrates how discrete kinematic measurements can unearth a galaxy's specific accretion history [Romanowsky et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ASPC..486..169R/abstract). Similarly, observations of the globular cluster system in M 104 (the Sombrero galaxy) reveal a decreasing velocity dispersion profile from the centre to the halo, alongside a metallicity distribution that reflects a complex assembly history [Fahrion et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...702A..59F/abstract). Furthermore, the evolutionary history of the host strongly dictates core properties: nuclear star clusters in late-type systems are typically rotation-dominated, whereas those in early-type hosts often behave as a [[slow-rotator|Slow rotator]] [Pinna et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...921....8P/abstract).

**Gas Inflows and Streaming Motions**
In addition to collisionless stellar substructures, the dissipative interstellar medium exhibits profound internal complexities. Non-axisymmetric potentials, such as those generated by galactic bars, exert gravitational torques that efficiently strip angular momentum from the gas. This drives continuous gas inflows toward the central parsecs, a process readily observed as [[non-circular-motions|Non-circular motions]] and strong streaming motions. 

These inflows frequently terminate in star-forming nuclear rings. While theoretically expected in barred galaxies, nuclear rings of similar physical scale (e.g., $2$ kpc in diameter) are also observed in unbarred galaxies like NGC 278. In such cases, the perturbed velocity field and resulting streaming motions are likely induced by a recent minor merger rather than secular bar dynamics [Knapen et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004A&A...423..481K/abstract). High-resolution mapping of targets like NGC 613 confirms that gas inflows precisely track the host's dust lanes, funneling material into inner-scale reservoirs that fuel ongoing star formation and nuclear activity [Silva-Lima et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540.2787S/abstract).

**Galactic Outflows and AGN Feedback**
Active galactic nuclei (AGN) and circumnuclear starbursts inject mechanical energy back into the interstellar medium, generating galactic outflows that significantly perturb central gas velocities. Identifying these outflows requires separating them from the underlying disk rotation. In the Seyfert 2 galaxy Mrk 573, for example, outflowing ionized gas extends to $< 1$ kpc, indicating that in situ acceleration occurs but may not be sufficiently powerful to evacuate the entire bulge [Fischer et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...834...30F/abstract). 

Distinguishing between AGN-driven outflows and other phenomena, such as supermassive black hole binaries or highly disturbed [[post-merger-galaxies|Post-merger galaxies]], remains an observational challenge. The literature explicitly notes the ambiguity in identifying the true nature of kinematically offset AGN. Integral field spectroscopy is crucial in these cases to confirm whether kinematically offset emission lines represent a central biconical outflow—such as the one aligned with the radio jet in NGC 613 [Silva-Lima et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540.2787S/abstract)—or a genuine bulk kinematic offset from an accreted satellite [Allen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451.2780A/abstract).

## The Milky Way as a Local Kinematic Benchmark

The Milky Way serves as an unprecedented laboratory for testing theories of galaxy formation, owing to our unique position to observe its stellar populations in fully resolved [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]]. Driven largely by high-precision astrometry from the *Gaia* mission, supplemented by ground-based spectroscopic surveys, the field of Galactic archaeology has transitioned from broad statistical models to detailed chemo-dynamical mapping of the Galaxy's internal structures.

The disk system of the Milky Way displays complex kinematics that challenge simple axisymmetric equilibrium models. To interpret these distributions, the [[besan-on-galaxy-model|Besançon Galaxy Model]] provides a dynamically self-consistent framework. By employing generalized Shu distribution functions with three integrals of motion, this model successfully recovers the Galactic potential and internal forces via the Jeans equations to an accuracy better than $1\%$ over most of the Galaxy's volume [Bienaymé et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...620A.103B/abstract). To further unravel these complex orbital structures, novel techniques like Orbital Torus Imaging combine stellar labels—such as element abundances—with dynamical invariants to precisely map orbits and constrain Galactic mass [Price-Whelan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910...17P/abstract). Within this potential, the disk is broadly separated into thin and [[thick-disks|Thick disks]]. High-resolution kinematic studies have revealed an "ultra metal-poor thick disk" extending to metallicities of $\text{[Fe/H]} \lesssim -4$ [Di Matteo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...636A.115D/abstract). The existence of stars with thick disk kinematics at such low metallicities suggests that the initial dissipative collapse forming the old Galactic disk must have been exceptionally rapid [Di Matteo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...636A.115D/abstract).

In the local volume, the velocity distribution is not smooth but contains significant substructure, most notably in the form of [[moving-groups|Moving groups]] (e.g., the Hercules stream). Rather than representing single-burst populations, these co-moving overdensities are primarily kinematic resonances induced by non-axisymmetric features, such as the outer Lindblad resonance of the central bar or transient spiral arms [Bovy et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhDT........86B/abstract). In the outer disk ($12 \text{--} 15 \text{ kpc}$), vertical velocity wobbles and outward radial motions of $\sim 8 \text{--} 16 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ indicate that the disk is actively responding to ongoing satellite perturbations [Casetti-Dinescu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...883....8C/abstract). Additionally, recent insights from simulations of Milky Way-like galaxies suggest that spiral arms may be transient, dynamically driven features rather than static density waves, often associated with sharp kinematic transitions across the disk [Durán-Camacho et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250613560D/abstract).

The Galactic halo, once modeled as a smooth, virialized component, is now understood to be highly heterogeneous. Precision kinematics reveal that the halo breaks both azimuthal and mirror symmetry, with the radial velocity dispersion $\sigma_r$ varying from a dynamically "cold" $70 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ to a "hot" $160 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ depending on the line of sight [Han et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240612969H/abstract). This patchiness is a direct signature of hierarchical assembly, where [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]] leave behind coherent stellar streams and phase-space debris [Smith et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..420..113S/abstract). For example, debris from the Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus merger is characterized by highly radially biased orbits and high $\sigma_r$, which contrasts sharply with the lower $\sigma_r$ and more [[isotropic-velocity-dispersion|Isotropic velocity dispersion]] of the in-situ halo population [Feltzing et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953..143F/abstract), [Han et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240612969H/abstract). Moreover, studies tracing the kinematics of blue horizontal branch field stars have demonstrated that the stellar halo velocity ellipsoid undergoes a broader structural shift, transitioning from strong radial anisotropy near the Sun to tangential anisotropy in the outer halo ($r > 20 \text{ kpc}$) [Sommer-Larsen et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994MNRAS.271...94S/abstract).

Finally, the central bulge presents a kinematic dichotomy linked to its chemical enrichment. While the dominant boxy/peanut-shaped bulge rotates cylindrically, the rotational signal steadily decreases at lower metallicities. For the most metal-poor stars ($\text{[Fe/H]} < -1.0$), rotation becomes negligible, while the velocity dispersion steepens with a gradient of $-44 \pm 4 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ dex}^{-1}$ [Arentsen et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491L..11A/abstract). This kinematic transition highlights the ongoing tension in distinguishing a classical, pressure-supported inner core from the secularly evolved, bar-driven pseudo-bulge.

## Evolution of Galaxy Kinematics over Cosmic Time

The kinematic properties of galaxies undergo profound changes over cosmic time. Observations from $z \sim 1$ to $z \sim 6$ track the [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|galaxy assembly process]], capturing the transformation from compact, highly turbulent systems in the early Universe to the dynamically relaxed Hubble sequence observed today [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). 

**High-Redshift Star-Forming Galaxies and Turbulent Disks**
At $z \gtrsim 1.5$, integral field spectroscopy (IFS) surveys such as the SINS survey have revolutionized our understanding of early galaxy kinematics [Förster Schreiber et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Msngr.145...39F/abstract). A defining feature of these high-redshift systems is their elevated internal turbulence, driven by systematically higher gas fractions. While their photometric morphologies often appear highly irregular or clumpy, kinematic maps reveal that a substantial fraction possess underlying [[ordered-rotation|Ordered rotation]]. Kinemetry applied to the SINS survey sample demonstrates that over 50% of these galaxies are well-described as single rotating disks, whereas the remainder exhibit the chaotic kinematic signatures of major mergers [Shapiro et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...682..231S/abstract). However, distinguishing between true merger-driven kinematic anomalies and internal instabilities within these gas-rich, turbulent disks remains an area of active debate in the literature.

Validating these gas-based kinematic measurements against collisionless stellar tracers is a crucial step in confirming this dynamical picture. Recent spatially resolved observations at $z \sim 2$ confirm that nebular emission accurately traces the kinematics of young stellar populations. Both components exhibit closely matched, high intrinsic velocity dispersions (e.g., $\sigma \approx 52-64$ km/s), supporting the paradigm of rotationally supported but vertically thick, highly turbulent disks at early epochs [Rhoades et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250322039R/abstract). Furthermore, Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) forward-modeling of gas kinematics in massive star-forming disks at $z \sim 1-2$ confirms that they are typically baryon-dominated within one effective radius, suggesting relatively low central dark matter fractions [Price et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922..143P/abstract).

**Intermediate Redshift Evolution**
The transition toward dynamically settled disks is a continuous process. At $z \sim 1$, data from the TKRS survey highlights that approximately $35\%$ of spatially resolved galaxies are already dispersion-dominated, suggesting an evolutionary state intricately linked to their irregular high-redshift morphologies [Weiner et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...653.1027W/abstract). Moving to intermediate epochs ($z \sim 0.4-0.75$), the IMAGES survey finds that $41 \pm 7\%$ of emission-line galaxies still exhibit anomalous or complex kinematics—a stark contrast to the local Universe, where such non-relaxed velocity fields are rare for similar mass ranges [Yang et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...477..789Y/abstract). This indicates that the [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]] of galaxy kinematics outpaces the evolution of broad photometric structure.

**Quiescent Galaxies and Angular Momentum Loss**
The kinematic evolution of early-type galaxies presents a notable departure from traditional local paradigms. At $z \sim 2$, massive quiescent galaxies ($M_{\rm dyn} \gtrsim 2 \times 10^{11} M_\odot$) are extremely compact—factors of 2 to 5 smaller than local counterparts—and surprisingly exhibit the kinematics of [[fast-rotators|Fast rotators]]. Direct measurements yield high maximum rotational velocities ($V_{\rm max} \approx 290-352$ km/s) and strong rotational support ($V/\sigma \approx 1.7-2.3$) [Newman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862..126N/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..291C/abstract). 

Because galaxies of this mass in the local Universe are almost exclusively pressure-supported [[slow-rotator|Slow rotator]]s, these findings imply that morphological quenching and kinematic transformation do not necessarily occur simultaneously. To further understand this transition phase, post-starburst (E+A) galaxies serve as ideal cosmic laboratories for studying the rapid kinematic and structural transformations from star-forming disks to quiescent spheroids [French et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASP..133g2001F/abstract). Over longer timescales, the requisite loss of specific angular momentum for the bulk population is likely driven by their subsequent merger history. Over billions of years, a sequence of dry (gas-poor) [[galaxy-mergers-and-interactions|Galaxy Mergers and Interactions]] drives size growth and dynamically heats the stellar orbits, eroding rotational support and transforming these fast-rotating compact objects into the massive, triaxial slow rotators seen today [Newman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...862..126N/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Next-generation observational facilities are poised to revolutionize our understanding of early galaxy assembly. The advent of JWST and the forthcoming Extremely Large Telescopes (ELTs) enables the extraction of both nebular and stellar kinematics at unprecedented spatial resolution for galaxies at $3 < z < 9$ [Perna et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IAUS..373...60P/abstract). A primary future objective is to map [[spatially-resolved-stellar-populations|Spatially resolved stellar populations]] and internal velocity fields to determine dynamical masses, trace non-virial motions such as active galactic nuclei outflows, and decipher how early, turbulent gas-rich disks evolve into the well-ordered systems of the local universe [Bik et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...666A.161B/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract). Resolving the kinematics of these first galaxies will help clarify the physical mechanisms that drive the transition of high-redshift [[fast-rotators|Fast rotators]] into the massive, quiescent [[slow-rotator|Slow rotator]] ellipticals observed today.

In the local universe, a major outstanding issue is the tension between observed low-mass galaxy kinematics and the theoretical predictions of the $\Lambda$ cold dark matter ($\Lambda$CDM) model, often termed the "small-scale crisis." Observations of dwarf galaxies frequently reveal central rotation curves that rise more slowly than expected for $\Lambda$CDM halos. However, deep, high-resolution H I measurements are beginning to close this gap. Recent studies show that the deepest kinematic measurements of low-mass systems increasingly align with $\Lambda$CDM predictions, particularly at the low-mass end of the Baryonic [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]], even showing hints of the theoretically predicted low-mass rollover in the relation [Sardone et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..135S/abstract). 

These discrepancies in the inner regions of galaxies intimately connect to the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] and the ongoing debate between dark matter models and alternative gravity theories. While [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] in spiral galaxies are classically explained by dark matter halos, alternative frameworks like Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) or the recently proposed $\kappa$-model [Pascoli et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Ap&SS.367...62P/abstract) attempt to reproduce these kinematic signatures through modifications to gravitational laws. Testing these paradigms remains a critical future direction, particularly using early-type galaxies where current observations conflict: faint, disky ellipticals sometimes show a surprising lack of dark matter signatures within two effective radii, challenging $\Lambda$CDM, whereas bright, boxy ellipticals exhibit high halo mass profiles that present significant problems for MOND [Romanowsky et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006EAS....20..119R/abstract).

Another persistent open question is the relative importance of stellar mass versus environment in driving kinematic transformations. While unified dynamical scaling parameters, such as $S_{0.5} = \sqrt{0.5 V_{\rm rot}^2 + \sigma^2}$, suggest that stellar mass fundamentally dictates a galaxy's dynamical support across all morphologies [Cortese et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...795L..37C/abstract), other studies detect significant environmental anti-correlations with stellar spin, implying that dense environments may independently suppress rotation through cluster pre-processing, mergers, and tidal interactions [Rutherford et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...918...84R/abstract). However, this conclusion remains highly debated; other recent work explicitly contradicts this, concluding that spin ($\lambda_{R_e}$) is primarily anti-correlated with stellar age, and that environment only significantly correlates with higher-order kinematic moments like $h_4$ [Foster et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PASA...42...35F/abstract). Future large-volume, spatially resolved surveys will need to definitively disentangle these variables, leveraging both gas and stellar tracers to map the environmental impact on galactic phase space.
