The galactic thin disk is the defining, actively star-forming structural component of most [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], including the [[milky-way|Milky Way]]. Characterized by a highly flattened geometry with a remarkably small vertical [[scaleheight-distribution|Scaleheight distribution]], the thin disk contains the vast majority of a galaxy's angular momentum, cold interstellar medium, and young, high-metallicity [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]] (Population I stars). Kinematically, it is supported almost entirely by [[ordered-rotation|Ordered rotation]], distinguishing it from the kinematically hotter, older, and more metal-poor [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] and [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]] in which it is typically embedded [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..226Y/abstract). The radial mass distribution of a thin disk is classically parameterized as a [[freeman-stellar-disk|Freeman stellar disk]] with an exponential decline in surface brightness, though modern observations reveal a complex architecture featuring abrupt truncations—such as the [[type-ii-disk-break-radius|Type II disk break radius]]—and multi-phase gas dynamics governed by critical surface density thresholds [Dalcanton et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...608..189D/abstract).

Within the broader cosmological framework, the existence and ubiquity of thin disks provide profound constraints on hierarchical [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|Galaxy assembly process lurk]]. N-body simulations demonstrate that dynamically cold thin disks are extremely fragile and easily heated or destroyed by major mergers. Consequently, their prevalence in the local Universe strongly supports a "disk rebuilding" paradigm, wherein rotationally supported structures can rapidly reform following gas-rich accretion events [Puech et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753..128P/abstract). Internally, the thin disk undergoes continuous [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] driven by non-axisymmetric perturbations like spiral density waves and bars. These internal mechanisms redistribute angular momentum, shape radial [[abundance-gradients|Abundance gradients]] through inside-out chemical enrichment, and funnel material inward to trigger nuclear activity. Despite their prominent visual appearance, kinematic studies using [[rotation-curves|Rotation curves]] and the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] suggest that most intermediate-to-late type thin disks are [[submaximal-discs|Submaximal discs]], contributing only a fraction of the total dynamical mass in the inner galaxy compared to the dominant dark matter halo [Courteau et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...513..561C/abstract).

Recent observational advances have fundamentally reshaped the study of thin disks, pushing the frontiers of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. Instruments such as ALMA and JWST have uncovered rotationally supported disk candidates as early as $z > 4$, challenging traditional models that predicted delayed disk formation, though the true kinematic nature of these early structures—whether they are genuinely cold rotating disks or dispersion-dominated systems with anisotropic orbits—remains a subject of intense debate [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...973L..29W/abstract). This article explores the historical development of the thin disk model, its vertical and radial structural morphology, and its complex interplay with the interstellar medium via the [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]]. Finally, it reviews several critical open questions in the field, including the precise physical mechanisms responsible for uniform vertical disk heating, the origins of structural asymmetry in [[lopsided-galaxies|Lopsided galaxies]], the environmental quenching of disks in [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]], and the stringent limits placed by local astrometry on exotic theoretical components such as a coplanar [[thin-dark-disk|Thin dark disk]].

## Historical Development of the Disk Model

The conceptualization of the galactic disk has undergone significant evolution since the early twentieth century. In the original [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]], galaxies were classified largely by the admixture of their rotationally supported disk and their spheroidal [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]]. Early evolutionary models proposed that the Hubble sequence itself might be interpreted as an age sequence, with Sa galaxies representing the oldest disks [Sommer-Larsen et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...457..118S/abstract). Early morphological studies recognized that spiral galaxies are dominated by a highly flattened, dynamically cold component containing stars, dust, and gas, but the detailed mathematical and physical description of this structure took decades to solidify. Modern near-infrared "dust penetrated" classifications have further refined this view, revealing that evolved stellar disks follow distinct pitch angle archetypes that are decoupled from optical Hubble types [Block et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999A&A...342..627B/abstract).

A major breakthrough in the parameterization of disks came with the formalization of the [[freeman-stellar-disk|Freeman stellar disk]]. Observations demonstrated that the radial surface brightness of most galactic disks is well-described by an exponential profile:
$$ I(R) = I_0 \exp\left(-\frac{R}{R_d}\right) $$
where $I_0$ is the central surface brightness and $R_d$ is the radial scale length. This exponential decline became the standard model for the thin disk's radial mass distribution, although later deep imaging surveys revealed that many disks exhibit sharp truncations or breaks at roughly $2.5$ to $3.5 R_d$ [Pohlen et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000A&A...357L...1P/abstract). Vertically, the thin disk was historically approximated using an isothermal sheet model, which yields a $\text{sech}^2(z/z_0)$ density distribution, though subsequent studies showed that a simple exponential $z$-profile often provides a better fit to the vertical stellar distribution.

The modern understanding of the thin disk as a distinct subcomponent rather than the *sole* disk structure emerged in the late 1970s and early 1980s. Burstein (1979) first noted faint, vertically extended envelopes of excess surface brightness in edge-on S0 galaxies that could not be fit by a single thin-disk profile. The subsequent discovery of the Milky Way's thick disk by Gilmore & Reid (1983) firmly established the thin-thick disk dichotomy. It became clear that the classical "thin disk" is embedded within a ubiquitous, kinematically hotter component—the [[thick-disks|Thick disks]]—that typically possesses a vertical scale height roughly two to three times larger and a comparable or slightly longer radial scale length [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..226Y/abstract). 

Historically, the existence of kinematically cold, thin disks posed a theoretical challenge within the context of hierarchical [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|Galaxy assembly process lurk]]. N-body simulations demonstrated that thin disks are extremely fragile and are easily heated, thickened, or entirely destroyed by major mergers with mass ratios $> 1:10$ [Purcell et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...694L..98P/abstract). This apparent contradiction led to an early "thin disk survival" paradigm, which posited that local thin disks must be ancient structures that survived strictly through unusually quiescent merger histories. 

However, this view has recently shifted toward a "disk rebuilding" scenario. Modern morpho-kinematic data and $\Lambda$CDM models suggest that up to half of local thin disks did not merely survive undisturbed, but were actually heavily disrupted and subsequently rebuilt from gas-rich major mergers over the last 9 Gyr [Puech et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753..128P/abstract). Today, the thin disk is recognized not as an isolated, static relic, but as an actively evolving structure that continuously forms stars and can dynamically regenerate following cosmological accretion events.

## Structural Morphology and Vertical Architecture

The three-dimensional [[stellar-density-profile|Stellar density profile]] of a thin disk is typically modeled as separable functions of galactocentric radius $R$ and height above the midplane $z$. Radially, the surface brightness generally follows an exponential decline, $I(R) = I_0 \exp(-R/R_d)$, characterizing a [[freeman-stellar-disk|Freeman stellar disk]]. Seen from a face-on perspective, stellar disks are not perfectly circular but follow a log-normal distribution of intrinsic ellipticities with a mean of roughly 0.06 [Andersen et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..275...39A/abstract). At the extremes of this radial distribution, some [[giant-low-surface-brightness-galaxies|Giant low surface brightness galaxies]] (low-surface-brightness "iceberg" galaxies) possess giant thin disks with scale lengths exceeding 50 kpc [Disney et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987Natur.329..203D/abstract). Vertically, the mass distribution is frequently approximated either by an isothermal sheet profile $\mathrm{sech}^2(z/z_0)$ or by a simple exponential $\exp(-|z|/h_z)$. Observations indicate that the [[scaleheight-distribution|Scaleheight distribution]] remains remarkably constant as a function of galactocentric radius. For a typical [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] like the [[milky-way|Milky Way]], the thin disk has a characteristic scale height $h_z \sim 300$ pc, making it roughly a factor of three thinner than the embedding [[thick-disks|Thick disks]]. The global flatness of thin disks is highly constrained, with mean scale length to scale height ratios $h_R/h_z$ observed in the range of $5.9 \pm 0.4$ [de Grijs et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996A&AS..117...19D/abstract) to $7.3 \pm 2.2$ [van der Kruit et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..273....7V/abstract). The intrinsic thickness of disks varies along the Hubble sequence, with Sc-type galaxies possessing disks roughly 40% thinner than Sab-Sb counterparts [Ma et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...388..389M/abstract).

Rather than extending indefinitely, the radial luminosity profiles of most thin disks exhibit a sharp downward truncation at large radii, referred to as the [[type-ii-disk-break-radius|Type II disk break radius]]. These truncations typically occur at $R_b \approx 2.5 - 4.5 R_d$ and are remarkably common, appearing in approximately 77% of edge-on thin disks [Comerón et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...98C/abstract). The physical origin of these breaks is closely linked to gas surface density thresholds for gravitational instability and star formation; truncations are observed where the underlying stellar mass density drops to $\sim 1.5 \pm 0.5 M_\odot \mathrm{pc}^{-2}$ and cold gas densities fall below $\sim 10 M_\odot \mathrm{pc}^{-2}$ [Díaz-García et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...667A.109D/abstract). While some surface brightness profiles appear to "anti-truncate" ([[type-iii-galaxy-profiles|Type III galaxy profiles]], where the slope flattens at large radii), the literature notes an ambiguity in these measurements: in edge-on systems, a majority of apparent Type III thin disk profiles may actually be observational artifacts caused by the superposition of the thin disk with a thick disk component, with the true thin disk anti-truncation fraction estimated at less than 15% [Comerón et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...98C/abstract).

The vertical structure of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] is multi-component and heavily mass-dependent. In massive, rapidly rotating galaxies (circular velocity $V_c > 120$ km/s), the thin disk dominates the total baryonic mass budget, and the ratio of thick-to-thin stellar disk mass is relatively constant at $M_T/M_t \sim 0.2 - 0.7$ [Comerón et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...98C/abstract). In contrast, for low-mass galaxies ($V_c < 120$ km/s), the mass fraction contributed by the thin disk drops significantly, and thick disk stars can contribute nearly half of the total luminosity and dominate the stellar mass [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..226Y/abstract). Note that there is a characteristic stellar mass threshold of approximately $2 \times 10^9 M_\odot$ below which galaxies become systematically thicker due to the increasing importance of turbulent motions compared to rotational support [Sánchez-Janssen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1009.2844S/abstract). Recent simulations suggest thick disks contribute approximately 35% of total disk mass on average, often best-fitted by multiple $\mathrm{sech}^2$ vertical components rather than a single exponential profile [Yi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..271....1Y/abstract). Furthermore, note that while thin disks are generally characterized by younger populations, geometrically thick disks exhibit strong radial age gradients, transitioning from ~9 Gyr in the center to ~5 Gyr in the outskirts [Martig et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..139M/abstract). 

At the other structural extreme, undisturbed fast-rotating spirals often harbor [[super-thin-disk-components|Super-thin disk components]], though disks this thin ($h_z \lesssim 200$ pc) have also been observed in disturbed massive spirals [Schechtman-Rook et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...795..136S/abstract). The presence of these super-thin structures correlates strongly with ongoing, high-efficiency star formation and distinctive infrared flux ratios (e.g., $f_{K_s}/f_{60\mu m} \le 0.12$), suggesting they represent the dynamically coldest populations that have not yet been subjected to secular disk heating or merger-driven thickening. Beyond the stellar component, the associated [[gaseous-rings|Gaseous rings]] (gas disks) are frequently warped along the major axis and line-of-sight, as evidenced by tilted-ring modeling in studies like the HEROES survey [Allaert et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...582A..18A/abstract).

## Kinematics and Spiral Dynamics

The kinematic structure of thin disks is defined by strong rotational support, with stars and cold gas moving on roughly circular, coplanar orbits. This macroscopic motion is well-described by [[rotation-curves|Rotation curves]], which typically rise rapidly in the inner regions before transitioning to a nearly constant velocity across the extended disk. The precise contribution of the baryonic thin disk to the overall gravitational potential remains a subject of active debate. Kinematic analyses of intermediate-to-late type spirals suggest that their disks are generally "submaximal," contributing only $15\%$ to $50\%$ of the dynamical mass within $2.2$ scale lengths ($h_R$) and implying dark matter dominance even in the inner regions [Courteau et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...513..561C/abstract), [Bershady et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...739L..47B/abstract). Conversely, early-type and massive high-surface-brightness systems may still approach a maximal disk configuration where baryons dominate the central potential [Lelli et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Galax...2..292L/abstract). Regardless of the exact baryonic fraction, the dynamical coldness of the thin disk is empirically robust; vertical stellar velocity dispersion scales tightly with the maximum rotation velocity as $\sigma_z \sim 0.26 V_{max}$ [Bershady et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...739L..47B/abstract). Accompanying these bulk motions, the thin disk also hosts large-scale galactic dynamos, which generate [[galactic-magnetic-field|Galactic magnetic field]] that can reach microgauss strengths and profoundly influence the local dynamics of the interstellar medium [Brandenburg et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ARA&A..61..561B/abstract).

The most prominent visual features of thin disks are [[material-spiral-arms|Material spiral arms]], which are not merely transient star-forming regions but true mass density perturbations affecting both young gas and old stellar populations. The mechanics of [[spiral-galaxy-dynamics|Spiral Galaxy Dynamics]] dictate that different kinematic sub-populations respond differently to these density waves. Dynamically colder populations (e.g., gas and young stars) exhibit a stronger fractional response to the spiral perturbation than kinematically hotter, older stars. Because the hotter populations are generally more metal-poor, this differential response naturally generates azimuthal metallicity variations across the disk, even in the absence of initial radial gradients [Khoperskov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...611L...2K/abstract). Cosmological simulations further indicate that the morphological persistence and growth of these spiral waves are jointly regulated by the gas fraction, disk shear, and the dynamical coldness of the disk [Ghosh et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251025848G/abstract). 

Over gigayear timescales, the internal dynamics of the thin disk drive the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]]. Non-axisymmetric structures, particularly central bars and spiral arms, exert gravitational torques that efficiently redistribute angular momentum [Combes et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..8616C/abstract), [Sellwood et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). These torques funnel cold gas inward, triggering nuclear starbursts and fueling [[galaxy-nuclei-and-complex-morphologies|Galaxy Nuclei and Complex Morphologies]] (active galactic nuclei). In the central regions, the subsequent vertical heating and bending instabilities of this accumulated disk material are thought to be the primary formation mechanism for [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). 

Furthermore, transient spiral modes play a critical role in reshaping the disk through radial migration. Stars migrating at the corotation resonance of a spiral pattern can experience significant changes in their orbital guiding radii without a corresponding increase in their random orbital energy (kinematic heating) [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014RvMP...86....1S/abstract). As a result, radial migration systematically rearranges the radial mass and metallicity distributions of the thin disk while preserving its structural thinness, significantly complicating the interpretation of radial chemical gradients as pure fossil records of inside-out formation.

## Stellar Populations and Chemical Enrichment

The [[stellar-component|Stellar component]] of galactic thin disks is predominantly composed of [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]] (Population I stars). These stars are generally younger and more metal-rich than the populations found in [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] and [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]]. The chemical abundances of these stars, along with the interstellar gas from which they form, serve as a fossil record of the galaxy's assembly and star-forming history.

Observations of local [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], including the [[milky-way|Milky Way]], reveal distinct chemical evolutionary tracks for the thin and thick disks. The thick disk is typically enhanced in $\alpha$-elements (e.g., [O/Fe] and [Mg/Fe]), reflecting a rapid, early burst of star formation. In contrast, the thin disk exhibits lower $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ ratios that decline monotonically with increasing metallicity [Franchini et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....161....9F/abstract). This pattern is the hallmark of prolonged chemical enrichment, where Type Ia supernovae gradually pollute the interstellar medium with iron over several gigayears. Despite the overall younger average age of the thin disk, recent surveys have identified an "old thin disk" population with ages exceeding 11 Gyr. This suggests that the early stages of thin disk assembly may have occurred concurrently with thick disk formation, prior to the major satellite accretions that were traditionally thought to heat and disrupt all early disk structures [Borbolato et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250400135B/abstract).

The spatial distribution of metals across thin disks provides strong evidence for an "inside-out" [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|Galaxy assembly process lurk]]. In this model, the dense inner regions of the disk collapse, form stars, and undergo chemical enrichment earlier and more efficiently than the diffuse outskirts, establishing prominent [[abundance-gradients|Abundance gradients]] [Matteucci et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..230..337M/abstract), [Muñoz-Mateos et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...658.1006M/abstract). As a result, the outer disk is systematically bluer and more metal-poor. The [[o-h-radial-gradient|O/H radial gradient]] in the gas phase is a particularly useful diagnostic of this process. Chemical evolution models indicate that the present-day slope of the oxygen gradient is highly sensitive to the critical gas surface density thresholds that govern star formation, while the time evolution of the gradient depends heavily on how star formation efficiency varies with galactocentric radius [Marcon-Uchida et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...520A..35M/abstract).

In addition to radial trends, thin disks frequently exhibit azimuthal metallicity variations. High-resolution $N$-body simulations have shown that metallicity can vary significantly across spiral arms. Rather than being exclusively the product of radial migration reshaping an initial radial gradient, these azimuthal variations can arise naturally from the differential response of distinct stellar populations to spiral density waves. Kinematically cold, metal-rich populations are more strongly concentrated within the spiral arms compared to kinematically hotter, metal-poor populations, creating observable abundance fluctuations along the azimuth [Khoperskov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...611L...2K/abstract).

The global metal content of thin disks is also tightly correlated with total galaxy mass and rotation velocity. [[high-metallicity-galaxies|High-metallicity galaxies]], which occupy the upper end of the mass-metallicity relation, have typically undergone efficient star formation and retained a large fraction of their synthesized heavy elements. Conversely, lower-mass disks with circular velocities $V_c < 120 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ are more stable against gravitational fragmentation, leading to star formation timescales that exceed their gas accretion timescales. This imbalance suppresses their effective nucleosynthetic yields, leaving them systematically more metal-poor than their massive counterparts [Dalcanton et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...608..189D/abstract). Ultimately, when star formation in these disks ceases, the thin disk structure is not necessarily destroyed; passive, non-star-forming disks represent approximately 30% of the [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] population, demonstrating that quenching does not always disrupt the fundamental disk architecture [Fraser-McKelvie et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..117F/abstract).

## Interstellar Medium and Star Formation History

The thin disk is the primary reservoir of a galaxy's interstellar medium (ISM) and the principal site of ongoing star formation. The ISM within the thin disk consists predominantly of cold neutral atomic hydrogen, molecular hydrogen, and interstellar dust. The distribution and phase balance of these components govern the [[galactic-star-formation-distribution|Galactic star formation distribution]] and morphological appearance of [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]].

In typical spiral galaxies, the inner regions of the thin disk are dominated by molecular gas. In the [[milky-way|Milky Way]], for example, a significant fraction of this dense gas is organized into the [[galactic-molecular-ring|Galactic molecular ring]]. Moving radially outward, the ISM transitions to being dominated by atomic hydrogen, which frequently extends well beyond the optical truncation of the stellar disk as part of the [[galactic-neutral-hydrogen-layer|Galactic neutral hydrogen layer]] [Fumagalli et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...490..571F/abstract). Recent deep observations suggest that this 'protodisk' region of neutral hydrogen may extend much further than typically recognized, reaching column densities detectable up to 60 kpc from the center [Bland-Hawthorn et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...849...51B/abstract). Furthermore, the detection of cold dust within these extended HI disks, such as in NGC 891, suggests that this peripheral gas may not be primordial but rather enriched by internal galactic processes [Popescu et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003A&A...410L..21P/abstract). 

The presence of prominent dust lanes—a defining characteristic of many edge-on spirals—is strongly mass-dependent. In massive rotating disks with circular velocities $V_c > 120$ km/s, disk instabilities cause the gas layer to fragment and collapse, leading to a relatively small gas scale height and the formation of sharp dust lanes. In contrast, lower-mass galaxies lack these narrow features, possessing a thicker, more diffuse, and turbulence-supported gas layer [Dalcanton et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...608..189D/abstract). Galaxies exhibiting these well-defined absorbing structures are sometimes morphologically classified as [[dust-lane-galaxies|Dust lane galaxies]]. The effective transparency of thin disks is an ongoing subject of debate, with suggestions that some disks are sufficiently opaque to significantly obscure light emitted from their far sides [Valentijn et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990Natur.346..153V/abstract).

Star formation within the thin disk is closely correlated with the underlying [[cold-gas-surface-density|Cold gas surface density]]. This relationship is parameterized globally and locally by the [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]], which posits a power-law dependence of the star formation rate surface density ($\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$) on the total gas surface density ($\Sigma_{\rm gas}$), typically taking the form $\Sigma_{\rm SFR} \propto \Sigma_{\rm gas}^N$ with the index $N$ falling between 1 and 2. Observationally, molecular gas depletion times in large spiral galaxies are remarkably constant, averaging $\sim 2$ Gyr [Schruba et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..292..311S/abstract). 

However, star formation does not proceed uniformly across the entire gas disk. Observations reveal abrupt truncations in the radial star formation profiles of thin disks, corresponding to critical gas surface density thresholds (often where the Toomre $Q$ parameter exceeds unity) below which gravitational instabilities cannot efficiently trigger cloud collapse [Pohlen et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000A&A...357L...1P/abstract), [Dalcanton et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...608..189D/abstract). Despite these theoretical thresholds, low-level star formation can persist in the far outer regions. Extended ultraviolet (XUV) emission has been detected in approximately 30% of disk galaxies out to 2-3 times the optical radius, indicating that sparse but persistent star formation continues in the atomic-dominated outer disk [Bush et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...683L..13B/abstract). Correspondingly, supernova activity continues well beyond the optical boundary, with core-collapse rates estimated at ~1.5 supernovae per millennium beyond the optical radius in spiral galaxies [Chakrabarti et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...863L...1C/abstract).

## Disk Formation and Assembly History

The standard paradigm for the growth of thin stellar components is the inside-out formation scenario. In this model of the [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|Galaxy assembly process lurk]], gas cools within dark matter halos, conserving its specific angular momentum to settle into a rotationally supported structure. Star formation occurs predominantly in the inner, denser regions first, with the star-forming disk gradually extending to larger radii over cosmic time [Matteucci et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..230..337M/abstract), [Boissier et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph..9120B/abstract). Observational evidence for this inside-out formation is robust, driven by the presence of negative [[abundance-gradients|Abundance gradients]] and specific star formation rate profiles that show older stellar populations concentrated near the galactic center, while younger stars populate the extended disk outskirts [Marcon-Uchida et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...520A..35M/abstract), [Muñoz-Mateos et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...658.1006M/abstract).

Placing thin disks within the hierarchical $\Lambda$CDM cosmological framework, however, presents a dynamical challenge. Thin disks are fundamentally fragile; collisionless $N$-body simulations demonstrate that mergers with mass ratios $> 1:10$ rapidly heat the kinematically cold stars, transforming thin disks into [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] with vertical scale heights increased by a factor of roughly three and vertical velocity dispersions more than doubled [Purcell et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...694L..98P/abstract), [Galán-de Anta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3939G/abstract). Given the high frequency of [[galactic-satellite-galaxies|Galactic satellite galaxies]] predicted by $\Lambda$CDM, the survival and ubiquity of thin disks in the local Universe initially appeared contradictory.

This tension is largely resolved by the disk rebuilding hypothesis. While major and intermediate mergers destroy existing thin stellar components, encounters between highly gas-rich progenitors (e.g., gas fractions of $f_{\rm gas} \approx 75\%-90\%$) can quickly reform a rotationally supported thin disk from the dissipative settling of the remnant gas [Yoachim et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006AJ....131..226Y/abstract). In parallel, minor mergers can drive the formation of thin, kinematically-cold nuclear disks and rings from satellite material deposited securely into the central potential [Eliche-Moral et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AIPC.1240..237E/abstract). Morpho-kinematic studies suggest that as many as half of all local thin disks did not passively survive the last 9 Gyr, but were actually destroyed and subsequently rebuilt following gas-rich major mergers [Puech et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753..128P/abstract). Indeed, simulations of gas-rich mergers at $z \sim 2$ produce remnants that closely match the kinematics and low velocity-field asymmetry of observed rotationally supported systems like BzK 15504 [Robertson et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...685L..27R/abstract). Continual gas accretion from the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] stabilizes these reformed disks, driving the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] and providing a fresh reservoir for star formation. For instance, cold accretion flows along cosmic filaments are directly linked to the robust growth and spin-alignment of assembling disk galaxies [Xu et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260116408X/abstract). Furthermore, the frequent presence of counter-rotating stellar disks provides clear evidence for external gas accretion from satellites or filaments arriving long after the primary disk's initial assembly [Gasymov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250402925G/abstract).

The emergence timeline of these structures is currently an area of active debate in [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. Recent observations have pushed the known epoch of disk formation much earlier than previously anticipated. ALMA observations of [C II] gas kinematics have revealed intense star-forming galaxies with [[ordered-rotation|Ordered rotation]] and bi-symmetric spiral morphology as early as $z=4.41$ [Tsukui et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Sci...372.1201T/abstract). Moreover, JWST has identified regular, edge-on stellar disks at even higher redshifts, such as D-CEERS-z5289 at $z=5.289$, which exhibits an effective radius of $\sim 1.3-1.4$ kpc and a stellar mass of $10^{9.5-10.0} M_\odot$ [Yan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240704209Y/abstract). Surprisingly massive structures have also been discovered, including a [[grand-design-spiral-galaxies|Grand design spiral galaxies]] with a half-light radius of 9.6 kpc at $z = 3.25$ residing in a dense environment. High-resolution cosmological hydrodynamic models, such as GIGAERIS, support these findings, demonstrating that Milky Way-sized halos can host kinematically distinct, thin rotating disks as early as $z \sim 8$ [Tamfal et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..106T/abstract).

However, the literature remains strongly conflicting regarding the kinematic nature of these early apparent disks. While many studies argue early disk structures are dynamically cold and supported by true ordered rotation, others contend—based on kinematic studies of local low-mass analogs—that many of these early structures may actually be dynamically warm or hot systems whose flattened shapes are driven by tangential orbital anisotropy rather than rotational support [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...973L..29W/abstract). Irrespective of their exact early kinematics, empirical measurements of rest-optical scale heights across $0.4 \leq z \leq 2.5$ reveal that high-redshift stellar disks must physically thicken after formation via dynamical heating over cosmic time to reproduce the local population [Hamilton-Campos et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956..147H/abstract).

## Scaling Relations and Dark Matter Integration

The structural and dynamical properties of thin disks are tightly constrained by fundamental scaling relations, most notably the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] and the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]]. Observations of local, non-interacting disk galaxies demonstrate robust power-law dependencies, with the observed rotational velocity scaling as $V_{obs} \propto L^{0.31}$ and the disk scale length scaling as $R_d \propto L^{0.33}$ [Courteau et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.10440C/abstract). Large SDSS surveys confirm the Freeman Law as a rigid upper limit for central surface brightness, while providing typical exponential scale lengths for canonical $10^{10.8} M_\odot$ galaxies [Fathi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1101.1339F/abstract). The slopes of these relations remain remarkably constant across different morphological types, although early-type disks exhibit faster rotation at a given luminosity than late-type disks due to their distinct star formation histories [Courteau et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.10440C/abstract). Additionally, analyses of gravitational torques suggest that disks are open systems that may double their stellar mass via continuous gas accretion over roughly 10 billion years [Block et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...394L..35B/abstract).

A critical application of these scaling relations is determining the relative contributions of baryonic and dark matter to the gravitational potential of the inner galaxy. Historically, dynamicists modeled spirals as [[maximum-disks|Maximum disks]], wherein the baryonic mass accounts for the vast majority (typically $\sim 85\%$ of the rotational support) of the dynamical mass within 2.2 disk scale lengths ($R \approx 2.2 h_R$). However, modern surveys strongly favor [[submaximal-discs|Submaximal discs]] for most intermediate-to-late type spirals. If disks were maximal, the residuals of the Tully-Fisher relation should correlate strongly with disk size or surface brightness; observations consistently show that these residuals are independent of surface brightness [Courteau et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...513..561C/abstract), [Courteau et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.10440C/abstract). 

Direct kinematic measurements of stellar velocity dispersions reinforce the submaximal paradigm. In the DiskMass Survey, the central vertical velocity dispersion of disk stars was found to scale as $\sigma_{z} \sim 0.26 V_{max}$ [Bershady et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...739L..47B/abstract). For standard assumptions of disk oblateness, this indicates that the stellar disk contributes only $15\%-30\%$ of the dynamical mass within $2.2 h_R$ [Bershady et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...739L..47B/abstract). The overall consensus places the baryonic mass contribution at $15\%-50\%$ in these inner regions for typical spirals, implying that dark matter heavily dominates the potential even at small radii [Courteau et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...513..561C/abstract). In this context, the [[universal-rotation-curves|Universal Rotation Curves]] (URC) provide a unified framework for the disk's mass distribution, showing that dark matter halo density cores scale predictably with galaxy mass [Salucci et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..244...53S/abstract). Furthermore, the observed flatness of spiral disks—specifically the [[scaleheight-distribution|Scaleheight distribution]] to scale length ratio—is theoretically inconsistent with maximal disk configurations [van der Kruit et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..273....7V/abstract).

Despite this consensus, the literature contains ongoing debate, particularly at the extremes of galaxy mass. While low-surface-brightness galaxies are unequivocally dark matter dominated, some high-surface-brightness and high-mass early-type spirals may still approach maximal disk configurations [Fuchs et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004A&A...427...95F/abstract). Additionally, a tight correlation between the inner circular-velocity gradient and the central surface brightness is naturally explained if the central baryonic fraction approaches unity [Lelli et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Galax...2..292L/abstract). 

Finally, regarding the physical integration of dark matter into the disk midplane, highly precise astrometric data from the *Gaia* mission have placed severe constraints on the presence of dark matter substructure within the Galactic disk itself. Theoretical models proposing dissipative, self-interacting dark matter suggest that a fraction of the dark matter halo could cool and collapse into a dense disk coplanar with the baryonic thin disk. However, kinematic tracking of stars in the solar neighborhood strongly disfavors the existence of a macroscopic, dissipative [[thin-dark-disk|Thin dark disk]] in the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] [Schutz et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvL.121h1101S/abstract). Thus, the dark matter regulating disk kinematics is overwhelmingly distributed in a roughly spherical or triaxial halo rather than a flattened co-rotating component.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite significant observational and theoretical progress, several fundamental questions regarding the formation, structural maintenance, and evolution of thin disks remain unresolved. While the disk rebuilding hypothesis successfully explains the high survival rate of thin disks in a hierarchical universe [Puech et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...753..128P/abstract), the exact physical mechanisms driving the vertical structure and steady maintenance of galaxies through [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] remain elusive. Observations show that the vertical scale heights of many thin disks are remarkably constant with galactocentric radius. This implies that the mechanism responsible for vertical heating must operate with equal efficiency across the entire disk extent—a severe constraint for which a natural physical explanation is still lacking. Furthermore, the literature remains divided on the origin of the older, thicker components; it is ambiguous whether [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] formed *in situ* from turbulent, gas-rich early disks, or whether they resulted from the rapid vertical heating of a precursor thin disk via satellite accretion [Vallenari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...451..125V/abstract).

Another prominent structural mystery is the origin of large-scale disk asymmetry. A significant fraction of spiral systems are classified as [[lopsided-galaxies|Lopsided galaxies]], displaying a pronounced $m=1$ non-axisymmetric pattern in their stellar distributions and [[rotation-curves|Rotation curves]]. While some theoretical models suggest this lopsidedness results from the disk responding to a similarly asymmetric dark matter halo potential [Jog et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...391..471J/abstract), other studies propose it traces recent, uneven external gas accretion from cosmic filaments [Combes et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..8616C/abstract). Another open area involves understanding the physical boundary conditions for galactic outflows into the circumgalactic space [Dahlem et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...457..121D/abstract). 

The influence of dense environments on disk survival is a critical area of ongoing research. In [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]], thin disks are subjected to ram-pressure stripping and tidal interactions, which strip cold gas and prematurely quench star formation. Untangling these environmental effects requires detailed comparisons of galaxy spatial and kinematic distributions against [[galaxy-cluster-mass-profiles|Galaxy-cluster mass profiles]], which trace the underlying dark matter potential wells governing these interactions. This environmental processing is intimately tied to [[morphological-downsizing|Morphological downsizing]], where the characteristic mass of actively star-forming disk galaxies decreases over cosmic time while massive systems transition into [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] [van der Kruit et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ARA&A..49..301V/abstract). To further complicate this picture, comparative studies of the Local Group suggest that while the Milky Way underwent quiescent evolution, the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] likely experienced a significant collision with a companion approximately 200 Myr ago [Yin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...505..497Y/abstract).

Finally, the true kinematic nature of apparent early disks at high redshift ($z > 3$) remains a primary objective for future spectroscopic surveys. Resolving the debate over whether these structures represent genuine, dynamically cold systems supported by [[ordered-rotation|Ordered rotation]] [Tamfal et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..106T/abstract), or transient, dispersion-dominated phases with anisotropic orbits [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...973L..29W/abstract), will require higher-resolution, spatially resolved kinematics from next-generation instruments.
