Galactic bulges are central, high-density stellar concentrations embedded within the inner regions of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]. Historically conceptualized as simple, scaled-down [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], bulges are now understood to be a heterogeneous class of objects shaped by diverse evolutionary pathways in the broader context of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] [Athanassoula et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AIPC..804..333A/abstract). The contemporary literature establishes a fundamental morphological and kinematic dichotomy between "classical" bulges and [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]]. Classical bulges are kinematically hot, dispersion-dominated spheroids characterized by steep surface brightness profiles (typically described by a Sérsic index $n > 2$). They are thought to assemble rapidly at high redshift through violent processes, such as major dissipational mergers or monolithic collapse, resulting in uniformly old, metal-rich [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]].

In contrast, [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] are kinematically cold, rotationally supported structures that exhibit flatter, near-exponential light profiles ($n \lesssim 2$). Rather than forming via early galactic collisions, pseudobulges assemble gradually through the internal secular evolution of the host disk [Kannappan et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127.1371K/abstract). Non-axisymmetric structures like [[galactic-bars|Galactic Bars]] redistribute angular momentum and drive gas inflows that trigger nuclear star formation, or undergo vertical buckling instabilities to produce geometrically thick, boxy or [[peanut-formation|peanut-shaped structures]] [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). A prototypical example of this secularly evolved geometry is the [[milky-way|Milky Way]], whose central bulge is overwhelmingly dominated by a cylindrically rotating, buckled bar rather than a classical merger remnant [Shen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720L..72S/abstract). Additionally, intermediate formation channels exist, such as [[clump-origin-bulges|Clump-origin bulges]], which arise at high redshift when massive gas-rich disks undergo [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]], causing giant star-forming clumps to migrate inward and coalesce at the galactic center [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514...77N/abstract).

The structural and kinematic properties of galactic bulges are inextricably linked to their host galaxy's global properties and central [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]]. Classical bulges strictly obey fundamental empirical scaling relations, such as the Kormendy relation, the Fundamental Plane, and the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ relation, implying a tight co-evolutionary coupling between the growth of the massive spheroid and its central black hole. Pseudobulges, however, frequently appear as outliers in these relations, suggesting that internal secular processes do not universally trigger the same feedback mechanisms that regulate black hole growth in classical merger-driven systems.

Despite a robust theoretical framework distinguishing these formation channels, significant open questions remain regarding bulge demographics and complex morphologies. High-resolution spectroscopy frequently reveals "composite bulges," wherein a compact classical spheroid overlaps with an extended pseudobulge, complicating efforts to isolate their distinct assembly histories and separating primordial remnants from accreted [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]]. Furthermore, the sheer prevalence of pseudobulges—and entirely bulgeless, pure-disk galaxies—in the local universe presents an ongoing challenge to the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological paradigm, which predicts that frequent hierarchical merging should make massive classical bulges nearly ubiquitous.

## Classification and Taxonomy of Galactic Bulges

Historically viewed as scaled-down ellipticals, galactic bulges are now recognized as a heterogeneous class of objects. Demographically, *red* spheroids alone account for roughly 39% of the local stellar mass budget, meaning spheroidal systems overall (including blue ellipticals and [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]]) account for approximately 42% of the local mass budget [Driver et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...657L..85D/abstract). Morphological and kinematic surveys broadly divide them into classical bulges, pseudobulges, and [[clump-origin-bulges|Clump-origin bulges]], reflecting distinct physical pathways in [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..431K/abstract). Furthermore, the GHOSTS survey indicates that massive [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] ($V_{\rm rot} > 200 \text{ km/s}$) possess extended stellar envelopes with power-law profiles that correlate with Hubble type and bulge-to-disk ratios, suggesting these structures may be more related to bulges than previously thought [de Jong et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..187D/abstract).

**Classical Bulges**
Classical bulges are spheroidal, kinematically hot stellar systems believed to form rapidly through hierarchical merging or violent dissipational collapse at early cosmic times [Jablonka et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996AJ....112.1415J/abstract). Photometrically, they are characterized by steep surface brightness profiles with a Sérsic index $n > 2$ and generally exhibit high bulge-to-total (B/T) luminosity ratios. Dynamically, they are dispersion-dominated and occupy the same region of the Fundamental Plane as [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. They obey similar scaling relations, such as the Kormendy relation and the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ relation, suggesting co-evolution with central supermassive black holes [MacArthur et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..245..443M/abstract), [Saglia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...818...47S/abstract).

**Pseudobulges (Disky and Boxy/Peanut)**
In contrast, pseudobulges are assembled gradually via internal secular evolution. Non-axisymmetric structures, such as bars and spiral arms, funnel gas toward the galactic center, building a rotationally supported, disk-like structure [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). Pseudobulges are typically well-described by near-exponential surface brightness profiles ($n < 2$) and lower B/T ratios [Gao et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..247...20G/abstract). They frequently exhibit ongoing star formation and extended star formation histories [Fisher et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697..630F/abstract). 

Pseudobulges are further subdivided into two morphological types: disky bulges, which form from centralized gas inflows, and boxy/peanut bulges, which represent the vertically thickened inner regions of galactic bars following buckling instabilities [Athanassoula et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0802.0151A/abstract). The [[milky-way|Milky Way]] bulge is a prototypical example of a boxy/peanut pseudobulge, exhibiting characteristic X-shaped morphology and cylindrical rotation [Shen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720L..72S/abstract). Demographically, galaxies that either host a pseudobulge or lack a central bulge entirely account for over 80% of galaxies with stellar masses $M_\star > 10^9 M_\odot$ in the local universe [Fisher et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...733L..47F/abstract). Unlike classical bulges, pseudobulges often appear as outliers on the Fundamental Plane, being larger and less dense for a given mass [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract).

**Clump-Origin Bulges**
A third class, [[clump-origin-bulges|Clump-origin bulges]], arises from the high-redshift universe where gas-rich disks undergo violent disk instability. In this scenario, massive star-forming clumps (with individual masses up to $M \sim 10^9 M_\odot$) migrate inward via dynamical friction and coalesce at the galactic center [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514...77N/abstract). High-resolution simulations show that these bulges possess hybrid properties: they are kinematically similar to pseudobulges (featuring significant rotation, flattened shapes, and near-exponential profiles) but possess the old, metal-rich stellar populations more characteristic of classical bulges [Inoue et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.1902I/abstract), [Inoue et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASPC..458..227I/abstract).

**Ambiguities and Composite Systems**
While this taxonomic framework is widely utilized, the literature notes significant complexity. Many galaxies host "composite bulges" where a compact classical core and an extended pseudobulge coexist in the same nucleus [Erwin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..309..359E/abstract). Furthermore, the assumption that all small bulges in late-type [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] are secularly evolved pseudobulges remains a subject of active debate. Recent kinematic studies reveal that some small bulges follow the scaling relations of massive ellipticals, suggesting they may be the early-formed, low-mass descendants of high-redshift compact "red nuggets" rather than products of recent secular disk evolution [Costantin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...601A..84C/abstract), [Costantin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889L...3C/abstract).

## Formation Scenarios: Mergers vs. Secular Evolution

The assembly of galactic bulges is broadly understood to proceed through two primary channels: violent hierarchical merging and slow, internal secular evolution [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..431K/abstract), [Gadotti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1208.2295G/abstract). These distinct pathways give rise to the well-established dichotomy between "classical bulges" and "[[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]]". Classical bulges are thought to form through major mergers or rapid monolithic collapse, producing kinematically hot, dispersion-dominated spheroids that resemble elliptical galaxies with Sérsic indices $n > 2$. Conversely, pseudobulges assemble via secular processes within the host disk, retaining disk-like kinematics, significant rotational support, and near-exponential light profiles ($n < 2$) [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract), [Gao et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..247...20G/abstract).

Secular evolution is primarily driven by non-axisymmetries in the galactic disk, such as [[galactic-bars|Galactic Bars]] and spiral arms. These structures redistribute angular momentum and drive gas inflow toward the center, fueling nuclear starbursts that gradually build a dense, rotationally supported pseudobulge [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). Simulations suggest that bulge growth can also be driven by [[galaxy-accretion|satellite accretion]]; as a satellite's orbit decays, it triggers transient bars that funnel disk material inward, increasing both the bulge-to-disk ratio and the Sérsic index [Eliche-Moral et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..1376E/abstract). Additionally, stellar bars themselves are subject to vertical buckling instabilities, which heat stellar orbits vertically to form thick, boxy, or [[peanut-formation|peanut-shaped structures]] that often exhibit an X-shape and cylindrical rotation (where rotational velocity is nearly constant with height above the midplane) [Shen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..233S/abstract), [Howard et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...702L.153H/abstract). The [[milky-way|Milky Way]] bulge is a prototypical example of this mechanism; kinematic surveys indicate that it is an edge-on, buckled bar rather than a merger-built spheroid, with any classical component contributing an upper limit of $\sim 8\%$ to the disk mass [Shen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720L..72S/abstract), [Wegg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1874W/abstract). 

An alternative pathway known as the "clump-origin" scenario operates at high redshift ($z \sim 1-3$), bridging aspects of both classical and secular formation. In massive, gas-rich disks typical of the early universe, [[violent-disk-instability|violent disk instabilities]] lead to the fragmentation of the disk into massive star-forming clumps ($10^8 - 10^9 M_\odot$). Driven by dynamical friction, these clumps migrate inward and coalesce at the galactic center [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514...77N/abstract), [Inoue et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.1902I/abstract). [[clump-origin-bulges|Clump-origin bulges]] resulting from this process typically consist of old, metal-rich stellar populations—resembling classical bulges—but exhibit the rotational support and structural flattening characteristic of secularly evolved pseudobulges [Inoue et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASPC..458..227I/abstract).

Modern observations reveal that these formation mechanisms are rarely mutually exclusive. Many massive galaxies host "composite bulges," in which a classical spheroid is embedded within a disky pseudobulge or nuclear disk [Erwin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..309..359E/abstract), [Cole et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..107C/abstract). Studies tracking bulge assembly across cosmic time indicate a two-stage process: an early, rapid assembly of compact spheroids at $z \gtrsim 1.5$, followed by extended secular disk evolution at lower redshifts [Conselice et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..245..429C/abstract), [Costantin et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913..125C/abstract). At intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 0.1-1.3$), approximately 60% of disk galaxies with bulges follow a red sequence consistent with passive evolution that began early in the universe [Domínguez-Palmero et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...489.1003D/abstract). In contrast, bulges in late-type galaxies at $z \sim 1$ show mean ages of approximately 1.3 Gyr, with stellar populations similar to their inner disks, strongly supporting a secular origin [Hathi et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...690.1866H/abstract). Correspondingly, cosmological observations indicate that the total mass of bulges and pseudobulges in the universe has potentially doubled since redshift $z=1$ due to ongoing secular processes [Combes et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..353..155C/abstract).

Despite the successes of this dual-pathway framework, the standard $\Lambda$CDM hierarchical merger paradigm faces significant tension regarding the high frequency of bulgeless galaxies. A significant fraction of local disk galaxies are "pure disk" systems devoid of any classical bulge. The survival of such dynamically fragile, pure disk structures over the last 8 billion years remains challenging to reconcile with the ubiquitous major and minor mergers predicted by hierarchical clustering models [Shen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720L..72S/abstract), [Sachdeva et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...820L...4S/abstract).

## Structural Morphology and Surface Brightness Profiles

The structural properties of galactic bulges are typically quantified through two-dimensional surface brightness decomposition, where the galaxy's light is modeled as the superposition of an outer disk and a central bulge [Méndez-Abreu et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...478..353M/abstract), [Gao et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..247...20G/abstract). The surface brightness profile of the bulge is most commonly parametrized by the Sérsic profile:
$$ I(R) = I_e \exp \left\{ -b_n \left[ \left( \frac{R}{R_e} \right)^{1/n} - 1 \right] \right\} $$
where $R_e$ is the effective radius enclosing half the total light, $I_e$ is the surface brightness at $R_e$, and $n$ is the Sérsic index describing the central concentration of the light profile.

The value of the Sérsic index provides a primary morphological distinction between the two main classes of bulges. "Classical" bulges typically exhibit steeper, more concentrated profiles with $n > 2$, structurally resembling scaled-down [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. In contrast, [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] are characterized by less concentrated profiles with $n \lesssim 2$, often approximating an exponential distribution ($n \sim 1$) similar to that of the surrounding [[disk-galaxies|galactic disk]] [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..431K/abstract). Near-infrared Hubble Space Telescope observations reveal that the average bulge-to-total flux ratio for early-type (S0-S0a) galaxies is approximately 0.25, and demonstrate that bulge luminosity—rather than the traditional Hubble type—is the primary scale governing disk parameters [Balcells et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...665.1104B/abstract). 

Simple one-dimensional profile fitting can sometimes misclassify these structures, making robust two-dimensional photometric decomposition essential to properly account for the presence of [[galactic-bars|Galactic Bars]], dust lanes, and compact [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]] [Böker et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003AJ....125.1073B/abstract), [Gao et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..247...20G/abstract). Near-infrared photometric surveys of early-type spirals show that bulges are rarely spherical, with a median intrinsic ellipticity of approximately 0.33 and shape indices typically ranging between $n=2$ and $3$ [Moriondo et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&AS..130...81M/abstract). The intrinsic three-dimensional geometry of bulges varies systematically with their Sérsic classification. Photometric studies and geometric modeling reveal that classical bulges ($n > 2$) often take the form of moderately flattened oblate spheroids. Analysis of structural parameters indicates that while classical bulges are often oblate spheroids, pseudobulges (with $n < 2$) frequently exhibit triaxial, nearly prolate geometries [Sotnikova et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1012.3176S/abstract). These structures are intrinsically flatter and more closely aligned with the kinematics and orientation of the surrounding disk [Méndez-Abreu et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...521A..71M/abstract), [Gao et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..247...20G/abstract).

In edge-on disk galaxies, a significant fraction of bulges—nearly half of those in early- to intermediate-type spirals—display complex isophotes that deviate from simple ellipses, appearing markedly boxy or [[peanut-formation|peanut-shaped]] (B/P) [Laurikainen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418...77L/abstract). These B/P structures are not distinct spheroidal components, but rather the vertically thickened inner regions of [[galactic-bars|Galactic Bars]]. They arise over secular timescales when a stellar bar undergoes a vertical buckling or bending instability, inflating the bar perpendicularly to the disk plane [Athanassoula et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0802.0151A/abstract), [Shen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720L..72S/abstract). 

High-resolution mapping and unsharp masking of B/P bulges frequently reveal an embedded X-shaped structure. This feature is composed of stars on vertically extended, bar-supporting resonant orbits [McClure et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008277M/abstract). The [[milky-way|Milky Way]] bulge is a prototypical example of this phenomenon, exhibiting a clear B/P shape and a prominent X-shaped structure traced most strongly by its relatively metal-rich stellar populations, whereas its metal-poor stars exhibit a more uniform, box-shaped distribution [Shen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720L..72S/abstract), [Wegg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1874W/abstract), [Gonzalez et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466L..93G/abstract). When observed face-on or at moderate inclinations, the geometric projection of these vertically thickened bars can instead appear as a more circular, lens-like morphological feature known as a barlens [Laurikainen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418...77L/abstract).

At the very centers of these structures, many galaxies also host nuclear [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] (NSCs). As the densest stellar systems in the universe, NSCs are common in both early- and late-type galaxies. They are thought to grow through similar secular processes—such as gas inflows and [[globular-clusters|globular cluster]] inspiral—that contribute to the broader buildup of pseudobulges [Cole et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..107C/abstract). A survey of massive disk galaxies within 20 Mpc found a high nucleation fraction of nearly 80%, indicating that nuclear star clusters are even more ubiquitous in late-type galaxies than previously estimated [Ashok et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958..100A/abstract).

## Kinematics and Internal Dynamics

The kinematic profiles of galactic bulges are fundamentally linked to their formation histories, broadly dividing them into two dynamical regimes: pressure-supported "hot" systems and rotation-dominated "cold" systems. The dynamical state is closely tied to the available gas reservoir: [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] possess molecular gas fractions similar to those of extended galactic disks, whereas classical bulges are generally gas-poor systems dynamically resembling [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]] [Fisher et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764..174F/abstract).

**Classical vs. Pseudobulge Kinematics**
Classical bulges exhibit a kinematically hot orbital structure characterized by a high velocity dispersion ($\sigma$) and relatively little ordered rotation. These systems are dynamically analogous to elliptical galaxies, supported against self-gravity primarily by the random motions of their constituent stars. As a result, they follow classical kinematic scaling relations, such as the Faber-Jackson relation and the tight correlation between central [[supermassive-black-holes|supermassive black hole]] mass and velocity dispersion ($M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$) [Kormendy et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...265..632K/abstract), [Saglia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...818...47S/abstract). For an idealized isotropic oblate rotator, the $V/\sigma$ ratio is strictly determined by the system's intrinsic flattening. Classical bulges generally possess $V/\sigma$ values that match or fall slightly below this theoretical prediction.

Conversely, [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] are kinematically cold, reflecting their origin in the secular, dissipative evolution of gas-rich [[thin-disks|Thin disks]]. They are rotation-dominated, exhibiting a $V/\sigma$ ratio that is significantly higher than expected for a pressure-supported oblate spheroid of the same ellipticity [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract). This high rotational support confirms that pseudobulges are fundamentally disk-like and are supported primarily by angular momentum rather than random kinetic energy. Full spectroscopic bulge-disk decomposition of surveys like MaNGA further highlights these dynamical differences, demonstrating that bulges consistently follow distinct specific angular momentum ($j_*$) versus stellar mass ($M_*$) relationships compared to their host disks, while also being systematically more metal-rich [Tabor et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.1546T/abstract).

**Bar-Driven Dynamics and Cylindrical Rotation**
A distinct kinematic signature arises in bulges formed through the vertical buckling instability of [[galactic-bars|Galactic Bars]]. In these boxy or [[peanut-formation|peanut-shaped bulges]], the vertical resonance of stellar orbits produces a highly anisotropic dispersion tensor. During the primary buckling phase, the vertical-to-radial anisotropic dispersion ratio ($\sigma_z^2 / \sigma_r^2$) increases significantly as stars are scattered out of the disk plane [Athanassoula et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0802.0151A/abstract). However, the transfer of angular momentum to the inner bulge via bars is highly dependent on the host galaxy's interstellar medium. Hydrodynamical simulations demonstrate that high gas content can actively inhibit the formation of strong bars, thereby limiting the amount of rotational velocity that classical bulges can acquire through subsequent secular evolution [Machado et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Univ...10..223M/abstract).

A defining characteristic of bar-driven structures is cylindrical rotation, wherein the mean rotational velocity $V_{\rm rot}$ remains roughly constant regardless of the height $z$ above the galactic midplane:
$$ \frac{\partial V_{\rm rot}}{\partial z} \approx 0 $$
Extensive spectroscopic surveys of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]], such as the Bulge Radial Velocity Assay (BRAVA), have confirmed that the Galactic bulge rotates cylindrically. This lack of a dynamically distinct, slowly rotating halo component at varying latitudes strongly supports the consensus that the Milky Way's bulge is predominantly a bar-driven structure lacking a massive classical merger-remnant component [Howard et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...702L.153H/abstract), [Shen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720L..72S/abstract).

**Composite Orbital Structures**
The internal dynamics of many local bulges are not purely hot or cold but composite. Advanced integral-field spectroscopy combined with Schwarzschild dynamical modeling reveals that distinct orbital structures frequently coexist within the same nuclear region [Erwin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..309..359E/abstract). In such composite bulges, a kinematically hot classical spheroid overlaps spatially with a cooler, rotation-supported pseudobulge or nuclear disk. Furthermore, dynamical fractionation can occur during bar formation: populations with different initial kinematics respond differently to the bar potential. This creates complex, metallicity-dependent kinematic structures where cooler populations are easily trapped into the prominent X-shaped orbital families, while hotter populations remain more uniformly distributed [Gonzalez et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466L..93G/abstract). 

## Stellar Populations and Chemical Enrichment

The chemical compositions and age distributions of bulge stars serve as a fossil record of their assembly, a field often termed stellar archaeology. The study of [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]] within bulges reveals a complex dichotomy that reflects their diverse formation mechanisms, distinguishing between early, rapid collapse and extended, secular evolution.

Classical bulges and massive spheroids generally consist of old, metal-rich stellar populations, similar to those found in [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. Studies of pure spheroids indicate a bimodal formation history: while a subset of ancient bulge relics assembled very rapidly at $z \sim 6$, the dominant wave of bulge formation and pure spheroid assembly actually occurred much later, peaking around $z \sim 1.1 - 1.3$ [Costantin et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...913..125C/abstract). Because of the intense star formation rates required for early, rapid assembly, chemical evolution models predict that the maximum level of deuterium (D) astration (destruction) in the galaxy occurs within the bulge [Matteucci et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.265..311M/abstract). These early-forming stars typically exhibit elevated [$\alpha$/Fe] enrichment, a hallmark of short star formation timescales ($\tau \lesssim 1$ Gyr) where the interstellar medium was enriched primarily by core-collapse supernovae before Type Ia supernovae could contribute significant iron [Gargiulo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.4133G/abstract). Mass-metallicity scaling relations demonstrate that more massive bulges are systematically more metal-rich than less massive ones and their surrounding disks [Jablonka et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996AJ....112.1415J/abstract), [Muñoz-Escobar et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251025642M/abstract). Furthermore, gaseous halos may play a role in recycling enriched stellar winds from the bulge back onto the thin disk, providing a unique chemical connection between the two components [Bekki et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...692L..24B/abstract).

In contrast, [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]]—which assemble via secular disk instabilities and gas inflow driven by [[galactic-bars|Galactic Bars]]—exhibit prolonged star formation histories. Their ongoing or recent star formation results in a higher specific star formation rate (SFR) and younger mean luminosity-weighted ages than those of classical bulges [Fisher et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697..630F/abstract), [Fisher et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...733L..47F/abstract). Analysis of mass-to-light ratios reveals that the luminosity-weighted average age of bulges in early-type spirals is often younger than that of later-type spirals, supporting widespread internal star formation in early-type systems [Yoshino et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008PASJ...60..493Y/abstract). Additionally, bulges residing in galaxies with low bulge-to-total (B/T) luminosity ratios often exhibit a "frosting" of young stars overlaying older populations, resulting in luminosity-weighted ages as young as 1 Gyr [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..225....6K/abstract).

Despite these general trends, separating bulge types purely by age is complicated. High-redshift disk instabilities can form [[clump-origin-bulges|Clump-origin bulges]] from the migration and coalescence of massive gas clumps ($M \sim 10^9 M_{\odot}$) [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514...77N/abstract). These bulges present a structural paradox: they are old and metal-rich like classical bulges, yet possess the disk-like kinematics and nearly exponential surface density profiles characteristic of pseudobulges [Inoue et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASPC..458..227I/abstract), [Inoue et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.1902I/abstract). Furthermore, while small bulges in late-type spirals are traditionally assumed to be secularly formed pseudobulges, kinematic evidence shows many actually follow the scaling relations of massive ellipticals, implying early, violent formation rather than secular growth [Costantin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...601A..84C/abstract), [Costantin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889L...3C/abstract).

The [[milky-way|Milky Way]] provides the most detailed template for resolved stellar archaeology. Tracing the three-dimensional structure using red clump stars confirms that the Galactic bulge is predominantly a boxy/[[peanut-formation|peanut-shaped]] bar that buckled out of the plane [Shen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720L..72S/abstract), [Wegg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1874W/abstract). Spectroscopic surveys of the bulge reveal a broad metallicity distribution function with distinct sub-components. The dominant bar component is metal-rich ($\text{[Fe/H]} \gtrsim -0.5$), but prominent metallicity gradients are observed, with the average metallicity decreasing further from the Galactic plane [Freeman et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.3660F/abstract), [Nataf et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PASA...34...41N/abstract). Furthermore, evidence exists for a distinct, metal-poor population ($\text{[M/H]} \sim -1.0$) concentrated in the inner bulge [Schultheis et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...584A..45S/abstract). Kinematic studies reveal a dependence on metallicity where these metal-poor stars ($[\text{Fe/H}] < -1.0$) exhibit lower rotation and higher velocity dispersion, leading many to identify them as an inward extension of the [[stellar-halos|stellar halo]] rather than the main bulge itself [Minniti et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PhDT........61M/abstract). This metal-poor component shows high [$\alpha$/Fe] enrichment and hot kinematics, maintaining an ongoing debate over whether it represents a minor classical bulge, the inner extension of the stellar halo, or the oldest stars formed *in situ* before the bar buckled [Schultheis et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...584A..45S/abstract), [Gargiulo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.4133G/abstract).

## Scaling Relations and Black Hole Co-evolution

Galactic bulges exhibit tight empirical correlations between their structural, photometric, and kinematic parameters, historically suggesting a unified formation scenario with [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. Classical bulges and intermediate-luminosity ellipticals occupy the same region of the Fundamental Plane, a three-dimensional parameter space linking the effective radius ($R_e$), central velocity dispersion ($\sigma$), and mean surface brightness ($\langle I \rangle_e$) [Kormendy et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...265..632K/abstract), [MacArthur et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..245..443M/abstract). Similarly, classical bulges adhere to the Kormendy relation, projecting as a continuous sequence alongside massive ellipticals. In contrast, [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] frequently appear as outliers in these relations; for a given stellar mass, they tend to be significantly larger and less dense than their classical counterparts [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract), [Ríos-López et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202546R/abstract). 

Despite this broad dichotomy, the scaling relations highlight the structural complexity of low-mass systems. Recent mass-based analyses of the Kormendy relation have identified populations of ultradense small bulges in late-type spirals that follow the scaling relations of massive ellipticals [Costantin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...601A..84C/abstract), [Gao et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..262...54G/abstract). Their adherence to the virial mass-velocity dispersion relation suggests they are dynamically relaxed systems bridging the gap between massive ellipticals and [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]]. This positions them as likely low-mass descendants of high-redshift "red nuggets" rather than secularly evolved pseudobulges [Costantin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889L...3C/abstract).

The structural properties of bulges are profoundly linked to the masses of their central [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] (SMBHs). It is a well-established empirical result that the black hole mass ($M_{\rm BH}$) correlates tightly with both the bulge velocity dispersion (the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ relation) and the total bulge stellar mass (the $M_{\rm BH}-M_{\rm bulge}$ relation). In classical bulges and early-type galaxies, the central black hole typically comprises roughly $0.1\%$ of the host spheroid's stellar mass:
$$ M_{\rm BH} \approx 10^{-3} M_{\rm bulge} $$
Bivariate correlations incorporating the bulge's spherical half-mass density ($\rho_h$) or scale length ($r_h$) can predict $M_{\rm BH}$ with an intrinsic scatter as low as $\approx 0.33$ dex for classical bulges and core ellipticals [Saglia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...818...47S/abstract). Interestingly, the remarkable tightness of these fundamental empirical scaling relations has even been proposed as a stringent test for Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), which predicts lower intrinsic scatter in these structural correlations than standard $\Lambda$CDM hierarchical models [Zhao et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...686.1019Z/abstract).

The tightness of these scaling relations for classical spheroids implies a strong co-evolutionary pathway, likely regulated by major mergers and subsequent feedback from [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]]. However, this co-evolutionary paradigm breaks down for pseudobulges. SMBHs hosted in pseudobulges fall systematically below the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ and $M_{\rm BH}-M_{\rm bulge}$ relations, indicating that the internal, secular processes responsible for building pseudobulges do not efficiently couple with black hole growth [Saglia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...818...47S/abstract), [Ríos-López et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202546R/abstract). Pseudobulges only approach the classical relations when their central spherical densities exceed a critical threshold ($\rho_h \gtrsim 10^{10} M_\odot \, \mathrm{kpc}^{-3}$) or their scale lengths are very compact ($r_h \lesssim 1 \, \mathrm{kpc}$), which may be necessary to trigger the feedback mechanisms that couple the components [Saglia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...818...47S/abstract). Furthermore, the angular momentum and spin-up modes of low-mass SMBHs in disk-like bulges appear distinct from the merger-driven evolution of high-mass SMBHs, pointing to inherently different evolutionary tracks for classical and secular systems [Wang et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833L...2W/abstract).

## The Milky Way Bulge: A Detailed Prototypical Case

The [[milky-way|Milky Way]] provides a uniquely detailed view of bulge structure and assembly. Historically conceptualized as a prototypical classical bulge formed via early galactic mergers, the central region of our Galaxy is now understood to be predominantly a secular, bar-driven structure. The Milky Way bulge contains approximately one-quarter of the total stellar mass of the Galaxy, estimated at $1.8 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$ [Zoccali et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..265..271Z/abstract).

Deep near-infrared photometric mapping, particularly from the VVV survey, has revolutionized our understanding of the bulge's three-dimensional density distribution. By tracing red clump giant stars, the VVV survey confirmed that the central Galaxy is dominated by a highly elongated [[galactic-bars|Galactic bar]] exhibiting strong triaxiality [Wegg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1874W/abstract). This bar is oriented at an angle of roughly $20^\circ$ to $27^\circ$ relative to the Sun-Galactic Center line [Shen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720L..72S/abstract), [Wegg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1874W/abstract). Furthermore, at heights $z \gtrsim 400 \text{ pc}$ above the Galactic plane, the density distribution reveals a distinct boxy/[[peanut-formation|peanut or X-shaped morphology]] [Wegg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1874W/abstract).

The kinematic signature of this structure has been comprehensively mapped by the BRAVA survey. BRAVA radial velocity profiles demonstrate that the bulge rotates cylindrically—meaning its rotational velocity $V$ remains nearly constant at different heights above the midplane [Howard et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...702L.153H/abstract). This cylindrical rotation is a quintessential hallmark of an edge-on bar that has undergone vertical buckling instabilities, strongly disfavoring a massive, pressure-supported classical spheroid as the primary bulge component [Shen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720L..72S/abstract), [Shen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..233S/abstract). Recent dynamical models of the inner Galaxy suggest the presence of a dark matter core and provide a revised bar pattern speed that places corotation at approximately 6 kpc [Gerhard et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..334...73G/abstract).

Despite the macroscopic dominance of the bar, the bulge harbors complex, overlapping [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]]. The structural distribution of these populations is highly dependent on metallicity. The X-shaped morphology is predominantly traced by relatively metal-rich stars ($[\rm{Fe/H}] \gtrsim -0.5$), whereas more metal-poor stars display a more uniform spatial distribution—a phenomenon theoretically attributed to "kinematic fractionation," where populations with different initial velocity dispersions respond differently to the bar's buckling phase [Zoccali et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PASA...33...25Z/abstract), [Gonzalez et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466L..93G/abstract).

The complex stellar demographics also reflect ongoing dynamical assembly from external sources. For instance, the bulge grows through the dissolution of primordial clusters; a recent example of this is the discovery of SOS1, a nitrogen-rich, second-generation star likely stripped from the globular cluster Terzan 5 by the Galactic bar's gravitational influence [Souza et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977L..33S/abstract). APOGEE surveys have similarly uncovered a wider subset of anomalous, nitrogen-rich field stars within the bulge that share chemical abundance patterns with [[star-clusters|nuclear star clusters]] or dissolved [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]], confirming that dissolved clusters contributed a non-negligible fraction of the bulge's mass [Schiavon et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465..501S/abstract), [Ferraro et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..483F/abstract).

Spectroscopic surveys have also identified a distinct, *in situ* metal-poor population ($[\rm{M/H}] \approx -1.0$) concentrated in the inner bulge [Schultheis et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...584A..45S/abstract). The exact nature of this population is the subject of active debate. While some literature interprets it as the inward extension of [[stellar-halos|Stellar halos]], others suggest it may represent a minor ancient classical bulge component formed via early dissipative collapse or the accretion of primordial clumps [Schultheis et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...584A..45S/abstract), [Nataf et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PASA...34...41N/abstract). However, if a classical component does exist, dynamical modeling of BRAVA data constrains its upper mass limit to roughly $8\%$ of the total disk mass [Shen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720L..72S/abstract). Additionally, combined constraints from microlensing and star counts estimate the dark matter mass fraction within the Milky Way's bulge region to be approximately 28% [Koshimoto et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...78K/abstract).

Consequently, the Milky Way bulge is currently viewed as a composite system. While minor traces of primordial hierarchical assembly likely reside at its heart, its bulk mass and structural properties are overwhelmingly the result of internal [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]] and bar instabilities [Gonzalez et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..199G/abstract), [Nataf et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PASA...34...41N/abstract).

## Bulges in the Context of Global Galaxy Evolution

The structural evolution of a galaxy, particularly the growth of its bulge, is fundamentally linked to its position on the star-forming main sequence and the eventual quenching of its star formation. Observations that decompose galaxies into their constituent parts reveal that the global star formation rate (SFR) of a galaxy is a composite of the independent evolutionary states of its bulge and disk [Morselli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...597A..97M/abstract). 

In the "compaction-depletion" scenario, episodes of significant gas inflow fuel both [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|central starbursts]] and the rapid growth of a dense central bulge. This high level of nuclear star formation temporarily drives the galaxy above the main sequence. The central regions are the first to experience star formation quenching—typically driven by rapid gas exhaustion and energy feedback from the central [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]]—which moves the galaxy down onto the main sequence. As the surrounding disk eventually exhausts its gas and ceases star formation, the galaxy transitions below the main sequence into the quiescent region [Morselli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...597A..97M/abstract). However, analysis of the Hubble Deep Fields suggests that many spiral bulges at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 0.6$) have experienced significant rejuvenation through secondary star formation bursts [Ellis et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...551..111E/abstract).

The chronology of this assembly exhibits a strong dependence on the total stellar mass. Massive lenticular and early-type spiral galaxies generally follow an [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]] pathway. In these systems (typically those with stellar masses $M_* \ge 10^{10} M_\odot$), the classical bulge builds its mass rapidly at early cosmic times and subsequently evolves passively, while the surrounding disk undergoes a much more extended period of star formation [Johnston et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.6141J/abstract). Conversely, in lower-mass galaxies, the bulge and disk either assemble concurrently or the bulge forms slowly over time via secular disk instabilities, yielding [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]].

The larger cosmological context and [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]] also play crucial roles, most notably manifesting in the well-known morphology-density relation, where bulge-dominated early-type galaxies are far more prevalent in the dense environments of galaxy clusters. However, separating the effects of mass from environment reveals a dichotomy between bulge and disk evolution. The colors and stellar populations of bulges are primarily determined by the galaxy's mass and are largely insensitive to the cluster environment [Hudson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.409..405H/abstract). In contrast, the disks of galaxies residing in clusters are significantly redder than those of comparable field galaxies. This indicates that while bulge formation is driven primarily by internal mass-dependent processes, the disks of [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] within clusters are highly susceptible to environmental quenching mechanisms such as ram-pressure stripping and strangulation, which starve the outer disk of cold gas without drastically altering the pre-existing bulge [Hudson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.409..405H/abstract). On a broader structural level, while simulations show a broad correlation between a galaxy's bulge mass and the number of dwarf satellite galaxies it hosts, this is merely a manifestation of the total halo mass; at a fixed stellar or halo mass, there is no underlying relation between bulge mass and satellite number [Javanmardi et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...870...50J/abstract).

Despite the successes of hierarchical merging models in explaining the origin of massive, quiescent classical bulges, the global demographics of bulges present a major challenge to the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. Over 80% of galaxies in the local volume with masses above $10^9 M_\odot$ lack a classical merger-built bulge, possessing either a pseudobulge or no bulge at all [Fisher et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...733L..47F/abstract). Furthermore, a stable fraction of these pure disk galaxies has survived without significant disruption since $z \sim 1$ [Sachdeva et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...820L...4S/abstract). The survival and prevalence of these systems suggest that the smooth accretion of cold gas along cosmic filaments, rather than violent hierarchical merging, is the dominant mode of evolution for the majority of disk galaxies.

## Historical Development of Bulge Theory

Historically, central [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] was interpreted through the lens of the traditional Hubble sequence, which positioned galactic bulges as "scaled-down" [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]]. In this classical framework, bulges were thought to be dynamically hot, dispersion-supported spheroids formed through rapid, monolithic collapse or violent major mergers. This view was heavily influenced by Walter Baade's World War II-era observations of the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] bulge through Baade's Window. By identifying these central stars as Population II, astronomers broadly assumed that all bulges consisted of uniformly old, metal-rich [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]] that were distinctly separate from their surrounding star-forming [[disk-galaxies|disks]].

A profound paradigm shift occurred over the late 20th and early 21st centuries, driven by improved kinematic surveys and high-resolution photometry. Observers noted that many bulges—particularly those in late-type star-forming disk galaxies—exhibited disk-like exponential surface brightness profiles (Sérsic index $n \lesssim 2$) and significant rotational support. This led to the formal recognition of [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]], which form not through mergers, but via the internal secular evolution of disks driven by non-axisymmetric instabilities such as [[galactic-bars|Galactic Bars]] [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..431K/abstract). Consequently, the literature now treats bulges as a heterogeneous class representing distinct evolutionary pathways rather than a single structural family, including [[peanut-formation|boxy/peanut-shaped structures]].

The classical merger-driven scenario aligns naturally with the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological framework of hierarchical clustering, where massive galaxies assemble via successive major and minor mergers [Athanassoula et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AIPC..804..333A/abstract). However, this theoretical picture struggles to explain the sheer numerical dominance of pseudobulges in the local universe and the survival of giant, pure-disk galaxies that lack a classical merger-remnant entirely [Sachdeva et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...820L...4S/abstract). A striking example of this tension is our own [[milky-way|Milky Way]]; wide-field kinematic data demonstrate that the Galactic bulge is overwhelmingly a boxy/peanut-shaped bar that buckled out of the disk, rotating cylindrically, with dynamical limits restricting any underlying classical component to a negligible fraction of the disk mass [Shen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720L..72S/abstract).

Modern bulge theory increasingly attempts to reconcile these local observations with high-redshift [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. Rather than a strict dichotomy between classical and secular origins, recent models propose evolutionary pathways where bulges build up through multiple phases. At $z > 1$, gas-rich disks are subject to violent instabilities, generating massive gas clumps that can migrate inward via dynamical friction to form old, metal-rich [[clump-origin-bulges|Clump-origin bulges]] that nonetheless retain pseudobulge-like kinematics [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514...77N/abstract), [Inoue et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.1902I/abstract). Furthermore, the compaction-depletion scenario suggests that subsequent phases of intense central gas inflow and star-formation quenching drive galaxies across the main sequence, structurally transforming their cores [Morselli et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...597A..97M/abstract). The contemporary consensus holds that bulges are complex, composite structures preserving the signatures of both early, rapid assembly and prolonged internal evolution.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

A major unresolved issue in extragalactic astrophysics is the tension between hierarchical clustering models and the prevalence of massive pure [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]. In the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology, frequent major and minor mergers are expected to invariably produce a classical spheroidal merger-remnant at the center of massive galaxies. However, observations indicate that approximately $15-18\%$ of disk galaxies in both the local universe and out to $z \sim 1$ are effectively bulgeless galaxies, lacking any discernible classical or pseudo-component [Sachdeva et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...820L...4S/abstract). The survival of these giant pure-disk systems (such as M101 and NGC 6946) without forming a massive central spheroid represents one of the most acute CDM challenges in models of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] [Kormendy et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..396..297K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..418..431K/abstract).

Even in systems where bulges are clearly present, accurately determining the mass fraction of classical versus secularly evolved components within composite bulges remains observationally difficult [Erwin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..309..359E/abstract). The [[milky-way|Milky Way]] itself exemplifies this debate. While kinematic surveys confirm the Galactic bulge is predominantly a boxy/[[peanut-formation|peanut-shaped bar]] that buckled vertically out of the disk [Shen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720L..72S/abstract), researchers continue to debate whether an ancient, metal-poor ($\text{[Fe/H]} \lesssim -1.0$) classical component lies hidden at its core [Nataf et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PASA...34...41N/abstract). Current kinematic models heavily restrict the mass of any such classical component [Shen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720L..72S/abstract), yet chemical abundance studies reveal distinct metal-poor populations that may be relics of an early, rapid formation phase [Schultheis et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...584A..45S/abstract). 

The evolutionary link between local bulges and high-redshift precursors is similarly ambiguous. While conventional models suggest that small, late-type bulges are nearly exclusively [[pseudobulges|Pseudobulges]] formed via internal secular evolution, recent observations show that some low-mass bulges still follow the fundamental scaling relations of massive elliptical galaxies [Costantin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...601A..84C/abstract). It has been hypothesized that these dynamically hot small bulges might be the low-mass descendants of compact "red nuggets" formed at high redshift [Costantin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889L...3C/abstract). Additionally, the paradigm of secular versus merger-driven formation is complicated by [[clump-origin-bulges|Clump-origin bulges]]. At $z \sim 1-2$, gas-rich disks undergo [[violent-disk-instability|Violent disk instability]], forming massive clumps that migrate inward via dynamical friction [Noguchi et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...514...77N/abstract). These clump-origin structures can possess the rotation-dominated kinematics of a pseudobulge while exhibiting the old, metal-rich stellar populations typical of classical bulges [Inoue et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.1902I/abstract), defying simple binary classification.

Resolving these tensions requires moving beyond simple photometric decomposition. Future progress relies heavily on sophisticated chemo-dynamical modeling that can simultaneously track stellar kinematics, ages, and abundance ratios (e.g., $[\alpha/\text{Fe}]$) across multiple overlapping components [Gargiulo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.4133G/abstract), [Di Matteo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PASA...33...27D/abstract). Such models are necessary to definitively separate accreted halo stars, trapped bar orbits, and primordial classical spheroids. Furthermore, constraining the co-evolution of these composite structures with their central [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]]—particularly establishing why pseudobulges often fail to follow the strict $M_{\text{BH}}-\sigma$ relations of classical bulges [Saglia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...818...47S/abstract)—will be a primary objective for the next generation of highly multiplexed spectroscopic surveys and high-resolution cosmological simulations.
