## Article

The **half-mass radius** ($r_{1/2}$ or $r_{\rm mass}$) is a fundamental spatial scale in astrophysics defined as the distance from the center of a [[self-gravitating-systems|self-gravitating system]] that encloses exactly half of its [[total-gravitating-mass|Total gravitating mass]]. While early studies of [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]] primarily relied on observational metrics such as [[isophotal-diameters|Isophotal diameters]] or the [[half-light-radius|Half-light radius]] to quantify physical extent [Hubble et al. (1936)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1936rene.book.....H/abstract)[de Vaucouleurs et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980AJ.....85..637D/abstract), the half-mass radius provides a more rigorous, physically motivated measure of mass concentration. It serves as a crucial parameter for evaluating theoretical models of galaxy assembly, linking the observable [[stellar-component|Stellar component]] to the underlying dynamical properties of host dark matter halos [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract). 

In practice, deriving the intrinsic half-mass radius is complicated by radial variations in the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]]. Because galactic centers generally host older, redder stellar populations and experience different dust attenuation than their outskirts, the stellar half-mass radius is systematically smaller—typically by about 25%—than the rest-frame optical [[half-light-radius|Half-light radius]] [Szomoru et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763...73S/abstract). This spatial discrepancy fundamentally alters the interpretation of [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] like the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]]. For instance, while light-weighted sizes suggest rapid spatial expansion of galaxies over cosmic time, mass-weighted analyses reveal much slower structural evolution, challenging standard paradigms of continuous inside-out growth. Dynamically, the half-mass scale also represents a privileged anchor point; the [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] enclosed within the 3D half-mass radius can be robustly determined from the line-of-sight velocity dispersion, remaining highly insensitive to unknown orbital anisotropies.

Beyond the luminous bodies of galaxies, the half-mass concept extends to non-baryonic structures, though calculating the total mass of extended dark matter halos requires defining physically motivated outer boundaries such as the [[splashback-radius|Splashback radius]]. Despite advances in spatially resolved mass modeling, deriving accurate half-mass radii faces ongoing challenges. Active open questions in the field include breaking the degeneracies between central dust obscuration and true stellar mass buildup at high redshift, understanding the impact of environmental stripping on satellite mass profiles, and determining whether the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] systematically varies with [[galactocentric-distance|Galactocentric distance]]. To address these tensions, modern extragalactic research increasingly contrasts the half-mass radius with alternative, physically motivated size indicators—such as gas density thresholds for star formation—to better capture the complex structural evolution of galaxies across cosmic time.

## Definition and Fundamental Concepts

The half-mass radius, often denoted $r_{1/2}$ or $r_{\rm mass}$, is a fundamental characteristic scale describing the spatial concentration of [[self-gravitating-systems|Self-gravitating systems]]. It is defined as the radius of a sphere that contains exactly half of the total [[enclosed-mass|Enclosed mass]] of the object. Mathematically, for a spherically symmetric system with a 3D [[spatial-density|Spatial density]] (or [[mass-density|Mass density]]) profile $\rho(r)$, the half-mass radius is found by solving:

$$ M(r_{1/2}) = \int_{0}^{r_{1/2}} 4\pi r^2 \rho(r) \, dr = \frac{1}{2} M_{\rm total} $$

where $M_{\rm total}$ is the total mass of the system.

In [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], directly measuring the intrinsic 3D half-mass radius is challenging due to projection effects and the necessary reliance on luminous tracers. Consequently, studies often substitute the 2D projected half-light radius (or effective radius, $R_e$). For systems with an unknown or large range in intrinsic triaxiality, an unbiased proxy for the intrinsic 3D median radius is approximately $1.3 R_e$, where $R_e$ is the circularized [[half-light-radius|Half-light radius]] [van de Ven et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...914...45V/abstract).

However, mapping light to mass introduces systematic biases. The half-mass radius of a galaxy is systematically smaller than its half-light radius, typically by 10% to 40% (averaging $\sim 25\%$) across a wide range of redshifts from $z \approx 0.5$ to $2.5$ [Szomoru et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763...73S/abstract)[Suess et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877..103S/abstract)[Miller et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945..155M/abstract). This spatial discrepancy is primarily driven by negative color gradients and radial variations in the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]]; galaxy centers are generally older, redder, and have higher mass-to-light ratios than their outskirts [Baes et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.182B/abstract).

This distinction has critical implications for interpreting structural evolution. While half-light radii show rapid galaxy size growth over cosmic time, half-mass radii evolve much more slowly. For example, studies of Milky Way-type progenitors indicate that their half-mass radius has remained nearly constant at roughly $2$ to $3 \text{ kpc}$ over the last 10 Gyr [Hasheminia et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932L..23H/abstract). Recent multi-method reviews of the Milky Way's mass place its [[total-mass-astrophysics|total virial mass]] at approximately $1.02 \times 10^{12} M_\odot$, providing a global scale against which its internal half-mass distribution is measured [Bobylev et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PPulO.228...57B/abstract). This foundational work on our galaxy's structure builds on early three-component models of the Milky Way, which successfully reconciled local kinematics with global mass distributions for the disk, spheroid, and corona [Ostriker et al. (1979)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1979IAUS...84..441O/abstract). Furthermore, local estimates of [[local-dark-matter-density|Local dark matter density]] at the Sun's location (approximately $0.43 \text{ GeV cm}^{-3}$) provide a benchmark for mass modeling within the Milky Way's half-mass scale [Nesti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.3670N/abstract). This suggests that the apparent size evolution of galaxies is heavily influenced by shifting light profiles and color gradients, rather than the pure physical expansion of the underlying mass distribution [Suess et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...885L..22S/abstract).

Dynamically, the half-mass radius is a privileged scale for [[mass-estimators|Mass estimators]]. By manipulating the spherical Jeans equation, it has been shown that the [[enclosed-mass|Enclosed mass]] within the 3D deprojected half-light (or half-mass) radius can be robustly determined from the line-of-sight velocity dispersion ($\sigma_{\rm los}$) with minimal assumptions about orbital anisotropy [Wolf et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..271..110W/abstract). The enclosed mass $M_{1/2}$ within $r_{1/2}$ is given by:

$$ M_{1/2} \simeq \frac{3 \langle\sigma_{\rm los}^2\rangle r_{1/2}}{G} \simeq \frac{4 \langle\sigma_{\rm los}^2\rangle R_e}{G} $$

where $G$ is the [[gravitational-constant|Gravitational constant]] and $\langle\sigma_{\rm los}^2\rangle$ is the luminosity-weighted square of the line-of-sight velocity dispersion. This formulation demonstrates that the mass enclosed at this characteristic radius is highly insensitive to spatial variations in stellar velocity dispersion anisotropy, making it a highly reliable anchor point for dynamical mass modeling.

The half-mass radius is equally fundamental for understanding star cluster evolution. For instance, observations of globular clusters indicate that the peak of the cluster mass function increases with their internal half-mass density, suggesting that disruption by two-body relaxation is less effective for diffuse systems [Goudfrooij et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...750..140G/abstract). In star clusters, [[core-radius|Core radius]] expansion can be significantly influenced by the presence of hard primordial binaries rather than external tidal fields [Wilkinson et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.343.1025W/abstract).

## Historical Development of Size Measures

Early definitions of galaxy size in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] were primarily driven by observational convenience rather than underlying physical theory [Chamba et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020RNAAS...4..117C/abstract). Historically, astronomers characterized the spatial extent of extended objects using [[isophotal-diameters|Isophotal diameters]], which are defined by curves of constant surface brightness. A classic example is the Holmberg radius, defined as the semimajor axis length of the isophote where the $B$-band surface brightness drops to $\mu_B = 26.5 \text{ mag arcsec}^{-2}$ (see [[holmberg-galaxy-properties|Holmberg Galaxy Properties]]). Extensive early surveys utilized these isophotal metrics to catalog diameters for galactic nuclei, lenses, and rings, which initially served as foundational [[standard-candles|distance indicators]] [de Vaucouleurs et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980AJ.....85..637D/abstract). Crucially, the determination of the Hubble constant historically set the scale and expansion age of the universe, which is required to convert observed angular sizes to true physical half-mass radii [Huchra et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992Sci...256..321H/abstract).

As photometric techniques advanced, the [[half-light-radius|Half-light radius]] (or effective radius, $R_e$) supplanted isophotal boundaries as the standard metric for galaxy size. Defined as the characteristic radius enclosing half of the total observed flux, $R_e$ provided a scale that could be mathematically coupled to standardized empirical models, such as the [[de-vaucouleurs-law|De Vaucouleurs law]] ($r^{1/4}$ profile) for elliptical galaxies or exponential profiles for [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]]. The effective radius became deeply embedded in the field, forming a primary axis for [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] like the fundamental plane. However, $R_e$ fundamentally measures the concentration of light rather than tracing the true physical boundary or mass distribution of a system [Chamba et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...633L...3C/abstract).

The modern transition from light-weighted to mass-weighted structural parameters was precipitated by the recognition that galaxies exhibit strong radial gradients in their stellar populations and dust attenuation. Early reviews of elliptical galaxies noted that they exhibit a wider range of radial variations in their mass-to-light ratios compared to spiral galaxies of similar luminosity [Kent et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ASPC...10..109K/abstract). Conversely, historical reviews of spiral galaxies emphasize the difficulty in disentangling disk and halo mass components within the optical half-mass radius [Bosma et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..197...91B/abstract). Because galactic centers generally host older, more metal-rich stellar populations compared to their outskirts, they possess a higher [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]] ($M/L$). Consequently, studies utilizing spatially resolved spectral energy distribution modeling have consistently demonstrated that the half-mass radius ($r_{\rm mass}$) is systematically smaller than the half-light radius [Szomoru et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763...73S/abstract). At wavelengths ranging from the optical to the near-infrared, the half-mass radius is typically 10% to 40% smaller than the effective radius. This offset is driven primarily (about 80%) by negative color gradients, with differential dust attenuation providing a secondary contribution [Suess et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877..103S/abstract)[Miller et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945..155M/abstract)[Baes et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.182B/abstract).

This historical shift in size definitions has forced a paradigm shift in our understanding of galaxy assembly and [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]]. Under the old paradigm relying on half-light measurements, galaxies appeared to undergo rapid size growth—often doubling or tripling in size—from $z \sim 2$ to the present day. However, recent analyses utilizing half-mass radii reveal that the physical "cores" of galaxies grow much more slowly. For example, the half-mass radii of Milky Way-type progenitors are found to remain roughly constant at $\sim 2$ to $3 \text{ kpc}$ over the last 10 Gyr [Hasheminia et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932L..23H/abstract). It is now understood that much of the historically observed size evolution was an artifact of light-weighting; the apparent growth is largely driven by evolving $M/L$ ratio gradients as star formation shifts to outer disks, rather than a rapid physical expansion of the bulk stellar mass [Suess et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...885L..22S/abstract).

## Comparison with Half-Light Radius and Color Gradients

In observational extragalactic astronomy, structural scales are most commonly measured using the [[half-light-radius|Half-light radius]] (or effective radius, $R_e$), which characterizes the spatial distribution of a galaxy's emitted flux. However, the half-mass radius ($r_{\rm mass}$), which encloses half of the total [[stellar-component|Stellar component]] mass, is a more fundamental physical parameter. The two radii generally differ because the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]] ($M/L$) is not constant with [[galactocentric-distance|Galactocentric distance]]. 

The primary driver of this spatial discrepancy is the presence of radial color gradients. Early models of spiral galaxies like M81 suggested that radial color gradients arise because the consumption fraction of gas into stars is a function of galactocentric radius [Segalovitz et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975A&A....40..401S/abstract). Most galaxies, both star-forming and quiescent, exhibit negative color gradients, meaning their central regions are redder and possess older stellar populations than their outskirts. Consequently, the central $M/L$ ratio is higher, leading the stellar mass to be more centrally concentrated than the observed optical light. Studies of mass-selected galaxies at redshifts $z = 0.5 - 2.5$ show that half-mass radii are systematically smaller than rest-frame optical half-light radii, typically by an average of $\sim 25\%$ [Szomoru et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763...73S/abstract). In spiral galaxies, the corotation radius often coincides with local minima or inflections in the radial metallicity distribution, further complicating the mapping of light to mass [Scarano et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..265..251S/abstract). In extreme cases—roughly 10% of massive galaxies, often those with extended disks and large central bulges—$r_{\rm mass}$ can be smaller than the half-light radius by more than a factor of two [Szomoru et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763...73S/abstract).

Because of these spatial gradients, the measured [[half-light-radius|Half-light radius]] is a strong function of the observed wavelength. For example, simulated and observed galaxies show that the effective radius in the optical $g$-band is, on average, $\sim 58\%$ larger than the true half-mass radius, whereas the offset in the near-infrared $K_s$-band is reduced to $\sim 13\%$ [Baes et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.182B/abstract). Stellar population gradients are responsible for approximately $80\%$ of this wavelength dependence, with differential dust attenuation contributing the remaining $20\%$ [Baes et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.182B/abstract). In certain high-redshift systems, the [[dust-continuum-half-light-radius|Dust-continuum half-light radius]] is also found to be significantly more compact than rest-frame optical sizes due to central obscuration [Popping et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.3321P/abstract).

The distinction between mass-weighted and light-weighted radii has profound implications for galaxy scaling laws and evolutionary models. The [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] derived using $r_{\rm mass}$ exhibits a $10 - 40\%$ lower normalization and a shallower [[logarithmic-slope|Logarithmic slope]] (by $\sim 0.1 - 0.3$ dex) compared to the relation based on $r_{\rm light}$ [Suess et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877..103S/abstract)[Miller et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945..155M/abstract). Furthermore, $r_{\rm mass}$ evolves much more slowly over cosmic time. While half-light radii suggest that galaxies undergo rapid, dramatic size growth from $z \sim 2$ to the present, mass profile analyses reveal that central [[stellar-surface-density|Stellar surface density]] builds up early, and the actual physical expansion of the mass distribution is significantly milder [Suess et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...885L..22S/abstract). For instance, the progenitors of Milky Way-type galaxies appear to have maintained a roughly constant half-mass radius of $2 - 3$ kpc over the past 10 Gyr [Hasheminia et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932L..23H/abstract).

It should be noted that the literature contains conflicting conclusions regarding the exact redshift evolution of the $r_{\rm mass}/r_{\rm light}$ ratio. While some analyses conclude the $\sim 25\%$ offset remains constant across $0.5 < z < 2.5$ [Szomoru et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763...73S/abstract), other studies find significant evolution, reporting that the median $r_{\rm mass}/r_{\rm light}$ ratio drops from approximately $0.75$ at $z = 2$ to $0.5$ at $z = 1$ [Miller et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945..155M/abstract). 

When full 3D spatial deprojection of mass is not feasible, geometric conversions can be applied to photometric data. For a galaxy population with an unknown triaxial shape distribution, theoretical deprojections show that an unbiased proxy for the intrinsic 3D median half-mass radius is $\approx 1.3 \times R_e$, where $R_e$ is the circularized projected [[half-light-radius|Half-light radius]] [van de Ven et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...914...45V/abstract). Even within our own galaxy, accurately determining structural scales can be complex. Recent findings indicate that the Milky Way possesses a broken-exponential radial structure, resulting in a half-light radius of roughly $5.75 \text{ kpc}$, which is significantly larger than previous single-exponential estimates [Lian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8.1302L/abstract). Furthermore, extended dark matter profiles profoundly affect measurements at high redshift; for example, rotation curves of galaxies at $z \sim 0.5$ confirm [[dark-matter-fraction|dark matter fractions]] of approximately 93% when measured out to 30 kpc [Magee et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023RNAAS...7..110M/abstract).

## Dynamical Estimation and Mass Modeling

In dispersion-supported systems—such as elliptical galaxies, globular clusters, and dwarf spheroidal galaxies—inferring the [[total-gravitating-mass|Total gravitating mass]] and its radial profile from stellar kinematics is traditionally complicated by the mass-anisotropy degeneracy. Because observers can only measure the projected line-of-sight velocity dispersion, it is mathematically difficult to separate the true enclosed mass from the unknown orbital anisotropy of the tracer stars. Historically, foundational models comparing de Vaucouleurs and King profiles established the use of projected velocity dispersion curves to derive total system masses [Bailey et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981MNRAS.194..195B/abstract). However, analyses of the spherical Jeans equation have demonstrated that this degeneracy is effectively minimized at a specific spatial scale: the 3D deprojected half-light radius (often used as an observational proxy for the half-mass radius). The mass enclosed within this radius can be robustly determined with only mild assumptions regarding the spatial variation of the stellar velocity dispersion anisotropy, provided the projected velocity dispersion profile remains relatively flat near the half-light radius [Wolf et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..271..110W/abstract). Rooted in the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|Virial Theorem and Parameters]], the enclosed [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] at this radius is given by the relation:

$$ M_{1/2} \simeq \frac{3 \langle \sigma_{\mathrm{los}}^2 \rangle r_{1/2}}{G} \simeq \frac{4 \langle \sigma_{\mathrm{los}}^2 \rangle R_e}{G} $$

where $\langle \sigma_{\mathrm{los}}^2 \rangle$ is the luminosity-weighted square of the line-of-sight velocity dispersion, $r_{1/2}$ is the 3D deprojected half-light radius, $R_e$ is the 2D projected effective radius, and $G$ is the [[gravitational-constant|Gravitational constant]] [Wolf et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..271..110W/abstract).

This estimator is highly valuable for calculating the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]] within the central regions of dispersion-supported galaxies. When applied to the broader population of dispersion-supported galaxies, the dynamical mass estimator reveals a distinct U-shaped relationship between the enclosed mass and the dynamical mass-to-light ratio ($\Upsilon_{1/2}$). While normal elliptical galaxies sit at the minimum of this relation with $\Upsilon_{1/2} \approx 3$, the mass-to-light ratio rises steeply to $\Upsilon_{1/2} \approx 3200$ for ultra-faint dwarf spheroidal galaxies, and exhibits a shallower rise to $\Upsilon_{1/2} \approx 800$ for massive galaxy cluster spheroids [Wolf et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..271..110W/abstract). These quantitative results confirm that ultra-faint dwarfs possess an overwhelmingly dominant [[dark-matter-fraction|Dark matter fraction]], and their half-mass kinematics are consistent with formation inside dark matter halos of approximately $3 \times 10^9 M_\odot$ [Wolf et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..271..110W/abstract). In gas-rich systems, similar extremes are found; for instance, observations of gas-rich dwarf irregulars like DDO 154 demonstrated that dark matter can dominate the mass profile (up to 90%) at virtually all radii [Rubin et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991AIPC..222..371R/abstract). Conversely, dynamical modeling of bright galaxies suggests that dark matter has a low density in their inner regions, meaning the bulk of the central mass resides in the disk [Sellwood et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998HiA....11..410S/abstract).

Beyond individual mass estimates, the connection between the half-mass radius and velocity dispersion fundamentally anchors galactic scaling relations. The stellar velocity dispersion measured within the effective radius acts as a reliable proxy for the velocity dispersion of the entire host dark matter halo [Schechter et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150802358S/abstract). Because dark matter halos are generally characterized by a single scale parameter, utilizing the central velocity dispersion provides a direct window into the virial radius and total halo mass. This linkage allows the stellar mass and half-mass radius axes of the Fundamental Plane to be non-dimensionalized by the halo mass and virial radius, respectively [Schechter et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..317...35S/abstract). Ultimately, robust dynamical mass estimators at the half-mass radius bridge the gap between local baryonic observables and global halo properties, shaping our understanding of the [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|Galaxy velocity dispersion function]] and hierarchical assembly.

Beyond stellar kinematics, gas dynamics provide an independent means of measuring the scale of dark matter halos. In particular, disturbances and spiral phases in extended atomic hydrogen (H I) disks can be used as a non-parametric probe to infer the scale radius of dark matter halos, allowing for detailed dynamical estimation far beyond the optical half-mass radius [Chakrabarti et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...771...98C/abstract). On larger scales, alternative gravitational models, such as those employing a [[galactic-potential|logarithmic potential]] at galactic scales, suggest that the true mass required to explain deflection angles may be lower than estimated under standard NFW assumptions [Das et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhyO...1500150D/abstract). Recent observations show that circular velocity curves remain flat out to nearly 1 Mpc, suggesting that galactic halos may be in isothermal equilibrium even at extreme radii [Hoeneisen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025IJAA...15....1H/abstract). At the smallest scales, the Bondi radius defines the zone of influence for accretion onto supermassive black holes; in M87, X-ray density profiles suggest accretion rates much lower than the classical Bondi rate [Russell et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451..588R/abstract).

## Scaling Laws and the Mass-Size Relation

Galaxies exhibit a tight set of [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] that link their spatial extent, characteristic velocity, and mass. Chief among these is the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]], which parameterizes how the intrinsic size of a galaxy—best characterized by its half-mass radius $r_{\rm mass}$—scales with its total [[stellar-component|stellar mass]] $M_*$. This correlation fundamentally reflects the physics of how baryons assemble, cool, and settle within their host dark matter halos [Navarro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ASSP...56..103N/abstract). For early-type galaxies, the mass-radius relation exhibits distinct regimes depending on the mass scale: high-mass systems follow a relatively steep [[logarithmic-slope|Logarithmic slope]] of $\log(R_e) \propto \log(M_*) \simeq 0.54$, whereas dwarf systems follow a much shallower slope of approximately $0.3$ [Chiosi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1206.2532C/abstract). 

Historically, the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] was quantified using the half-light (or effective) radius. However, negative radial gradients in stellar age and metallicity cause $r_{\rm mass}$ to be systematically smaller than the half-light radius. When calibrated using true half-mass radii, the empirical $r_{\rm mass}-M_*$ relation differs significantly from its light-weighted counterpart. The normalization of the mass-size relation is typically $10\%$ to $40\%$ smaller, and the slope is approximately $0.1$ to $0.3$ dex shallower [Suess et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877..103S/abstract)[Miller et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945..155M/abstract). This flattening holds for both star-forming and quiescent populations and heavily influences the theoretical constraints placed on galaxy assembly models. Note that while minor mergers introduce scatter in the effective radius, major mergers significantly drive the correlation between stellar age, metallicity, and velocity dispersion across the Fundamental Plane [Porter et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1201.5918P/abstract).

The substitution of half-mass for half-light radii also dramatically alters the inferred structural evolution of galaxies over cosmic time. While half-light radii show rapid spatial expansion from $z \sim 2$ to the present, half-mass radii evolve much more slowly. For example, at a characteristic stellar mass of $M_* = 10^{10.5} M_\odot$, the half-mass radii of star-forming and quiescent galaxies grow by only $\sim 1\%$ and $\sim 8\%$, respectively, between $z \sim 2.25$ and $z \sim 1.25$—a stark contrast to the $\sim 37\%$ and $\sim 47\%$ growth inferred from half-light measurements [Suess et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877..103S/abstract). Furthermore, studies of Milky Way progenitors indicate that their half-mass radii remained remarkably constant at roughly $2-3\text{ kpc}$ over the last $10\text{ Gyr}$, even as their total stellar mass increased by an order of magnitude. This challenges pure inside-out growth paradigms, suggesting instead a more complex assembly history involving central compaction and bar formation [Hasheminia et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932L..23H/abstract).

The half-mass radius is intrinsically linked to the underlying kinematic properties of a galaxy through global dynamical scaling laws. For rotating disk galaxies, the [[tully-fisher-relation|Tully-Fisher relation]] tightly connects the characteristic rotational velocity and [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] to the baryonic mass [Lelli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102499L/abstract)[Marongwe et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251120188M/abstract). In dispersion-supported systems, the stellar velocity dispersion serves as a robust proxy for the halo velocity dispersion. By non-dimensionalizing the stellar mass and half-mass radius axes by the total halo mass and virial radius, respectively, these empirical scaling laws can be analytically mapped to the scale parameters of the host dark matter halo, revealing a continuous structural framework across multiple orders of magnitude in mass [Schechter et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..317...35S/abstract). 

Similar scaling relations extend to other baryonic components and sub-structures. The H I size-mass relation, for instance, is remarkably tight across various galaxy types and is mathematically driven by the self-similar structure of extended gas disks [Stevens et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490...96S/abstract). Cosmological simulations like Simba confirm the robustness of the H I size-mass relation across various feedback prescriptions, attributed to the inherent self-similarity of H I mass distributions [Rabyang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251204582R/abstract). On smaller scales, investigations into star clusters highlight that cluster mass is a more fundamental predictor of radius than either age or the host galaxy's star formation rate, which is consistent with the behavior of systems in virial equilibrium [Sebastian et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260200445S/abstract).

## Dark Matter Halos and the Splashback Radius

While the half-mass radius of a luminous galaxy provides a robust measure of its stellar extent, extending this concept to the host dark matter halo requires defining a physical outer boundary. Spherically averaged dark matter halos are typically modeled with double power-law [[density-profiles|Density profiles]], such as the NFW profile or the [[einasto-profile|Einasto profile]]. In the NFW profile, the [[enclosed-mass|Enclosed mass]] diverges logarithmically with radius, meaning a halo "total mass"—and therefore its half-mass scale—is fundamentally dependent on the chosen truncation radius. Historically, [[dark-matter-halo-scales|Dark Matter Halo Scales]] have been parameterized by virial overdensity limits (e.g., $R_{200m}$). However, these are based on spherical collapse models rather than the local dynamics of accreting material. Analyses of the circumgalactic medium suggest that the hot gas component within the virial radius accounts for only ~8% of the expected baryons, leaving the 'missing baryon' problem largely unresolved [Li et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...855L..24L/abstract).

Recent advances in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] have shifted focus to the [[splashback-radius|Splashback radius]] ($R_{\rm sp}$) as a more physically motivated halo boundary. Defined dynamically as the apocenter of the first orbit of newly accreted material, the splashback radius separates the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|virialized]], orbiting interior of the halo from the infalling exterior of the cosmic web [Diemer et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...843..140D/abstract). At this boundary, particles accumulate, creating a sharp caustic in phase space that corresponds to the steepest logarithmic slope in the radial density profile:
$$ \min \left( \frac{d \log \rho}{d \log r} \right) $$
Defining the total [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] within $R_{\rm sp}$ yields a physically grounded framework for determining the exact half-mass radius of the extended dark matter component. In simulations, the splashback radius coincides with the inner boundary of the cluster infall region where radial infall ceases to dominate the dynamics [Pizzardo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A..82P/abstract).

The splashback scale manifests directly in the [[cumulative-mass-profiles|Cumulative mass profiles]] and phase-space structure of galaxy clusters. It has been observationally identified as a sharp truncation in the projected phase-space distribution of satellite galaxies, termed the "edge radius" [Tomooka et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.1291T/abstract). This halo edge radius can also be statistically distinguished using line-of-sight velocity information, effectively separating orbiting, infalling, and interloping galaxy populations [Aung et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.3981A/abstract). Furthermore, $R_{\rm sp}$ has been successfully detected in the intracluster light of individual massive clusters, marking the first time this dynamical boundary was observed in the extended stellar halo [Gonzalez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507..963G/abstract). To map these boundaries across populations, weak lensing surveys allow for the probing of dark matter halo extent and shape out to several hundreds of kiloparsecs from the galaxy center [Kuijken et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gama.conf..361K/abstract).

The exact ratio of the splashback radius to the traditional virial radius ($R_{\rm sp}/R_{\rm 200m}$) is highly sensitive to the halo's mass accretion rate, $\Gamma$; rapidly accreting halos exhibit smaller normalized splashback radii [Diemer et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...843..140D/abstract). Moreover, extensive weak lensing surveys show a characteristic U-shaped mass evolution for $R_{\rm sp}/R_{\rm 200m}$ across a wide range of cluster masses [Xu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971..157X/abstract). Beyond the splashback boundary, mass scaling relations can now analytically connect the inner halo to the turnaround radius—the distance where cosmic expansion balances gravitational attraction, typically located at $\sim 3 R_{\rm vir}$ [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240218634K/abstract). However, the turn-around radius, while useful for cosmology, is subject to roughly 20% measurement error due to the non-spherical nature of cluster environments and filaments [Hansen et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...01..048H/abstract). Within these environments, the structural scales of galaxies are strongly influenced by their accretion history. Notably, satellite galaxies are found to be systematically smaller than central galaxies of the same mass, with their size being proportional to the halo's virial radius at the time of their infall [Hearin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489.1805H/abstract).

Despite its theoretical appeal, there are active tensions in measuring the splashback radius. Measurements derived from galaxy number density profiles consistently yield smaller values for $R_{\rm sp}$ than those predicted from the underlying dark matter mass density. While dynamical friction effectively decays the orbits of subhalos—which would pull the observable splashback radius inward—simulations indicate that this effect alone is insufficient to fully explain the discrepancy in clusters with masses above $10^{14} M_\odot$ [O'Shea et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240518468O/abstract). Furthermore, because galaxy clusters are rarely spherical, non-spherical accretion along cosmic filaments significantly distorts the local boundary. Estimates of $R_{\rm sp}$ derived from local gas density or pressure profiles in these highly disturbed regions often overestimate the radius compared to the baseline dark matter profile [Lebeau et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A..19L/abstract). Resolving these environmental effects remains necessary to standardize the splashback radius as the definitive boundary for calculating halo masses.

## Cosmic Evolution and Progenitor Bias

Tracing the [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]] of galaxy sizes reveals a stark divergence between light-weighted and mass-weighted metrics. Observations indicate that much of the apparent size growth of galaxies over cosmic time is driven by evolving radial gradients in the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]] rather than the physical expansion of the [[stellar-component|Stellar component]]. Because galaxy centers tend to be older, redder, and more obscured by dust than their outskirts, the half-mass radius ($r_{\rm mass}$) is systematically smaller than the [[half-light-radius|Half-light radius]] ($r_{\rm light}$) across cosmic epochs [Szomoru et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763...73S/abstract). 

Quantitatively, this discrepancy fundamentally alters the observed [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]]. For galaxies with a stellar mass of $M_* = 10^{10.5} M_\odot$, $r_{\rm mass}$ increases by only $\sim 1\%$ for star-forming systems and $\sim 8\%$ for quiescent systems between $z \sim 2.25$ and $z \sim 1.25$ [Suess et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877..103S/abstract). Over the exact same interval, the corresponding half-light radii undergo a dramatic apparent growth of $\sim 37\%$ and $\sim 47\%$, respectively. At $1.0 \le z \le 2.5$, the normalizations of the $r_{\rm mass}$--$M_*$ relations are typically 10% to 40% smaller than those of the $r_{\rm light}$ relation, and their logarithmic slopes are shallower by $\sim 0.1-0.3$ dex [Suess et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877..103S/abstract)[Miller et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945..155M/abstract). The strong color gradients driving this divergence evolve rapidly down to $z \sim 1$, before remaining relatively constant at lower redshifts [Suess et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...885L..22S/abstract).

The exceptionally slow evolution of the half-mass radius challenges traditional models of inside-out galaxy growth. For example, studies tracking the progenitors of Milky Way-type galaxies show that they grew self-similarly on spatially resolved scales. The half-mass radius of these progenitors remained nearly constant at $2-3$ kpc over the past 10 Gyr, even as their total stellar mass increased by a factor of 10 [Hasheminia et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932L..23H/abstract). During this time, the radius containing the inner 20% of the mass actually contracted by 60% between $z=2.0$ and $z=0.7$. This structural evolution points to the early assembly of a thick disk and the accumulation of mass in a central bar, rather than continuous inside-out growth [Hasheminia et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932L..23H/abstract). Advanced gravitational imaging has recently allowed the detection of extremely low-mass objects ($\sim 10^6 M_\odot$) at cosmological distances ($z \approx 0.88$), providing a new test for sub-galactic structure models [Powell et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251007382P/abstract).

For quiescent galaxies, the modest physical growth in $r_{\rm mass}$ is broadly consistent with expansion driven by minor mergers, though the exact evolutionary pathway depends sensitively on the assumed [[specific-merger-rate|Specific merger rate]]. However, the interpretation of this size evolution is complicated by progenitor bias. Because galaxies that quench and join the quiescent population at later times are physically larger than those that quenched early, the average size of the quiescent population artificially inflates over time. Untangling true physical accretion from progenitor bias requires precise constraints on the [[formation-epoch|Formation epoch]] of the constituent stellar populations. The balance between these effects remains a subject of active debate, and the nearly static $r_{\rm mass}$ evolution of star-forming galaxies remains a point of tension with contemporary cosmological simulations, which generally predict more substantial mass-weighted size growth [Suess et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...885L..22S/abstract).

## Insights from Hydrodynamical Simulations

State-of-the-art cosmological simulations use the half-mass radius ($r_{\rm mass}$) as a fundamental metric for [[galaxy-structure|Galaxy structure]], offering a more direct physical probe of the [[stellar-component|Stellar component]] than the observationally accessible [[half-light-radius|Half-light radius]] ($r_e$). By generating mock observations from hydrodynamical outputs, theorists can map intrinsic 3D mass distributions to projected 2D light profiles. These mock profiles are frequently evaluated using standard analytical forms like the [[sersic-profile|Sersic profile]]. Such theoretical results are then compared against real-world observational data, which employs flexible, non-parametric techniques—such as Multi-Gaussian Expansion applied to real imaging data—to accurately capture gradients in the mass-to-light ratio [Miller et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945..155M/abstract).

Coupling radiative transfer codes (such as SKIRT) to hydrodynamical simulations like TNG50 reveals that the measured $r_e$ systematically exceeds $r_{\rm mass}$ across different wavebands. For instance, the optical $g$-band effective radius is found to be, on average, $\sim 58\%$ larger than the true stellar half-mass radius [Baes et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.182B/abstract). Detailed mock observations attribute approximately 80% of this spatial discrepancy to stellar population gradients—where older, redder stars predominantly reside in the galaxy's inner regions—and the remaining 20% to differential dust attenuation [Baes et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.182B/abstract). 

The spatial distribution of dust further complicates the interpretation of high-redshift galaxy sizes. Simulated main-sequence galaxies demonstrate that the [[dust-continuum-half-light-radius|Dust-continuum half-light radius]] at $850\ \mu\text{m}$ is significantly more compact than the rest-frame optical or near-infrared effective radii. However, in simulations, this apparent compactness is primarily driven by the severe obscuration of central stellar light, which artificially increases the measured extent of the near-infrared disc, rather than signifying the actual physical build-up of a dense central stellar core [Popping et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.3321P/abstract).

The half-mass radius also serves as a critical parameter for calibrating sub-grid feedback mechanisms. For example, maintaining the observed lack of correlation between the star formation rate and radius at a fixed stellar mass requires simulated models to systematically scale down galactic wind strengths in smaller galaxies [Lin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...93L/abstract). 

Despite the successes of hydrodynamical models, significant tensions remain between the observational literature and current numerical simulations regarding the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] and its redshift evolution. Observational studies explicitly calculating $r_{\rm mass}$ indicate that the half-mass radii of star-forming galaxies evolve much more slowly from $z \sim 2.5$ than their half-light counterparts, a result that strongly conflicts with the more rapid size growth predicted by many cosmological simulations and semi-analytic models [Suess et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...885L..22S/abstract). Furthermore, empirical evidence suggests that the half-mass radius of Milky Way-type progenitors has remained roughly constant at $r_{\rm mass} \sim 2-3\ \text{kpc}$ over the last 10 Gyr. This marked lack of "inside-out" growth directly contradicts the predictions of several state-of-the-art hydrodynamical simulations, presenting an ongoing challenge for models of galaxy assembly [Hasheminia et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932L..23H/abstract).

## Recent Advances: Physically Motivated Size Indicators

Traditional structural metrics, including the half-mass and half-light radii, are largely phenomenological. Because they are defined by integrating over [[stellar-isophotes|Stellar isophotes]] or parameterized by empirical models such as the [[sersic-profile|Sersic profile]], they predominantly measure the central concentration of a galaxy rather than its global physical extent [Chamba et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...633L...3C/abstract)[Chamba et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020RNAAS...4..117C/abstract). Consequently, studies tracking the half-mass radius over cosmic time often find slow evolutionary growth, masking the active assembly of the outer disk. To resolve this, recent literature increasingly advocates for replacing percentile-based radii with physically motivated size indicators tied to the boundaries of star formation and gas accretion.

One prominent alternative defines the "edge" of a galaxy as the radial location where the cold gas density drops below the critical threshold required to sustain star formation [Chamba et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...633L...3C/abstract). Applying this physically motivated boundary has profound implications for classifying distinct galactic populations. For instance, when evaluated using the star-formation threshold radius rather than the standard effective radius, ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs) exhibit sizes consistent with standard dwarf galaxies rather than Milky Way-sized systems, resolving much of the tension surrounding their structural nature [Chamba et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...633L...3C/abstract). Furthermore, utilizing this definition tightens fundamental scaling laws; the intrinsic scatter of the [[size-mass-relation|Size-mass relation]] reduces to just 0.06 dex, a factor of three smaller than relations calibrated to the effective radius [Chamba et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020RNAAS...4..117C/abstract).

Tracing this physical boundary over cosmic time reveals significant outer-galaxy assembly. Using the truncation edge of the stellar surface mass density profile as an observational proxy for the star-formation threshold, recent surveys of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] show that Milky Way-mass systems ($M_* \sim 5 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$) have doubled in physical extent since $z=1$ [Buitrago et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...682A.110B/abstract). This equates to an average radial growth rate of $\approx 1.5$ kpc Gyr$^{-1}$. Over this 8 Gyr period, the surface stellar mass density at the galaxy edge has fallen by more than an order of magnitude, from $\sim 13\ M_\odot \text{ pc}^{-2}$ at $z=1$ to $\sim 1\ M_\odot \text{ pc}^{-2}$ at $z=0$ [Buitrago et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...682A.110B/abstract).

The push for physically motivated radii also clarifies the interplay between galaxy size, the [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]], and feedback. Observations show little to no correlation between the total star formation rate (SFR) and the effective radius at a fixed stellar mass [Lin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...93L/abstract). Under the framework of the [[kennicutt-schmidt-law|Kennicutt-Schmidt law]], this null correlation implies that compact galaxies must possess systematically lower gas fractions than extended galaxies of equivalent mass to maintain the same total SFR. This constraint points to size-dependent evolutionary pathways, where smaller galaxies require weaker galactic winds to balance their mass accretion and star formation [Lin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...93L/abstract). 

Finally, for analyses retaining percentile-based structural models, researchers increasingly advocate abandoning the singular $r_{50}$ in favor of multiple percentile radii. For example, utilizing the inner radius $r_{20}$ probes localized star-formation processes, while the outer radius $r_{80}$ traces late-time accretion and the structural coupling between galaxies and their extended dark matter halos [Miller et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872L..14M/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite significant advances in spatially resolved photometry and mass modeling, several systematic uncertainties and theoretical tensions limit the precision of half-mass radius ($r_{\rm mass}$) measurements. A primary challenge is accurately mapping the stellar mass-to-light ratio across galaxies. This conversion inherently relies on the assumed [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. While the IMF is often treated as universal in standard models, any systematic spatial variations in the IMF within a galaxy would severely bias the derived radial mass profiles and the resulting $r_{\rm mass}$.

The role of central dust extinction remains another major confounding factor. Observations consistently show that $r_{\rm mass}$ is typically $10\%$ to $40\%$ smaller than the [[half-light-radius|Half-light radius]] ($r_{\rm light}$) due to negative color gradients, where galactic centers appear redder than their outskirts [Szomoru et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763...73S/abstract)[Suess et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877..103S/abstract). While stellar population gradients are the dominant driver of this effect, differential dust attenuation is estimated to contribute $\sim 20\%$ to the wavelength dependence of the effective radius [Baes et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.182B/abstract). In extreme cases, severe central dust obscuration can mimic the color signatures of older stellar populations. Consequently, it remains an open question whether the highly compact dust-continuum sizes observed in high-redshift ($z > 1$) galaxies represent a genuine build-up of a dense central stellar component or are merely an artifact of obscuration [Popping et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.3321P/abstract). High-resolution sub-millimeter observations are required to definitively break the degeneracies between dust extinction and intrinsic stellar mass structure [Szomoru et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763...73S/abstract).

The theoretical interpretation of $r_{\rm mass}$ evolution is also subject to active debate. For instance, while some semi-analytic models and cosmological simulations predict significant "inside-out" growth for Milky Way-type galaxies, recent observational studies suggest their half-mass radii have remained remarkably constant at roughly $2$ to $3\ {\rm kpc}$ over the last $10\ {\rm Gyr}$ [Hasheminia et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932L..23H/abstract). This tension highlights the need for a deeper understanding of how structural scales assemble over cosmic time.

In light of these challenges, the astrophysics community is exploring alternative metrics and scaling relations. Some researchers suggest that $r_{50}$ (the half-mass or half-light radius) convolves too many physical processes, advocating instead for the simultaneous use of inner and outer radii—such as $r_{20}$ and $r_{80}$—to independently trace star formation and halo accretion, respectively [Miller et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872L..14M/abstract). Conversely, the mass enclosed within the 3D half-light radius ($M_{1/2}$) continues to be utilized as a highly robust [[mass-proxy|Mass proxy]], as it can be determined from the line-of-sight velocity dispersion largely independent of orbital anisotropy [Wolf et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..271..110W/abstract). 

Finally, determining how environmental processes, such as ram pressure and tidal stripping, differentially truncate the mass profiles of satellite galaxies remains an ongoing challenge [Roediger et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.380.1399R/abstract)[Gajda et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...819...20G/abstract). Accurate $r_{\rm mass}$ measurements are essential for properly linking the baryonic distribution to the underlying dark matter halo. Precisely mapping this structural connection out to large radii will be crucial for testing the empirical limits of the [[radial-acceleration-relation|Radial Acceleration Relation]] and distinguishing between standard cold dark matter paradigms and modified gravity theories [Lelli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102499L/abstract). At smaller stellar scales, defining the boundary of mass distribution remains challenging; alternative boundaries for stellar systems include the 'stability radius,' which is interior to the tidal radius and marks the zone where orbits become unstable due to three-body interactions [Kennedy et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.3328K/abstract). Beyond typical galactic applications, characteristic radii are increasingly used to parameterize the inner structures of active galactic nuclei. For instance, recent microlensing studies of quasars find that the half-light radius of the broad emission line region is roughly six times larger than the radius of the UV-optical continuum source, presenting new challenges for modeling the environments around supermassive black holes [Hutsemékers et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A..10H/abstract).
