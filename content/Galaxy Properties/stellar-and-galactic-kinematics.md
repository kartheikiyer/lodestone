**Stellar and galactic kinematics** is the branch of astrophysics dedicated to characterizing the three-dimensional [[velocity-statistics-and-kinematics|Velocity Statistics and Kinematics]] of stars and gas within [[galaxy-populations|Galaxy populations]]. By combining line-of-sight [[galaxy-galaxy-radial-velocity|radial velocities]] obtained through spectroscopy with [[transverse-kinematics-and-distances|transverse proper motions]] measured via precision astrometry, researchers can reconstruct the [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]] of galactic [[stellar-tracers|Stellar tracers]]. A fundamental tool in this analysis is the line-of-sight velocity distribution (LOSVD), which captures the integrated kinematic profile of unresolved [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]]. Analyzing these motions allows astrophysicists to trace the underlying [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]], quantify the total [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]], and infer the distribution of invisible components such as extended dark matter halos and central supermassive black holes. The foundational principles of this field integrate classical mechanics with collisionless [[stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|Stellar and Galactic Dynamics]], forming the backbone of our understanding of [[galaxy-morphology-and-alignment|galactic structure]] and [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|Galaxy and Universe evolution]] [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract), [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

The kinematic properties of galaxies exhibit profound correlations with their [[galaxy-morphology-and-alignment|morphology]] and [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|assembly histories]]. In [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]], the dominant kinematic signature is [[ordered-rotation|Ordered rotation]]; the observation of extended [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] at large radii provided the first definitive dynamical evidence for dark matter [van der Kruit et al. (1978)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1978ARA&A..16..103V/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982SAAS...12..115K/abstract). Conversely, [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|early-type galaxies]] are primarily supported by the random motions of their stars. Modern [[spatially-resolved-galaxy-analysis|integral field spectroscopy]] has revolutionized the classification of these systems, bifurcating them into rotationally-supported [[fast-rotators|Fast rotators]] and pressure-supported slow rotators [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract). Because observations are generally limited to projected coordinates and line-of-sight velocities, characterizing these dispersion-dominated systems requires breaking the mass-anisotropy degeneracy. This is typically achieved by measuring higher-order deviations from a Gaussian LOSVD, such as the [[fourth-order-gauss-hermite-moment|Fourth-order Gauss-Hermite moment]]. At a higher resolution, our own Milky Way serves as a critical template, where large-scale surveys map the complex interplay between [[thin-disks|Thin disks]], [[thick-disks|Thick disks]], and a highly substructured stellar halo.

Beyond measuring static mass distributions, kinematics act as a fossil record of [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|Galaxy and Universe evolution]]. Because collisionless systems conserve phase-space density, the remnants of [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]] persist as coherent [[stellar-streams-and-moving-groups|Stellar Streams and Moving Groups]] long after spatial mixing has occurred, providing a direct window into hierarchical merging. Furthermore, kinematic studies across cosmic time trace the transition from highly turbulent, gas-rich systems at [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|high redshift]] to the dynamically settled galaxies of the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]]. Despite vast observational improvements, several open questions remain at the forefront of the field. Active debates include the triaxiality and spatial extent of dark matter halos, the physical origins of anomalous fast rotators that lack expected kinematic signatures, and emerging tensions regarding whether high-redshift galaxies possess substantially lower dark matter fractions within their optical radii compared to local disk galaxies.

## Fundamental Observables and Statistical Measures

The quantitative study of galactic and stellar dynamics relies on mapping the [[velocity-statistics-and-kinematics|3D velocity field]] of mass tracers. Observationally, this velocity field is decomposed into the line-of-sight component, measured as a [[galaxy-galaxy-radial-velocity|radial velocity]], and the transverse component, measured as a [[transverse-kinematics-and-distances|proper motion]]. 

**Radial Velocity and the LOSVD**
Radial velocities are derived from the Doppler shifts of absorption or emission lines in [[stellar-tracers|Stellar tracers]] and interstellar gas. In external galaxies, integrated starlight provides the line-of-sight velocity distribution (LOSVD). Modern [[spatially-resolved-galaxy-analysis|integral field unit (IFU)]] and multislit spectroscopic surveys use advanced spaxel cross-correlation techniques to measure the LOSVD even in regions where the surface brightness is orders of magnitude fainter than the night sky, achieving reliable kinematics at signal-to-noise ratios as low as $\sim 1$ [Pastorello et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PASA...33...35P/abstract), [Bag et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.2278B/abstract). Recent findings also demonstrate that the degree of small-scale kinematic fluctuations in velocity maps follows an inverse log-linear relation with the galaxy's [[stellar-surface-density|Stellar surface density]] [Zhong et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957L..12Z/abstract).

While simple, pressure-supported non-rotating systems typically possess roughly Gaussian LOSVDs, the presence of substantial rotation, embedded disks, or strong [[galaxy-velocity-anisotropy-profiles|orbital anisotropies]] introduces significant deviations from a simple Gaussian profile. These deviations are parameterized using [[fourth-order-gauss-hermite-moment|Gauss-Hermite moments]], where the LOSVD is expanded as:
$$ \mathcal{L}(v) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{(v - V)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \left[ 1 + h_3 H_3\left(\frac{v - V}{\sigma}\right) + h_4 H_4\left(\frac{v - V}{\sigma}\right) + \dots \right] $$
Here, $V$ is the mean [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]], $\sigma$ is the [[galaxy-velocity-dispersion-function|velocity dispersion]], $h_3$ quantifies the skewness (asymmetric deviations), and $h_4$ quantifies the kurtosis (symmetric peaking or flattening) of the profile [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract). 

Measuring these higher-order Gauss-Hermite moments is critical for breaking the mass-anisotropy degeneracy. A flat velocity dispersion profile at large radii can indicate either an extended dark matter halo or a stellar population with [[strong-tangential-anisotropies|tangentially anisotropic orbits]]; similarly, a central peak in $\sigma$ can indicate a supermassive black hole or [[strong-velocity-anisotropies|radial anisotropy]] [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract), [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract), [Gerhard et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..136..248G/abstract). Furthermore, the relationship between $h_3$ and $V/\sigma$ is a powerful diagnostic of galaxy assembly. A strong $h_3 - V/\sigma$ anti-correlation is typically the kinematic signature of embedded [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] in fast rotators. However, the literature notes an unresolved tension: a subset of systems (designated "Class 5" fast rotators) exhibit unexpectedly weak anti-correlations. This anomaly could imply a formation history dominated by gas-poor mergers, or it may simply reflect internal morphological disturbances such as [[non-axisymmetric-pattern|bars]] or counter-rotating bulges [van de Sande et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835..104V/abstract).

**Proper Motions and the Velocity Ellipsoid**
While radial velocities constrain the LOSVD, proper motions provide the transverse kinematic components necessary for full [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]] mapping. Within the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]], astrometric missions like Gaia and [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Hubble Space Telescope]] observational programs have enabled precision proper motion measurements, extending kinematic mapping to the Galactic bulge and surrounding [[stellar-streams-and-moving-groups|satellite streams]] [Kuijken et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..317..310K/abstract), [Smith et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..420..113S/abstract). Historically, compiling robust proper motion catalogs was challenging, and severe unresolved discrepancies existed between early fundamental reference frames, such as the tension between the Lick and Russian proper motion programs [Vasilevskis et al. (1973)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1973VA.....15..145V/abstract). Modern massive catalogs, such as those derived from the SkyMapper Southern Survey using the SPar algorithm, provide simultaneous stellar parameters, [[transverse-kinematics-and-distances|distances]], and extinctions for over 141 million stars in the [[galactic-disk-structure-and-dynamics|Galactic disk]], further expanding the dataset for large-scale kinematic analysis [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250323067S/abstract).

Combining radial and transverse velocities allows astronomers to compute the velocity ellipsoid, which characterizes the 3D velocity dispersion tensor of a stellar population in the context of [[stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|Stellar and Galactic Dynamics]]. In the Milky Way, the velocity ellipsoid of [[stellar-substructure|halo stars]] is found to be aligned with a spherical coordinate system and is spatially invariant over large volumes. Conversely, disk stars exhibit a velocity ellipsoid where all three dispersion components increase with vertical distance from the midplane. High-precision astrometric surveys have revealed that the velocity distribution of local [[galactic-stellar-populations|K and M dwarfs]] is highly complex and multi-modal, demonstrating that the standard assumption of a smooth, Gaussian Schwarzschild ellipsoid breaks down in the presence of local substructure [Bond et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...716....1B/abstract).

## Historical Development and Technical Evolution

The study of [[galaxy-kinematics|Galaxy kinematics]] began at the turn of the twentieth century, catalyzed by the first successful optical spectrum of [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|M31]] obtained by Scheiner in 1898 [Rubin et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...451..419R/abstract). For much of the following century, dynamical data relied heavily on long-slit spectroscopy. While long-slit spectroscopy was instrumental in discovering extended [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] in disk galaxies, its application to [[ellipsoidal-galaxies|Ellipsoidal galaxies]] in the 1970s fundamentally altered galactic dynamics. Spectroscopic observations demonstrated that [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|massive ellipticals]] possess ordered rotation velocities that fall well below the theoretical prediction for rotationally flattened, isotropic oblate spheroids. This proved that early-type galaxies are primarily supported by [[galaxy-velocity-anisotropy-profiles|anisotropic velocity dispersions]] rather than ordered rotation [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). On larger scales, technical advances also led to the discovery of bulk large-scale streaming motions of galaxies, revealing that the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Group]] is moving toward massive mass concentrations such as the Great Attractor [Dressler et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987SciAm.257c..46D/abstract).

However, one-dimensional slit measurements struggled to capture complex, multi-component dynamics. The transition to two-dimensional [[spatially-resolved-galaxy-analysis|Spatially Resolved Galaxy Analysis]] was driven by the development of the Integral field unit (IFU). By providing simultaneous spectral and spatial coverage, IFUs enabled the construction of complete 2D kinematic maps. Large-scale IFU campaigns, such as the [[atlas3d-survey|Atlas3D survey]] and the SAMI Galaxy Survey, redefined the morphological classification of early-type galaxies by differentiating them into fast and slow rotators based on their [[galaxy-stellar-angular-momentum|stellar angular momentum]] [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract). Crucially, IFUs allow the extraction of the line-of-sight velocity distribution (LOSVD) beyond simple Gaussian profiles. By quantifying the asymmetric deviation ($h_3$) and the [[fourth-order-gauss-hermite-moment|Fourth-order Gauss-Hermite moment]] ($h_4$), researchers can break the mass-anisotropy degeneracy inherent in collisionless systems, yielding robust dynamical mass profiles and distinguishing rotationally supported disks from gas-poor merger remnants [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract), [van de Sande et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835..104V/abstract). 

Further methodological advances have pushed kinematic measurements into the faint outskirts of galaxies. Novel multi-slit mask designs, such as the SuperSKiMS technique, permit the extraction of stellar kinematics at large radii where the surface brightness is orders of magnitude fainter than the sky [Pastorello et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PASA...33...35P/abstract). Similarly, modern spaxel cross-correlation algorithms can recover reliable line-of-sight velocities at signal-to-noise ratios as low as $\sim 1$, mitigating the historical limitations of template-fitting approaches [Bag et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.2278B/abstract). Furthermore, high-throughput spectrographs on large telescopes like the Southern African Large Telescope (SALT) are uniquely suited to measuring stellar velocity ellipsoids at gigaparsec distances, enabling astronomers to probe lopsided halos and extended dark matter distributions via [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Galaxy Surveys and Catalogs]].

In parallel to extragalactic spectroscopy, the measurement of proper motions within the Milky Way has undergone a massive technical evolution. Early mid-century proper motion catalogs were restricted by small sample sizes, precessional errors, and local dust obscuration. Ground-based initiatives like the [[lick-astrographic-survey|Lick astrographic survey]] and contemporaneous Russian programs attempted to establish a fundamental kinematic reference frame using background galaxies, though these pioneering efforts suffered from unresolved systematic disagreements regarding the absolute proper motions of stars [Vasilevskis et al. (1973)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1973VA.....15..145V/abstract). 

The landscape of Galactic kinematics was subsequently revolutionized by space-based astrometry. Following the baseline established by Hipparcos, earlier wide-field ground-based surveys like [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|SDSS]] and [[rave-survey|RAVE]] had already begun dismantling the classical view of a smooth, equilibrium Milky Way, revealing a highly dynamic system dominated by [[stellar-substructure|Stellar substructure]], tidal streams, and ongoing accretion events. The modern Gaia mission was anticipated to vastly expand upon this perspective by delivering extreme-precision 6D phase space coordinates for over a billion stars [Ivezić et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..251I/abstract), [Smith et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..420..113S/abstract).

## Kinematics of Disk Systems and Rotation Curves

The kinematics of [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] are primarily characterized by the ordered rotation of stars and cold gas within the disk plane. This bulk motion is quantified by the [[rotation-curves|rotation curve]], $V_{\rm rot}(R)$, which traces the azimuthal velocity as a function of [[galactocentric-distance|galactocentric radius]] [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Observationally, rotation curves are derived from the Doppler shifts of emission lines, utilizing H$\alpha$ from HII regions or CO transitions in the inner disk, and 21-cm HI emission to trace the kinematics out to large radii where the stellar surface brightness fades [Knapen et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528..219K/abstract), [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). In the simplest axisymmetric approximation, the circular velocity balances the local gravitational potential $\Phi$, such that $V_{\rm rot}^2(R) = R \frac{d\Phi}{dR} \approx \frac{G M(<R)}{R}$.

In the massive local universe, [[rotation-curves|Rotation curves]] typically exhibit a steep central rise followed by an extended, nearly constant velocity plateau [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Because a constant asymptotic circular velocity requires the enclosed dynamical mass to scale linearly with radius ($M(<R) \propto R$), these [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] provide the foundational dynamical evidence that [[spiral-galaxy-dynamics|spiral galaxies]] are embedded in extended, unseen dark matter halos [van der Kruit et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990mwg..conf..185V/abstract), [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract), [Sofue et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013pss5.book..985S/abstract). In many systems, asymptotic mass estimates exceed the luminous stellar mass by factors of four or more in the outer disk [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). However, the universality of this dark matter dominance is an area of active debate at [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|high redshift]]. Recent integral field spectroscopy surveys of star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 1.5-2$ (e.g., the KURVS survey) suggest that the dark matter fraction within the optical radius may be significantly lower than in local equivalents [Dudzeviciute et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021essp.confE..47D/abstract), even though many high-$z$ systems already exhibit the ordered rotation typical of settled [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] [Shapiro et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...682..231S/abstract).

While circular orbits dominate the kinetic energy of a disk, high-resolution 2D velocity fields consistently reveal significant [[non-circular-motions|Non-circular motions]] driven by internal instabilities and environmental factors [van der Kruit et al. (1978)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1978ARA&A..16..103V/abstract), [Knapen et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528..219K/abstract). In [[non-axisymmetric-pattern|barred galaxies]], large-scale kinematic surveys such as SDSS-IV/MaNGA demonstrate elevated radial and tangential velocity modes along the bar axis [DiGiorgio Zanger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240711908D/abstract). Stellar kinematics are also now being used to estimate the ages of galactic bars, providing a critical timescale for the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]] [Gadotti et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006fdg..conf..527G/abstract). In ringed nonbarred spirals like NGC 7531, the intermediate zones between the bright inner ring and outer arms can exhibit intrinsically oval kinematics [Buta et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJS...64....1B/abstract). Similarly, gas streaming motions across [[material-spiral-arms|spiral arms]] provide direct kinematic signatures of density wave shocks [Shetty et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...665.1138S/abstract). In grand-design spirals like M51, CO and H$\alpha$ velocity maps show large radial and tangential [[distorted-flows|streaming velocities]]; however, varying mass fluxes and potential out-of-plane motions in the inner $\sim 800$ pc suggest that these spiral arms are not strictly in a quasi-steady state [Shetty et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...665.1138S/abstract). In our own Galaxy, [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|Gaia DR3]] has enabled the precise 3D kinematic mapping of young stellar object (YSO) and molecular cloud complexes, demonstrating that the molecular interstellar medium generally follows Galactic rotation with only slight [[peculiar-velocity-field|peculiar velocities]] [Zhou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250918496Z/abstract).

Furthermore, tidal interactions with satellite galaxies can strongly perturb disk kinematics. In M51, passages of the satellite NGC 5195 through the disk plane have stimulated dominant spiral modes and induced distinct kinematic "kinks"—sharp radial velocity reversals—along the arms [Font et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966..110F/abstract). Measuring these non-axisymmetric deviations, often via harmonic decomposition or the [[tilted-ring-method|Tilted-ring method]], remains critical for accurately estimating the underlying [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] and understanding the secular evolution of spiral systems [Knapen et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528..219K/abstract), [DiGiorgio Zanger et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240711908D/abstract).

## Early-Type Galaxies: Fast and Slow Rotators

Historically classified by their position on the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]], early-type galaxies (ETGs)—comprising ellipticals and lenticulars—are now more accurately understood through a kinematic dichotomy. Driven by the advent of integral field spectroscopy (IFS) in campaigns like the [[atlas3d-survey|Atlas3D survey]] and the SAMI Galaxy Survey, ETGs are broadly separated into rotationally supported "fast rotators" and pressure-supported slow rotators [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract). This kinematic framework captures the physical assembly histories of galaxies far better than morphological [[isophotal-analysis|Isophotal analysis]] alone. 

Slow rotators are typically massive systems that dominate the ETG population above a critical mass threshold [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract). They exhibit weakly [[galaxy-shapes|triaxial structures]] and [[stellar-isophotes|boxy isophotes]], lacking significant ordered rotation [Bender et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987MitAG..70..226B/abstract). Kinematically, they are characterized by a low ratio of maximum line-of-sight streaming velocity to velocity dispersion, $V/\sigma$, which falls well below the theoretical expectation for an isotropic oblate rotator: $V/\sigma \ll \sqrt{\epsilon/(1-\epsilon)}$, where $\epsilon$ is the [[ellipticity-function|ellipticity]] [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). These massive, slowly rotating ETGs are thought to assemble primarily through gas-poor (dry) major mergers at the centers of massive dark matter halos [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract). Historically, it has been established that the dynamical properties and rotation of elliptical galaxies depend significantly on their luminosity; in particular, the [[bright-galaxies|brightest cluster galaxies]] often show virtually no ordered rotation, completely relying on velocity dispersion for support [Davies et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...266..516D/abstract). Observations of [[stellar-tracers|ionized gas]] in early-type galaxies reveal that half exhibit irregular gaseous velocity profiles, and many show [[stellar-gas-misalignments|gas/star counter-rotation]] or [[morphological-inner-structures|kinematically decoupled cores]] [Caon et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJS..127...39C/abstract). Studies of dust-lane ellipticals complement this, revealing instances where the rotation axes of the gas and stars are orthogonal to one another, indicating complex dynamical decoupling within triaxial potentials [Sharples et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983MNRAS.202...37S/abstract).

Fast rotators make up the vast majority of lower-mass ETGs. They are characterized by disky isophotes and internal dynamics that parallel those of disk galaxies, indicating the presence of an embedded [[stellar-component|Stellar component]] configured as a rapidly rotating disk [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract). While early photometric models assumed such galaxies were isotropic oblate rotators, modern IFS data reveal that low-luminosity fast rotators possess significant [[galaxy-velocity-anisotropy-profiles|velocity anisotropy]] $\delta$, often reaching values up to $0.5$ [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Their evolutionary tracks are dominated by gas accretion, bulge growth, and the subsequent quenching of star formation, rather than by dry mergers [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract).

To accurately map the internal dynamics of these systems and break the mass-anisotropy degeneracy, dynamical models rely heavily on high-order Gauss-Hermite moments, particularly the third ($h_3$, measuring skewness) and the [[fourth-order-gauss-hermite-moment|Fourth-order Gauss-Hermite moment]] ($h_4$, measuring kurtosis) of the line-of-sight velocity distribution [Gerhard et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..136..248G/abstract). Fast rotators generally exhibit a strong anti-correlation between $h_3$ and $V/\sigma$, which is a hallmark signature of embedded stellar disks. However, there is ongoing tension in the literature regarding a subset of galaxies designated as "Class 5" fast rotators. These systems display a weak $h_3-V/\sigma$ anti-correlation. Cosmological simulations predict these should be disk-less galaxies formed via gas-poor mergers, yet morphological examinations often reveal large stellar disks, counter-rotating bulges, or bars in edge-on systems, leaving their exact physical origin ambiguous and heavily debated [van de Sande et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835..104V/abstract). To probe the outer extents of these galaxies, [[globular-clusters|globular cluster systems]] provide a critical alternative kinematic tracer for the rotation and mass profiles of elliptical galaxy halos beyond the extent of the integrated stellar light [Romanowsky et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..9251R/abstract).

The fast and slow rotator dichotomy is intimately linked with [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] and [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]]. Across the fast rotator sequence, parameters such as age, metallicity, $\alpha$-element enhancement, and the weight of the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] all increase smoothly with the central mass density slope and bulge mass fraction [Cappellari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54..597C/abstract). Furthermore, a tight three-dimensional scaling relation links the [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass]] $M_*$, the structural shape parameterized by the [[sersic-profile|Sersic profile index]] $n$, and the mass-to-light ratio $M_*/L$. This tight plane confirms that the kinematic state and shape of an ETG are fundamentally inseparable from its stellar population history [D'Onofrio et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...727L...6D/abstract).

## The Milky Way: A Template for Stellar Kinematics

The Milky Way offers an unparalleled "internal perspective" for dissecting [[stellar-tracers|Stellar tracers]] on a star-by-star basis, serving as a primary template for galaxy dynamics [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract). However, mapping its global kinematics is inherently complicated by severe dust obscuration and the geometric challenges of determining precise distances and proper motions from our vantage point within the disk [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Beyond the immediate Milky Way disk, detailed star-by-star CMD synthesis and spectroscopy in [[galactic-satellite-galaxies|Local Group dwarf galaxies]] provide precise [[stellar-population-synthesis|star-formation histories]] and kinematic maps that reveal spatial and temporal variations in stellar populations [Tolstoy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ARA&A..47..371T/abstract).

In the Solar neighborhood, the local kinematics of the disk are classically parameterized by the Oort constants in the context of the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]]. Historically, analyses of local stellar kinematics found no evidence for significant non-luminous mass in the Solar neighborhood, effectively constraining models of dissipative dark matter [Gilmore et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ARA&A..27..555G/abstract). Recent measurements using M-type stars constrain the Oort constants, yielding a local [[rotation-velocity|Rotation velocity]] of roughly $V_0 \approx 257$ km s$^{-1}$ [Elsanhoury et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AN....342..989E/abstract). To synthesize these diverse datasets, modern tools like the *galkin* compilation integrate decades of kinematic measurements from stars, gas, and masers to optimize the Milky Way's rotation curve between 3 and 20 kpc [Pato et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017SoftX...6...54P/abstract).

Beyond the immediate Solar neighborhood, large-scale spectroscopic campaigns like the [[rave-survey|RAVE survey]] and [[galaxy-surveys-and-catalogs|SDSS]] have mapped the vertical stratification of the [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] and [[thick-disks|Thick disks]]. The literature reflects an active debate regarding the exact nature of this transition. While some analyses of proper motions and photometry argue that the thin and thick disks represent kinematically continuous components with smooth velocity dispersion gradients [Karaali et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PASA...31...13K/abstract), high-precision chemical and kinematic data support a more discrete architecture. Specifically, studies identify overlapping populations within the thick disk, notably a metal-weak thick disk (MWTD). The MWTD exhibits a rotational velocity of $V_{\phi} \approx 150$ km s$^{-1}$, which represents a significant rotational lag of $\sim 30$ km s$^{-1}$ relative to the canonical thick disk, alongside a velocity dispersion of $\sigma \approx 60$ km s$^{-1}$ [Carollo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887...22C/abstract). This distinct kinematic signature suggests an origin either from an early dwarf galaxy merger or a dynamically heated precursor disk. Further spectroscopic studies of the thick disk-halo interface place the last significant accretion event leading to the thick disk formation at approximately 10000 Myr ago [Gilmore et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..228..225G/abstract).

Moving outward, the stellar halo is characterized by a velocity ellipsoid that aligns with a spherical coordinate system and appears spatially invariant over large volumes [Bond et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...716....1B/abstract). To isolate true halo kinematics, metal-poor stellar populations located more than 1 kpc from the [[internal-galactic-structure|Galactic plane]] provide a clearer picture of the halo's kinematic transition from the thick disk [Beers et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJS...96..175B/abstract). Horizontal Branch stars serve as robust [[stellar-tracers|Stellar tracers]] of the Galactic stellar halo, providing measurements of the [[stellar-density-profile|halo density profile]] and kinematics across several regions of the Galaxy [Layden et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..136...14L/abstract). Recent analyses of high-precision stellar halo and dwarf galaxy satellite kinematics suggest the Milky Way is a compact, thermalized system with Keplerian signatures that help quantify local [[galactic-escape-velocity|escape velocity profiles]] and [[radial-acceleration-relation|Mass Discrepancy-Acceleration relations]] [La Fortune et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200810445L/abstract). 

However, the assumption of a smooth, well-mixed halo has been overturned. The kinematics of metal-poor stars are dominated by profound [[stellar-substructure|Stellar substructure]], confirming that the Galactic halo was largely assembled via hierarchical accretion [Ivezić et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..251I/abstract). Furthermore, the literature is currently conflicted on whether the overarching [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]] is spherical or triaxial; kinematic groups or "fossils" in the halo may require a triaxial dark matter halo to remain stable, challenging standard spherical models [Rojas-Niño et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757L..28R/abstract), [Rojas-Niño et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...805...29R/abstract). Extending this template to our nearest neighbor, the SPLASH survey of [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|M31]]'s stellar halo reveals a significant population of heated disk stars in its inner halo and a continuous metallicity gradient extending out to 100 kpc, demonstrating that large-scale kinematic features are ubiquitous in local spiral halos [Gilbert et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUS..317..134G/abstract).

In the inner Galaxy, the Galactic Bulge presents a highly complex, non-axisymmetric kinematic environment. To isolate these structural components, population synthesis models, combined with large surveys like Gaia and RAVE, allow for the robust kinematic separation of bulge stars from overlapping foreground and background populations [Reylé et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASA...21..138R/abstract). Spectroscopic studies demonstrate a strong correlation between kinematics and [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|metallicity]]: metal-poor stars exhibit high velocity dispersions and low rotation, identifying them as an overlapping [[morphological-inner-structures|inner-halo population]], whereas metal-rich stars are strongly rotationally supported [Minniti et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994PASP..106..813M/abstract). Deeper within the central $\sim 200$ pc lies the [[morphological-inner-structures|nuclear stellar disk]]. Kinematic measurements of classical Cepheids in this region reveal a complex picture: while some trace the nuclear disk's rapid rotation curve, indicating they formed *in situ* deeply embedded within the Galactic center [Matsunaga et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799...46M/abstract), others have been observed moving significantly slower than Galactic rotation, complicating the kinematic interpretation [Tanioka et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...842..104T/abstract).

## Kinematic Substructures and Dynamical Assembly

The standard cosmological framework of galaxy assembly is dominated by hierarchical merging, a [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|process]] that continues to shape the [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]] to the present day [Schweizer et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998giis.conf..105S/abstract), [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Rather than forming smooth, monolithic structures, galactic halos and disks retain the "fossil record" of these accretion events. Because collisionless stellar systems conserve [[phase-space-density|Phase space density]], the remnants of [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]] remain coherent in velocity space long after they have spatially dispersed. These kinematic substructures—predominantly observed as [[stellar-streams-and-moving-groups|Stellar Streams and Moving Groups]]—provide critical constraints on both the merging history and the [[galaxy-cluster-mass-profiles|dark matter distribution]] of the host galaxy [Ivezić et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..251I/abstract), [Smith et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..420..113S/abstract). Beyond individual stellar streams, systematic retrograde motions observed in specific groups of [[globular-clusters|globular clusters]] suggest that the Galactic halo may have formed from the coalescence of several distinct progenitor galaxies [Rodgers et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApJ...283L...5R/abstract).

Advances from large-area sky surveys (e.g., SDSS, RAVE, and Gaia) have fundamentally shifted our understanding of the Milky Way from a smooth-component model to a highly substructured, non-equilibrium system [Ivezić et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..251I/abstract). In the Milky Way halo, the Sagittarius dwarf tidal stream and the Monoceros overdensity present distinct kinematic signatures. The Monoceros structure exhibits azimuthal velocities in the range of $200 < v_\phi < 250$ km s$^{-1}$, though its chemistry suggests it may be a perturbed in situ thin disk population rather than an accreted remnant [Borbolato et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960...52B/abstract). Deeper in the Galaxy, [[thick-disks|Thick disks]] exhibit internal kinematic substructure; observations indicate a "Metal-Weak Thick Disk" (MWTD) population with a rotational velocity of $\sim 150$ km s$^{-1}$, lagging the canonical thick disk by $\sim 30$ km s$^{-1}$. With a velocity dispersion of $60$ km s$^{-1}$, this MWTD likely originated from the merger of a dwarf galaxy during the early assembly of the Milky Way [Carollo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887...22C/abstract).

The process of tidal disruption is actively observed in surviving [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]]. Measurements of the diffuse Milky Way satellites Antlia 2 and Crater 2 reveal pronounced velocity gradients across their extent, consistent with particle stream simulations of active disruption [Ji et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...921...32J/abstract). However, Antlia 2 presents a notable tension: its updated metallicity places it accurately on the standard [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|luminosity-metallicity relation]], implying minimal historical mass loss. This creates a severe tension with dynamical models of its kinematics, which require it to have lost $>90\%$ of its initial stellar mass to tides [Ji et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...921...32J/abstract). 

In more extreme cases of tidal stripping, only the densest central regions of accreted satellites survive. The massive globular cluster Omega Centauri ($\omega$ Cen) is widely considered to be the stripped nucleus of a disrupted dwarf galaxy. High-precision 3D kinematics of $\omega$ Cen show isotropic velocity dispersions in its core that transition to [[strong-velocity-anisotropies|radial anisotropy]] at larger radii, alongside an elongation of the velocity dispersion field that mirrors its photometric flattening [Häberle et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983...95H/abstract).

Finally, the persistence of these kinematic fossils serves as a powerful diagnostic of the global dark matter distribution. Theoretical models indicate that distinct kinematic stellar groups may only be preserved over cosmological timescales if the host's dark matter halo is triaxial; in purely spherical or MOND-like potentials, these structures would thoroughly phase-mix and disappear [Rojas-Niño et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757L..28R/abstract), [Rojas-Niño et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...805...29R/abstract). Consequently, the identification and kinematic mapping of halo substructures remains a primary method for measuring the 3D shape and mass profile of dark matter halos.

## Nuclear Kinematics and Supermassive Black Holes

At the dynamical centers of many galaxies, high-resolution kinematic observations reveal steep gradients in rotation and central velocity dispersion. These dense environments are primarily probed to measure the masses of central supermassive black holes (SMBHs) and to study the assembly of [[galaxy-nuclei-and-complex-morphologies|nuclear star clusters]].

**Supermassive Black Holes**
The kinematic signature of a SMBH is a line-of-sight velocity dispersion profile, $\sigma_p(R)$, that rises more sharply toward the center than can be accounted for by the gravitational potential of the visible stellar mass. In the local Universe, central black holes appear nearly ubiquitous in spheroids, with masses constituting roughly $0.1\%$ of the host spheroid's stellar mass [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). However, robust [[mass-determination|Mass determination]] requires breaking the mass-anisotropy degeneracy, since a radially anisotropic velocity distribution can mimic the kinematic signature of a central point mass. This degeneracy is typically resolved by measuring the higher-order moments of the line-of-sight velocity distribution, such as $h_3$ and the [[fourth-order-gauss-hermite-moment|Fourth-order Gauss-Hermite moment]] ($h_4$) [Gerhard et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..136..248G/abstract).

Spatially resolved observations have yielded precise SMBH masses for several nearby systems. In the Milky Way, tracking [[stellar-dynamics-and-kinematics|individual stellar orbits]] implies a central mass of $\sim 3 \times 10^6 M_\odot$ [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Early extensive mapping of stellar kinematics in the central 10 pc confirmed that the dynamical center of the Milky Way is coincident with the IRS 16 complex within $\pm 10''$ [McGinn et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ApJ...338..824M/abstract). In NGC 4258, water masers tracing a gas disk at radii $<0.13$ pc exhibit perfect [[centrifugal-and-centripetal-dynamics|Keplerian motion]], demonstrating a central mass of $\sim 3.6 \times 10^7 M_\odot$ [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). In massive elliptical galaxies like M87, stellar kinematics point to much larger SMBHs of $\sim 3 \times 10^9 M_\odot$ [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Despite these successes, the universality of massive "dead quasars" in all [[bright-galaxies|luminous galaxies]] remains actively debated. For instance, dynamical models assuming a constant stellar mass-to-light ratio *without* a massive SMBH can adequately reproduce the stellar kinematics of some nearby massive spirals, such as NGC 7331 [Bower et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...402...76B/abstract).

**Nuclear Gas Kinematics**
Along with stellar tracers, [[nuclear-gas-kinematics|Nuclear gas kinematics]] provide complementary constraints on the central potential. Small disks of ionized gas offer direct measures of enclosed mass via their [[rotation-curves|Rotation curves]]. However, because gas is collisional and highly responsive to non-gravitational forces, these kinematic maps must be interpreted with caution. Gas kinematics are also sensitive to secondary infall events, which can result in complex dynamic phenomena such as counter-rotating components. In the disk galaxy NGC 7217, for instance, approximately $30\%$ of the central stars circulate in the opposite direction to the main body, pointing to substantial past accretion of kinematically misaligned gas that subsequently formed stars [Kuijken et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PASP..105.1016K/abstract). In the nuclei of spiral galaxies, gas kinematics are also tied to star formation bursts, which may be driven inward by bar structures and accompanied by variations in the local [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] [Oconnell et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983HiA.....6..147O/abstract). Extreme nuclear gas kinematics can also trace relativistic motions; for example, the broad, asymmetric fluorescent Fe K line in the active galaxy MCG-6-30-15 implies gas velocities of $\sim 10^5 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ originating just a few Schwarzschild radii from the SMBH [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

**Nuclear Star Clusters**
Nuclear star clusters (NSCs) are massive, compact stellar systems residing at galactic centers. Their internal kinematics strongly correlate with the properties of their host galaxies, indicating coupled formation histories. Notably, there is a near equality between the root-mean-square (rms) velocity dispersion of an NSC and that of its host galaxy, suggesting that their energy per unit mass is fundamentally linked [Leigh et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451..859L/abstract). Furthermore, [[galaxy-nuclei-and-complex-morphologies|NSC kinematics]] vary systematically across the [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]]: late-type galaxies typically host rotation-dominated NSCs, likely formed via gas channeled to the center during the [[secular-evolution-of-galaxies|Secular evolution of galaxies]], whereas early-type galaxies generally feature slowly rotating, pressure-supported NSCs . To synthesize these overlapping central phenomena, there are ongoing observational efforts dedicated to systematically correlating the masses of both nuclear star clusters and supermassive black holes with the global kinematic features of their host galaxies [Cherepashchuk et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARep...54..578C/abstract).

## Kinematic Evolution Across Cosmic Time

The kinematic properties of galaxies exhibit profound transformations over cosmic time, reflecting the transition from an epoch dominated by rapid gas accretion and frequent major mergers to the secularly evolving, relaxed systems observed in the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]]. Kinematic surveys of distant galaxies directly constrain [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] by tracing the assembly of stellar and gaseous mass.

At $z \sim 2$, during the peak of the [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|cosmic star formation history]], [[spatially-resolved-galaxy-analysis|integral field spectroscopy]] (IFS) has been instrumental in mapping the internal dynamics of massive [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]]. Observations from the SINS survey revealed a [[irregular-internal-dynamics|highly turbulent interstellar medium]] with elevated velocity dispersions [Förster Schreiber et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Msngr.145...39F/abstract). Despite this turbulence and high star formation rate, [[galaxy-kinematics|kinemetry analyses]] show that $>50\%$ of these massive systems exhibit velocity fields consistent with ordered, rotating disks, while the remainder are dominated by the chaotic kinematics of major mergers [Shapiro et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...682..231S/abstract). The prevalence of rotationally supported, albeit turbulent, disks at $z \sim 2$ suggests that rapid, smooth accretion of cold gas played a major role in early galaxy assembly, contrary to models relying exclusively on hierarchical merging. Indeed, comprehensive studies find that both hierarchical mergers and steady gas accretion are roughly equally important in the assembly of massive galaxies between $z=1-3$ [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract).

A significant area of active debate is the relative contribution of dark matter to the [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] at high redshift. In the local universe, [[flat-rotation-curves|Flat rotation curves]] at large radii are the cornerstone of the dark matter paradigm. However, recent ultra-deep IFS campaigns, such as the KURVS survey measuring H$\alpha$ rotation curves out to $\sim 15$ kpc at $z \sim 1.5$, indicate that the dark matter fraction within the optical radius of these early galaxies may be significantly lower than in local disks [Dudzeviciute et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021essp.confE..47D/abstract). If confirmed, this tension implies that high-redshift galactic disks are strongly baryon-dominated in their inner regions, and that the dark matter fraction within the disk radius evolves significantly as galaxies grow.

By intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 0.6$), the [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]] of kinematics traces the gradual settling of the [[stellar-component|Stellar component]]. The evolution of the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]] at $z > 0.5$ is heavily driven by a population of physically small, exceptionally luminous late-type galaxies. These blue galaxies possess internal velocity widths comparable to those of typical local irregulars, despite being roughly two magnitudes brighter [Mallen-Ornelas et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph..4187M/abstract). Over time, the turbulent kinematics of these intermediate systems dissipate, allowing them to settle into the dynamically cold [[thin-disks|Thin disks]] ubiquitous in the modern universe. Environmental effects also drive kinematic evolution; for example, in cluster environments like the Virgo cluster, ionized gas velocity fields are often significantly more disturbed than the corresponding stellar kinematics, a discrepancy that reflects simultaneous [[galaxy-cluster-dynamical-state|gravitational interactions]] and ICM-ISM ram-pressure stripping [Cortés et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006RMxAC..26Q.189C/abstract). Furthermore, analyses of cluster populations demonstrate that spirals exhibit multimodal velocity distributions and higher velocity dispersions compared to the more relaxed populations [Scodeggio et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...444...41S/abstract). Additionally, kinematic studies of metal-enriched gas at large galactocentric distances around intermediate-redshift galaxies provide complementary clues to the assembly history of galactic halos [Churchill et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..240...31C/abstract).

In contrast to the late-time settling of disk galaxies, the kinematics of early-type galaxies point to a much earlier stabilization. Measurements of central velocity dispersions in cluster ellipticals confirm that [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]] like the Faber-Jackson relation were already well-defined by $z = 0.18$ [Franx et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PASP..105.1058F/abstract). Furthermore, studies of the Fundamental Plane out to $z \sim 1$ show a slope and scatter nearly identical to those at $z = 0$. The primary difference across this redshift baseline is a systematic zero-point shift governed by the passive evolution of the mass-to-light ratio in the context of [[galaxy-scaling-relations|Galaxy Scaling Relations]]. This kinematic stability implies that massive ellipticals assembled the bulk of their stellar mass and reached virial equilibrium at $z > 2$, evolving passively thereafter.

## Dynamical Modeling and Potential Reconstruction

To reconstruct the [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]] and accurately measure the [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] of galaxies, astrophysicists rely heavily on kinematic observables. A fundamental limitation in modeling collisionless stellar systems, such as elliptical galaxies and the bulges of spiral galaxies, is the mass-anisotropy degeneracy [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Because observations only provide the projected, two-dimensional positions and line-of-sight velocities of [[stellar-tracers|Stellar tracers]], different combinations of mass profiles and orbital structures can produce identical velocity dispersion profiles.

For instance, an observed line-of-sight velocity dispersion profile that remains constant or rises at large radii could indicate that the galaxy is embedded in a massive dark matter halo, but it could equally result from a stellar velocity distribution that becomes increasingly radially or tangentially anisotropic in the outer regions [Gerhard et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..136..248G/abstract). Similarly, a sharp central peak in $\sigma_p(R)$ might signal the presence of a supermassive black hole, or it could simply reflect strong radial anisotropy near the core [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). 

To break the mass-anisotropy degeneracy, models must incorporate the full shape of the line-of-sight velocity distribution (LOSVD) rather than just its second moment. This is achieved by measuring higher-order terms like the skewness ($h_3$) and the [[fourth-order-gauss-hermite-moment|Fourth-order Gauss-Hermite moment]] ($h_4$). Modern integral field spectroscopy maps these high-order moments in 2D; for example, a strong anti-correlation between $h_3$ and the $V/\sigma$ ratio is a robust indicator of an embedded, rotationally supported stellar disk [van de Sande et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835..104V/abstract).

Mathematically, the degeneracy is resolved using sophisticated orbit superposition techniques, most notably Schwarzschild modeling in the context of [[stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|Stellar and Galactic Dynamics]]. In this approach, a parameterized gravitational potential is assumed, and the equations of motion are integrated for thousands of representative orbits. The orbits are then assigned statistical weights to simultaneously reproduce the observed spatial surface brightness, rotation velocity, and higher-order velocity moments [Bower et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...402...76B/abstract), [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

Applying these dynamical models has yielded profound insights into galaxy assembly, though the literature highlights several ongoing tensions:
*   **Dark Matter Halos:** In disk galaxies, spatially extended [[rotation-curves|Rotation curves]] provide unambiguous evidence for dark matter [Sofue et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013pss5.book..985S/abstract). In early-type galaxies, however, the kinematic extraction of halo masses reveals a dichotomy: while bright, boxy ellipticals exhibit strong kinematic signatures of dark matter—with recent dynamical analyses showing nearly flat [[halo-circular-velocity-curve|circular velocity curves]] for luminous elliptical galaxies extending out to approximately 2 effective radii [Gerhard et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003mglh.conf...62G/abstract)—faint, disky ellipticals often do not, posing a challenge to standard structure formation models [Romanowsky et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006EAS....20..119R/abstract). 
*   **Halo Shape:** Kinematic modeling is also used to constrain the triaxiality of dark matter halos. Theoretical models predict that triaxial potentials can support stable kinematic groups or "fossils" from early accretion events, structures that cannot persist in purely spherical halos [Rojas-Niño et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757L..28R/abstract), [Rojas-Niño et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...805...29R/abstract).
*   **Cosmological Evolution:** There is an active debate regarding the dominance of dark matter at high redshift. While local galaxies are heavily dark matter-dominated in their outskirts, recent high-redshift surveys find that the dark matter contribution within the optical radius may be significantly lower than in comparable modern-day galaxies [Dudzeviciute et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021essp.confE..47D/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite decades of progress in mapping the phase space of the Milky Way and nearby systems, fundamental gaps remain in our understanding of galactic dynamics. Many clues about the [[galaxy-assembly-process-lurk|galaxy assembly process]] lurk in the faint outer regions of galaxies, where [[intrinsic-galaxy-and-stellar-properties|low surface brightness]] presents a formidable observational challenge [Ferguson et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASSP....3..239F/abstract). Probing the kinematics of these extended regions is essential for reconstructing hierarchical accretion histories, yet current data often yield conflicting interpretations. In the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]], for example, the diffuse Milky Way satellite Antlia 2 exhibits an updated metallicity consistent with minimal historical mass loss, yet kinematic models suggest it must have lost over $90\%$ of its mass to tidal disruption . Additionally, galaxy halos can be probed via absorption, revealing that halo gas kinematics are often uncorrelated with orientation but strongly linked to morphological asymmetry, further complicating dynamical models of halo assembly [Churchill et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..331..387C/abstract).

A major unresolved issue is the exact 3D shape and extent of the dark matter distribution in galactic halos. While standard cosmology predicts non-spherical halos, definitively detecting the triaxiality of dark halos remains difficult. Theoretical models demonstrate that the hierarchical assembly of the Milky Way should leave long-lasting stellar kinematic fossils if the underlying halo potential is triaxial [Rojas-Niño et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757L..28R/abstract). Because these stable kinematic groups are predicted to exist only in triaxial potentials, their detection would provide robust confirmation of cosmological dark matter models [Rojas-Niño et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...805...29R/abstract). Furthermore, the universality of dark matter dominance is increasingly being questioned. Kinematic observations indicate that faint, disky ellipticals often lack the strong dark matter signatures seen in bright, boxy ellipticals, posing a direct challenge to standard predictions [Romanowsky et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006EAS....20..119R/abstract). Additionally, high-redshift integral field spectroscopy from surveys like KURVS suggests that the dark matter fractions within the optical radii of star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 1.5-2$ may be significantly lower than in their local counterparts [Dudzeviciute et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021essp.confE..47D/abstract).

Within the inner regions of galaxies, the classification of [[fast-rotators|Fast rotators]] also presents unsolved puzzles. High-order Gauss-Hermite moments have revealed "Class 5" fast rotators that fail to display the characteristic anti-correlation between the third-order moment $h_3$ and $V/\sigma$ [van de Sande et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835..104V/abstract). It remains ambiguous whether these anomalous kinematics represent pristine disk-less galaxies formed via gas-poor mergers, or if they merely reflect complex internal structures such as counter-rotating bulges or edge-on bars.

Addressing these outstanding questions will depend heavily on the upcoming generation of telescopes via massive [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]]. Disentangling the kinematics, age, and metallicity of highly crowded or dust-obscured regions—such as the [[galaxy-nuclei-and-complex-morphologies|nuclear star clusters]] of the Milky Way—requires unprecedented spatial resolution and infrared sensitivity [Matsunaga et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181001022M/abstract). More broadly, the next generation of observatories will supply the photon-gathering power needed to push detailed, resolved [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] analyses well beyond the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]], enabling astronomers to measure intrinsic kinematic distribution functions across the entire [[hubble-sequence|Hubble sequence]] [Wyse et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006IAUS..232..140W/abstract). Looking ahead, massive spectroscopic campaigns will provide complete chrono-chemo-kinematical data across dwarf galaxies and streams, offering unprecedented resolving power to refine hierarchical formation models [Skúladóttir et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Msngr.190...19S/abstract). This capability will be pivotal in moving beyond the limitations of the [[integrated-light-method|Integrated light method]], ultimately tracking the chemo-kinematic evolution of galaxies on a star-by-star basis.
