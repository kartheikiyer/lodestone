In astrophysics, massive stars are fundamentally defined as those with initial masses exceeding approximately $8 M_\odot$. This mass threshold represents a critical physical boundary where a [[stellar-formation-and-protostars|protostar's formation]] timescale is longer than its Kelvin-Helmholtz timescale—the time required to radiate away its gravitational potential energy. Consequently, these objects undergo [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] [Krumholz et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.10412K/abstract) and ignite central [[core-hydrogen-burning|Core-hydrogen burning]] while still actively accreting material from their natal [[molecular-clouds-and-gas-clumping|molecular clouds]]. Driven by extreme core temperatures, their [[main-sequence-evolution|Main Sequence Evolution]] is characterized by radiative envelopes, electron-scattering opacities, and immense luminosities that frequently push them toward the Eddington limit, where outward radiation pressure balances inward gravity. Because their luminosity scales roughly as the cube of their mass, massive stars consume their nuclear fuel rapidly, restricting their lifespans to merely a few million to tens of millions of years.

Despite their rarity within the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], massive stars are the principal engines of galactic evolution and [[chemical-evolution-and-nucleosynthesis|Chemical Evolution and Nucleosynthesis]]. They are central to understanding the evolution of the interstellar medium, [[early-galaxy-formation|galaxy formation]], and the reionization of the universe [Kennicutt et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IAUS..227....3K/abstract). Born predominantly within dense [[young-massive-clusters|Young massive clusters]] [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract), their intense ultraviolet radiation and powerful [[mass-loss-processes|stellar winds]] dominate the energy budget of the interstellar medium, providing crucial feedback that regulates subsequent [[massive-star-formation|Massive Star Formation]]. As they evolve off the [[zero-age-main-sequence|Zero Age Main Sequence]], they traverse complex [[evolutionary-tracks|Evolutionary tracks]], transitioning into [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]], luminous blue variables, or stripped-envelope [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]]. Modern [[stellar-evolution-and-modeling|Stellar Evolution and Modeling]] has undergone a paradigm shift by recognizing that the vast majority of these stars reside in close multiple systems. Consequently, their evolutionary trajectories are profoundly altered by rotation, tidal interactions, and [[mass-transfer|Mass transfer]] within [[massive-binary-stars|Massive binary stars]] [Langer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..107L/abstract).

The terminal phases of massive stars are as violent as their brief lives. They serve as primary [[r-process-nucleosynthesis-sites|r-process nucleosynthesis sites]] and sources of [[alpha-element-nucleosynthesis|Alpha-Element Nucleosynthesis]], forging the heavy elements that enrich subsequent stellar generations. Most massive stars end their lives by undergoing [[stellar-core-collapse|Stellar core collapse]], triggering luminous [[core-collapse-supernovae|Core-Collapse Supernovae]] that leave behind [[compact-objects-and-binary-mergers|compact remnants]] such as [[neutron-star-physics|neutron stars]] or [[massive-compact-objects|black holes]]. However, significant open questions remain regarding the precise mapping between [[progenitor-properties|Progenitor properties]] and final explosion mechanisms. Current research highlights severe tensions between theoretical models and observations, notably the "weak-wind problem," which challenges established mass loss physics, and the "failed supernova" hypothesis. The latter suggests that a substantial fraction of stars above $\sim 18 M_\odot$ may bypass a bright explosion and collapse directly into black holes [Suzuki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...852..101S/abstract), [Tsujimoto et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.3475T/abstract), posing a major challenge to current models of galactic oxygen enrichment.

## Physical Foundations and the Massive Star Limit

The physical boundary that distinguishes massive stars—typically defined as those with initial masses $M \gtrsim 8 M_\odot$—is fundamentally rooted in the competing timescales of [[massive-star-formation|Massive Star Formation]] and thermal equilibrium. This transition is governed by the relationship between the protostellar [[accretion-processes|accretion]] timescale and the Kelvin-Helmholtz time, $t_{\rm KH}$, which is the time required for a star to radiate away its gravitational potential energy:

$$ t_{\rm KH} = \frac{G M_\star^2}{R L} $$

where $M_\star$, $R$, and $L$ are the [[intrinsic-galaxy-and-stellar-properties|stellar mass]], radius, and luminosity, respectively. 

For low-mass stars, the formation timescale is shorter than $t_{\rm KH}$, meaning they finish accreting before arriving at the [[zero-age-main-sequence|Zero Age Main Sequence]]. In contrast, for stars above $\sim 8 M_\odot$, the formation timescale exceeds $t_{\rm KH}$. Consequently, massive protostars contract and ignite [[core-hydrogen-burning|Core-hydrogen burning]] while still deeply embedded and actively accreting gas from their natal molecular cores.

Because nuclear fusion initiates during the accretion phase, the luminosity of a massive protostar is dominated by hydrogen burning rather than gravitational accretion. This generates intense radiative feedback, primarily through photoionization and radiation pressure exerted on the surrounding gas and dust. As the protostar grows in mass, its immense luminosity pushes the object toward the Eddington limit—the critical point at which outward radiation pressure equals the inward gravitational force. This profound radiation pressure plays a decisive role in regulating gas infall, ultimately shaping the upper limits of the [[initial-mass-function-imf|initial mass function]] and determining the dispersal of gas in nascent [[star-clusters-and-complexes|Star Clusters and Complexes]]. However, the broader physical framework is still fraught with "big problems," notably the lack of a comprehensive quantitative theory for how efficiently interstellar gas converts to stars, and the ongoing debate regarding the true environmental dependence of the initial mass function across diverse galactic contexts [Krumholz et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhR...539...49K/abstract).

During their [[main-sequence-evolution|Main Sequence Evolution]], the internal physics of massive stars diverge significantly from their lower-mass counterparts. Driven by extreme core temperatures, energy transport in massive star envelopes is largely radiative, and electron scattering acts as the dominant source of opacity. This fundamentally alters the luminosity-mass relation, flattening it from the steep $L \propto M^5$ scaling typical of near-solar mass stars to approximately $L \propto M^3$. Because luminosity dictates the rate of fuel consumption, massive stars possess severely truncated lifespans. For example, a $10 M_\odot$ star has a main-sequence lifetime of only $\sim 10^7$ years, whereas a $100 M_\odot$ star exhausts its core hydrogen in just a few million years.

The extreme luminosities of these objects continually push them against the Eddington limit. Detailed hydrodynamic models demonstrate that stars with initial masses $M \gtrsim 40 M_\odot$ frequently exceed the local Eddington limit in the partial ionization zones of hydrogen, helium, or iron within their envelopes [Sanyal et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...580A..20S/abstract). This localized violation of the Eddington limit triggers severe subphotospheric inflation, causing the stellar envelope's radius to expand by factors of up to 40. This inflation induces density inversions, convective instabilities, and a marked decrease in effective temperature [Sanyal et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...580A..20S/abstract). Ultimately, this radiation-driven structural inflation establishes a direct link between the internal limits of stellar stability and the powerful [[mass-loss-processes|Mass Loss Processes]] that dictate the star's evolutionary track and final fate.

## Formation Mechanisms and Protostellar Feedback

The birth of massive stars occurs deep within the dense, cold environments of [[molecular-clouds-and-gas-clumping|giant molecular clouds]] and, more specifically, Infrared Dark Clouds (IRDCs) [Tan et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..332..139T/abstract). Unlike their low-mass counterparts, massive stars are rarely born in isolation; they condense within complex, fragmented molecular clumps that typically form [[young-massive-clusters|Young massive clusters]]. Observations of [[star-forming-regions-and-cores|star-forming regions]] indicate that massive stars do not necessarily form at the exact centers or highest density peaks of their parent clouds, and their formation can be locally boosted in the vicinity of existing stellar clusters [Ochsendorf et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...43O/abstract).

The exact mechanism by which massive stars assemble their mass has historically centered on two competing models: the monolithic [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] of massive, gravitationally bound pre-stellar cores, and the competitive accretion of unbound gas within a common cluster potential. Theoretical and observational consensus increasingly favors the gravitational collapse scenario, as estimated competitive mass accretion rates in observed star-forming environments are insufficient to build up the requisite stellar masses [Krumholz et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.10412K/abstract). During the initial collapse of a massive core, the gas falls inward to form a rotationally supported [[stellar-accretion|accretion disk]]. The theoretical [[accretion-processes|mass accretion rate]] $\dot{M}$ in these environments can be approximated by $\dot{M} \sim 30 c_s^3 / G$, where $c_s$ is the isothermal sound speed, although internal turbulence and magnetic fields significantly modify the infall dynamics. 

A defining characteristic of massive star formation is the onset of intense protostellar feedback while the star is still undergoing stellar accretion. For stars with final masses $M \gtrsim 8 M_\odot$, the Kelvin-Helmholtz timescale, $t_{\rm KH} = G M_\star^2 / (R L)$, is shorter than the cloud's dynamical formation timescale. Consequently, the central protostar reaches the [[zero-age-main-sequence|Zero Age Main Sequence]] and ignites nuclear burning before it has finished accreting its envelope. This early ignition generates extreme radiative feedback in the form of ultraviolet photoionization and [[mass-loss-processes|radiation pressure]]. Once the luminosity approaches the Eddington limit, radiation pressure on the accreting dust and gas can become strong enough to evaporate the circumstellar disk and halt further mass assembly [Hosokawa et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...334.1250H/abstract). 

In addition to radiative effects, massive protostars inject substantial mechanical energy into their surroundings. High-resolution magnetohydrodynamic simulations demonstrate that the collapse of massive cores generates highly collimated magneto-centrifugal jets as well as slower, wide-angle magnetic-pressure-driven tower flows [Kölligan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...620A.182K/abstract). These outflows interact strongly with the infalling envelope, entraining large amounts of ambient material. The combination of jets and tower flows operates with an ejection-to-accretion efficiency of approximately $10\%$, efficiently transporting angular momentum outward and contributing to the dispersal of the natal cloud [Kölligan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...620A.182K/abstract). Ultimately, the competition between the inward mass accretion rate and the outward forces of radiative and mechanical feedback determines the final upper mass limit of the newly formed star. Recent observational advancements continue to refine our understanding of this feedback. The PDRs4All program utilizes JWST to study radiative feedback in regions like the Orion Bar, providing critical benchmarks for photo-dissociation region models [Berné et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASP..134e4301B/abstract). On larger scales, the Massive Star-forming Regions Omnibus X-ray Catalog (MOXC2) has identified hot plasmas threading massive star-forming regions, serving as a key indicator of the mechanical feedback generated by massive stellar winds and early [[core-collapse-supernovae|supernovae]] [Townsley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJS..235...43T/abstract). The MYStIX survey demonstrates that restricting searches to X-ray point sources helps identify [[ob-stars|OB populations]] obscured by high extinction, revealing that current catalogs for many star-forming regions are less than 50% complete [Povich et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838...61P/abstract). Furthermore, recent multi-wavelength studies in the Large Magellanic Cloud's N11 B star-forming region reveal a clear [[sequential-star-formation|Sequential star formation]] scenario driven by protostellar feedback. In this region, researchers have quantified the specific ionizing photon budgets ($Q_{\mathrm{H}}$, $Q_{\mathrm{HeI}}$, $Q_{\mathrm{HeII}}$) provided by the dominant [[o-type-stars|O-type population]], mapping how their immense ultraviolet radiation dictates the evolutionary progression of the surrounding cloud [Gómez-González et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241114149G/abstract).

## Evolutionary Tracks and Post-Main-Sequence Phases

Following [[main-sequence-evolution|Main Sequence Evolution]], massive stars traverse the [[evolutionary-tracks|Hertzsprung-Russell (H-R) diagram]] at a rapid pace. Because these stars maintain radiative equilibrium during their subsequent burning phases, their luminosities remain relatively constant, resulting in nearly horizontal evolutionary tracks across the H-R diagram. However, the precise evolutionary pathway is highly sensitive to the initial stellar mass, [[stellar-populations-by-metallicity|metallicity]], rotation rate, and whether the star resides in a binary system [Langer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..107L/abstract). 

For stars with initial masses $M \gtrsim 40 M_\odot$, the immense radiation pressure causes the stellar envelope to approach or exceed the local Eddington limit. The critical threshold where outward radiation pressure balances inward gravity is known as the Eddington limit. In the envelopes of massive stars, this limit is preferentially exceeded within the partial ionization zones of iron, helium, and hydrogen, where the local opacity peaks. This local violation of the Eddington limit triggers subphotospheric inflation, which can increase the stellar radius by up to a factor of 40 and drastically lower the effective temperature, $T_{\rm eff}$ [Sanyal et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...580A..20S/abstract). The most extreme cases of envelope inflation are linked to Luminous Blue Variables (LBVs), which are characterized by violent, eruptive mass-loss episodes. Quasi-periodic modulations in the radio light curves of certain [[core-collapse-supernovae|Core-Collapse Supernovae]] indicate that Luminous Blue Variables may serve as the direct progenitors for some explosive transients [Vink et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007RMxAC..30...17V/abstract).

Less massive stars, typically in the range of $8 \lesssim M \lesssim 40 M_\odot$, cool and expand to become [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]] (RSGs). Observational evidence robustly links RSGs as the progenitors of [[individual-supernovae-and-subtypes|Type IIP supernovae]] [Arcavi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book..239A/abstract), [Schawinski et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Sci...321..223S/abstract). However, the exact upper mass limit for stars that successfully explode as RSGs remains a subject of active debate, with observational searches suggesting a potential cut-off around $\sim 18 M_\odot$ [Suzuki et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...852..101S/abstract), [Tsujimoto et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.3475T/abstract).

Stars that manage to strip away their outer hydrogen envelopes—either through intrinsic line-driven winds or via mass transfer in [[massive-binary-stars|Massive binary stars]]—transition into the Wolf-Rayet phase. [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]] are defined by extreme temperatures ($T_{\rm eff} \gtrsim 30$ kK) and powerful, dense stellar winds [Shenar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241004436S/abstract). In the Large Magellanic Cloud, the bulk of the hydrogen-depleted Wolf-Rayet population exhibits luminosities in the range of $5.3 \leq \log(L/L_\odot) \leq 5.8$, suggesting they originate from $20-40 M_\odot$ progenitors that evolved through a prior RSG phase. In contrast, the most luminous Wolf-Rayet stars ($\log(L/L_\odot) > 6$), which often retain some hydrogen, are thought to descend directly from the main sequence at very high initial masses [Hainich et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...565A..27H/abstract). Notably, current stellar evolution models struggle to completely reproduce the observed properties of Wolf-Rayet populations; the models frequently predict more compact, hotter stars, whereas the observed stars display larger radii and cooler temperatures, a discrepancy likely caused by unaccounted subphotospheric inflation [Hainich et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...565A..27H/abstract). To resolve these discrepancies and rigorously test stellar evolutionary theory, it remains vitally important to conduct wide-field surveys that yield meaningful statistics on massive star populations distributed across the entire Hertzsprung-Russell diagram in [[satellite-galaxies|nearby galaxies]] [Massey et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..425....3M/abstract).

## Massive Binary Stars and Rotational Mixing

The traditional paradigm of massive star evolution has undergone a "binarity revolution," driven by the realization that close binarity is the norm rather than the exception. Most massive stars reside in [[massive-binary-stars|multiple systems]], meaning their evolutionary tracks cannot be accurately modeled in isolation [Langer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..107L/abstract). Through [[mass-transfer|Mass transfer]], envelope stripping, and [[stellar-mergers|Stellar mergers]], [[massive-binary-stars|Massive binary stars]] drastically alter their evolutionary lifecycles and final fates compared to single-star models [Vanbeveren et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998brbi.book.....V/abstract), [Marchant et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231101865M/abstract). 

Binary interactions profoundly influence the pre-supernova structure of both the mass donor and the mass gainer. Comprehensive grids of binary evolution models demonstrate that mass gainers can evolve into [[luminous-stars|luminous blue or yellow supergiants]] prior to [[stellar-core-collapse|Stellar core collapse]], a phase where isolated single stars of equivalent mass would typically remain [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]] [Jin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251019965J/abstract). Furthermore, envelope stripping in binary systems is a primary formation channel for [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]] and a critical mechanism for producing [[stripped-envelope-supernovae|Stripped-Envelope Supernovae]] (e.g., Types Ib and Ic) [Langer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..107L/abstract). 

In tandem with binarity, stellar rotation plays a critical role in the internal evolution of massive stars. Massive stars rotate faster on average than lower-mass stars, triggering hydrodynamical instabilities that transport both [[stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|angular momentum]] and [[nucleosynthesis|nucleosynthetic products]] from the core to the stellar surface [Martins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A..31M/abstract). This rotationally-induced mixing is highly sensitive to the initial [[stellar-populations-by-metallicity|metallicity]] ($Z$). At lower metallicities, [[mass-loss-processes|stellar winds]] are weaker, allowing the star to retain more angular momentum. Consequently, chemical mixing is predicted to be stronger at low $Z$. Spectroscopic surveys of [[o-type-stars|O-type stars]] in the Magellanic Clouds confirm this, showing that nitrogen surface enrichment is significantly stronger in these lower-metallicity environments than in Galactic counterparts [Martins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A..31M/abstract). In fact, rotational mixing is an essential requirement in evolutionary models to explain the production of primary nitrogen in [[low-metallicity-galaxies|metal-poor galaxies]] [Matteucci et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..250..391M/abstract).

In extreme low-metallicity environments, rapid rotation can lead to entirely novel evolutionary pathways. Models of stars with initial rotational velocities of $v \approx 300 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ at sub-solar metallicities predict the formation of "TWUIN" (Transparent Wind Ultraviolet INtense) stars. These objects are nearly as compact as Wolf-Rayet stars and reach effective temperatures of $T_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 90 \text{ kK}$, but unlike classical Wolf-Rayet stars, they maintain optically thin winds. Such rapidly rotating, metal-poor stars are theorized to be progenitors of long-duration [[stellar-transients|gamma-ray bursts]], as their weak winds prevent the catastrophic shedding of angular momentum necessary to power a collapsar engine [Vink et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007RMxAC..30...17V/abstract), [Szécsi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhDT.......375S/abstract).

Despite these theoretical successes, tensions remain in the literature. Distinguishing the surface abundance signatures caused by isolated rotational mixing from those produced by binary mass transfer is observationally challenging, though detailed [[chemical-abundances-and-nucleosynthesis|surface isotopic abundances]] are emerging as a metric to separate binary interaction products from single stars [Jin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251019965J/abstract). Additionally, validating the exact efficiency of chemical mixing and angular momentum transport is complicated by observational biases, as many current spectroscopic samples are skewed toward slow rotators [Martins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A..31M/abstract). Further resolution of these competing effects is required to accurately map massive star progenitors to the diverse landscape of [[core-collapse-supernovae|Core-Collapse Supernovae]].

## Nucleosynthesis and Galactic Chemical Enrichment

Massive stars ($M \gtrsim 8 M_\odot$) are the primary engines of [[nucleosynthesis|Nucleosynthesis]] in the Universe, driving the [[chemical-enrichment-history|Chemical enrichment history]] of galaxies. During their relatively short [[main-sequence-evolution|main-sequence]] lifetimes ($t \sim 10^7$ yr), these stars synthesize progressively heavier elements through successive phases of hydrostatic core and shell burning, culminating in the production of iron-peak elements. The synthesized material is subsequently distributed into the interstellar medium (ISM) via powerful [[mass-loss-processes|stellar winds]] and terminal [[core-collapse-supernovae|Core-Collapse Supernovae]] [Nomoto et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ARA&A..51..457N/abstract).

The chemical signature of massive stars is strongly dominated by [[alpha-element-nucleosynthesis|Alpha-Element Nucleosynthesis]]. Elements such as oxygen, neon, magnesium, and silicon are synthesized abundantly and ejected during core collapse. While massive stars also produce iron-peak elements in their deep interiors, extreme temperatures during the explosion photodisintegrate some of these nuclei, and a significant fraction of the remaining iron is often locked inside the collapsing stellar remnant. Consequently, massive stars are the dominant cosmic source of alpha-elements, whereas [[type-ia-supernovae|Type Ia Supernovae]]—which operate on much longer evolutionary timescales ($\sim 10^8 - 10^9$ yr)—provide the bulk of galactic iron. This inherent time delay governs the $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ abundance ratio over a galaxy's [[star-formation-history|star formation history]] [Matteucci et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..250..391M/abstract).

Ejection mechanisms depend heavily on the initial stellar mass. Stars in the $8 \lesssim M/M_\odot \lesssim 20$ range enrich the ISM predominantly through supernova explosions, while stars with $M \gtrsim 20 M_\odot$ contribute substantial nucleosynthetic yields via pre-supernova mass loss. In late evolutionary stages, complex internal dynamics such as carbon-oxygen (C-O) shell mergers can drastically alter nucleosynthetic outcomes. Recent models indicate that C-O shell mergers—which typically occur when the CO core mass is $M_{\mathrm{CO}} < 4.90 M_\odot$ and the central $^{12}$C mass fraction is $X_{\mathrm{C12}} < 0.277$—can account for the production of odd-Z elements like scandium and potassium, resolving long-standing underproduction issues in standard yields [Roberti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250418867R/abstract). Furthermore, massive stars and their explosive remnants serve as critical [[r-process-nucleosynthesis-sites|r-process nucleosynthesis sites]], facilitating the rapid [[heavy-element-nucleosynthesis|neutron-capture processes]] required to form the heaviest elements.

Despite significant theoretical advances, major tensions remain in massive star nucleosynthesis models. Massive stars are primary Galactic producers of $^{26}\mathrm{Al}$, with Type II supernovae and [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]] contributing significantly to the observed live $^{26}\mathrm{Al}$ mass in the Galaxy [Signore et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993A&AS...97..141S/abstract). However, observations of Galactic $^{60}\mathrm{Fe}/^{26}\mathrm{Al}$ ratios from $\gamma$-ray telescopes persistently conflict with theoretical predictions. Recent experimental constraints on neutron-capture cross-sections have actually increased the predicted $^{60}\mathrm{Fe}$ yields, worsening the discrepancy and implying that current stellar interior models may be missing fundamental mixing physics [Spyrou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatCo..15.9608S/abstract). 

Finally, the upper-mass limits of supernova progenitors pose a severe challenge to galactic oxygen budgets. As noted previously, observational searches suggest that a substantial fraction of stars with initial masses $>18 M_\odot$ may experience "failed supernovae," collapsing directly into black holes without a luminous explosion. However, chemical evolution models demonstrate that if all stars above $17 M_\odot$ fail to eject their oxygen-rich layers, the Galaxy would suffer a severe oxygen deficit. Thus, to reconcile these populations, a significant fraction of very massive stars must still successfully expel their oxygen envelopes, either via successful delayed explosions or extreme pre-collapse mass loss [Suzuki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...852..101S/abstract).

## Young Massive Clusters (YMCs) and Dense Systems

[[young-massive-clusters|Young massive clusters]] (YMCs) represent extreme environments of vigorous star formation and serve as fundamental building blocks of galaxies [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract). Loosely defined as dense stellar aggregates with total masses $M_{cl} \gtrsim 10^4 M_\odot$ and ages $\lesssim 100$ Myr, these systems are critical laboratories for probing massive star evolution, early stellar dynamics, and the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] within coeval populations [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract), [Longmore et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014prpl.conf..291L/abstract).

The formation of massive stars within these dense systems is driven by the [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] of individual [[star-forming-regions-and-cores|molecular cores]], rather than through the competitive accretion of unbound gas from the wider cluster reservoir [Henning et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990FCPh...14..321H/abstract), [Krumholz et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.10412K/abstract). Observations indicate that massive stars do not exclusively form at the highest density peaks or exact centers of their parent [[molecular-clouds-and-gas-clumping|giant molecular clouds]] [Ochsendorf et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...43O/abstract). Once formed, massive stars rapidly dominate the cluster's energy budget. Radiative feedback—specifically photoionization and radiation pressure—plays a more immediate and disruptive role than subsequent [[core-collapse-supernovae|core-collapse supernovae]] in expelling residual gas, increasing the internal gas energy, and potentially quenching subsequent star formation [Guillard et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.5001G/abstract). The efficiency of this gas expulsion is closely tied to the "infant mortality" of [[star-clusters|star clusters]], as it is estimated that only $\sim 10\%$ of clusters survive to old age [Kroupa et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..9370K/abstract), [Longmore et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014prpl.conf..291L/abstract). However, depending on their initial mass function and the severity of environmental disruption processes, some YMCs have the potential to survive and evolve into long-lived [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]].

Several YMCs in the Milky Way are benchmark systems for massive star research. [[westerlund-1|Westerlund 1]], the most massive young open cluster known in the Galaxy, boasts a dynamical mass $M_{dyn} \sim 1.3 \times 10^5 M_\odot$ [Negueruela et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..250..301N/abstract). It contains an unusually rich population of post-main-sequence massive stars, including over 30 luminous supergiants and nearly 100 other less evolved massive stars, alongside a large known population of [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]], making it a unique template for tracing evolutionary sequences at roughly constant luminosity [Negueruela et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..250..301N/abstract), [Negueruela et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014mysc.conf....9N/abstract). Near [[the-galactic-center|The Galactic Center]], the [[arches-cluster|Arches cluster]] and the [[quintuplet-cluster|Quintuplet cluster]] define the upper mass limits of Galactic cluster mass functions [Figer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...49F/abstract). The mass spectrum of the Arches cluster suggests a stellar upper mass cutoff of $\sim 150-200 M_\odot$ [Figer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...49F/abstract).

YMCs also provide a stringent test for the universality of the initial mass function. While the IMF is broadly observed to be robust with a constant upper-mass limit across diverse environments [Oey et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007astro.ph..3036O/abstract), [Longmore et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014prpl.conf..291L/abstract), the literature remains in tension regarding extreme environments. For instance, preliminary observations of the Arches cluster have suggested a [[top-heavy-imf|Top-heavy IMF]], implying that the yield of massive stars may be environmentally dependent in the dense, high-tide regions near the Galactic Center [Figer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...49F/abstract).

From a dynamical perspective, the structural evolution of dense YMCs is deeply intertwined with the life cycles of their massive stellar constituents. For clusters with a short initial [[relaxation-time|Relaxation time]] ($\le 100$ Myr), early dynamical expansion is largely powered by [[mass-loss|Mass loss]] from massive stars residing in the cluster core. The energy released by these stellar winds balances the energy transported outward by [[two-body-relaxation|Two-body relaxation]] [Gieles et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASPC..470..339G/abstract). Furthermore, high-resolution kinematic studies show that massive clusters with $M_{cl} \gtrsim 10^5 M_\odot$ accrete gas from their local environment and inherit substantial angular momentum, exhibiting ordered rotation (up to $v \sim 8.5$ km s$^{-1}$) and asymmetric line-of-sight velocity distributions at formation [Lahén et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904...71L/abstract). At galactic centers, dense [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|nuclear star clusters]] exhibit a complex co-evolution and scaling relationship with central [[supermassive-black-holes|massive black holes]]. In these extreme environments, massive black hole mergers can dynamically heat, modify, or even destroy components of the surrounding nuclear star cluster [Antonini et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806L...8A/abstract). From a theoretical perspective, the high-velocity, high-density environment near an isolated massive black hole provides a unique laboratory for unusual stellar birth and complex dynamical interactions, continually challenging our standard models of star formation and cluster dynamics [Alexander et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55...17A/abstract).

## Core-Collapse Supernovae and Final Fates

The evolution of stars with initial masses $\gtrsim 8 M_\odot$ culminates in the exhaustion of nuclear fuel in their cores. Unable to support themselves against gravity, these iron cores collapse until they reach nuclear densities, triggering a rebound that drives a shock wave through the stellar envelope. This catastrophic event produces a [[core-collapse-supernovae|core-collapse supernova]]. While the total energy released during the collapse is immense, typically only a small fraction ($\sim 1\%$) of this energy is transferred to the envelope to power the visible explosion.

The observational diversity of CCSNe is dictated by the progenitor's mass, metallicity, rotation, and binarity [Langer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..107L/abstract). [[type-ii-supernovae|Type II Supernovae]] are characterized by prominent hydrogen lines and represent the most common outcome. The dominant subclass, Type IIP (plateau), robustly originates from [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]] progenitors. This mapping was observationally cemented by the detection of a radiative shock breakout from a red supergiant in the ultraviolet light curve of SNLS-04D2dc [Schawinski et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Sci...321..223S/abstract). Similarly, the detection of extremely luminous X-ray outbursts marking the 'shock breakout' at the moment of explosion provides a primary method for catching supernovae at their very birth [Soderberg et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Natur.453..469S/abstract). Other progenitor mappings include Type IIn events driven by [[luminous-stars|luminous blue variable]] eruptions, Type IIb events from partially stripped binary companions, and SN 1987A-like events from blue supergiant progenitors [Arcavi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book..239A/abstract). Observations of massive stars like Eta Carinae suggest that pre-supernova eruptive mass ejection episodes occur, potentially explaining why some Type IIn supernovae appear to be non-terminal outbursts rather than genuine core-collapse events [van Dyk et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..332...47V/abstract). Massive stars that have entirely lost their hydrogen envelopes, such as [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]], explode as [[stripped-envelope-supernovae|Stripped-Envelope Supernovae]] (Types Ib and Ic).

A major unresolved tension in stellar astrophysics concerns the upper mass limit for successful supernova explosions. While traditional theory assumed that most stars up to $\sim 100 M_\odot$ end in luminous explosions, observational searches for progenitors have revealed a stark scarcity of CCSNe originating from stars more massive than $16-18 M_\odot$ [Suzuki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...852..101S/abstract), [Tsujimoto et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.3475T/abstract). As discussed earlier, this absence supports the "failed supernova" hypothesis, wherein the shock wave stalls and the star collapses directly into a black hole—a scenario that must be carefully reconciled with galactic [[chemical-evolution-and-nucleosynthesis|chemical evolution]] constraints. 

The ultimate fate of a collapsing core is deeply tied to its late-stage interior structure. Recent 1D evolutionary models have identified a "monotonicity principle" for massive star cores, where the density, entropy, and chemical distributions at the onset of collapse can smoothly predict explosion properties such as the final [[proto-neutron-stars|Proto-neutron stars]] mass and the [[explosion-energy|Explosion energy]] [Takahashi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...19T/abstract). Furthermore, pre-supernova structural shifts, such as late-stage carbon-oxygen (C-O) shell mergers, significantly alter the internal fuel distribution and play a critical role in solving the theoretical underproduction of odd-Z elements like scandium and potassium [Roberti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250418867R/abstract). To directly probe these nucleosynthetic yields, astronomers rely on observations of supernovae in their late, optically thin nebular phases. Recent advancements in multi-line radiative transfer modeling and Monte Carlo techniques have become essential to interpret these nebular-phase supernova observations accurately, allowing researchers to unveil the masses and precise compositions of the material produced deep within stellar interiors [Jerkstrand et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhDT........90J/abstract).

At the extreme end of the mass spectrum, stars die via more exotic mechanisms. Progenitors with massive, rapidly rotating cores can drive weak relativistic jets upon collapse, producing broad-lined Type Ic supernovae (Type Ic-BL) and fast extragalactic X-ray transients [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1073S/abstract). Meanwhile, [[very-massive-stars|Very massive stars]] ($150-300 M_\odot$) may encounter the pair-production instability, undergoing complete thermonuclear disruption. Because strong stellar winds at solar metallicity rapidly strip mass and reduce the core below the critical threshold, [[pisne-models|pair-instability supernovae]] (PISNe) are strictly restricted to low-metallicity environments.

## The Impact of Metallicity and the Early Universe

Studies of [[stellar-populations-by-metallicity|Stellar Populations by Metallicity]] reveal that the evolution of massive stars is fundamentally altered in [[low-metallicity-galaxies|Low-metallicity galaxies]] and the early universe. Because radiatively driven stellar winds scale strongly with metallicity ($Z$), metal-poor stars exhibit significantly reduced [[mass-loss-processes|mass-loss rates]] and retain more of their mass and angular momentum throughout their lifetimes [Langer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..107L/abstract). To empirically constrain these effects, the ULLYSES (UV Legacy Library of Young Stars as Essential Standards) program and the complementary XShootU survey are mapping the ultraviolet and optical spectra of massive stars in the Magellanic Clouds [Vink et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...675A.154V/abstract). These observations confirm that rotational mixing is highly efficient at low metallicities, dredging up CNO-cycle products and leading to early and pronounced surface nitrogen enrichment [Martins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A..31M/abstract). At these extreme low metallicities, the previously mentioned "TWUIN" stars can become a dominant source of radiative feedback. Their optically thin winds and extreme temperatures ($\sim 90,000$ K) potentially produce the intense ionizing radiation observed in ultra-metal-poor [[dwarf-galaxy-physical-processes|dwarf galaxies]] like I Zwicky 18 [Szécsi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhDT.......375S/abstract).

The most extreme low-metallicity environments existed during the epoch of [[first-star-formation|First star formation]], which produced metal-free (Population III) stars. Without efficient radiative cooling pathways, the cooling timescale in primordial gas clouds was longer than the gravitational collapse timescale, meaning these clouds were unable to fragment to form low-mass systems. This historically led to the prediction of a top-heavy [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] dominated by isolated, extremely massive stars. However, modern radiation-hydrodynamics simulations demonstrate that Population III stars likely formed in binary or multiple systems rather than in strict isolation [Wise et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1201.4820W/abstract). This multiplicity arises because disk fragmentation ultimately determines the initial mass distribution in the early universe. The immense radiation and mechanical feedback from these Population III stars played a pivotal role in heating and evacuating gas within early dark matter minihalos, thereby regulating subsequent star formation and cosmic reionization [Greif et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ComAC...2....3G/abstract). Furthermore, protostellar radiation feedback is highly effective at evaporating the circumstellar accretion disk, which can halt stellar growth at masses as low as $\sim 43\ M_\odot$ [Hosokawa et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...334.1250H/abstract).

The reduced mass-loss rates at low $Z$ theoretically allow slowly rotating or non-rotating stars with initial masses of $150-300\ M_\odot$ to retain their massive envelopes and end their lives as [[pisne-models|pair-instability supernovae]] (PISNe) [Yusof et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1012.3649Y/abstract). However, a significant tension exists: the unique nucleosynthetic signatures of PISNe are conspicuously absent in the oldest, extremely metal-poor stars of the Galactic halo. This suggests either that protostellar feedback successfully halted stellar growth before reaching the PISN mass regime [Hosokawa et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...334.1250H/abstract), or that these stars underwent direct [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] to form black holes without ejecting their yields [Suzuki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...852..101S/abstract).

To explore these early-universe phenomena, astronomers increasingly rely on local extragalactic analogs. For example, massive OBA-type stars have been confirmed in the Sagittarius Dwarf Irregular Galaxy at a remarkably low oxygen abundance of $12 + \log(\text{O/H}) = 7.37$ [Garcia et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474L..66G/abstract). Beyond their individual explosion fates, low-metallicity massive stars are also integral to the chemical enrichment of [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]]. It is theorized that the slow, dense stellar winds from metal-poor cool supergiants may have provided the enriched material that coalesced to form subsequent generations of low-mass stars, offering a mechanism to explain the complex abundance anomalies found within these ancient stellar systems [Szécsi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhDT.......375S/abstract). Moreover, recent discoveries such as the C-19 stellar stream provide compelling evidence that intact globular clusters could form at metallicities significantly below the purported $[\mathrm{Fe/H}] \sim -2.7$ floor in the early Universe [Martin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Natur.601...45M/abstract). Furthermore, the early formation of the Milky Way can be mapped using [[lower-subgiant-branch|subgiant stars]], showing that thick disk formation began only $\sim 0.8$ Gyr after the Big Bang [Xiang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Natur.603..599X/abstract).

## Historical Development of Massive Star Models

Historically, early theoretical frameworks treated massive stars primarily as isolated, spherically symmetric objects in hydrostatic equilibrium. In these classical models, [[evolutionary-tracks|Evolutionary tracks]] on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram were dictated almost entirely by the star's initial mass and chemical composition. Because the dynamical time scale is much shorter than the thermal and nuclear time scales ($t_{\rm dyn} \ll t_{\rm th} \ll t_{\rm nuc}$), evolution was modeled via static structure equations, with the star merely adjusting to localized changes in chemical composition driven by nuclear burning. 

A major paradigm shift—often termed the "binarity revolution"—occurred when observations established that the vast majority of massive stars reside in close binary or multiple systems. It is now understood that binary interactions, including [[mass-transfer|Mass transfer]], envelope stripping, and [[stellar-mergers|Stellar mergers]], fundamentally alter the evolutionary pathways and lifetimes of these stars [Langer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..107L/abstract). Consequently, isolated single-star models are insufficient for accurate population synthesis, prompting the development of comprehensive grids of [[massive-binary-stars|Massive binary stars]] to track interaction products and their exotic final states [Marchant et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231101865M/abstract).

Modern simulations have grown increasingly complex to accommodate critical internal physics, specifically rotation, magnetic fields, and revised [[mass-loss-processes|Mass Loss Processes]]. Observationally, magnetic fields in these objects are being rigorously characterized by the Magnetism in Massive Stars (MiMeS) project, which has surveyed the magnetic properties of massive stars across various Galactic clusters and associations [Alecian et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012sf2a.conf..401A/abstract). Rotation triggers hydrodynamical instabilities that transport angular momentum and mix CNO-cycle nucleosynthesis products to the stellar surface—an effect that models predict to be heavily dependent on initial metallicity [Martins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A..31M/abstract). Furthermore, theoretical mass-loss rates driven by line-radiation pressure have been iteratively revised downward in recent years. These updated mass-loss prescriptions dramatically impact the predicted core masses prior to collapse, altering expectations for the formation rates of [[compact-objects-and-binary-mergers|Compact Objects and Binary Mergers]] and offering potential solutions to issues like the missing [[red-supergiants|red supergiant]] problem [Merritt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250717052M/abstract). To capture this complexity, modern researchers have computed comprehensive presupernova evolution grids spanning initial masses of $13-120 M_\odot$ across diverse metallicities. These grids underscore the critical importance of the interplay between mass loss and internal mixing in determining the final pre-collapse structure and subsequent explosion properties [Limongi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book..513L/abstract).

Advanced models must also account for extreme radiation pressure in the upper mass limits. Detailed 1D hydrodynamical simulations reveal that stars with masses $\gtrsim 40 M_\odot$ routinely exceed the local Eddington limit in their envelopes (specifically in the partial ionization zones of iron and helium). This leads to subphotospheric inflation, which can increase the stellar radius by up to a factor of 40 and significantly lower the effective temperature, explaining discrepancies between older evolutionary tracks and the observed properties of massive stars [Sanyal et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...580A..20S/abstract).

This transition from idealized models to modern physical frameworks has been driven by synergistic advances in computation and observation. On the theoretical side, the foundational work of the [[geneva-stellar-evolution-models|Geneva stellar evolution models]] has been complemented by flexible, open-source codes like MESA, which allow researchers to compute extensive grids incorporating differential rotation, magnetic angular momentum transport, and binary physics [Jin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251019965J/abstract). Observationally, these models are rigorously tested against large-scale spectroscopic campaigns. Notably, the VLT-FLAMES Survey of massive stars acquired high-resolution spectra of over 800 stars across the Milky Way and Magellanic Clouds, providing the unprecedented statistical samples required to constrain modern prescriptions for rotational mixing and metallicity-dependent mass loss [Evans et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Msngr.131...25E/abstract).

## Open Questions: The Weak-Wind Problem and Exotic Progenitors

Despite significant advances in [[stellar-evolution-and-modeling|Stellar Evolution and Modeling]], the precise mapping between [[progenitor-properties|Progenitor properties]] and their final remnants remains an active area of debate. A primary source of this uncertainty is the "weak-wind problem" [Sander et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260100373S/abstract). High-resolution X-ray spectroscopy from observatories like XMM-Newton and Chandra of [[ob-stars|OB stars]] provides critical constraints on stellar wind parameters and the structure of shocked gas in stellar outflows [Güdel et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&ARv..17..309G/abstract). Modern theoretical and observational efforts have led to a systematic downward revision of theoretical [[mass-loss|Mass loss]] rates for massive stars across various evolutionary phases [Merritt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250717052M/abstract). This reduction complicates our understanding of how stars shed their hydrogen envelopes. If mass loss is weaker than previously assumed, it becomes difficult to explain the observed populations of [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]] and the exact mechanisms of envelope stripping without invoking high rates of binary interaction [Langer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..107L/abstract). Furthermore, these revised wind rates inject significant uncertainty into predictions of final pre-supernova core masses and the resulting black hole mass spectrum [Merritt et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250717052M/abstract).

A major tension in the field relates to the aforementioned fate of stars above $\sim 18 M_\odot$. Reconciling the direct progenitor identifications, which show a scarcity of supernovae above this mass [Tsujimoto et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.3475T/abstract), with Galactic [[chemical-evolution-and-nucleosynthesis|Chemical Evolution and Nucleosynthesis]] constraints remains a central problem. If these massive stars collapse completely, they trap their synthesized heavy elements, failing to reproduce the observed solar abundances of oxygen [Suzuki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...852..101S/abstract). Solving this requires identifying whether the oxygen-rich layers are ejected via successful but optically faint explosions, or extreme late-stage mass loss. Furthermore, massive stars collapsing directly into black holes are posited as the primary progenitors for Ultra-Luminous X-ray sources (ULXs) [Mapelli et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AN....332..414M/abstract).

The theoretical endpoints of [[very-massive-stars|Very massive stars]] ($150-300 M_\odot$) remain highly ambiguous. Even at sub-solar Large Magellanic Cloud metallicities, recent evolutionary models of the exceptionally massive stars in the R136 cluster suggest that severe mass loss may prevent these stars from ever reaching the pair-instability regime, instead leading to Type Ib/c supernovae [Keszthelyi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250615230K/abstract). Other transient observations also challenge existing paradigms, such as the discovery of the Type Ibn supernova PS1-12sk in an elliptical galaxy devoid of active star formation, which contradicts the standard assumption that all such transients require a massive, short-lived progenitor [Hosseinzadeh et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...871L...9H/abstract). To definitively test the theoretical properties and evolutionary endpoints of these extreme objects, astronomers must look to next-generation space missions. The unprecedented high spatial and spectral resolution of the future Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO) will be fundamentally required to resolve individual very massive stars within distant, dense [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|starburst]] environments [Martins et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250703371M/abstract).

Finally, the literature continues to explore exotic stellar configurations, such as [[thorne-zytkow-objects|Thorne-Zytkow objects]] (TZOs). These hypothetical entities consist of [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]] harboring degenerate neutron cores, formed either through unstable mass transfer in massive X-ray binaries or via the direct collision of a neutron star and a massive companion [Podsiadlowski et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.274..485P/abstract). While predicted to exhibit anomalous surface abundances of lithium and rapid neutron-capture (rp-process) elements, definitively confirming their existence remains challenging. The final fates of TZOs are highly uncertain, potentially ending in a neutrino runaway, the formation of a millisecond pulsar, or accretion-induced collapse into a black hole [Podsiadlowski et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.274..485P/abstract).
