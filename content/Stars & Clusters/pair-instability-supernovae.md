A **pair-instability supernova (PISN)** is a rare and exceptionally energetic class of [[thermonuclear-explosions|Thermonuclear explosions]] that completely disrupts extremely massive stars, typically those with zero-age main-sequence masses between $140$ and $260 M_\odot$. The phenomenon diverges fundamentally from the textbook model of stellar death. While standard [[type-ii-supernovae|Type II Supernovae]] and other [[core-collapse-supernovae|Core-Collapse Supernovae]] are triggered by the catastrophic implosion of an iron core in stars above $8 M_\odot$ and leave behind a compact remnant, a PISN is triggered much earlier in the stellar life cycle. In the massive carbon-oxygen cores of PISN progenitors, core temperatures become high enough—and densities low enough—that pressure-supporting gamma-ray photons spontaneously convert into electron-positron pairs. This sudden loss of radiation pressure causes the core to rapidly contract. The resulting spike in temperature and density ignites a runaway oxygen burning process that releases more nuclear energy than the star's total gravitational binding energy, obliterating the star entirely [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract), [Kozyreva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.146K/abstract).

Because the explosion unbinds the entire star, pair-instability supernovae leave no black hole or neutron star remnant behind, instead ejecting a massive reservoir of newly synthesized heavy elements into the interstellar medium. The nucleosynthetic yield of a PISN is highly distinct, characterized by massive quantities of $\alpha$-elements, a pronounced deficiency in odd-charged elements (the "odd-even effect"), and immense amounts of radioactive nickel-56 ($^{56}\mathrm{Ni}$) [Kozyreva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.146K/abstract). In some cases, a single event can produce $>3 M_\odot$ of $^{56}\mathrm{Ni}$, the radioactive decay of which powers characteristically luminous and slowly fading light curves. Due to the requirement that progenitors retain massive cores without losing them to metal-driven stellar winds, PISNe are theoretically favored in pristine, low-metallicity environments. Consequently, they are thought to have been a dominant mechanism for the chemical enrichment of the early universe, driven primarily by the first generation of metal-free Population III stars [Pan et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.2701P/abstract). 

Despite a robust theoretical foundation within [[supernova-physics|Supernova Physics]], the unambiguous observational confirmation of a true PISN remains a major open challenge in time-domain astronomy. While high-luminosity transients like SN 2007bi have historically been proposed as prime candidates, there is active debate over whether such slow-fading events are genuine pair-instability explosions or standard core-collapse events energized by the spin-down of a central magnetar [Nicholl et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.502..346N/abstract). This article explores the physical explosion mechanism and progenitor evolution of PISNe, details their unique chemical enrichment signatures, and examines their complex cosmic rates. It also addresses ongoing tensions in the field—including the difficulty of distinguishing total disruptions from non-terminal pulsational pair-instability events—and highlights how upcoming observations by JWST and the Euclid mission aim to finally identify these [[exotic-supernovae-and-progenitor-models|Exotic Supernovae and Progenitor Models]].

## Theoretical Physics and Explosion Mechanism

In standard massive stars ($M \gtrsim 8 M_\odot$), stable nuclear burning progresses until an inert iron core is formed. This core eventually undergoes a catastrophic implosion, leading to [[core-collapse-supernovae|Core-Collapse Supernovae]] that frequently manifest observationally as [[type-ii-supernovae|Type II Supernovae]]. However, for extremely massive stars with initial masses spanning $140 \lesssim M \lesssim 260 M_\odot$, the evolutionary trajectory diverges significantly. These stars develop massive carbon-oxygen cores weighing between $65 M_\odot$ and $130 M_\odot$ [Pan et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.2701P/abstract), [Nicholl et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.502..346N/abstract). Within this mass regime, exceedingly high temperatures are reached at relatively low densities prior to the onset of stable oxygen ignition [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract).

Under these specific thermodynamic conditions, the stellar radiation field contains a substantial fraction of highly energetic photons. When the energy of these pressure-supporting photons exceeds the rest-mass threshold required to create electron-positron pairs ($\gamma \gamma \rightarrow e^+ e^-$), the stellar equation of state is fundamentally altered [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract). The conversion of photon energy into the rest mass of electron-positron pairs acts as a severe energy sink, precipitating a sudden drop in thermal pressure. This loss of support initiates a rapid and violent gravitational contraction of the oxygen core. 

As the massive core collapses inward, the internal temperature and density skyrocket, triggering a catastrophic thermonuclear runaway. The explosive burning of oxygen and heavier elements releases an immense amount of nuclear energy. Unlike conventional core-collapse mechanisms—where the implosion leaves behind a compact remnant and only $\sim 1\%$ of the liberated gravitational energy is successfully transferred to unbind the envelope—the nuclear energy released in a pair-instability event exceeds the entire gravitational binding energy of the star. This translates to pure [[thermonuclear-explosions|Thermonuclear explosions]] that result in the total disruption of the stellar structure [Kozyreva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.146K/abstract).

The explosive nucleosynthesis associated with this total disruption synthesizes massive quantities of heavy elements, including enormous yields of radioactive $^{56}\mathrm{Ni}$. Theoretical models and analyses of luminous candidates like SN 2007bi (which possessed an estimated core mass of $\sim 100 M_\odot$) demonstrate that the explosion can generate $>3 M_\odot$ of $^{56}\mathrm{Ni}$ [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract). The radioactive decay of this vast nickel mass to cobalt and iron is what ultimately powers the exceptionally bright and slowly evolving light curves characteristic of pair-instability supernovae [Nicholl et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.502..346N/abstract).

## Evolution of Massive Progenitors

The standard paradigm of massive stellar evolution dictates that stars with a zero-age main-sequence mass $M \gtrsim 8\ M_{\odot}$ eventually undergo iron core collapse, generally producing [[type-ii-supernovae|Type II Supernovae]] or other [[core-collapse-supernovae|Core-Collapse Supernovae]]. However, stars with initial masses between approximately $140\ M_{\odot}$ and $260\ M_{\odot}$ follow a remarkably different evolutionary path [Pan et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.2701P/abstract). During their late-stage stellar evolution, these extremely massive progenitors develop massive carbon-oxygen cores. If the core mass exceeds roughly $50\ M_{\odot}$, the star enters a critical thermodynamic regime of high temperature and relatively low density. In this state, the conversion of pressure-supporting photons into electron-positron pairs occurs prior to oxygen ignition [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract). This sudden loss of radiation pressure induces a rapid contraction, which in turn ignites a runaway [[thermonuclear-explosions|thermonuclear explosion]] that completely unbinds the star [Kozyreva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.146K/abstract).

The primary barrier to a star reaching this requisite pre-supernova core mass is stellar mass loss, which is highly sensitive to the metallicity of the progenitor's environment. Strong line-driven winds in metal-rich stars typically strip away the stellar envelope, preventing the core from growing to the critical threshold for pair-instability. Consequently, pair-instability supernovae are theoretically favored in pristine or highly metal-poor environments. The classic progenitors for these events are Population III stars in the early universe, which formed from primordial, zero-metallicity gas and are expected to have had a top-heavy initial mass function [Pan et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.2701P/abstract). However, recent evolutionary models demonstrate that the pair-instability mechanism is not strictly confined to the high-redshift universe. Progenitors can also arise at later epochs in environments where the metallicity is $Z < Z_{\odot}/3$ [Kozyreva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.146K/abstract), potentially forming in chemically inhomogeneous, metal-poor pockets of gas at lower redshifts [Briel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.3907B/abstract).

The precise zero-age main-sequence mass limits for pair-instability depend strongly on theoretical assumptions regarding stellar mixing, rotation, and mass-loss rates. Furthermore, the mapping of progenitor models to specific supernova types, including PISNe, is highly sensitive to both rotation and binarity [Langer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..107L/abstract). Hydrodynamic evolutionary models successfully produce pair-instability events from $150\ M_{\odot}$ and $250\ M_{\odot}$ progenitors at a metallicity of $Z = 0.001$ [Kozyreva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.146K/abstract). Stars with masses slightly below this critical range may instead undergo pulsational pair-instability, where they eject massive shells of material during energetic pulses before eventually succumbing to core collapse [Nicholl et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..893N/abstract). 

Observationally verifying these massive progenitor models remains challenging due to significant ambiguities in the literature regarding the interpretation of candidate events. For example, the superluminous transient SN 2007bi was initially identified as a strong pair-instability candidate, with models suggesting it originated from a progenitor with an exploding core mass $\sim 100\ M_{\odot}$ [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract). However, this interpretation is actively debated. Subsequent studies have demonstrated that the blue colors and relatively fast rise times of SN 2007bi and similar slow-fading superluminous supernovae may be better explained by the spin-down of a central magnetar rather than a true pair-instability explosion [Nicholl et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.502..346N/abstract). Until a transient is found that unambiguously matches both the light curve and the unique nucleosynthetic signature of a completely disrupted massive core, the exact frequency and nature of these events remains a major open question in the study of [[exotic-supernovae-and-progenitor-models|Exotic Supernovae and Progenitor Models]].

## Nucleosynthesis and Chemical Enrichment

Unlike standard [[core-collapse-supernovae|Core-Collapse Supernovae]] or [[type-ii-supernovae|Type II Supernovae]]—which are triggered by iron core collapse and transfer only $\sim 1\%$ of their energy to the stellar envelope—pair-instability supernovae (PISNe) are total [[thermonuclear-explosions|Thermonuclear explosions]] that completely disrupt the progenitor star [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract), [Kozyreva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.146K/abstract). Because the entire massive core is processed and dispersed, the nucleosynthesis and chemical enrichment signatures of PISNe are highly distinct from other transient classes.

The immense scale of the thermonuclear burning produces extreme quantities of radioactive nickel-56 ($^{56}\mathrm{Ni}$), which acts as the primary power source for the supernova's slowly fading, superluminous light curve. Observational modeling of the PISN candidate SN 2007bi implies an ejected $^{56}\mathrm{Ni}$ mass of $>3~M_\odot$ [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract). This yield vastly exceeds the typical nickel production of both core-collapse events and [[type-ia-supernovae|Type Ia Supernovae]]. Additionally, direct observations of the gamma-ray line flux from the $^{56}\mathrm{Ni}$ decay chain could provide critical constraints on nucleosynthetic yields. However, while this gamma-ray emission peaks roughly 70 days after onset in standard Type I supernovae [Gehrels et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJ...322..215G/abstract), the immensely larger ejecta mass of a PISN dictates a considerably longer diffusion time before gamma rays can escape.

In addition to iron-peak elements, PISN ejecta are characterized by the copious production of $\alpha$-elements accompanied by a severe deficiency in odd-charged (odd-$Z$) elements [Kozyreva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.146K/abstract). This nucleosynthetic "odd-even effect" is most pronounced among intermediate-mass elements ranging from silicon to calcium. The strength of the odd-even variance is metallicity-dependent; while it is an extreme hallmark of Population III (zero-metallicity) PISNe, hydrodynamical models indicate that the effect is significantly weaker in explosions occurring at local, low-metallicity environments near $Z = 0.001$ [Kozyreva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.146K/abstract).

Because PISNe eject such a massive reservoir of metals, they have the potential to profoundly influence cosmic chemical enrichment. Distinct PISN abundance patterns have been identified in both high-redshift environments and local metal-poor stars. For example, the broad-line region of the $z=7.54$ quasar ULAS J1342+0928 exhibits an exceptionally low magnesium-to-iron ratio ($[\mathrm{Mg/Fe}] = -1.11 \pm 0.12$), a pattern best explained by enrichment from a $150-300~M_\odot$ Pop III PISN [Yoshii et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937...61Y/abstract). Similarly, the extremely metal-poor Galactic halo star LAMOST J1010+2358 ($[\mathrm{Fe/H}] = -2.4$) exhibits an abundance profile requiring a $250-260~M_\odot$ PISN progenitor to reproduce its chemical signature [Koutsouridou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962L..26K/abstract). 

On macroscopic scales, PISNe have been proposed as a source of the enhanced metallicities observed in the intracluster medium of galaxy clusters. Including PISNe in chemical evolution models naturally increases the abundance of intermediate-mass elements relative to both oxygen and iron, satisfying X-ray cluster observations without necessitating an unphysical overproduction of [[type-ia-supernovae|Type Ia Supernovae]] [Morsony et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441.2134M/abstract). Note that while PISNe are a major source of intermediate-mass elements in galaxy clusters, other exotic transients like calcium-rich gap transients are also required to fully reproduce the anomalous calcium abundances observed in the intracluster medium [Mulchaey et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...780L..34M/abstract). However, the literature reveals tensions regarding the universality of PISN enrichment in all massive starburst environments. In the starburst galaxy M82, the observed energy-to-heavy-element mass ratio of the hot plasma is considerably larger than what PISN models predict; instead, chemical and energetic constraints in M82 are better reproduced by the nucleosynthesis of [[hypernovae|Hypernovae]] [Umeda et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...578..855U/abstract), [Nomoto et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003IAUS..212..395N/abstract).

## Historical Development and Candidate Identification

For decades following their theoretical prediction, pair-instability supernovae (PISNe) remained purely hypothetical. Models dictated that these events, triggered by pair-production in the oxygen cores of very massive stars ($140 \lesssim M \lesssim 260 M_\odot$), would result in complete [[thermonuclear-explosions|Thermonuclear explosions]] that overcome the star's gravitational binding energy entirely. Finding observational counterparts proved difficult, largely because such massive progenitors are predicted to be exceedingly rare in the modern, higher-metallicity universe.

The observational landscape shifted significantly with the discovery of SN 2007bi, a luminous and slowly evolving transient located in a metal-poor dwarf galaxy ($Z \approx Z_\odot/3$). Initial analyses posited SN 2007bi as the first compelling PISN candidate [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract). The event's extreme luminosity and extended light curve were remarkably consistent with theoretical models of a PISN powered by the radioactive decay of a massive iron-peak yield. Specifically, the data were fitted to a progenitor with an exploding core mass of $M_{\rm core} \sim 100 M_\odot$, which synthesized $>3 M_\odot$ of radioactive $^{56}$Ni during the explosion [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract). 

However, candidate classification based solely on slow light-curve evolution is heavily debated. The PISN interpretation of SN 2007bi and similar slowly fading superluminous supernovae was directly challenged by the discovery of subsequent transients that exhibited fast rise times and blue colors—features incompatible with the extended diffusion times expected from the massive ejecta of true PISNe [Nicholl et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.502..346N/abstract). Instead, these light curves could be accurately reproduced by models invoking $10-16 M_\odot$ of ejecta energized by the spin-down of a central magnetar [Nicholl et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.502..346N/abstract). This tension has prompted a paradigm shift in [[supernova-physics|Supernova Physics]]: it is now widely recognized that magnetars and circumstellar material (CSM) interaction can mimic the photometric signatures of PISNe. Consequently, the true nature of SN 2007bi remains explicitly unresolved in the literature.

Despite the ambiguity surrounding earlier candidates, the search for definitive PISNe has continued, yielding increasingly extreme events. For example, the exceptionally energetic transient SN 2016aps radiated $\gtrsim 5 \times 10^{51}$ erg and exhibited narrow hydrogen emission lines indicative of dense CSM interaction [Nicholl et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..893N/abstract). While initially evaluated as a potential PISN, its energy budget and massive CSM envelope have made it a prime candidate for a non-terminal *pulsational* pair-instability event (discussed further below).

Ultimately, the observational history of PISN candidates is characterized by a persistent debate over the physical mechanisms powering the most luminous stellar explosions. Because engine-driven mimics and interaction-powered transients can replicate the highly luminous and slowly evolving light curves once thought to be the unique fingerprints of a PISN, unambiguous candidate identification remains elusive. Current estimates constrain the local rate of true pair-instability events to be exceptionally low, perhaps $< 6 \times 10^{-6}$ times the standard core-collapse rate [Nicholl et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.502..346N/abstract).

## PISNe in the Early Universe

Pair-instability supernovae (PISNe) are intrinsically linked to the first generations of star formation in the early universe. Theoretical models predict that pristine Population III (Pop III) stars with initial masses between $140 M_\odot$ and $260 M_\odot$ end their lives as PISNe [Pan et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.2701P/abstract). Because stellar mass loss via radiatively driven winds is heavily suppressed in zero- and low-metallicity environments ($Z < Z_\odot/3$), these early stars can retain the massive oxygen cores necessary to encounter the pair-production instability [Kozyreva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.146K/abstract).

During the epoch of reionization ($z \ge 6$), Pop III stars and their subsequent PISNe may have contributed significantly to the cosmic ionizing photon budget. If the Pop III initial mass function (IMF) was sufficiently top-heavy, PISNe would be numerous enough to contaminate the bright end of the high-redshift galaxy luminosity function, presenting a prime observational target for infrared observatories capable of probing this era [Pan et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.2701P/abstract). However, the exact peak of cosmic PISN rates remains sensitive to model assumptions. Recent cosmological simulations incorporating galactic chemical inhomogeneities suggest that the peak redshift of PISN formation may be delayed from $z \approx 6$ down to $z \approx 3.5$, as metal-poor pockets of gas continue to produce massive progenitors even as global galactic metallicities rise [Briel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.3907B/abstract).

Because PISNe disperse their entire nucleosynthetic yield into the surrounding medium, their unique chemical signatures—such as massive $\alpha$-element yields and pronounced odd-even variances—are actively sought to trace the first generation of star formation. As detailed in the nucleosynthesis discussion above, candidate PISN enrichment patterns have been observed in both high-redshift quasar environments and local extremely metal-poor halo stars [Yoshii et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937...61Y/abstract), [Koutsouridou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962L..26K/abstract).

In early protogalaxies ($M \sim 10^7 M_\odot$), the immense kinetic energy of a PISN dictates the efficiency of metal mixing and mechanical feedback. Explosions with energies $E \gtrsim 5 \times 10^{52}$ erg can eject substantial fractions of the protogalactic gas, though the heavy elements often remain trapped in a hot, rarefied cavity. Conversely, less energetic events ($E \lesssim 3 \times 10^{52}$ erg) undergo cavity collapse, which mixes metals much more efficiently into the central regions of the protogalaxy [Vasiliev et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARep...56..895V/abstract).

The expanding PISN shock wave also plays a critical role in regulating cosmic star formation. The compression of ambient gas by the PISN-driven shell can trigger gravitational fragmentation. Unlike standard [[core-collapse-supernovae|Core-Collapse Supernovae]], which require ambient densities of $n_0 > 300$ cm$^{-3}$ to induce shell fragmentation, the massive energy injection of a PISN can trigger star formation at significantly lower densities [Salvaterra et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004NewA...10..113S/abstract). Consequently, Pop III PISNe are thought to have been primary drivers of self-propagating star formation, directly seeding the second generation of low-mass, long-lived, extremely metal-poor stars [Salvaterra et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004NewA...10..113S/abstract).

## Cosmic Rates and Host Environments

The formation of pair-instability supernovae (PISNe) is strongly governed by the metallicity of their host environments. Theoretical models require progenitors with initial masses $M_{\mathrm{ZAMS}} \gtrsim 140 M_{\odot}$, which can only avoid severe mass loss through stellar winds if they form in extremely metal-poor gas, typically $Z < Z_{\odot}/3$ [Kozyreva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.146K/abstract), [Briel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.3907B/abstract). Consequently, the majority of PISNe are expected to arise from Population III stars in the early universe. However, contemporary research shows that the dominant contribution to the cosmic PISN rate actually originates from environments with metallicities between $Z \sim 10^{-3}$ and $10^{-2}$, challenging the classical assumption that they are strictly confined to zero-metallicity regimes [Gabrielli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.534..151G/abstract). 

In the local universe, PISNe are exceedingly rare. Their local volumetric rate is estimated to be less than $6 \times 10^{-6}$ times that of standard [[core-collapse-supernovae|Core-Collapse Supernovae]] [Nicholl et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.502..346N/abstract). When they do occur at low redshifts, they are strongly favored to reside in dwarf galaxies, which often retain pristine, unenriched gas. The archetypal PISN candidate, SN 2007bi, was discovered in exactly such a metal-poor dwarf galaxy, indicating that these environments are capable of hosting stars massive enough to trigger the instability [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract). 

Furthermore, high-resolution cosmological simulations utilizing the IllustrisTNG framework demonstrate that massive, globally metal-enriched galaxies can still harbor chemically inhomogeneous regions. These isolated metal-poor pockets allow for the continued formation of PISN progenitors at much lower redshifts than previously assumed [Briel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.3907B/abstract). Accounting for these galactic chemical inhomogeneities fundamentally alters the predicted cosmic rate. When assuming uniform mixing within galaxies, models predict a peak PISN formation rate at a redshift of $z \approx 6$. However, when incorporating the inhomogeneous metallicity distributions from IllustrisTNG, the peak shifts significantly forward in cosmic time to $z \approx 3.5$ [Briel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.3907B/abstract). This delayed peak also increases the local ($z = 0$) PISN rate by over an order of magnitude, raising theoretical estimates from $1.9$ to $29\ \mathrm{Gpc}^{-3}\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ [Briel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.3907B/abstract).

Despite these refined environmental models, the absolute cosmic rate of PISNe remains highly ambiguous. Current theoretical estimates span a staggering seven orders of magnitude due to compounding uncertainties regarding the upper-mass limit of the stellar initial mass function (IMF), the critical core masses required to trigger the instability, and the interplay between the maximum metallicity for PISN progenitors and the dispersion of galaxy metallicity distributions [Gabrielli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.534..151G/abstract). The paucity of unambiguous observational detections is further compounded by the finding that only about $12\%$ of helium PISN progenitors are massive enough to produce super-luminous transient events; the remainder likely result in fainter explosions that elude current time-domain surveys [Briel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.3907B/abstract). Future wide-field space missions, such as the Euclid-Deep survey, are projected to detect $\sim 13.8$ PISNe per year if chemical inhomogeneities are fully accounted for, providing a critical test for these theoretical rates and the true metallicity dependence of the most massive stars [Briel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.3907B/abstract).

## Distinction from Pulsational Pair-Instability

True pair-instability supernovae (PISNe) strictly require a massive oxygen core capable of generating runaway [[thermonuclear-explosions|Thermonuclear explosions]] energetic enough to exceed the star's gravitational binding energy. These events, typically arising from stars with initial zero-age main sequence (ZAMS) masses of $140 \lesssim M_{\rm ZAMS} \lesssim 260 M_\odot$, result in the complete disruption of the progenitor star, leaving no compact remnant behind [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract), [Kozyreva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.146K/abstract). 

However, for slightly less massive progenitors—stars with initial masses in the transitional range of $100 \lesssim M_{\rm ZAMS} \lesssim 140 M_\odot$ (corresponding to helium cores $\gtrsim 50 M_\odot$)—the pair-production instability operates differently. In this regime, the conversion of pressure-supporting photons into electron-positron pairs induces a violent contraction and subsequent thermonuclear ignition, but the resulting energy release is insufficient to unbind the entire star. Instead, the star undergoes a pulsational pair-instability supernova (PPISN) [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract), [Nicholl et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..893N/abstract).

During a PPISN, the star experiences one or more severe mass loss episodes driven by thermonuclear pulses. Following violent mass ejections, these stars ultimately end up as iron-core-collapse supernovae [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract). Consequently, unlike true PISNe, a PPISN ends its life as one of the traditional [[core-collapse-supernovae|Core-Collapse Supernovae]].

The observational signatures of PPISNe are largely dictated by the collision between the final supernova ejecta and the massive circumstellar medium (CSM) expelled during the prior pulsational phases. The shock interaction converts kinetic energy into thermal radiation with remarkable efficiency, often producing a superluminous transient. A notable observational candidate for this mechanism is the Type IIn event SN 2016aps. This extremely energetic transient radiated $\gtrsim 5 \times 10^{51}$ erg of thermal energy and had an estimated total mass (ejecta plus CSM) exceeding $50$ to $100 M_\odot$ [Nicholl et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..893N/abstract). The total kinetic energy of the event was constrained to be $\gtrsim 10^{52}$ erg. The requirement of both a hyper-energetic explosion and a massive, pre-existing dense CSM makes the pulsational pair-instability mechanism a leading physical interpretation for such exceptionally bright superluminous supernovae [Nicholl et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..893N/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite robust theoretical predictions that stars with initial masses between $140 \ M_\odot$ and $260 \ M_\odot$ will undergo total disruption, the unambiguous observational verification of pair-instability supernovae (PISNe) remains a major open question. High-luminosity, slow-fading events such as SN 2007bi have been heavily cited as primary PISN candidates, with light curves and spectra consistent with core masses $\sim 100 \ M_\odot$ and immense $^{56}\rm{Ni}$ yields of $>3 \ M_\odot$ [Gal-Yam et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..624G/abstract). However, the literature is currently in explicit conflict regarding this interpretation. Counter-studies demonstrate that the blue colors and relatively fast rise times of many slowly fading superluminous supernovae may be better explained by magnetar-energized ejecta rather than radioactive nickel [Nicholl et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.502..346N/abstract). Consequently, distinguishing true PISNe from highly energetic [[core-collapse-supernovae|Core-Collapse Supernovae]] or magnetar mimics remains a critical unresolved challenge. To help resolve these current classification ambiguities, observations of gamma-ray lines from radioactive decay (like $^{56}\mathrm{Ni}$) offer a diagnostic tool independent of the thermal state of the debris [Isern et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NewAR..9201606I/abstract).

A fundamental theoretical uncertainty lies in the predicted cosmic rate of PISNe, which current estimates place anywhere across a seven-order-of-magnitude range. This vast uncertainty is heavily dependent on stellar modeling assumptions, particularly the physical criteria for pair-production instability and the upper mass limit of the stellar initial mass function [Gabrielli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.534..151G/abstract). While PISNe are predominantly associated with zero-metallicity Population III stars in the early universe, nucleosynthesis and stellar evolution models indicate they can also occur at metallicities up to $Z \lesssim Z_\odot/3$ [Kozyreva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.146K/abstract). If galactic chemical inhomogeneities are modeled, metal-poor pockets of gas in otherwise enriched galaxies could permit the formation of PISN progenitors at significantly lower redshifts, potentially shifting the peak PISN formation epoch from $z = 6$ down to $z = 3.5$ [Briel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.3907B/abstract). 

Upcoming observational campaigns are poised to address these ambiguities. Because PISNe result in complete [[thermonuclear-explosions|Thermonuclear explosions]] that completely obliterate the progenitor, they are predicted to have important implications for the black hole mass spectrum [Kozyreva et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...566A.146K/abstract), [Gabrielli et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.534..151G/abstract). Detecting high-redshift PISNe with JWST will provide direct constraints on the Population III initial mass function and help clarify the contribution of very massive stars to the cosmic budget of ionizing photons during the epoch of reionization [Pan et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.2701P/abstract). 

Closer to home, wide-field time-domain surveys are expected to finally uncover a definitive population of these transients. For instance, the Euclid-Deep survey is predicted to detect between $\approx 7$ and $83$ PISNe over its six-year mission lifetime, depending on whether chemical inhomogeneities are accounted for [Briel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.3907B/abstract). Notably, recent theoretical light curve modeling suggests that only $\approx 12\%$ of helium PISN progenitors are massive enough to power a superluminous event [Briel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.3907B/abstract). This implies that the elusive nature of PISNe may partially be due to many events being fainter than historically anticipated, requiring updated search strategies to properly distinguish them from standard [[type-ii-supernovae|Type II Supernovae]] in future surveys.
