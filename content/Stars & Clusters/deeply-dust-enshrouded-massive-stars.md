#
**Deeply dust-enshrouded massive stars** represent a broad demographic of high-mass ($M \gtrsim 8 M_\odot$) astrophysical objects that are hidden behind extreme optical extinction. This profound obscuration originates from two primary mechanisms: dense, natal interstellar material surrounding [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] at the onset of their lifespans, or opaque, self-produced circumstellar envelopes generated during late-stage [[mass-loss-processes|Mass Loss Processes]]. Because thick dust efficiently absorbs short-wavelength ultraviolet and optical stellar radiation and re-radiates it thermally, these stars universally exhibit extreme mid-to-far infrared excesses. Consequently, multi-wavelength facilities like the Spitzer Space Telescope and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) are essential to penetrate the obscuring material and accurately characterize these populations—a methodological shift in observational astronomy built upon early infrared space surveys and foundational optical classifications [Conti et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...274..302C/abstract). 

Within the broader context of [[stellar-formation-and-protostars|Stellar formation and protostars]], the deeply embedded phase of a massive star is exceptionally brief. Unlike their low-mass counterparts, massive protostars have accretion timescales that exceed their Kelvin-Helmholtz contraction times. As a result, they often reach the [[zero-age-main-sequence|Zero Age Main Sequence]] and initiate core hydrogen burning while still actively accreting from their natal molecular cloud cores. At the opposite evolutionary extreme, [[evolved-stars|Evolved stars]] such as [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]] and late-type [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]] experience transient, terminal phases of episodic mass loss that create thick dusty shrouds. These short-lived, self-obscured epochs—often lasting less than $10^4$ years—are critically important for understanding the final stages of stellar evolution, as they have been identified as the elusive [[supernova-progenitors|Supernova progenitors]] for intermediate-luminosity optical outbursts and other [[exotic-transients|Exotic transients]] [Thompson et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705.1364T/abstract).

The study of dust-enshrouded massive stars is deeply intertwined with extreme galactic environments and global galaxy evolution. In the high-density Central Molecular Zone of the Milky Way, populations of isolated, hyper-luminous enshrouded stars challenge standard models of clustered star formation [Oskinova et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3357O/abstract), though extraordinary compact [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] of massive stars also exist in these dense regions [Serabyn et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.394..448S/abstract). On extragalactic scales, immense populations of these obscured young stars drive the bolometric output of [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|Dust-Obscured Star Formation and Galaxies]], contributing a significant fraction of the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|cosmic star-formation rate density]] at high redshift. The intense radiation and chemical yields provided by these stars are highly sensitive to their host environment. Recent observations of extreme, dust-rich starbursts suggest they may produce "top-heavy" stellar [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|initial mass functions]] (IMFs) [Zhang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.558..260Z/abstract). This challenges textbook paradigms and contrasts with theories of the integrated galactic initial mass function (IGIMF), which predict that composite stellar populations should actually have steeper mass functions with fewer massive stars [Kroupa et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IAUS..227..423K/abstract). These variations directly impact theoretical models of [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] and the chemical evolution of the Universe.

This article provides a comprehensive overview of the physical properties, observational techniques, and cosmological impacts of deeply dust-enshrouded massive stars. It details the methodologies used to derive complex infrared extinction laws and explores the extreme environments hosting these stars, from the Milky Way's center to distant [[low-metallicity-galaxies|Low-metallicity galaxies]]. Finally, the article surveys current open questions in the field, including the precise binary fraction of massive [[young-stellar-objects|Young stellar objects]], whether isolated massive stars form *in situ* or are dynamically ejected [[runaway-stars|Runaway stars]], and the fundamental physical triggers responsible for the short-lived, massive dust-producing eruptions that immediately precede core collapse.

## Historical Development of Infrared Surveys

The systematic census of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] has historically been severely biased by their propensity to form and evolve in regions of high column density. Because massive stars possess relatively short main-sequence lifetimes, they often remain deeply embedded within their natal molecular clouds. Furthermore, the most luminous evolved phases, such as [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]] and [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]], frequently experience intense episodic mass loss that produces opaque circumstellar dust shells. Consequently, optical surveys miss up to 50% of OB stars in active star-forming complexes and are entirely blind to the earliest stages of [[massive-star-formation|Massive Star Formation]] [Marston et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013msao.confE.167M/abstract), [Povich et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838...61P/abstract). 

The transition to the infrared era fundamentally altered the demographic study of these populations. Early space-based facilities like the Infrared Astronomical Satellite (IRAS) provided the first all-sky mid- and far-infrared maps, discovering entire classes of dust-obscured phenomena where immense ultraviolet flux from young stars is reprocessed into the infrared by dust. Data from IRAS also led to unexpected discoveries outside the Galactic plane, such as the identification of faint, dust-enshrouded carbon stars at high Galactic latitudes and large distances (20-50 kpc), suggesting the presence of such deeply obscured objects even within the Galactic halo [Cutri et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989AJ.....97..866C/abstract).

A critical breakthrough for resolved stellar populations came with the Spitzer Space Telescope. The Spitzer GLIMPSE (Galactic Legacy Infrared Mid-Plane Survey Extraordinaire) survey utilized the Infrared Array Camera (IRAC) to map the Galactic plane, identifying hundreds of massive [[young-stellar-objects|Young stellar objects]] and Extended Green Objects (EGOs) characterized by active outflows and rapid accretion [Chen et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJS..206....9C/abstract). Utilizing the 3.6 $\mu$m and 4.5 $\mu$m IRAC bands, researchers were also able to identify the extremely rare, self-obscured progenitors of SN 2008S-like transients, determining them to be dusty massive stars with bolometric luminosities of $L \approx 5 \times 10^4 L_{\odot}$ [Thompson et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705.1364T/abstract), [Khan et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...715.1094K/abstract). Complementary observations with the Multiband Imaging Photometer for Spitzer (MIPS) at 24 $\mu$m resolved the complex circumstellar dust shells around the Galaxy's most luminous [[evolved-stars|Evolved stars]], such as the isolated Wolf-Rayet star WR 102ka [Barniske et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...486..971B/abstract).

Complementary to these space-based missions, the Red MSX Source (RMS) survey provided a systematic ground- and space-based follow-up strategy. By surveying approximately 2,000 color-selected candidates, the RMS survey was able to robustly identify massive young stellar objects throughout the Galaxy and trace massive star formation on a galactic scale [Hoare et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IAUS..227..370H/abstract).

At longer wavelengths, far-infrared and submillimeter surveys have been instrumental in tracing the cold dust components. The APEX Telescope Large Area Survey of the Galaxy (ATLASGAL) mapped the inner Galaxy at 870 $\mu$m, systematically cataloging the dense clumps that host massive star formation. By cross-matching ATLASGAL clumps with infrared surveys, astronomers identified $\sim 1700$ deeply embedded massive stars, confirming that massive star formation is strongly correlated with the highest column density regions ($N_{\rm H} > 10^{23} \text{ cm}^{-2}$) of centrally condensed clumps [Urquhart et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1555U/abstract). Simultaneously, the Herschel Space Observatory mapped the dustiest post-main-sequence stars in the Magellanic Clouds, identifying highly enshrouded evolved stars and stellar end-products [Jones et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...811..145J/abstract).

Presently, the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is extending the reach of infrared surveys to extragalactic and low-metallicity environments. JWST's unprecedented sensitivity has enabled the resolution of dusty stellar populations in extremely metal-poor galaxies like I Zw 18 ($Z \approx 0.03 Z_{\odot}$). By identifying dust-enshrouded young stellar objects and evolved massive stars in such pristine environments, JWST provides a critical observational window into the star formation processes that likely dominated the early Universe [Hirschauer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168...23H/abstract).

## Observational Techniques and Extinction Laws

Observing deeply dust-enshrouded [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] requires operating at near-infrared (NIR) and mid-infrared (MIR) wavelengths, where the optical depth of the circumstellar and interstellar medium is significantly reduced. Searches for these elusive objects heavily utilize photometric color selections. For instance, observations employing the $3.6~\mu\mathrm{m}$ and $4.5~\mu\mathrm{m}$ bands of the Spitzer Space Telescope have been instrumental in cataloging self-obscured massive stars and identifying the rare, highly obscured progenitors of SN 2008S-like transients in nearby galaxies [Khan et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...715.1094K/abstract), [Britavskiy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...584A..33B/abstract). By leveraging both NIR colors, such as $J - K_s$, and MIR photometry (e.g., from the AKARI satellite), observers can isolate the infrared excess characteristic of hot circumstellar dust shells and distinguish between oxygen-rich and carbon-rich dust chemistries [Matsunaga et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018cwla.conf..167M/abstract).

Extracting intrinsic stellar properties from infrared photometry relies critically on the application of accurate extinction laws. However, the exact form of the extinction curve often varies significantly in dense regions of [[deeply-embedded-star-formation|Deeply embedded star formation]]. Studies utilizing combined near-IR and mid-IR photometry have derived robust $1\text{--}10~\mu\mathrm{m}$ empirical extinction laws specifically for [[o-type-stars|O-type stars]] located in the solar neighborhood [Maíz Apellániz et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015hsa8.conf..402M/abstract). Concurrently, tailored optical extinction laws have been derived to provide better fits for specific extreme environments like the 30 Doradus region [Maíz Apellániz et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015hsa8.conf..402M/abstract). In heavily obscured sightlines towards massive [[star-clusters|Star clusters]], observations can reveal anomalous color excesses; for example, main-sequence fitting in the Galactic cluster RSGC2 yields $E(J-K) = 1.6$~mag, indicating either an unusual local extinction law or a much lower-than-expected near-infrared extinction [Froebrich et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IJAA....3..161F/abstract). If the applied extinction law does not account for these local dust properties, the derived absolute magnitudes and stellar masses may be subject to severe systematic errors. The literature remains ambiguous regarding whether a universal infrared extinction law can be broadly applied to such extreme environments. 

To robustly characterize heavily extinguished sources, researchers frequently employ spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting. By matching multi-band photometry from $1~\mu\mathrm{m}$ to $8~\mu\mathrm{m}$ to reddened stellar atmosphere models, it is possible to break degeneracies between intrinsic stellar color and interstellar reddening [Povich et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838...61P/abstract). For embedded [[young-stellar-objects|Young stellar objects]], SEDs are typically fitted with radiative transfer models that incorporate massive accreting envelopes and circumstellar disks. This allows for the extraction of evolutionary stages, bolometric luminosities, and fractional dust masses [Jones et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490..832J/abstract). Similar SED modeling is used to estimate mass-loss rates for evolved, dust-enshrouded [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]] and [[asymptotic-giant-branch|Asymptotic giant branch]] stars [Abdollahi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...948...63A/abstract).

The deployment of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has revolutionized the observational landscape for deeply enshrouded populations. With the unprecedented spatial resolution and sensitivity of its NIRCam and MIRI instruments, it is now possible to perform detailed photometric and SED analyses on dust-enshrouded massive stars in highly crowded and distant extragalactic environments. For example, deep JWST multi-band imaging (ranging from the F115W to F1800W filters) of the extremely metal-poor galaxy I Zw 18 has enabled the direct identification of dusty stellar populations at metallicities of roughly $3\%~Z_\odot$. This capability probes the formation and mass-loss mechanisms of massive stars under conditions analogous to the early Universe, well beyond the reach of previous observatories [Hirschauer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168...23H/abstract), [Hirschauer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240306980H/abstract).

## Deeply Embedded Protostellar Populations

The earliest stages of [[massive-star-formation|Massive Star Formation]] occur within the dense, self-gravitating cores of giant molecular clouds. Under the standard theoretical paradigm, these [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] form via the gravitational collapse of dense gas. Unlike their low-mass counterparts, massive protostars have formation timescales that exceed their Kelvin-Helmholtz timescale, 
$$t_{\rm KH} = \frac{G M_\star^2}{R L}$$
where $M_\star$, $R$, and $L$ are the mass, radius, and luminosity of the star. Consequently, these objects ignite central nuclear burning while still accreting gas from their natal envelopes, producing powerful radiative and mechanical feedback that strongly influences the surrounding environment.

The initial, pre-stellar phase of this process is exceptionally brief. Submillimeter surveys, such as ATLASGAL, have demonstrated that the most massive starless clumps—characterized by peak column densities exceeding $1 \times 10^{23} \text{ cm}^{-2}$—have lifetimes on the order of only $(6 \pm 5) \times 10^4$ years [Tackenberg et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...540A.113T/abstract). Observations of [[deeply-embedded-star-formation|Deeply embedded star formation]] indicate that the overarching clump structure is largely established prior to the onset of stellar birth and changes minimally as the embedded object evolves toward the main sequence [Urquhart et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1555U/abstract). Current kinematic data suggest that strong self-gravity drives global, large-scale collapse in these regions, which fragment as they accumulate mass [Csengeri et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...601A..60C/abstract).

Because optical and near-infrared light is completely extinguished by high dust column densities, [[stellar-formation-and-protostars|protostars]] in this regime are primarily investigated through mid-infrared, submillimeter, and radio diagnostics. For instance, far-infrared continuum mapping of the NGC 3576 filament has successfully identified massive Class 0 and Class I protostellar sources that are the precursors to stars with masses up to 50 $M_\odot$ [André et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...490L..27A/abstract). Infrared surveys have additionally identified Extended Green Objects (EGOs) as robust candidates for massive [[young-stellar-objects|Young stellar objects]] at the earliest evolutionary stages. EGOs exhibit rapid accretion and are strongly associated with active outflows; approximately 70% of these objects are coincident with 95 GHz Class I methanol masers, a key tracer of shocked outflowing gas [Chen et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJS..206....9C/abstract). Further cross-matching of radio and submillimeter surveys has cataloged roughly 1700 embedded massive stars across the inner Galaxy, revealing a tight linear correlation between the host clump mass and the bolometric luminosity of the protostellar system [Urquhart et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1555U/abstract).

The exact physical locations and triggering mechanisms of massive star formation within [[star-forming-regions-and-cores|Star-Forming Regions and Cores]] remain a subject of active debate in the literature. While embedded massive stars are generally highly correlated with the regions of highest column density [Urquhart et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1555U/abstract), some multi-wavelength surveys challenge the simplest monolithic cloud collapse models. For instance, observations in the Large Magellanic Cloud indicate that massive stars do not typically form at the absolute centers or the highest column density peaks of their parent clouds when resolved at $\sim 6 \text{ pc}$ scales, but instead cluster over multiple generations [Ochsendorf et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...43O/abstract). Similarly, observations of the Pismis 24 cluster in the Galactic star-forming region NGC 6357 show evidence of extensive sub-clustering and potential triggered star formation propagating into its surrounding molecular clumps [Massi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...573A..95M/abstract). Furthermore, extreme environments like the Galactic Center's Central Molecular Zone (CMZ) reveal significant spatial variability in star formation efficiency. While some massive clouds in the CMZ remain surprisingly quiescent, others host rich populations of deeply embedded protostellar candidates that are actively traced by water masers and dense dust cores [Lu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..322...99L/abstract). Furthermore, recent JWST and ground-based findings suggest that the timescales of this phase may be strongly environment-dependent, revealing actively accreting young stellar objects in the Small Magellanic Cloud and the outer Galaxy at ages significantly older than previously thought, up to 10-20 Myr [Biazzo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025spf..confE...5B/abstract).

## Massive Stars in the Galactic Center and Central Molecular Zone

The Milky Way's Galactic Center and the surrounding Central Molecular Zone (CMZ)—spanning the inner $\sim 500$ pc of the Galaxy—host over 10% of the known [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] in the Galaxy [Figer et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003IAUS..212..487F/abstract). Because of the extreme optical extinction along the line of sight, the massive stellar population of this region is heavily dust-enshrouded and must be probed via infrared and X-ray observations [Mauerhan et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...725..188M/abstract), [Lu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..322...99L/abstract). 

The CMZ is characterized by extreme physical conditions, including molecular clouds with masses of $\sim 10^5 M_\odot$, high turbulence, and strong tidal shear from the supermassive black hole Sgr A$^*$ [Lu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..322...99L/abstract). While standard models previously suggested that these tidal forces would disrupt molecular clouds and suppress star formation, recent hydrodynamic models demonstrate that the compression of gas clumps on highly eccentric orbits can drive local densities above the tidal limit, enabling *in situ* massive star formation [Jalali et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.1205J/abstract). Indeed, radio and submillimeter surveys have revealed populations of deeply embedded [[young-stellar-objects|Young stellar objects]] and water masers in CMZ clouds such as Sgr C and the 20 km s$^{-1}$ cloud, confirming ongoing, enshrouded star formation [Lu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..322...99L/abstract).

Historically, massive star formation in the Galactic Center was thought to be primarily confined to three extraordinarily massive [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] formed within the last 5 Myr, which include the [[arches-cluster|Arches cluster]] and the [[quintuplet-cluster|Quintuplet cluster]] [Figer et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003IAUS..212..487F/abstract). However, narrowband Paschen-$\alpha$ and $K$-band spectroscopic surveys have since uncovered a substantial population of isolated hot, evolved stars, including [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]] and [[o-type-stars|O-type stars]] [Mauerhan et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...725..188M/abstract), [Clark et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...649A..43C/abstract). There is an active debate in the literature regarding the origin of these isolated stars: while some are likely "runaways" ejected from known clusters via dynamical interactions, others appear to have formed in isolation [Oskinova et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3357O/abstract).

The dense environment of the Galactic center and the wider bulge also preserves the chemical and dynamic signatures of the earliest massive stars. For instance, fast-rotating metal-poor massive stars, or "spinstars," are thought to be responsible for the anomalous s-process abundances observed in the oldest stars of the Galactic bulge [Chiappini et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Natur.472..454C/abstract). Furthermore, as massive clusters form in these high-density regions, they may trap older galactic field stars; this process provides a theoretical framework for the complex stellar populations observed in massive clusters such as Omega Centauri [Fellhauer et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.372..338F/abstract).

A premier example of a deeply enshrouded, isolated object in this region is WR 102ka, widely known as the Peony nebula star. Spectroscopic modeling confirms it is one of the most luminous stars in the Milky Way, classified as a late-type nitrogen-rich Wolf-Rayet (WN) star [Barniske et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...486..971B/abstract). WR 102ka is surrounded by a compact, peony-shaped circumstellar nebula prominently visible at $24\ \mu\mathrm{m}$; this represents the first reported presence of dust in the close vicinity of a WN star [Barniske et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...486..971B/abstract). Crucially, high-resolution integral-field spectroscopy has revealed no associated star cluster around WR 102ka, strongly supporting the hypothesis that some of the most massive stars in the Galaxy can form *in situ* outside of massive clusters [Oskinova et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3357O/abstract).

Collectively, the clustered and isolated populations within the CMZ yield a census of $\gtrsim 320$ spectroscopically classified massive stars that are expected to undergo core-collapse within the next $\sim 20$ Myr [Clark et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...649A..43C/abstract). Many of these stars are currently undergoing short-lived, deeply dust-enshrouded evolutionary phases, making the CMZ a critical laboratory for understanding the episodic mass-loss and late-stage evolution of [[supernova-progenitors|Supernova progenitors]] in high-density environments.

## Evolved Dusty Massive Stars: RSGs and WR Stars

As massive stars evolve off the main sequence and traverse the [[hertzsprung-russell-diagram|Hertzsprung-Russell diagram]], they experience phases of intense [[mass-loss-processes|Mass Loss Processes]]. In certain evolved states, particularly as [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]] (RSGs), [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]] (WRs), and [[luminous-blue-variables|Luminous blue variables]] (LBVs), the expelled material can condense into thick circumstellar dust shells. This deeply enshrouded phase is exceptionally brief but critically dictates the star's terminal evolution and interaction with its surrounding medium. Theoretical models emphasize that presupernova massive star evolution is extraordinarily sensitive to initial rotation and binarity, and observations of diverse Wolf-Rayet populations provide critical clues for modeling these complex, branching paths toward core-collapse [Langer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..107L/abstract).

Observations of RSGs across the Local Group reveal that their maximum bolometric luminosity remains remarkably constant at $\log(L/L_\odot) \approx 5.5$, independent of host metallicity down to $[\mathrm{Fe/H}] \approx -1$ dex [Britavskiy et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...631A..95B/abstract). To emphasize the statistical scale of these populations compared to the Milky Way, recent wide-field censuses have cataloged approximately 6400 red supergiants in M31 and 2850 in M33 [Massey et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....161...79M/abstract). During their terminal stages, some RSGs experience extreme episodic mass loss, becoming completely obscured at optical wavelengths. These dust-enshrouded stars have been identified as the [[supernova-progenitors|Supernova progenitors]] for a specific class of intermediate-luminosity optical transients, such as SN 2008S and the 2008 NGC 300 transient [Thompson et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705.1364T/abstract), [Khan et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...715.1094K/abstract). These progenitors exhibit bolometric luminosities of $\approx 5 \times 10^4 L_\odot$ and extreme mid-infrared excesses. Galaxy-wide surveys indicate that such heavily obscured RSGs are exceedingly rare, comprising $\lesssim 10^{-4}$ of the massive star population. This extreme rarity constrains the duration of the dust-enshrouded phase to $< 10^4$ years prior to explosion or eruption [Thompson et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705.1364T/abstract). However, the literature remains conflicted on whether these transients represent true terminal electron-capture supernovae of $\sim 8-12 M_\odot$ stars or non-terminal giant eruptions of more massive stars.

While dust formation is traditionally associated with the cool atmospheres of RSGs, [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]]—particularly those of the late carbon sequence (WCL)—are also prolific dust producers. The Galactic Center, a region of extreme extinction, hosts a substantial population of isolated, heavily obscured WR stars that are identifiable only via their Paschen-$\alpha$ emission excesses in the infrared [Mauerhan et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...725..188M/abstract). Among the most extreme examples is the WN-type star WR 102ka (the Peony nebula star), which ranks among the most luminous stars in the Milky Way. Observations of WR 102ka and WR 102c have revealed compact, infrared-bright dusty nebulae and pure-rotational transitions of molecular hydrogen in their close vicinity, demonstrating that even hot, hydrogen-depleted WN stars can be intimately associated with dense circumstellar dust and gas [Barniske et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...486..971B/abstract).

[[luminous-blue-variables|Luminous blue variables]] represent another transitional, dust-producing phase for the most massive stars. Characterized by giant eruptions analogous to $\eta$ Carinae, LBVs eject massive amounts of processed material that subsequently cools and forms thick dusty envelopes. These giant eruptions are sometimes mistakenly observed as Type IIn supernovae (often termed "supernova impostors"), where the most massive stars experience cataclysmic mass loss but actually survive the event [van Dyk et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..332...47V/abstract). Despite ongoing multi-wavelength surveys, the exact physical triggers driving these giant eruptions and the subsequent episodic mass loss remain poorly constrained, particularly regarding how dust production scales with the metallicity of the host galaxy [Bonanos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..77B/abstract). In metal-poor environments, some evolved massive stars may skip a luminous supernova phase altogether and collapse directly into massive black holes, a pathway that is a viable engine for the ultra-luminous X-ray sources (ULXs) observed in metal-poor galaxies [Mapelli et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AN....332..414M/abstract).

## The Impact of Metallicity on Dust Production

The ambient metallicity of a host galaxy profoundly influences the evolution, mass-loss mechanisms, and dust production of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]]. Historically, it was uncertain whether robust dust condensation could occur in metal-poor environments where the global dust-to-gas ratio is intrinsically low. However, recent observations have confirmed the presence of deeply dust-enshrouded massive stars even in the most chemically pristine local environments. For instance, in the blue compact dwarf galaxy I Zw 18, which has an oxygen abundance of only $\approx 3\%$ of the solar value, infrared imaging has revealed heavily dust-enshrouded [[young-stellar-objects|Young stellar objects]] and dusty [[evolved-stars|Evolved stars]], demonstrating that massive stars can form and produce dust in conditions analogous to the early Universe [Hirschauer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168...23H/abstract), [Hirschauer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240306980H/abstract).

Across the Local Group, mid-infrared and far-infrared surveys have cataloged luminous, dust-enshrouded [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] in various dwarf irregular galaxies. Observations of galaxies such as Sextans A, WLM, and the Magellanic Clouds have identified extreme, self-obscured [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]] and [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]] [Jones et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...811..145J/abstract), [Britavskiy et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...584A..33B/abstract). Moving to even more extreme environments, researchers have identified OBA-type stars and heavily enshrouded red supergiant candidates in the Sagittarius Dwarf Irregular Galaxy, representing potentially the most metal-poor massive stars identified to date within the Local Group [Garcia et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474L..66G/abstract). Furthermore, the galactic environment and gravitational interactions actively shape the distribution of these populations; recent analyses of massive star candidates in the Small Magellanic Cloud (SMC) reveal that massive star superstructures exhibit kinematic evidence of tidal stretching or ram pressure from interaction with the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC), rather than simple rotation [Nakano et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250212251N/abstract). 

In these low-metallicity regimes, the reduction in line-driven stellar wind mass-loss rates alters stellar angular momentum evolution, resulting in a substantially higher fraction of rapidly rotating OBe stars, which can comprise up to $30\%$ of the massive stellar population [Schootemeijer et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...667A.100S/abstract). Indeed, observations of early-type stars in the Magellanic Clouds provide clear evidence for faster stellar rotational velocities and reduced stellar wind mass-loss rates compared to their Galactic analogs [Evans et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IAUS..256..325E/abstract). This faster rotation facilitates enhanced internal mixing; specifically, surface nitrogen enrichment in [[o-type-stars|O-type stars]] is observed to be stronger in these metal-poor populations, directly supporting models of rotationally induced chemical mixing [Martins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A..31M/abstract). Despite these evolutionary shifts, the maximum bolometric luminosity of red supergiants appears remarkably invariant to metallicity, plateauing at $\log(L/L_\odot) \approx 5.5$ even down to $[\mathrm{Fe/H}] \approx -1.0$, although their minimum effective temperatures are systematically hotter at lower metallicities [Britavskiy et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...631A..95B/abstract). Because quiescent stellar winds are weaker in metal-poor galaxies, episodic mass-loss events are hypothesized to be a critically important mechanism for generating circumstellar dust and driving [[chemical-abundances|chemical enrichment]] in these environments, an effect that current surveys are actively working to constrain [Bonanos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..77B/abstract).

At the opposite extreme, massive stars in high-metallicity, dust-rich starburst galaxies exhibit intense star formation localized in dense molecular cores. The high ambient dust-to-gas ratio in these regions deeply obscures both the formation and terminal phases of massive stars, rapidly reprocessing their ultraviolet radiation into the far-infrared. Notably, the chemical environment in these vigorous starbursts may directly influence stellar mass assembly. Isotopic ratio observations of distant, dust-enshrouded starbursts suggest a "top-heavy" initial mass function, implying an overabundance of massive stars compared to the Milky Way, which accelerates local [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] evolution [Zhang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.558..260Z/abstract). 

However, the universality of the initial mass function across different chemical and density environments remains the subject of active and unresolved debate in the literature. While dusty starbursts show strong evidence of top-heavy distributions, ultra-deep spectroscopy of massive quiescent galaxies at $z \sim 0.7$ indicates a "bottom-heavy" mass function dominated by low-mass stars [Cheng et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260120864C/abstract). This tension highlights that the relationship between galactic metallicity, dust enshrouding, and the resulting mass spectrum of massive stars is highly complex and cannot be entirely generalized by standard models derived solely from the Milky Way.

## Deeply Buried Starbursts and Galaxy Evolution

In both the local Universe and at high redshift, the most intense periods of stellar mass assembly occur within [[dust-obscured-star-formation-and-galaxies|Dust-Obscured Star Formation and Galaxies]]. During these global starburst events, the prodigious ultraviolet radiation emitted by nascent massive stars is entirely absorbed by dense molecular clouds and re-emitted in the far-infrared (FIR). In extreme cases, such systems manifest as Ultraluminous Infrared Galaxies (ULIRGs), defined by total infrared luminosities of $L_{\rm FIR} > 10^{12} L_\odot$. While ULIRGs host extreme starbursts, observations demonstrate that the bolometric luminosity of the most deeply buried of these systems is actually dominated by dust-enshrouded active galactic nuclei (AGNs). In fact, AGNs can power $>75\%$ of the bolometric emission in extreme cases, meaning that attributing the bulk of this FIR luminosity solely to massive star formation can lead to severe overestimations of the star formation rate by factors of 2 to 4 [Marshall et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...858...59M/abstract), [McKinney et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...921...55M/abstract).

Populations of [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|High-redshift dusty star-forming galaxies]] are critical components in reconstructing the [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]]. Submillimeter observations have identified extraordinary individual systems at even earlier epochs, such as a dust-obscured 'maximum starburst' galaxy at a redshift of $z = 6.34$, which formed stars at a staggering rate approximately 2,000 times that of the Milky Way [Riechers et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Natur.496..329R/abstract). To understand the fuel for such intense cosmic star formation, broader surveys of typical [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 1-2$) reveal that they are sustained by cold molecular gas fractions averaging approximately 34% at $z=1.2$ and 44% at $z=2.3$ of their total baryonic mass—three to ten times higher than what is observed in present-day massive spiral galaxies [Tacconi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.463..781T/abstract). Within these immense gas reservoirs, Young Massive Clusters (YMCs) act as fundamental building blocks of galaxies; particularly in starburst and interacting systems, YMCs serve as dense nurseries for exotic, heavily enshrouded massive stellar populations [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract). Recent synergies between JWST and ALMA further underscore the importance of this obscured phase, demonstrating that dust-enshrouded star formation contributed approximately $20\%$ of the total cosmic star-formation rate density ($\rho_{\rm SFR}$) at $z \simeq 8$, and potentially up to $5\%$ as early as $z \simeq 10$ [Liu et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026MNRAS.545f1961L/abstract). These rapid, optically thick starbursts are widely considered the primary assembly epoch for the progenitors of present-day [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|Elliptical and Early-Type Galaxies]], a link historically supported by the discovery of extremely bright sub-millimeter sources ($S_{850\mu\mathrm{m}} \sim 10$ mJy) at high redshift [Dunlop et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001dmsi.conf...11D/abstract).

The relationship between starbursts and quiescent galaxies is further illuminated by extreme high-redshift systems such as the 'Jekyll and Hyde' pair at $z = 3.714$. In this gas-rich halo, the galaxy 'Hyde' exhibits intense dust-enshrouded star formation ($\approx 300\ M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$) in close proximity to its massive, recently quenched companion 'Jekyll', highlighting accelerated chemical enhancement and quenching mechanisms in the early Universe [Pérez-González et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1240P/abstract). The rapid, intense nature of these early bursts is evidenced by the chemical signatures they leave behind; for example, the extremely high magnesium-to-iron abundance ratios measured in massive quiescent galaxies at $z=2.1$ constrain their major star-forming phase to a remarkably short timescale of only 0.1 to 0.5 billion years [Kriek et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.540..248K/abstract). Even after these massive galaxies quench to become present-day elliptical and early-type galaxies, they are not entirely devoid of young stars. Residual star formation is ubiquitous in red massive early-type galaxies, where young stars typically comprise a minor but persistent mass fraction of approximately $0.5\%$ over the last 2 Gyr [Salvador-Rusiñol et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..252S/abstract).

A major theoretical complication in studying dust-enshrouded starbursts is the assumption of the stellar initial mass function (IMF). Extrapolating the total star-formation rate from the FIR luminosities generated by obscured massive stars requires assuming an IMF shape, which standard models have historically treated as universal. However, modern literature presents significant tension regarding the IMF in extreme environments. Observations of the $^{13}\mathrm{CO}/\mathrm{C}^{18}\mathrm{O}$ isotopic abundance ratio in dust-enshrouded starbursts at $z \sim 2-3$ provide unambiguous evidence for a "top-heavy" IMF, indicating that these gas-rich, intense environments form a considerably higher fraction of massive stars than ordinary spiral galaxies like the Milky Way [Zhang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.558..260Z/abstract). 

Conversely, the exact evolutionary mapping between these dusty starbursts and their descendants remains ambiguous due to conflicting IMF measurements in later evolutionary stages. Ultra-deep spectroscopy of massive quiescent galaxies at $z \sim 0.7$ suggests a "bottom-heavy" IMF (an excess of low-mass stars), which can increase the inferred total stellar mass of these massive galaxies by factors of $3-4$ [Cheng et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260120864C/abstract). This unresolved tension explicitly challenges the paradigm of a universal IMF, indicating that the relative production of massive stars may be strongly regulated by local gas density, metallicity, and the global galactic environment during the starburst phase.

## Progenitors of Dusty Transients and Supernovae

The final stages of massive star evolution immediately preceding [[stellar-death-hypothesis|stellar death]] or major eruptive events are often characterized by extreme, short-lived phases of episodic mass loss. This intense mass shedding forms dense, optically thick circumstellar envelopes, temporarily transforming the objects into deeply dust-enshrouded [[massive-progenitor-stars|Massive progenitor stars]].

Recent interest in these extreme stellar states has been driven by the discovery of a novel class of obscured luminous transients, typified by SN 2008S and the 2008 transient in NGC 300 [Thompson et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705.1364T/abstract). These [[exotic-transients|Exotic transients]] are optically faint compared to typical supernovae, exhibiting peak absolute visual magnitudes of
$$ -13 \gtrsim M_V \gtrsim -15 $$
alongside narrow Balmer and [Ca II] emission lines in their spectra [Thompson et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705.1364T/abstract). Pre-explosion archival imaging revealed that the [[supernova-progenitors|Supernova progenitors]] were deeply dust-enshrouded stars with highly red mid-infrared colors and modest bolometric luminosities of $L \approx 5 \times 10^4 L_\odot$ [Thompson et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705.1364T/abstract).

A striking tension exists between the observed rate of these transients and the demographics of their quiescent progenitors. The volumetric rate of SN 2008S-like events is relatively high—estimated to be up to $\sim 20\%$ of the local [[core-collapse-supernova-rate|Core-collapse supernova rate]] in star-forming galaxies [Thompson et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705.1364T/abstract). However, systematic mid-infrared surveys of nearby galaxies (such as M33, M81, and NGC 6946) have shown that only a negligible fraction ($\lesssim 10^{-4}$) of the massive star population occupies the same heavily obscured region of color-magnitude space as these progenitors, often numbering no more than one per galaxy at any given time [Thompson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705.1364T/abstract), [Khan et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...715.1094K/abstract). To reconcile the high transient rate with the extreme rarity of the progenitors, the dust-enshrouded phase must be exceedingly brief, lasting $\lesssim 10^4$ years immediately prior to the explosion [Thompson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705.1364T/abstract), [Khan et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...715.1094K/abstract). 

The exact physical nature of these transients and their progenitors remains a subject of active debate in the literature. The inferred luminosities suggest relatively low initial masses in the $8 - 12 M_\odot$ range. One leading hypothesis posits that these events are terminal electron-capture supernovae resulting from the collapse of an O-Ne-Mg core in extreme super-[[asymptotic-giant-branch|Asymptotic giant branch]] stars [Thompson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705.1364T/abstract), [Javadi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.3394J/abstract). Alternatively, the literature is ambiguous as to whether these events represent true terminal explosions; some models suggest they may instead be non-terminal, giant LBV-like eruptions (resembling those of [[luminous-blue-variables|Luminous blue variables]]) triggered by instabilities in dust-enshrouded massive stars [Thompson et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705.1364T/abstract). 

Regardless of the terminal versus eruptive nature of the transients, the existence of SN 2008S and its analogs confirms that [[mass-loss-processes|episodic mass loss]] plays a critical role in the final evolutionary stages of certain massive stars. This process shrouds the progenitors in self-produced dust just millennia before their ultimate disruption, severely complicating the mapping between initial stellar mass and final explosive outcomes [Bonanos et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A..77B/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The physical triggers governing the short-lived, deeply dust-enshrouded phases of evolved [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] remain a primary open question. While these extreme phases are known to immediately precede luminous transients such as SN 2008S and the NGC 300 transient, the literature is conflicted on the fundamental nature of these outbursts. It remains debated whether these events represent terminal electron-capture supernovae of $8-12\ M_{\odot}$ stars, or if they are non-terminal, giant eruptions (similar to Luminous Blue Variables) from more massive progenitors [Thompson et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705.1364T/abstract), [Khan et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...715.1094K/abstract). Determining the intrinsic properties of the most massive objects is inherently fraught; significant challenges remain in establishing accurate masses for the most luminous single [[very-massive-stars|Very massive stars]] due to profound uncertainties in evolutionary models and theoretical bolometric luminosity conversions [Martins et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ASSL..412....9M/abstract). Furthermore, efforts to understand the fundamental properties of these objects must account for internal magnetic fields; however, recent spectropolarimetric surveys have found no formal magnetic field detections in a sample of 41 very massive stars in the Magellanic Clouds, providing significant constraints on their formation mechanisms [Bagnulo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...635A.163B/abstract).

The role of binarity in massive star formation is another critical unknown. The exact binary fraction of massive [[young-stellar-objects|Young stellar objects]] (MYSOs) is notoriously difficult to constrain due to high extinction and crowding. Recent infrared surveys suggest a binary fraction between $49\%$ and $64\%$ at spatial scales of $1000$ to $100,000$ AU, but this figure likely approaches $\sim100\%$ when observational selection biases are removed [Shenton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.8054S/abstract). The ubiquity of close companions complicates theoretical models of [[mass-loss-processes|Mass Loss Processes]] and stellar feedback, as binary interactions and mass transfer are suspected to dominate the production of ionizing radiation [Senchyna et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.6112S/abstract). Additionally, high-energy observations by the INTEGRAL observatory have revealed a major, previously hidden population of highly absorbed Supergiant X-ray Binaries. These wind-fed accretors are intimately linked to massive star-forming regions and present new challenges for models of binary evolution in obscured environments [Chaty et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..422..243C/abstract).

The origin of isolated and field massive stars in extreme environments, particularly within the Galactic Center, is also hotly debated. Infrared observations have uncovered highly luminous, isolated [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]] such as WR 102ka (the Peony nebula star) [Barniske et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...486..971B/abstract). It is currently ambiguous whether such stars formed *in situ*—potentially enabled by the compression of gas clumps on highly eccentric orbits near the supermassive black hole [Jalali et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.1205J/abstract)—or if they are simply runaways that were dynamically ejected from known [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] [Oskinova et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3357O/abstract). On a broader scale, observations indicate that field massive stars comprise 20-25% of the massive stellar population in typical star-forming galaxies, originating both through *in situ* isolated formation and as runaways dynamically ejected from parent clusters [Oey et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASPC..465..431O/abstract).

The universality of the stellar initial mass function (IMF) across different galactic environments is similarly contested. Isotope ratio measurements in high-redshift, heavily dust-obscured starbursts indicate a "top-heavy" IMF populated by an excess of massive stars [Zhang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.558..260Z/abstract). Conversely, ultra-deep spectroscopy of massive quiescent galaxies at $z\sim0.7$ suggests a "bottom-heavy" IMF. This discrepancy increases inferred stellar masses by factors of $3-4$ and creates significant tension with standard galaxy formation models [Cheng et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260120864C/abstract).

Finally, tracing dust-enshrouded star formation into the early Universe represents a major future frontier. The role of chemically homogeneous evolution and rapid rotation in [[low-metallicity-galaxies|Low-metallicity galaxies]] remains ambiguous, though such processes are hypothesized to drive the high-excitation ionizing flux observed in extremely metal-poor systems like I Zw 18 [Szécsi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250621442S/abstract). Resolving these uncertainties requires robust spectral libraries; to this end, the Hubble Space Telescope's ULLYSES program is actively constructing a definitive ultraviolet spectroscopic library to accurately characterize how massive stars evolve and produce ionizing photons across different metallicities [Roman-Duval et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985..109R/abstract). With the advent of JWST MIRI and NIRCam, it is now possible to resolve and detect individual dust-enshrouded YSOs and evolved stars in these primitive analogues [Hirschauer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168...23H/abstract). These observations will be vital for bridging the gap between local starbursts and the elusive Population III stars, ultimately determining how the first generations of massive stars formed, evolved, and enriched the primordial cosmos.
