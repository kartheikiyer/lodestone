## Revised Full Draft


**Young massive clusters (YMCs)** are exceptionally dense, gravitationally bound aggregates of young stars characterized by total stellar masses exceeding $10^4 M_\odot$ and ages younger than $\sim 100$ Myr [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract). With typical half-mass radii of just 1 to 5 parsecs, these compact systems achieve extreme central stellar mass surface densities (often reaching $\sim 10^{11} M_\odot \, \text{kpc}^{-2}$), representing the most intense mode of [[massive-star-formation|Massive Star Formation]] in the local Universe. Historically, the astronomical community divided stellar aggregates into two distinct categories: loosely bound, low-mass [[open-clusters|Open clusters]] residing in galactic disks, and ancient, massive [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] confined to galactic halos. However, high-resolution observations by the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) revealed a prolific population of young, extraordinarily luminous [[super-star-clusters|Super star clusters]] in interacting and [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|starburst galaxies]] [Whitmore et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.12546W/abstract). This discovery effectively bridged the morphological gap between these populations, demonstrating that the formation of dense, massive clusters is a continuous cosmic process driven by high environmental pressure rather than an epoch-specific phenomenon.

The formation and early evolution of YMCs dictate their long-term survival and structural properties. To accumulate immense gas reservoirs before stellar feedback can disperse the natal cloud, YMCs preferentially form in high-pressure environments triggered by gas-rich galaxy mergers, violent colliding flows, and active [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|Starburst Galaxy Evolution]]. Upon formation, YMCs undergo rapid internal dynamical evolution. Within their first few million years, processes such as mass segregation rapidly concentrate [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] toward the cluster core, fundamentally altering the system's gravitational potential well and driving intense radiative and mechanical feedback. This feedback initiates a critical competition between rapid gas exhaustion and violent gas expulsion, determining whether the nascent system remains gravitationally bound or dissolves into the diffuse galactic field through "infant mortality." 

Because their dynamical masses, extreme volume densities, and structural properties closely match those of ancient globulars, YMCs are widely regarded as the contemporary analogues of their evolutionary progenitors [Ho et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...472..600H/abstract). Under this paradigm, an ancient [[globular-clusters|globular cluster]] is essentially an ancient YMC that formed in the extreme environments of the early Universe and successfully survived billions of years of internal dynamical relaxation and external tidal stripping [de Grijs et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0711.3540D/abstract). Recently, this evolutionary link has been bolstered by direct observation: JWST has resolved gravitationally bound YMCs in highly magnified [[very-young-galaxies|high-redshift galaxies]] ($z > 6$), confirming that these extreme clusters were prolific during the epoch of reionization and played a disproportionately large role in contributing ionizing photons to the intergalactic medium.

Despite extensive observational and theoretical progress, YMCs serve as critical laboratories for several fiercely debated open questions in astrophysics. Chief among these is the universality of the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]; while YMCs in extreme environments like [[the-galactic-center|The Galactic Center]] appear to host a top-heavy stellar population, it remains unclear if this represents a genuine environmental dependence, a deterministic self-regulated formation process, or merely an artifact of rapid dynamical mass segregation. Additionally, researchers are actively investigating the origins of multiple [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]]—star-to-star light-element abundance variations once thought strictly confined to ancient clusters but recently detected in [[intermediate-age-stellar-populations|intermediate-age massive clusters]]. Finally, the extreme stellar densities found in YMC cores make them prime theoretical sites for runaway stellar collisions, raising the possibility that they act as incubators for [[massive-black-hole-seeds|Massive black hole seeds]] that ultimately grow into the [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] observed in galactic nuclei.

## Definition and Physical Properties

Young massive clusters (YMCs) are typically defined as dense, gravitationally bound aggregates of young stars with total stellar masses $M \gtrsim 10^4 M_\odot$ and ages younger than $\sim 100$ Myr [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract). The lower mass threshold distinguishes YMCs from the majority of galactic [[open-clusters|Open clusters]] and intermediate-mass clusters, which typically inhabit the $10^3 - 10^4 M_\odot$ regime [Richards et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....144...89R/abstract). To be robustly classified as a legitimate star cluster rather than a transient, expanding stellar association, a YMC must have an age greater than a few times its internal crossing time. The distinction between bound clusters and expanding associations is situated within a broader continuum; star formation frequently exhibits a hierarchical, fractal nature, clustering across physical scales ranging from parsec-sized associations to kiloparsec-sized [[star-clusters-and-complexes|star-forming complexes]] [Gouliermis et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASP..130g2001G/abstract). The dynamical age limit ensures that the system has survived the expulsion of its natal gas and achieved a state of virial equilibrium [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract).

YMCs are characterized by their extreme compactness, typically exhibiting half-mass radii $R_{\rm eff}$ on the order of $1$ to $5$ pc. Simulations and observations of local YMC populations suggest that initial cluster half-mass radii follow a lognormal distribution with a mean of $2.57$ pc and a dispersion of $1.59$ pc [Webb et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.1933W/abstract). Researchers have proposed using a compactness parameter involving cluster mass and half-light radius to distinguish potential young globular cluster progenitors from more loosely bound [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] [Fritze-v. Alvensleben et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASSL..329..209F/abstract). However, clusters formed within extended tidal debris, such as those found in Stephan's Quintet, often exhibit significantly larger effective radii ($R_{\rm eff} \sim 12-15$ pc) compared to those forming within galactic disks [Trancho et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748..102T/abstract). Due to their high masses and small radii, YMCs achieve remarkable central densities. The maximum stellar mass surface density in these [[dense-stellar-systems|Dense stellar systems]] is observed to be roughly constant across different environments at $\Sigma_{\max} \sim 10^{11} M_\odot \, \text{kpc}^{-2}$ (or $\sim 20 \text{ g cm}^{-2}$). This universal upper limit is likely set by stellar feedback from [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] regulating the maximum density to which the cluster can grow before radiation fields and winds clear out the remaining gas [Hopkins et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.401L..19H/abstract). In the most extreme [[early-galaxy-formation|early-Universe]] environments, such as the gravitationally lensed high-redshift YMCs observed in the Sunrise arc, stellar mass surface densities can comfortably exceed $10^3 M_\odot \, \text{pc}^{-2}$ and even reach a few $10^5 M_\odot \, \text{pc}^{-2}$ [Vanzella et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...53V/abstract).

While low-mass [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] do not generally exhibit a correlation between mass and radius, the most massive YMCs ($M \gtrsim 10^{6.5} M_\odot$) begin to demonstrate a mass-radius relation that blurs the morphological distinction between massive clusters and ultra-compact [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxies]] [Kissler-Patig et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...448.1031K/abstract). Indeed, the structural gap between massive star clusters and galaxies is increasingly populated by objects such as ultra-compact dwarfs (UCDs) and [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|compact elliptical galaxies]], which may originate from the tidal stripping of nucleated [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxies]] [Norris et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1151N/abstract). When interpreting YMC radii, however, the observed sizes of clusters at ages of several tens of millions of years should not be taken as their initial formation values. Rapid early expansion driven by stellar evolution mass-loss and internal dynamical relaxation significantly modifies their structure within the first few hundred million years [Webb et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.1933W/abstract).

A fundamental physical property of YMCs is their structural and dynamical similarity to ancient [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]]. High-dispersion spectroscopic observations of extragalactic YMCs, such as the central cluster in NGC 1705, yield dynamical masses ($\sim 8 \times 10^4 M_\odot$) and volume mass densities ($\sim 2.7 \times 10^4 M_\odot \, \text{pc}^{-3}$) that perfectly match the properties of typical globulars [Ho et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...472..600H/abstract). Consequently, a widespread consensus in the literature holds that YMCs are the present-day analogues of ancient globular clusters; an ancient globular cluster is effectively an ancient YMC that has survived for $10-12$ Gyr of stellar and dynamical evolution without succumbing to tidal disruption [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract), [Ho et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...472..600H/abstract), [Vanzella et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...53V/abstract).

## Historical Context and the HST Revolution

For decades, the study of stellar aggregates was constrained by a strict, binary traditional classification. Under this paradigm, stellar systems were divided into two distinct families: loosely bound, young, low-mass [[open-clusters|Open clusters]] residing in galactic disks, and dense, ancient, high-mass [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] predominantly found in galactic halos and bulges [Maíz-Apellániz et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..315..231M/abstract). Consequently, the formation of globular clusters was widely considered a unique, epoch-specific phenomenon strictly confined to the early Universe, functionally disconnected from contemporary star formation.

This traditional classification was fundamentally overturned in the 1990s by the deployment of the HST. By providing unprecedented optical spatial resolution—capable of resolving structures ten times smaller than ground-based observatories—the HST enabled the detailed study of extragalactic starbursts and interacting galaxies [Whitmore et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.12546W/abstract). These observations revealed a prolific population of extraordinarily luminous, compact stellar systems actively forming in the local Universe. Initially termed [[super-star-clusters|Super star clusters]] (SSCs) and now broadly referred to as young massive clusters (YMCs), these objects routinely exhibit stellar masses between $10^3$ and $10^8 M_\odot$, half-light radii of a few parsecs, and extreme mass densities reaching $\rho \sim 10^5 M_\odot \text{ pc}^{-3}$ [Whitmore et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.12546W/abstract), [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract).

A watershed moment during this observational revolution was the detailed characterization of the Antennae Galaxy (NGC 4038/4039). As the nearest prototypical merging system, the Antennae Galaxy was found to host hundreds of young ($1-500$ Myr), highly concentrated clusters [Whitmore et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002IAUS..207..367W/abstract), [Wilson et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...599.1049W/abstract). Early HST observations also identified young massive clusters in the starburst galaxy NGC 253, including one with an inferred mass exceeding 1.5 million solar masses [Watson et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996AJ....112..534W/abstract). High-dispersion spectroscopy and subsequent virial mass estimates of these SSCs, alongside similar objects in starburst dwarf galaxies like NGC 1705, demonstrated that their dynamical masses and structural properties were virtually identical to those of ancient [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] [Ho et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...472..600H/abstract).

These discoveries catalyzed a major paradigm shift: rather than being exclusively primordial relics, ancient globular clusters are now understood to be the surviving descendants of YMCs that formed under similarly extreme, high-pressure conditions in the early Universe [Whitmore et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.12546W/abstract), [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract). Space-based observations demonstrated that [[massive-cluster-formation|Massive cluster formation]] is a universal process that scales with the host galaxy's star formation rate and environmental pressure, effectively blurring the rigid boundaries between open, massive, and globular clusters [Larsen et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002IAUS..207..421L/abstract). 

However, while the structural similarities between YMCs and ancient clusters are firmly established, the literature maintains an active debate regarding the exact evolutionary mapping between the two populations. The long-term survival of YMCs is heavily dependent on factors that are still not fully constrained, such as the exact shape of the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], internal stellar feedback, and the external tidal field of their host galaxies [de Grijs et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0711.3540D/abstract), [Bastian et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016EAS....80....5B/abstract). It remains ambiguous what fraction of these nascent SSCs will survive the violent relaxation and tidal stripping required to dynamically evolve into the classical, long-lived [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] observed in galactic halos today.

## Formation Environments and Triggering Mechanisms

Young massive clusters (YMCs) preferentially form in interstellar environments characterized by extreme densities and pressures. Unlike typical [[open-clusters|Open clusters]], YMCs require these high-pressure conditions to rapidly accumulate massive amounts of gas within a compact volume before intense stellar feedback from [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] can disperse the natal cloud [Kruijssen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014CQGra..31x4006K/abstract), [Longmore et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014prpl.conf..291L/abstract). Such conditions are naturally met in the central regions of galaxies (e.g., [[the-galactic-center|The Galactic Center]]), during [[starburst-galaxy-evolution|Starburst Galaxy Evolution]], and within violently interacting systems.

Major [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] processes, particularly gas-rich galaxy mergers, are prolific drivers of YMC formation. Tidal interactions during mergers—such as those observed in the Antennae galaxies—compress the interstellar medium, funneling gas into supergiant molecular complexes with masses reaching up to $9 \times 10^8 M_\odot$ [Wilson et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...599.1049W/abstract). Within these massive complexes, fast H I gas collisions at velocities of $\sim 100\ \mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}$ induce severe shock compression, which can trigger the global gravitational collapse of precursor clumps exceeding $10^4 M_\odot$ [Maeda et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908....2M/abstract). Observations of six nearby galaxies indicate that the mass function indices for both [[star-forming-regions-and-cores|giant molecular clouds]] (GMCs) and YMCs are approximately $-2$, suggesting that the [[high-star-formation-efficiency|star formation efficiency]] within these clouds is broadly independent of the initial cloud mass [Mok et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...893..135M/abstract). Simulations demonstrate that colliding flows with velocities $\gtrsim 20\ \mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}$ in moderate-density clouds ($n \sim 100\ \mathrm{cm}^{-3}$) are capable of converting substantial gas mass into stars over timescales of just $\sim 1\ \mathrm{Myr}$ [Dobbs et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496L...1D/abstract). 

In these highly turbulent environments, GMCs act as the primary incubators for clustered star formation. Observations of the overlap region in the Antennae indicate that approximately $50\%$ of the free-free emission in massive GMCs originates from embedded, gravitationally bound YMCs [He et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928...57H/abstract). The assembly of these clusters can proceed via an "in-situ" mode—where gas is globally accumulated to protostellar densities prior to the onset of star formation, a mechanism dominant in the Galactic Center—or via a "conveyor belt" mode in galactic disks, where star formation occurs simultaneously with gas accumulation [Longmore et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014prpl.conf..291L/abstract). In dwarf starburst galaxies like Henize 2-10, N-body simulations suggest that the orbital decay and merging of multiple YMCs will likely drive the formation of a [[compact-galactic-nuclei|nuclear star cluster]] and a nuclear stellar disk [Arca-Sedda et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160104861A/abstract).

The survival of a nascent YMC into a bound state is strongly dependent on both its star formation efficiency (SFE, the fraction of gas converted into stars) and its cluster formation efficiency (CFE, conventionally denoted as $\Gamma$, representing the fraction of newly formed stars that reside in gravitationally bound clusters). In fact, regarding CFE, theoretical frameworks such as the "Gamma-1" model posit that $\Gamma = 1$, meaning essentially all stars may initially form in bound clusters that later disperse, rather than forming in isolated field conditions [Dinnbier et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...660A..61D/abstract). There is an active debate in the literature regarding whether these efficiencies are universal constants or vary significantly with environmental pressure. While typical galactic star formation exhibits low efficiencies, high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations of extreme starburst environments suggest that the *cluster formation efficiency* ($\Gamma$) can vary from $\sim 20\%$ to as high as $80\%$ at the peak of a starburst [Lahén et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...891....2L/abstract). Concurrently, local SFEs can also reach extreme values of up to $80\%$ under such high-density conditions [Garcia et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025OJAp....8E.146G/abstract). During their most heavily obscured infant stages, these systems can be observed within $10\ \text{Mpc}$ as Radio-Infrared Supernebulae (RISNe)—deeply embedded clusters that exhibit extremely [[high-star-formation-efficiency|high star formation efficiencies]] [Beck et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IJMPD..2430002B/abstract). High SFE naturally mitigates the disruptive effects of gas expulsion; furthermore, analytical and numerical models suggest that the formation of YMCs is dominated by gas exhaustion rather than gas expulsion [Longmore et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014prpl.conf..291L/abstract). Even in scenarios with lower global SFEs ($\sim 15\%$), bound clusters can survive instantaneous gas expulsion if the residual gas density profile is significantly shallower than the stellar density profile [Shukirgaliyev et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...605A.119S/abstract).

## Internal Dynamics and Mass Segregation

The internal structure of a young massive cluster (YMC) is highly time-dependent. During their first few hundred million years, YMCs undergo significant dynamical evolution driven by both internal processes, such as mass loss from stellar evolution, and external perturbations from the galactic tidal field. Consequently, the present-day observed sizes and masses of YMCs should not be adopted as their initial values at the time of formation [Webb et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.1933W/abstract). $N$-body simulations indicate that while the initial half-mass radii ($r_h$) of YMCs often follow a lognormal distribution with a mean around $2.5$ pc [Webb et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.1933W/abstract), the clusters undergo rapid early expansion. For massive [[star-clusters|Star clusters]], the time evolution of the half-mass radius has been analytically modeled to scale as
$$r_h/\text{pc} = 0.34 (t_\text{age}/\text{Myr})^{2/3}$$
[Fujii et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...817....4F/abstract). Furthermore, any primordial substructure present during the cluster's formation can temporarily boost the rate of close stellar encounters, although this elevated encounter rate typically subsides after a single dynamical crossing time as the cluster relaxes and its substructure is erased [Craig et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...769..150C/abstract).

As YMCs dynamically relax, they undergo mass segregation, a process where kinetic energy equipartition drives the most massive stars to sink toward the center of the cluster's gravitational potential well. This phenomenon is facilitated by dynamical friction, which can operate on a timescale much shorter than the overall two-body relaxation time of the system. In extremely dense environments, such as those found near [[the-galactic-center|The Galactic Center]], mass segregation proceeds exceptionally rapidly. Simulations of systems analogous to the [[arches-cluster|Arches cluster]] reveal that within just $\sim 3.5$ Myr, more than $90\%$ of stars with masses $M > 10 M_\odot$ can become concentrated within the cluster's half-mass radius [Portegies Zwart et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322..439P/abstract). The efficiency of this segregation is highly sensitive to the external environment; clusters that are strongly perturbed by the galactic tidal field exhibit significantly less mass segregation, as tidal stripping continuously alters the cluster's structure and the depth of its potential well [Portegies Zwart et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322..439P/abstract). Crucially, this rapid central accumulation of massive stars can create an artificially top-heavy stellar mass distribution in the cluster core. This effect contributes to an active debate over whether the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] in extreme environments genuinely favors [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] [Figer et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001sgnf.conf...13F/abstract), or if observed variations from a universal IMF merely reflect rapid dynamical mass segregation combined with observational biases [Ascenso et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007A&A...466..137A/abstract).

The rapid centralization of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] via mass segregation leads to exceptionally high stellar densities in the cluster core. Under such extreme conditions, the probability of direct stellar collisions increases dramatically. Extensive $N$-body and Monte Carlo simulations featuring initial core densities of $10^6$ to $4 \times 10^8 M_\odot \text{ pc}^{-3}$ suggest that these collisions can trigger a runaway growth process, forming a very massive star at the cluster center [Askar et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251003766A/abstract). This central object can subsequently collapse to form an intermediate-mass black hole (IMBH) with a mass of $10^3$ to $10^4 M_\odot$ within the first $\leq 5$ Myr of the cluster's evolution [Askar et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251003766A/abstract). Furthermore, the dense dynamical assembly within massive YMCs could contribute up to half of the observed binary black hole mergers detected via gravitational waves [Bruel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250303810B/abstract). Such collision-driven mechanisms highlight YMCs as critical laboratories for the study of [[dense-stellar-systems|Dense stellar systems]] and potential progenitors of [[massive-black-hole-seeds|Massive black hole seeds]]. Furthermore, the extreme internal dynamics and powerful stellar winds of massive stars within YMCs like NGC 6618 identify them as potential cosmic ray accelerators, exhibiting complex point-like and extended gamma-ray emission components [Liu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513.4747L/abstract).

## The Initial Mass Function and Massive Star Populations

Young massive clusters (YMCs) serve as coeval, chemically homogeneous laboratories for testing [[massive-star-physics|Massive Star Physics]] and stellar evolution across large populations of stars [Davies et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..425...37D/abstract). A central debate in the study of YMCs is whether the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] is a universal probability density function (PDF) or if it varies intrinsically under extreme environmental conditions. 

Textbook models typically treat the IMF as a universal, stochastically sampled function. However, observations of YMCs in high-density environments, such as [[the-galactic-center|The Galactic Center]], have revealed possible deviations. Studies of the [[arches-cluster|Arches cluster]] and [[quintuplet-cluster|Quintuplet cluster]] have indicated a "top-heavy" IMF that is unusually rich in [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] compared to a standard Salpeter or Kroupa mass function [Figer et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001sgnf.conf...13F/abstract), [Figer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...49F/abstract). The literature remains divided on whether this reflects a genuine environmental dependence or is merely an artifact of observational biases and rapid dynamical evolution [Ascenso et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007A&A...466..137A/abstract), [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract), [Longmore et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014prpl.conf..291L/abstract). 

The fundamental assumption that stellar masses are sampled randomly from a PDF has also been challenged by the statistical properties of YMCs. Analyses of the relationship between the mass of the most massive star in a cluster ($m_{\max}$) and the total cluster mass ($M_{\rm ecl}$) show a tight distribution that contradicts pure stochastic sampling at a $>4.5\sigma$ confidence level [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...670A.151Y/abstract). Instead, these findings favor a deterministic or self-regulated model of cluster formation, wherein stellar feedback from stars $\gtrsim 13 M_\odot$ restricts further gas accretion and defines the cluster's maximum stellar mass [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...670A.151Y/abstract). Furthermore, empirical observations of the Arches cluster suggest the existence of a strict physical upper mass cutoff of $150-200 M_\odot$ [Figer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...49F/abstract).

Because they thoroughly sample the high-mass tail of the IMF, YMCs frequently host rare, short-lived stellar evolutionary phases. YMCs are premier environments for studying [[wolf-rayet-star-populations|Wolf-Rayet star populations]], which are critical drivers of mechanical feedback and chemical enrichment at high metallicities. For example, the distant massive cluster VVV CL074 hosts several late-type nitrogen- and carbon-rich Wolf-Rayet stars (WN8 and WC8-9). These specific stellar types trace the evolutionary endpoints of progenitor stars with initial masses of $40-60 M_\odot$ [Martins et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...627A.170M/abstract). Similarly, red supergiants (RSGs) in YMCs serve as powerful diagnostic tools. Due to severe dust extinction in the Galactic plane, infrared spectroscopy of RSGs is often the only viable method to directly measure the metallicity of embedded YMCs, as demonstrated by the precise iron abundance ($\mathrm{[Fe/H]} = -0.07 \pm 0.02$) derived from RSGs (classified as [[luminous-dust-producing-stars|Luminous dust-producing stars]]) in the cluster VdBH 222 [Asa'd et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...900..138A/abstract).

Finally, observational measurements of the IMF and the distribution of these massive stars are heavily complicated by the internal dynamics of YMCs. Mass segregation occurs exceptionally fast in dense cluster cores. High-resolution $N$-body simulations calibrated to the Arches cluster demonstrate that $>90\%$ of stars more massive than $10 M_\odot$ can sink within the cluster's half-mass radius in merely $\sim 3.5$ Myr [Portegies Zwart et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322..439P/abstract). Consequently, observing the central regions of a YMC will yield an artificially shallow (top-heavy) mass function if this rapid dynamical segregation is not carefully disentangled from the primordial IMF.

## Stellar Feedback and Cluster Survival

The early evolution of young massive clusters is profoundly shaped by the [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] they host. In the first few million years, mechanical and radiative stellar feedback—dominated by intense radiation pressure, photoionization, and massive stellar winds (which, depending on metallicity and evolutionary stage, exhibit typical average velocities of $500-1000$ $\mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}$ in low-metallicity environments [Larkin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251006100L/abstract))—regulates the local star formation environment. This energetic feedback is theorized to enforce a strict upper limit on the maximum stellar surface density ($\Sigma_{\rm max}$) in [[dense-stellar-systems|Dense stellar systems]]. Across diverse environments, from young starburst clusters to ancient [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]], this limit is observed to be remarkably constant at $\Sigma_{\rm max} \sim 10^{11} M_\odot {\rm kpc}^{-2}$ [Hopkins et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.401L..19H/abstract). 

As stellar feedback clears the natal molecular cloud, the rapid removal of residual gas alters the gravitational potential of the cluster. In the classical theoretical framework, if this gas expulsion occurs on a timescale shorter than the dynamical crossing time, and the [[high-star-formation-efficiency|star formation efficiency]] (SFE) is low, the system becomes unbound. The cluster subsequently dissolves, dispersing its stars into the galactic field—a process termed "infant mortality" [Kroupa et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..9370K/abstract). Observational studies of [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] in nearby galaxies suggest that infant mortality is highly efficient, with some estimates indicating that only $\sim 10\%$ of stars form in long-lived, bound clusters, while the vast majority rapidly join the diffuse field population [Maschberger et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.411.1495M/abstract).

However, the literature exhibits tension regarding the universality and destructiveness of gas expulsion. Recent models suggest that the formation of many massive clusters may be dominated by gas exhaustion rather than violent gas expulsion, resulting in systems that are dynamically stable from a very young age [Longmore et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014prpl.conf..291L/abstract). In the most compact and dense clouds, the global star cluster wind can even be dynamically suppressed, preventing the rapid blow-out of leftover gas [Silich et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944L..32S/abstract). Furthermore, the required SFE for a cluster to remain bound is heavily dependent on its internal structure. If the residual gas has a density profile shallower than the embedded stellar distribution, young clusters can survive instantaneous gas expulsion with a global SFE as low as $15\%$ [Shukirgaliyev et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...605A.119S/abstract), significantly lower than the historically assumed $33\%$ threshold.

For clusters that remain bound after the embedded phase, long-term survival is dictated by a combination of internal dynamics and external galactic forces. Over the first few hundred million years, mass loss associated with stellar evolution causes the cluster to rapidly expand [Webb et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501.1933W/abstract). Concurrently, the cluster is subject to tidal stripping from the host galaxy's gravitational field. Clusters born in stronger tidal fields lose mass more rapidly and reach core collapse sooner [Rodriguez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521..124R/abstract). Ultimately, the competition between internal expansion, gas exhaustion, and external tidal stripping determines whether a young massive cluster will dissolve into the galactic field or survive for gigayears as a modern analogue to the ancient [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]].

## YMCs in the Milky Way and Local Group

Young massive clusters (YMCs) in the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] and Local Group serve as critical, well-resolved laboratories for studying [[massive-star-formation|Massive Star Formation]] and early cluster dynamics. Because individual stars can be resolved, these nearby systems provide stringent observational constraints on stellar evolution models and the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] that are impossible to obtain in distant extragalactic environments [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract), [Davies et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..425...37D/abstract).

**The Galactic Center Clusters**
The extreme, high-pressure environment of [[the-galactic-center|The Galactic Center]] hosts some of the most massive young clusters in the Galaxy, most notably the [[arches-cluster|Arches cluster]] and the [[quintuplet-cluster|Quintuplet cluster]] [Figer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...49F/abstract). Both clusters boast total masses exceeding $10^4 M_\odot$ and ages $< 5$ Myr. Due to the intense tidal field of the central Milky Way, these clusters are subject to rapid dynamical evolution. Direct $N$-body simulations indicate that mass segregation occurs extraordinarily fast; in the Arches cluster, $>90\%$ of stars more massive than $\sim 10 M_\odot$ segregate into the central half-mass radius within just $\sim 3.5$ Myr [Portegies Zwart et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322..439P/abstract). Furthermore, these nuclear clusters are expected to completely dissolve within $10-70$ Myr, with their projected stellar densities dropping below the dense background level in only $\sim 20$ Myr, making older generations virtually undetectable [Portegies Zwart et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...546L.101P/abstract), [Portegies Zwart et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..228..131P/abstract). Intriguingly, while the Galactic Center is famous for these dense clusters, recent constraints show that the population of outlying or isolated massive stars within the Central Molecular Zone is comparable in size to the cluster population, and may even exceed it [Clark et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...649A..43C/abstract). Furthermore, a subset of massive Galactic clusters, including [[omega-centauri|Omega Centauri]] and NGC 6273, are increasingly identified as the remnant [[compact-galactic-nuclei|nuclear star clusters]] of ancient accreted galaxies rather than genuine globular clusters [Pfeffer et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.2514P/abstract).

The stellar mass distribution within these central clusters is the subject of active debate. Some observational analyses suggest a top-heavy [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], implying a deterministic or self-regulated star formation process unique to high-density environments, potentially violating the assumption of a universal probability density function via the $m_{\rm max}-M_{\rm ecl}$ relation [Figer et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001sgnf.conf...13F/abstract), [Figer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...49F/abstract), [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...670A.151Y/abstract). Conversely, other studies argue that the IMF is fundamentally universal across environments, and that the apparent top-heavy signatures are artifacts of observational biases or rapid dynamical mass segregation [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract), [Longmore et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014prpl.conf..291L/abstract).

**Galactic Disk and Local Group Environments**
Beyond the nuclear region, the Galactic disk hosts massive starburst clusters such as [[westerlund-1|Westerlund 1]] and those located in the Scutum Complex. Westerlund 1 provides a massive, coeval population of post-main-sequence massive stars, serving as a critical testbed for [[massive-star-physics|Massive Star Physics]] and stellar feedback [Negueruela et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014mysc.conf....9N/abstract).

In the Local Group, the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC) offers a slightly lower-metallicity environment rich in YMCs. The most prominent example is [[r136|R136]], the central ionizing cluster of the 30 Doradus region. With a total mass exceeding $10^5 M_\odot$, [[r136|R136]] represents a scale of clustered star formation rarely seen in the Milky Way. Magnetohydrodynamic simulations suggest that systems as massive as R136 can be triggered by fast ($v \sim 100\ \mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}$) H I gas collisions, which induce global gravitational collapse in the precursor clouds even in low-metallicity environments [Maeda et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908....2M/abstract). 

Studies of LMC clusters also challenge the paradigm of monolithic cluster formation. Observations indicate that massive stars do not typically form strictly at the highest column densities of their parent giant molecular clouds; instead, star formation can cluster over multiple generations and propagate on timescales of up to $10$ Myr [Ochsendorf et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832...43O/abstract). Ultimately, tracking the intermediate-age descendants of these systems in the Magellanic Clouds provides crucial links between local YMCs, [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]], and the complex [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] observed in ancient galactic halos.

## The Progenitor Link to Ancient Globular Clusters

The paradigm of star cluster evolution has increasingly recognized that cluster formation is a continuous, universal process across cosmic time. Young massive clusters (YMCs) observed in the local universe share fundamental physical properties with ancient [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] (GCs), possessing comparable masses ($10^4$ to $10^7 M_\odot$), compact half-mass radii ($R_h \sim 1-5 \text{ pc}$), and high central stellar densities [Ho et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...472..600H/abstract), [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract). This striking structural similarity supports the hypothesis that YMCs are globular cluster progenitors, representing the modern-day analogs of the intense starburst episodes that shaped the early Universe [de Grijs et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0711.3540D/abstract), [Bastian et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016EAS....80....5B/abstract).

A long-standing challenge to the YMC-GC connection was the presence of multiple [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] (MPs) in ancient GCs. These star-to-star light-element abundance variations (e.g., He, C, N, O, Na) were historically assumed to be a unique feature of the early Universe [Gratton et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&ARv..20...50G/abstract). However, high-precision photometry has recently revealed MPs in intermediate-age massive clusters as young as $\sim 1.7 - 2 \text{ Gyr}$, such as NGC 2173 and NGC 1978 in the Magellanic Clouds [Martocchia et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.2688M/abstract), [Kapse et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927L..10K/abstract). While the precise physical mechanism behind MP formation remains an active area of debate, the discovery of a transitional age threshold for MPs bridges the evolutionary gap, implying that YMCs and ancient GCs share a continuous evolutionary pathway.

For a nascent YMC to survive and evolve into a GC, it must withstand intense early stellar feedback and resist long-term tidal disruption. The probability of survival is heavily dependent on the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], the external tidal field, and the cluster's initial compactness [de Grijs et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0711.3540D/abstract), [Kruijssen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014CQGra..31x4006K/abstract). Theoretical models suggest that the high-pressure, gas-rich environments characteristic of high-redshift galaxies naturally produced clusters that were highly compact, scaling as $R_{\star} \propto M_{\star}^{1/2} \Sigma_g^{-1/2}$, which increased their resilience to tidal shocks [Choksi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.5492C/abstract). Direct observational evidence of this process has been captured by JWST in the highly magnified Sunrise Arc at $z \sim 6$, revealing gravitationally bound YMCs with extreme stellar surface densities exceeding $10^3 M_\odot \text{ pc}^{-2}$ that represent bona fide GCs in their infancy [Vanzella et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...53V/abstract).

Because they act as highly durable fossil records, these clusters are critical tracers of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. The bimodal color and metallicity distributions characteristic of many galactic GC systems can be naturally reproduced by models of hierarchical merging [Gnedin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..266..250G/abstract), [Muratov et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...718.1266M/abstract). In this framework, metal-poor (blue) clusters formed early in low-mass protogalactic fragments, whereas metal-rich (red) clusters formed later in massive, gas-rich merger-induced starbursts. This co-evolution produces a distinct age-metallicity relation, where the average metallicity of clusters scales with host galaxy mass and assembly epoch [Choksi et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.2343C/abstract), [Horta et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.4768H/abstract). A compelling confirmation of this model is found in [[massive-relic-galaxies|Massive relic galaxies]] such as NGC 1277, an un-evolved "red nugget" that lacks a recently accreted stellar halo. The GC system of NGC 1277 is unimodal and exclusively red, demonstrating that the blue GC population in typical massive galaxies is almost entirely accreted from [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] during later hierarchical merging events [Beasley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.555..483B/abstract). Additionally, YMCs and ancient clusters trace the assembly history of galactic nuclei. In particular, there is a transition mass in host galaxies at $\sim 10^9 M_\odot$, where the formation of [[compact-galactic-nuclei|nuclear star clusters]] shifts from being dominated by the inspiral and accretion of globular clusters to being driven by in-situ star formation [Neumayer et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&ARv..28....4N/abstract). This ongoing evolutionary linkage is further reinforced by observations of giant elliptical galaxies; for instance, in NGC 1275 within the Perseus cluster, researchers have observed the sustained formation of thousands of massive, progenitor globular clusters at a steady rate over the past 1 Gyr, fueled by cooling intracluster gas [Lim et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatAs...4..153L/abstract).

## Multiple Populations and Chemical Anomalies

For decades, massive star clusters were regarded as the quintessential [[simple-stellar-populations|Simple stellar populations]]—coeval groups of stars sharing an identical initial chemical composition. However, high-resolution spectroscopy and photometry have overturned this paradigm, revealing that nearly all ancient [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] host multiple stellar populations (MPs) characterized by star-to-star abundance variations in light elements such as He, C, N, O, Na, and Al [Gratton et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&ARv..20...50G/abstract), [Milone et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Univ....8..359M/abstract). Some exceptionally massive systems, such as [[omega-centauri|Omega Centauri]] and Terzan 5, even exhibit significant spreads in iron abundance and age, suggesting they may be the remnant nuclei of [[accreted-dwarf-galaxies|Accreted dwarf galaxies]] [Lee et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..480L/abstract), [Ferraro et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..483F/abstract).

To determine if young massive clusters are the present-day progenitors of ancient globular clusters, researchers have actively searched for similar chemical anomalies in younger systems. Observations indicate a stark age dependence in the onset of MPs, which act as a potential chemical clock for cluster evolution. Multiple populations have been unambiguously detected in intermediate-age clusters in the Magellanic Clouds down to ages of $\sim 1.7{-}2$ Gyr. For example, NGC 1978 ($\sim 2$ Gyr) and NGC 2173 ($\sim 1.7$ Gyr) both exhibit MPs, with the latter showing a distinct bifurcation at the tip of the red giant branch [Martocchia et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.2688M/abstract), [Kapse et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927L..10K/abstract). In contrast, observations of younger massive clusters, such as the $\sim 20$ Myr old Galactic cluster VdBH 222, show a chemically homogeneous population with no measurable spread in $\mathrm{[Fe/H]}$ or light elements [Asa'd et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...900..138A/abstract). 

The apparent restriction of MPs to clusters older than $\sim 1.7$ Gyr introduces significant tension into cluster formation models. If young massive clusters and ancient globular clusters share a common evolutionary pathway, the mechanism producing abundance variations must either be delayed or dependent on conditions unique to earlier cosmic epochs. Various self-enrichment scenarios propose that "polluter" stars from a first generation—such as intermediate-mass asymptotic-giant-branch stars—contaminate the intra-cluster medium from which subsequent [[stellar-generations|Stellar generations]] form [Gratton et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&ARv..20...50G/abstract), [Lee et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.462..480L/abstract). Cosmological simulations demonstrate that extended star formation histories with metal pollution from supernova ejecta can replicate heavy-element spreads, but they frequently fail to reproduce the specific light-element variations observed in the literature [Brown et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...864...94B/abstract). 

Furthermore, a correlation between nitrogen abundance and total cluster mass has been observed, providing a crucial constraint on the mass-loss and internal chemical evolution histories of these systems [Schiavon et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...776L...7S/abstract). Alternative models circumvent self-enrichment entirely by proposing that a newborn massive cluster might gravitationally trap older field stars from its host galaxy. Simulations suggest that a massive stellar supercluster could accrete up to $\sim 40\%$ of its mass in surrounding disc stars if the velocity dispersions are comparable, creating an apparent multi-age and multi-metallicity population [Fellhauer et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.372..338F/abstract). 

Currently, the literature remains conflicted. The exact physical nature of the polluters responsible for light-element abundance variations, and whether the observed absence of MPs in the youngest clusters represents a true physical threshold or an observational artifact, remain hotly debated open questions.

## YMCs at High Redshift and the Reionization Era

The formation of [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] during the first billion years of cosmic history represents a critical frontier in understanding both the origins of ancient [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] and the drivers of the epoch of cosmic reionization. While studies historically relied on local analogues such as young massive clusters (YMCs) in nearby starburst galaxies, the advent of JWST, combined with strong gravitational lensing, has enabled the direct resolution of these objects in the early universe.

A landmark discovery in this regime is the highly magnified "Sunrise Arc" galaxy at $z \sim 6$ [Vanzella et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...53V/abstract). Within this lensed system, JWST/NIRCam imaging has identified six distinct YMCs with intrinsic radii spanning from approximately $1$ to $20 \text{ pc}$ and estimated stellar masses of $10^6 \text{--} 10^7 M_{\odot}$ [Vanzella et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...53V/abstract). These clusters exhibit extreme compactness, achieving stellar mass surface densities exceeding $10^3 M_{\odot} \text{ pc}^{-2}$ and reaching up to a few $10^5 M_{\odot} \text{ pc}^{-2}$. Fits to their spectral energy distributions reveal young ages ranging from $1$ to $30 \text{ Myr}$, indicating that these high-redshift clusters are gravitationally bound and are likely the direct precursors to metal-poor globular clusters observed in the local universe [Vanzella et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...53V/abstract). Similar networks of massive star clusters have been detected at even higher redshifts. For instance, the "Firefly Sparkle" system at $z = 8.304$ contains central clusters displaying nebular-dominated spectra indicative of highly metal-poor environments and a potentially top-heavy [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] [Mowla et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240208696M/abstract), while the Cosmic Gems arc reveals cluster formation at $z = 10.2$ [Mayer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241100670M/abstract).

These primordial YMCs played a disproportionately large role in driving cosmic reionization. In the Sunrise Arc, the youngest stellar clusters (ages $< 5 \text{ Myr}$) are embedded within a $200 \text{ pc}$ star-forming complex and exhibit prominent $\mathrm{H}\beta + [\mathrm{O\,III}]$ emission lines with rest-frame equivalent widths exceeding $1000 \text{ \AA}$ [Vanzella et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...53V/abstract). Such dense systems dominate the ionizing photon production of their host galaxies, achieving remarkably high production efficiencies of $\log(\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}\,[\mathrm{Hz\,erg}^{-1}]) \sim 25.7$ [Vanzella et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...53V/abstract). Theoretical models and observations suggest that the intense, concentrated radiation fields from such clusters carve out ionized channels in the surrounding interstellar medium, thereby greatly enhancing the escape fraction of Lyman continuum radiation into the intergalactic medium [Vanzella et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...53V/abstract). Furthermore, [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] and subsequent high-mass X-ray binaries forming within these primordial YMCs would have generated substantial ionizing UV and X-ray fluxes, cementing their role as critical engines of reionization [Power et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.395.1146P/abstract).

The physical conditions of early galaxy assembly—characterized by highly pressurized, gas-rich environments—naturally favor the in-situ formation of YMCs through rapid galactic disk fragmentation [Mayer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241100670M/abstract). Under these extreme conditions, YMCs can constitute $20\%$ to $40\%$ of the total stellar mass of their host [[very-young-galaxies|Very Young Galaxies]] [Mayer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241100670M/abstract). Due to their extreme stellar densities, these clusters are also theorized to be birthplaces for massive black hole seeds. Rapid stellar collisions within the cluster cores may quickly form intermediate-mass black holes that can subsequently sink to the galactic center, offering a plausible formation pathway for the unexpectedly massive [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] discovered by JWST at $z > 6$ [Mayer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241100670M/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the evolutionary link between young massive clusters (YMCs) and ancient [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] is increasingly well-established, several fundamental questions regarding their internal physics and formation mechanisms remain fiercely debated. 

A primary tension in the literature concerns **IMF universality**. Standard textbook models often treat the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] as a universal probability density function. However, this assumption is challenged in the extreme, high-density environments where YMCs form. Observations of [[the-galactic-center|The Galactic Center]] suggest a potentially top-heavy IMF that is disproportionately rich in massive stars [Figer et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001sgnf.conf...13F/abstract), [Figer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...49F/abstract). Conversely, other researchers argue that the IMF is strictly universal and that apparent environmental variations arise from observational biases, or that stellar sampling is a deterministic, self-regulated process tightly constrained by the total cluster mass (the $m_{\rm max}-M_{\rm ecl}$ relation) rather than stochastic sampling [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...670A.151Y/abstract).

Another major unresolved issue is the origin of multiple [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]]. Ancient globular clusters ubiquitously host multiple stellar generations exhibiting distinct light-element abundance variations (e.g., He, C, N, O, Na). Recent surveys have revealed a sharp age transition for the onset of this phenomenon: multiple populations are present in intermediate-age clusters down to $\sim 1.7 - 2.0$ Gyr (such as NGC 1978 and NGC 2173) [Martocchia et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.2688M/abstract), [Kapse et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927L..10K/abstract), but are completely absent in very young clusters like VdBH 222 [Asa'd et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...900..138A/abstract). The specific physical mechanism and the nature of the "polluters" (e.g., asymptotic giant branch stars vs. fast-rotating massive stars) responsible for this chemical enrichment remain intensely debated [Gratton et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&ARv..20...50G/abstract).

The extreme stellar densities found in the cores of YMCs also make them prime theoretical candidates for the dynamical formation of intermediate-mass black holes (IMBHs). Direct $N$-body simulations indicate that runaway stellar collisions can form IMBHs of $10^3 - 10^4\ M_{\odot}$ in $< 5$ Myr [Askar et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251003766A/abstract). At high redshift, these dense Pop III and early YMC environments may provide the elusive [[massive-black-hole-seeds|Massive black hole seeds]] required to quickly grow the [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] observed in the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]] [Mayer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241100670M/abstract), [Reinoso et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320415R/abstract). 

Furthermore, the exact kinetics of the transition from a dense molecular cloud to a bound star cluster remain ambiguous. Specifically, the interplay between stellar feedback and **gas consumption timescales** dictates whether a cluster survives its infancy. It is actively debated whether YMC formation is halted primarily by rapid gas exhaustion or by violent gas expulsion (which can lead to cluster dissolution). Recent hydrodynamical simulations suggest that in sufficiently high-pressure environments, local star formation efficiencies can reach up to $\sim 80\%$, thereby minimizing early tidal disruption and favoring gas exhaustion [Longmore et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014prpl.conf..291L/abstract), [Garcia et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025OJAp....8E.146G/abstract).

Another significant open question pertains to the chemical boundaries of cluster formation. The recent discovery of the extremely metal-poor cluster RBC EXT8 in the Andromeda Galaxy (M31) fundamentally challenges the previously assumed concept of a strict metallicity floor for globular cluster formation [Larsen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Sci...370..970L/abstract). Understanding how such extreme structures formed in nearly pristine gas will be a major target for next-generation observatories.

Looking forward, the next decade promises significant observational breakthroughs. While JWST has already begun detecting the high-redshift progenitors of YMCs during the epoch of reionization ($z > 6$) [Vanzella et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...53V/abstract), as well as at cosmic noon ($z=2.53$) [Whitaker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250107627W/abstract), they remain largely unresolved. Future extremely large telescopes will bridge this gap; upcoming 30-meter-class facilities will provide the resolving power necessary to obtain critical demographic snapshots of YMC populations at intermediate redshifts. These facilities will rigorously test IMF variations and cluster survival models across a broader swath of cosmic time [Kruijssen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250116438K/abstract).
