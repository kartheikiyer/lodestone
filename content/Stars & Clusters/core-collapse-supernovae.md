**Core-Collapse Supernovae** (CCSNe) represent the catastrophic explosive deaths of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] with initial zero-age main-sequence masses typically exceeding $8 M_\odot$. As these [[massive-progenitor-stars|massive progenitors]] reach the end of their [[thermonuclear-explosions|thermonuclear evolution]], they develop degenerate iron cores supported against gravity almost entirely by electron degeneracy pressure. Once the core accretes enough ash from surrounding silicon shell burning to exceed the [[chandrasekhar-mass|Chandrasekhar mass]], endothermic processes—specifically the photodisintegration of iron nuclei and the rapid capture of electrons onto protons—remove the core's pressure support and trigger a rapid [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] [Chiu et al. (1964)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1964AnPhy..26..364C/abstract). The imploding core falls inward until it reaches nuclear densities, at which point the stiffening nuclear equation of state causes the inner core to rebound. This "bounce" forms a hot, dense compact object—typically one of the universe's newborn [[proto-neutron-stars|Proto-neutron stars]]—and launches a supersonic hydrodynamic shock wave outward into the infalling stellar envelope. Releasing approximately $10^{53}$ ergs of gravitational binding energy, these explosive events are fundamental drivers of cosmic evolution; they inject massive amounts of kinetic energy into the interstellar medium to carve out [[supershells|Supershells]] and synthesize the vast majority of the universe's oxygen and $\alpha$-elements [Fryer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..302.....F/abstract).

Despite decades of theoretical and computational advancements, the precise physics governing shock revival remains an active area of research. The initial shock wave rapidly stalls due to neutrino losses and the photodisintegration of infalling heavy nuclei. The standard paradigm for re-energizing this shock relies on a delayed neutrino-heating mechanism, where a small fraction of the immense neutrino flux emitted by the cooling core deposits thermal energy behind the shock, aided by crucial multidimensional fluid instabilities like the Standing Accretion Shock Instability (SASI). However, the extreme explosion energies of [[hypernovae|Hypernovae]] [Nomoto et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003IAUS..212..395N/abstract) and the highly asymmetric ejecta morphologies observed in young remnants have prompted the exploration of alternative [[explosion-mechanisms|Explosion mechanisms]], including magnetorotational engines [Winteler et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...750L..22W/abstract) and the Jittering Jets Explosion Mechanism (JJEM) [Soker et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..31S/abstract), [Soker et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Galax..12...29S/abstract). Observationally, CCSNe exhibit a rich morphological diversity driven by the extent of pre-collapse envelope stripping. Progenitors that retain their massive hydrogen envelopes—such as [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]]—explode as [[type-ii-supernovae|Type II Supernovae]], while those stripped of their outer layers by strong stellar winds or binary interactions produce [[stripped-envelope-supernovae|Stripped-Envelope Supernovae]] (Types Ib, Ic, and IIb).

Modern supernova research is increasingly driven by multi-messenger observations and the statistical mapping of progenitors to their final [[stellar-remnant|Stellar remnant]] outcomes. The prompt emission of thermal neutrinos and gravitational waves provides direct, unattenuated probes of the core's internal hydrodynamics prior to electromagnetic shock breakout. These multi-messenger signals are critical for addressing major open questions in [[stellar-death-hypothesis|stellar death]], particularly the "failed supernova" hypothesis. This hypothesis posits that a significant fraction of massive stars (especially those $>17 M_\odot$) may not achieve successful shock revival, instead undergoing direct [[stellar-core-collapse|Stellar core collapse]] into [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]] via massive [[fallback-accretion|Fallback accretion]]. Determining the exact mass thresholds for black hole versus neutron star formation, constraining the birth fraction of highly magnetized remnants (magnetars), and identifying the specific [[r-process-nucleosynthesis-sites|r-process nucleosynthesis sites]] responsible for the universe's heaviest elements stand as primary objectives for the next generation of 3D hydrodynamic simulations and multi-messenger observatories.

## Introduction to the Core-Collapse Mechanism

The sequence of events leading to a core-collapse supernova begins during the late evolutionary stages of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] with initial masses $M \gtrsim 8 M_\odot$ [Limongi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book..513L/abstract), [Arcavi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book..239A/abstract). Recent studies have identified an inherent monotonicity in the cores of these massive stars, suggesting that their lifetimes, core structures, and final compact [[stellar-remnant|remnants]] can be robustly ordered into a single evolutionary sequence [Takahashi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...19T/abstract). Through successive phases of nuclear fusion, these stars progressively synthesize heavier elements, culminating in the formation of a degenerate iron core. Because iron-group elements possess the highest nuclear binding energy per nucleon, further fusion cannot yield a net release of energy. Consequently, the iron core is supported against gravity almost entirely by electron degeneracy pressure.

As silicon shell burning continues to deposit ash onto the core, its mass grows until it approaches the [[chandrasekhar-mass|Chandrasekhar mass]]. When central temperatures reach $T \sim 6 \times 10^9$ K and densities exceed $\rho \sim 10^9$ g cm$^{-3}$, two endothermic instabilities remove the core's pressure support, triggering catastrophic [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]]: the photodisintegration of iron nuclei into alpha particles and neutrons, and the capture of electrons by protons and heavy nuclei [Chiu et al. (1964)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1964AnPhy..26..364C/abstract). 

The collapse proceeds nearly in free-fall until the inner core reaches nuclear densities ($\rho \sim 10^{14}$ g cm$^{-3}$). At this point, the strong nuclear force and neutron degeneracy pressure severely stiffen the equation of state. The inner core abruptly halts and rebounds, forming a hot, dense [[proto-neutron-stars|proto-neutron star]] and driving a supersonic hydrodynamic shock wave outward into the infalling outer core material. 

However, this initial shock wave is rapidly sapped of its kinetic energy by the photodisintegration of the infalling heavy nuclei and the escape of electron neutrinos. Within milliseconds, the shock stalls and turns into an accretion shock. To successfully eject the stellar envelope, the star requires a mechanism to revive this stalled shock. The widely accepted standard model is the delayed neutrino-driven shock mechanism. Over a cooling timescale of $\sim 20$ seconds, the proto-neutron star sheds its lepton number and releases roughly $\sim 10^{53}$ erg of gravitational binding energy, primarily as a massive flux of neutrinos [Roberts et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book.1605R/abstract). A small fraction—on the order of $1\%$—of this neutrino energy is absorbed by the dense plasma immediately behind the stalled shock, depositing thermal energy, driving convective instabilities, and eventually re-energizing the shock to explode the star.

While the delayed neutrino-heating mechanism is the consensus model for standard core-collapse events, the literature contains active debate regarding its universality. For instance, [[hypernovae|Hypernovae]] exhibit extreme explosion energies ($E > 10^{52}$ erg) that significantly strain the theoretical efficiency limits of neutrino heating [Nomoto et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003IAUS..212..395N/abstract). To explain such energetic or highly aspherical explosions, researchers have proposed auxiliary or alternative [[explosion-mechanisms|Explosion mechanisms]]. These include magnetorotational mechanisms driven by rapidly rotating cores [Winteler et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...750L..22W/abstract), or the Jittering Jets Explosion Mechanism (JJEM), which posits that stochastic accretion drives bipolar jets that are primarily responsible for the explosion [Soker et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..31S/abstract), [Soker et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Galax..12...29S/abstract). The exact mapping between progenitor mass and a successful explosion remains highly complex and is central to the "failed supernova" hypothesis, which corresponds to the [[stellar-death-hypothesis|Stellar death hypothesis]] suggesting some high-mass stars may never achieve shock revival at all [Suzuki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...852..101S/abstract).

## Historical Development of Supernova Theory

The theoretical framework for [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] in massive stars was established in the mid-twentieth century, evolving from early thermodynamic arguments to the complex, multidimensional models of the modern era. By the early 1960s, stellar collapse theory posited that massive stars develop an iron core that ultimately becomes vulnerable to instability. Chiu (1964) demonstrated that as core temperatures reach $T \sim 6 \times 10^9$ K, a combination of hydrostatic instability—driven by the photodisintegration of iron into helium—and dynamical instability caused by neutrino emission forces a [[catastrophic-core-collapse|Catastrophic core collapse]] [Chiu et al. (1964)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1964AnPhy..26..364C/abstract). Because neutrino emission carries away energy highly efficiently, theoretical models showed that the collapse proceeds under near-zero temperature conditions relative to the electron Fermi energy, halting only when neutron degeneracy pressure supports the newly formed [[proto-neutron-stars|proto-neutron star]] [Chiu et al. (1964)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1964AnPhy..26..364C/abstract).

For decades, empirical constraints on [[core-collapse-supernova-rate|supernova rates]] and mechanisms relied heavily on the localized historical record of naked-eye events and the distribution of galactic remnants [Lerche et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981Ap&SS..74..273L/abstract). The observational landscape was fundamentally revolutionized by the explosion of SN 1987A in the Large Magellanic Cloud. SN 1987A provided the first direct detection of neutrinos from a collapsing stellar core (recorded by the Kamiokande, IMB, and Baksan observatories), spectacularly validating the core-collapse hypothesis and the predicted energy budget of the explosion [Marti-Magro et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170500675M/abstract). Furthermore, SN 1987A forced a major paradigm shift in progenitor identification: its distinctive, long-rising light curve proved that [[type-ii-supernovae|Type II Supernovae]] can originate from compact blue supergiants, overturning the long-held assumption that all such events arise exclusively from [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]] [Taddia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...588A...5T/abstract), [Arcavi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book..239A/abstract).

In the modern computational era, the advent of high-resolution 3D simulations has addressed the historical failure of 1D spherical models to reliably produce successful explosions. Modern multidimensional models demonstrate that the post-bounce core is highly unstable. Non-axisymmetric accretion flows and hydrodynamic instabilities—most notably the standing accretion shock instability (SASI) and large-scale convection—are now understood to be critical for reviving the stalled shock wave by efficiently mixing neutrino-heated matter [Hayama et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvL.116o1102H/abstract), [Abdikamalov et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201004356A/abstract). These turbulent, multidimensional dynamics within newborn remnants are also predicted to be the primary source of gravitational wave emission during the explosion [Abdikamalov et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201004356A/abstract).

Despite these advancements, the literature reflects significant ambiguity regarding the definitive [[explosion-mechanisms|explosion mechanism]]. While delayed neutrino heating remains the consensus standard model, tension exists with alternative paradigms. For instance, the JJEM has been proposed to explain point-symmetric morphologies observed in recent analyses of supernova remnants, suggesting that non-relativistic bipolar jets may drive many core-collapse events, potentially contradicting purely neutrino-driven predictions [Couch et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...696..953C/abstract), [Soker et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..31S/abstract), [Soker et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Galax..12...29S/abstract). Finally, alongside advances in hydrodynamics, some theorists have explored more fundamental physics frameworks. For instance, there are theoretical efforts extending supersymmetric models to core-collapse supernovae, aiming to provide a unified picture of both [[thermonuclear-explosions|thermonuclear]] and core-collapse events via phase transitions to an exact supersymmetric background [Clavelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190811397C/abstract).

## Diversity of Progenitors and Classification

The observational diversity of core-collapse supernovae (CCSNe) originates from the varied final states of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] with initial masses $M \gtrsim 8 M_\odot$ [Arcavi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book..239A/abstract), [Limongi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book..513L/abstract). Classification is fundamentally driven by the presence or absence of hydrogen and helium in early-time optical spectra, which acts as a direct proxy for the degree of envelope stripping the progenitor experienced prior to [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]]. To handle large observational datasets, citizen science projects (like Galaxy Zoo Supernovae) have proven highly effective, successfully identifying and classifying transient candidates with remarkable accuracy compared to professional scanners [Smith et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412.1309S/abstract).

**Hydrogen-Rich Supernovae**
[[type-ii-supernovae|Type II Supernovae]] retain a significant fraction of their hydrogen envelopes. The most common subclass, Type IIP (plateau), is robustly linked to the explosion of [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]] [Arcavi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book..239A/abstract). Recent modeling of the plateau phase light curves reveals significant diversity in the hydrogen-rich envelope masses, indicating that standard stellar wind models may not account for this observed variance; consequently, these progenitors likely experience extensive mass loss beyond standard prescriptions prior to explosion [Fang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...978...35F/abstract). Furthermore, spatial correlations with H$\alpha$ emission suggest that a significant 'runaway' fraction—up to 40%—exists for the progenitors of Type II supernovae, heavily influencing their observed local environments [James et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...453...57J/abstract). This runaway behavior is often linked to the effects of supernovae in [[compact-objects-and-binary-mergers|binary systems]], which can drastically alter binary orbits and violently eject secondary stars as runaway massive stars with velocities reaching hundreds of kilometers per second . 

While the lack of highly massive progenitors in pre-explosion imaging has led to the "Red Supergiant Problem"—the [[stellar-death-hypothesis|hypothesis]] that stars $>17-18 M_\odot$ may fail to explode—statistical constraints on galactic oxygen enrichment strongly oppose this, requiring a significant fraction of these massive stars to successfully eject their oxygen layers [Suzuki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...852..101S/abstract). 

Other hydrogen-rich subtypes reflect distinct progenitor structures or circumstellar environments. Type IIL events display a linear post-peak decline, though their specific progenitors remain elusive [Arcavi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book..239A/abstract). Type IIn supernovae exhibit narrow emission lines indicative of strong shock interaction with a dense circumstellar medium, frequently linked to eruptive mass loss from luminous blue variables [Arcavi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book..239A/abstract), [Vink et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007RMxAC..30...17V/abstract). Some proposed $\eta$ Carinae analogs suggest that a subset of these Type IIn events may not actually be terminal core-collapse supernovae, but rather cataclysmic, non-terminal eruptions of very massive stars [van Dyk et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASPC..332...47V/abstract). Although such superluminous events are immensely powerful, studies evaluating their biospheric threat indicate that ionization and ozone depletion from an $\eta$ Carinae-like eruption at a distance of $\sim 2.3$ kpc would likely be negligible for Earth [Thomas et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AsBio...8....9T/abstract). Additionally, a rare subclass of long-rising, SN 1987A-like events—comprising $\approx 1.4\%$ of the volumetric CCSN rate—originates from compact blue supergiants rather than extended red supergiants [Taddia et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...588A...5T/abstract), [Sit et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...959..142S/abstract), [Pastorello et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...537A.141P/abstract).

**Stripped-Envelope Supernovae**
Transients that have lost their outer hydrogen (and sometimes helium) envelopes are collectively termed [[stripped-envelope-supernovae|Stripped-Envelope Supernovae]]. Type IIb initially show hydrogen but transition to helium-dominated spectra, pointing to progenitors that retained only a tenuous hydrogen envelope. Type Ib are entirely stripped of hydrogen, while Type Ic are stripped of both hydrogen and helium [Bianco et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..213...19B/abstract), [Arcavi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...721..777A/abstract). A highly energetic sub-population, [[broad-line-ic-supernovae|Broad-line Ic supernovae]] (SNe Ic-BL), exhibit exceptionally high expansion velocities and are occasionally associated with long-duration gamma-ray bursts [Kelly et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...687.1201K/abstract), [Arcavi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...721..777A/abstract). Detailed photometric observations of Type Ic-BL supernovae, such as SN 2016coi and 2018ebt, have defined their typical class behavior and late-stage decline rates [Tsvetkov et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PZ.....40....1T/abstract). Furthermore, quasi-bolometric modeling of events like SN 2010bh has enabled researchers to rigorously derive key physical parameters, including the ejected nickel mass and total explosion kinetic energy [Cano et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1208.0307C/abstract).

The mechanism driving this extensive mass loss remains a subject of active debate. The classical single-star scenario posits that very massive progenitors ($>30 M_\odot$) shed their envelopes via strong, metallicity-driven stellar winds, appearing as classical Wolf-Rayet stars prior to collapse. This is supported by the observation that standard SNe Ic preferentially erupt in high-metallicity environments where line-driven winds are most efficient [Arcavi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...721..777A/abstract), [Modjaz et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...731L...4M/abstract). Spatial distribution models of spiral galaxies have also been used to constrain the minimum mass of these single-star SN Ic progenitors to approximately $25 M_\odot$ [Raskin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...689..358R/abstract). Conversely, in low-metallicity dwarf galaxies, there is a pronounced excess of SNe Ib, IIb, and Ic-BL relative to SNe Ic, suggesting that reduced wind mass loss allows progenitors to retain some helium or hydrogen [Arcavi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...721..777A/abstract).

However, evidence increasingly favors binary interactions (such as Roche-lobe overflow) as the dominant stripping mechanism for a large fraction of stripped-envelope progenitors. Spatial correlations reveal that Type Ic and Type II supernovae occur in environments with similar molecular gas densities, implying that their progenitors share comparable lifetimes and initial masses, contrary to the strict $>30 M_\odot$ single-star requirement [Solar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatCo..15.7667S/abstract). Furthermore, binary interactions naturally account for the formation of lower-mass stripped cores and provide a unified evolutionary framework for the observed continuum of Type IIb, Ib, and Ic events [Sabach et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...806...73S/abstract), . Supporting the distinction between core-collapse events and [[thermonuclear-explosions|thermonuclear explosions]], statistical analyses of the supernova remnant sample in the Large Magellanic Cloud demonstrate that core-collapse remnants are significantly brighter radio emitters than [[type-ia-supernovae|Type Ia Supernovae]] remnants [Bozzetto et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJS..230....2B/abstract).

It is important to note that not all transients exhibiting stripped-envelope features arise from massive star core collapse. For instance, SN 2005E is representative of a faint class of Type Ib supernovae that originate from low-mass, old progenitors—likely helium-accreting white dwarfs in binary systems—mimicking the spectra of core-collapse events [Perets et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.465..322P/abstract). Similarly, an emerging class of bright, fast-evolving supernovae with extremely low-mass ejecta ($<0.15 M_\odot$), such as SN 2002bj and SN 1885A, are also thought to arise from white dwarf progenitors rather than traditional core collapse [Perets et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...730...89P/abstract). Further complicating progenitor models, observations of the Type Ibn supernova PS1-12sk, which exploded in an elliptical galaxy with no detectable star formation, strongly challenge the assumption that all interacting or stripped events must come from young, massive stars [Hosseinzadeh et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...871L...9H/abstract).

## Explosion Dynamics and Shock Revival

Following [[stellar-core-collapse|Stellar core collapse]], the outgoing shock wave rapidly loses energy to the photodisintegration of heavy nuclei and the escape of neutrinos, causing it to stall within the dense stellar core. In the standard delayed neutrino-heating model, the stalled shock is revived by the deposition of thermal energy from a fraction of the neutrinos emitted by the cooling [[proto-neutron-stars|Proto-neutron stars]] [Roberts et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book.1605R/abstract). However, one-dimensional, spherically symmetric models consistently fail to produce successful explosions. It is now well established that multi-dimensional hydrodynamic instabilities are strictly required to break spherical symmetry, increase the residency time of matter in the gain region, and ultimately drive shock revival [Abdikamalov et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201004356A/abstract).

A critical mechanism in this multidimensional framework is the Standing Accretion Shock Instability (SASI). The SASI induces large-scale, non-radial oscillations of the stalled shock front. These violent hydrodynamic instabilities excite $g$-mode oscillations within the newborn compact objects, which are anticipated to be the dominant source of gravitational wave emission in typical, slowly rotating progenitors. These $g$-modes are excited hundreds of milliseconds after the initial core bounce and can persist for several hundred milliseconds [Ott et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvL..96t1102O/abstract), [Abdikamalov et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201004356A/abstract). The SASI also imprints modulations on the emitted multi-messenger signals, providing a potentially observable diagnostic of internal explosion dynamics prior to shock breakout.

While neutrino heating remains the consensus framework for the majority of core-collapse events, alternative [[explosion-mechanisms|Explosion mechanisms]] are necessary to explain highly energetic or strongly asymmetric explosions. In rare, rapidly rotating progenitors with strong magnetic fields, magnetorotationally driven supernovae can launch bipolar jets, potentially serving as a crucial site for [[r-process-nucleosynthesis-sites|r-process nucleosynthesis]] in the early Galaxy [Winteler et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...750L..22W/abstract). Even in less extreme cases, simulations of jet-driven explosions demonstrate that nonrelativistic bipolar jets with a high thermal-to-kinetic energy fraction can efficiently deposit energy throughout the ejecta. This process generates large-scale asymmetries that successfully reproduce substructures in spectral lines (such as H$\alpha$ and He I $\lambda 10830$) observed in Type II supernovae [Couch et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...696..953C/abstract). Moreover, observations of transients such as EP240414a, a soft X-ray transient linked to a Type Ic supernova, provide compelling evidence for successful but weak relativistic jets, demonstrating that jet-driven dynamics can operate even in progenitors with small core angular momentum [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1073S/abstract).

Currently, the literature exhibits a significant and unresolved tension regarding the primary driver of the explosion in standard supernovae. The Jittering Jets Explosion Mechanism (JJEM) has been proposed as a fundamental alternative to the standard delayed neutrino-heating model. Proponents of the JJEM argue that point-symmetric morphologies observed in several supernova remnants—most notably the jet-shaped rim-nozzle "keyhole" structure in the inner ejecta of SN 1987A—strongly indicate that intermittent, bipolar jets actively shape the ejecta of many core-collapse events [Soker et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..31S/abstract), [Soker et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Galax..12...29S/abstract). This structural point-symmetry is difficult to reconcile with the stochastic, chaotic convective plumes predicted by purely neutrino-driven explosions, leaving the relative dominance of jet-driven versus neutrino-driven shock revival as an area of active debate [Soker et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..31S/abstract).

## Multi-messenger Signatures: Neutrinos and Gravitational Waves

The [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] of a massive star's core generates a profound multi-messenger signal, primarily in the form of neutrinos and gravitational waves (GWs), which escape the progenitor hours to days before the shock breaks out of the stellar envelope. These prompt emissions serve as a direct probe of the internal [[explosion-mechanisms|Explosion mechanisms]] and the deep physical state of the core.

Following core bounce, a hot, dense [[proto-neutron-stars|proto-neutron star]] is formed. Over a period of $\sim 20$ seconds, it cools and contracts, radiating the vast majority of its gravitational binding energy as MeV-scale neutrinos [Roberts et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book.1605R/abstract). The temporal and spectral evolution of this neutrino burst is highly sensitive to the nuclear equation of state, convective instabilities, and the properties of the neutrino winds that dictate subsequent nucleosynthesis. Detailed modeling suggests that underground neutrino observatories (such as Super-Kamiokande, DUNE, and IceCube) can use features in the neutrino light curves to distinguish between spherically symmetric failed explosions and successful, asymmetric shock revivals [Seadrow et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.4710S/abstract). Next-generation facilities like the KM3NeT neutrino telescope enhance this capability by utilizing multi-PMT optical modules to precisely discriminate between true supernova signals and optical background noise, significantly optimizing sensitivity to events within the Galactic bulge [KM3NeT Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251118565K/abstract). 

Because they escape the star immediately, thermal neutrinos serve as a crucial early warning trigger. Historic data from facilities like the MiniBooNE neutrino detector have already placed observational constraints on the Galactic [[core-collapse-supernova-rate|Core-collapse supernova rate]], establishing an upper limit of $<0.69$ events per year based on long-term neutrino searches [Aguilar-Arevalo et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PhRvD..81c2001A/abstract). The SNEWS 2.0 network upgrades modern early-warning capabilities by coordinating global neutrino detectors to optimize automated, low-latency alerts for prompt electromagnetic follow-up [Al Kharusi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NJPh...23c1201A/abstract).

Gravitational waves offer a complementary hydrodynamic probe of the central engine. In typical, slowly rotating progenitors, the dominant GW emission arises from core g-mode oscillations within compact remnants. These oscillations are excited by multi-dimensional fluid instabilities, such as the standing accretion shock instability (SASI) and post-bounce convection, typically beginning hundreds of milliseconds after bounce and lasting for several hundred milliseconds [Ott et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvL..96t1102O/abstract), [Abdikamalov et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201004356A/abstract). In rarer cases where the progenitor core rotates rapidly (periods $\lesssim 2$ seconds prior to collapse), centrifugal deformation and nonaxisymmetric instabilities generate strong, circularly polarized GWs [Hayama et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvL.116o1102H/abstract). Estimates for GW detection ranges vary significantly based on progenitor rotation; standard signals may currently only be visible within the Galaxy, while rapidly rotating models could be detected out to megaparsec distances by third-generation instruments [Abdikamalov et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201004356A/abstract). 

Furthermore, the anisotropic emission of neutrinos induces a distinct, low-frequency permanent strain known as gravitational wave memory. This "neutrino memory" is predicted to be the most detectable component of the overall GW signal for future space-based interferometers like DECIGO, with its polarization angle offering a unique identifier to separate it from matter-induced memory [Choi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240801525C/abstract). 

Crucially, these multi-messenger signals will allow astrophysicists to characterize "dark" collapses, where an electromagnetic transient is either heavily obscured or completely absent. While previous studies have shown that SASI-induced long-duration sinusoidal modulations in the neutrino signal can identify a failed explosion, recent work demonstrates that observing the abrupt termination and mode shifts of GW emission alone can accurately distinguish between an obscured successful explosion, a failed supernova, and an explosion followed by the rapid formation of [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]] via massive [[fallback-accretion|Fallback accretion]] [Powell et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603581P/abstract), [Powell et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025CQGra..42u5002P/abstract).

Beyond the prompt core emission, high-energy neutrinos and gamma rays can be generated days later if the expanding ejecta collides with a dense circumstellar medium, forming a subshock that accelerates cosmic rays. However, recent joint observations by IceCube and the Fermi LAT of the nearby interacting SN 2023ixf placed strict upper limits on this process. Constraining the time-integrated energy flux to $E^2 dN/dE < 0.35$ GeV cm$^{-2}$, these results limit the early cosmic-ray acceleration efficiency in such events to $<10\%$ [Mand et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250707097M/abstract), [Kimura et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...984..103K/abstract). To synthesize these diverse observational channels, researchers increasingly employ parameterized mixing models that tie together ejecta remnant properties, light curves, and transient gamma-ray emissions, providing a holistic multi-messenger framework for quantifying the underlying supernova engine [Fryer et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956...19F/abstract).

## Nucleosynthesis and Chemical Enrichment

Core-collapse supernovae (CCSNe) are the primary engines of chemical evolution in the Universe, responsible for synthesizing and dispersing the majority of the oxygen and $\alpha$-elements (such as Ne, Mg, Si, S, and Ca) found in the cosmos. The nucleosynthetic yields of these explosions depend sensitively on the progenitor's initial mass, metallicity, and explosion energy, leaving distinct chemical signatures in the interstellar medium (ISM) and subsequent stellar generations.

**Oxygen and $\alpha$-Element Production**
Massive stars synthesize vast quantities of oxygen and $\alpha$-elements during their hydrostatic burning phases, which are subsequently ejected in the supernova explosion. This creates a critical observational constraint on stellar death models. For instance, while some theoretical models suggest a "failed supernova" hypothesis—the [[stellar-death-hypothesis|Stellar death hypothesis]] where stars with initial masses $> 17 M_\odot$ collapse directly into [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]] without a bright explosion—galactic chemical evolution models indicate that this would severely underproduce the observed oxygen abundance in the Milky Way. To match the solar oxygen inventory, a significant fraction of stars up to $\sim 100 M_\odot$ must successfully eject their oxygen-rich layers, either through successful shock revival or extensive pre-collapse mass loss [Suzuki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...852..101S/abstract). To properly incorporate these yields into global models, researchers have developed theoretical methods for solving detailed chemical evolution that assume instantaneous recycling for massive stars while elegantly accounting for delayed [[type-ia-supernovae|Type Ia Supernovae]] yields using Laplace transforms [Vincenzo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.2939V/abstract).

**Iron-Peak Elements and Hypernovae**
While [[type-ia-supernovae|Type Ia Supernovae]] are the dominant source of iron-peak elements at late cosmic times (redshifts $z \lesssim 1.4$) [Ettori et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.362..110E/abstract), CCSNe are the sole source of iron-peak enrichment in the early Universe. Normal CCSNe eject moderate amounts of radioactive $^{56}\mathrm{Ni}$. However, explaining the peculiar abundance patterns of extremely metal-poor stars requires the inclusion of [[hypernovae|Hypernovae]]. These highly energetic explosions ($E > 10^{52}$ erg) undergo an enhanced $\alpha$-rich freeze-out, which produces elevated $\mathrm{[Zn/Fe]}$ and $\mathrm{[Co/Fe]}$ ratios, alongside suppressed $\mathrm{[Mn/Fe]}$ and $\mathrm{[Cr/Fe]}$ yields [Nomoto et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003IAUS..212..395N/abstract). Furthermore, [[electron-capture-supernovae|Electron-capture supernovae]] (ECSNe) are necessary to explain the solar abundances of specific, problematic neutron-rich isotopes; models of ECSNe successfully reproduce the solar inventory of $^{48}\mathrm{Ca}$, $^{50}\mathrm{Ti}$, $^{54}\mathrm{Cr}$, and $^{82}\mathrm{Se}$ without introducing anomalous overproductions of other elements [Jones et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...882..170J/abstract).

**r-Process Nucleosynthesis**
The exact identity of the [[r-process-nucleosynthesis-sites|r-process nucleosynthesis sites]] responsible for the rapid neutron-capture process remains heavily debated in the literature. Early models proposed that neutrino-driven winds emanating from cooling [[proto-neutron-stars|Proto-neutron stars]] could provide the requisite neutron-rich conditions [Wanajo et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...577..853W/abstract). However, standard neutrino-driven explosions often fail to reach the necessary entropies or low electron fractions required to synthesize the heaviest r-process elements. Alternatively, magnetorotationally driven supernovae—characterized by rapidly rotating progenitors and intense magnetic fields forming bipolar jets—have been shown to successfully reproduce the second and third solar r-process peaks [Winteler et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...750L..22W/abstract). Detailed isotopic abundance distributions suggest that CCSNe and collapsars likely dominated r-process enrichment in the early Galaxy, prior to the delayed onset of neutron star mergers [Yamazaki et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933..112Y/abstract).

**Galactic Dissemination**
Following the explosion, heavy elements are injected into the ISM, driving the formation of superbubbles and regulating galactic star formation. Galactic supernova feedback can drive powerful winds that carry these heavy elements out to distances of $180$ kpc, significantly enriching the intergalactic medium (IGM) [Binney et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph..8365B/abstract). The chemical imprint of the very first CCSNe can be observed in high-redshift Damped Lyman Alpha (DLA) systems. For example, a DLA at $z = 2.6183$ exhibits an extremely low metallicity ($\mathrm{[C/H]} \approx -2.83$) with a CNO and $\alpha$-element abundance pattern perfectly consistent with enrichment by a single generation of zero-metallicity core-collapse supernovae and hypernovae, capturing the primordial transition of the intergalactic medium before localized star formation dominated the metal budget [Erni et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...451...19E/abstract). In such protogalaxy environments, the mixing dynamics of heavy elements are complex; highly energetic explosions of the first Population III supernovae often lead to low mixing efficiency, allowing pockets of pristine gas to persist [Vasiliev et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARep...56..895V/abstract). Furthermore, the chemical profiles of ancient stellar populations preserve these primordial yields; for example, chemodynamical studies of carbon-enhanced metal-poor stars link their distinct abundance patterns to enrichment by early Population II core-collapse supernovae [Shejeelammal et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.2323S/abstract). In more evolved, larger-scale environments, theoretical supernova explosion models can be ranked by comparing their predicted yields against actual observations of the intracluster medium (ICM). For example, comparisons with Suzaku X-ray abundance ratios in the ICM strongly favor chemical enrichment models dominated by Type II supernovae originating from progenitors with non-zero metallicity [Batalha et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..262...27B/abstract). Feedback from these events also carves out massive [[supershells|Supershells]] that dominate the interstellar structure.

## Circumstellar Medium Interaction and Shock Breakout

The first electromagnetic signature of a core-collapse supernova occurs when the explosion shock wave, driven by the core bounce, breaks through the stellar surface. This event, known as shock breakout, produces a brief but intense flash of ultraviolet (UV) and X-ray radiation. Observations of this radiative precursor provide a direct probe of the progenitor star's initial radius and surface properties. For instance, UV light curves obtained by the GALEX space telescope for the supernova SNLS-04D2dc captured the initial expansion of the star at the beginning of the explosion, directly linking the event to an extended red supergiant progenitor [Schawinski et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008Sci...321..223S/abstract).

Following shock breakout, the rapidly expanding supernova ejecta collide with the circumstellar medium (CSM)—material previously shed by the progenitor star through stellar winds or eruptive mass-loss episodes. This collision drives a high-velocity forward shock into the CSM and a reverse shock back into the ejecta, heating the gas and producing thermal X-ray emission whose luminosity evolves as the shock sweeps through the surrounding density profile [Ramakrishnan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...901..119R/abstract). In cases where the progenitor experienced extreme mass loss, the resulting dense CSM strongly interacts with the ejecta, producing narrow to intermediate-width hydrogen emission lines. This spectroscopic signature defines the Type IIn subclass of [[type-ii-supernovae|Type II Supernovae]]. The [[massive-stars|progenitors]] of Type IIn events are frequently theorized to be Luminous Blue Variables, which can experience episodic mass-loss rates as high as $10^{-2} M_\odot \, \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ prior to [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] [Arcavi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book..239A/abstract), [Hess et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022icrc.confE.809H/abstract).

The dense CSM interaction region is also a theorized site for particle acceleration. The forward shock expanding into a dense wind can potentially accelerate cosmic rays to PeV energies, leading to the emission of TeV gamma rays and high-energy neutrinos via $\pi^0$ decay in hadronic interactions [Hess et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022icrc.confE.809H/abstract). To better understand this process, researchers employ 3D simulations of young core-collapse supernova remnants (such as Cassiopeia A) undergoing efficient particle acceleration. These models are essential for comparing the relative contributions of hadronic and leptonic acceleration components within the complex, asymmetric environments carved by progenitor winds prior to explosion [Ferrand et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016sros.confE..92F/abstract). However, multi-messenger constraints on nearby events suggest that this process may be regulated by strong radiation pressure. For the nearby interacting SN 2023ixf, observations by the IceCube Neutrino Observatory yielded non-detections, establishing time-integrated energy flux ($E^2 dN/dE$) upper limits of $0.35 \, \mathrm{GeV \, cm^{-2}}$ over a 32-day window [Mand et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250707097M/abstract). Corresponding gamma-ray upper limits from the Fermi Large Area Telescope constrain the cosmic-ray production efficiency in the early interaction phase to $<10\%$ [Kimura et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...984..103K/abstract).

## Statistical Distribution and Galactic Feedback

Because [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] with initial masses $\gtrsim 8 M_\odot$ have short evolutionary lifespans, the [[core-collapse-supernova-rate|Core-collapse supernova rate]] closely tracks the cosmic star formation history. Historically, early theoretical rate predictions for [[type-ia-supernovae|SNe Ia]], Ib, and II in the Milky Way and M31 aligned different supernova types with specific progenitor channels, laying the foundation for modern comparative studies by differentiating between single massive stars and binary systems [Matteucci et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ComAp..12..245M/abstract). In the local Universe, the volumetric rate of core-collapse events is approximately $7.0 \times 10^{-5} h_{70}^3 \text{ yr}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-3}$ [Pessi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...703A..34P/abstract), and there is general agreement among various local surveys, which highlights the utility of hydrogen-rich and stripped-envelope events as excellent probes of young stellar populations [Turatto et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASSL..255..361T/abstract). This rate increases significantly with redshift, peaking alongside the cosmic star formation rate to reach $\sim 9.57 \times 10^{-4} h_{70}^3 \text{ yr}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-3}$ at $z \approx 1.1$ [Dahlen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757...70D/abstract). 

Within the Milky Way, gamma-ray measurements of $^{26}\text{Al}$ decay constrain the Galactic core-collapse rate to $3.4 \pm 2.8$ events per century, corresponding to a global star formation rate of $\approx 5 M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$ [Timmes et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...479..760T/abstract). Furthermore, measurements of the core-collapse supernova rate evolution over cosmic time are highly compatible with a standard initial mass function and suggest a minimum progenitor mass threshold of at least $\approx 10 M_\odot$ [Blanc et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008NewA...13..606B/abstract). Core-collapse supernovae act as unambiguous tracers of recent star formation even in early-type elliptical galaxies. The mean specific star formation rate derived from supernova counts suggests that early-type galaxies still contribute a non-negligible fraction ($\sim 11\%$) to the present-day cosmic star formation budget [Sedgwick et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210613812S/abstract). Furthermore, in heavily obscured environments such as Luminous Infrared Galaxies (LIRGs), the use of adaptive optics has enabled the direct discovery of core-collapse supernovae deeply embedded within their nuclear regions [Kankare et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...744L..19K/abstract).

The relative fractions of core-collapse subtypes in the local Universe are heavily dominated by hydrogen-rich Type II events ($\sim 63.2\%$), followed by [[stripped-envelope-supernovae|Stripped-Envelope Supernovae]] ($\sim 32.3\%$) and interacting Type IIn events ($\sim 4.4\%$) [Pessi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...703A..34P/abstract). The occurrence of these events per unit star formation strongly decreases at higher host oxygen abundances [Pessi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...955L..29P/abstract). Metallicity also influences the subtype distribution by modulating stellar wind mass-loss. Normal Type Ic supernovae are preferentially found in the brightest, most metal-rich regions of giant galaxies, whereas dwarf galaxies exhibit a significant excess of Type Ib and [[broad-line-ic-supernovae|Broad-line Ic supernovae]] [Arcavi et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...721..777A/abstract), [Modjaz et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...731L...4M/abstract). However, the literature presents conflicting views on the dominant progenitor channel for stripped-envelope events. While the metallicity dependence points toward wind-driven mass loss in single, very massive stars, recent spatial correlations of molecular gas densities show that Type II and Type Ic progenitors have comparable initial masses and lifetimes, strongly supporting a binary interaction channel for envelope stripping [Solar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatCo..15.7667S/abstract).

Beyond tracing star formation, the kinetic energy injected by core-collapse events—typically $\sim 10^{51}$ erg per explosion—provides critical mechanical and thermal feedback that regulates galaxy evolution [Lozinskaia et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986sswi.book.....L/abstract), [Wang et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..296..273W/abstract). Because massive progenitors predominantly form in clustered environments, sequential explosions in OB associations carve out large cavities known as [[supershells|Supershells]] or superbubbles, which can expand to radii of $200-1000$ pc [Tomisaka et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981Ap&SS..78..273T/abstract). Comprehensive reviews of supernova feedback on the interstellar medium have extensively documented the exchange of mass and energy between supernovae and star-forming molecular clouds. On smaller scales, the rapid expulsion of residual gas by supernovae in young [[star-forming-regions-and-cores|Star-Forming Regions and Cores]] drives cluster dissolution, populating the galactic halo with field stars [Baumgardt et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.384.1231B/abstract). Furthermore, localized feedback in nuclear regions can drastically alter central gas dynamics; hydrodynamical simulations indicate that supernova shells can help remove angular momentum from the central gas, potentially fueling polar disc-like structures and accretion disks around supermassive black holes [Emsellem et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.2468E/abstract), [Palouš et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...644A..72P/abstract).

## Compact Remnants: From Neutron Stars to Black Holes

The catastrophic [[stellar-core-collapse|Stellar core collapse]] of a massive star ultimately leaves behind a dense central remnant. The immediate product of this collapse is a hot, lepton-rich [[proto-neutron-stars|proto-neutron star]] (PNS). Over the first $\sim 20$ seconds post-bounce, the PNS undergoes rapid contraction and lepton depletion, radiating the bulk of its gravitational binding energy as neutrinos [Roberts et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book.1605R/abstract). During this highly dynamic cooling phase, convection and internal oscillations—particularly fluid $g$-modes—excite gravitational waves that act as a direct probe of the dense matter equation of state and the internal hydrodynamics of the collapsing core [Ott et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvL..96t1102O/abstract), [Abdikamalov et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201004356A/abstract).

If the supernova shock is successfully revived, the PNS cools into a stable neutron star. Recent population synthesis studies suggest that the classical rotation-powered pulsar channel may not be the dominant outcome; up to $\sim 50\%$ of core-collapse remnants may be born as magnetars [Pardo-Araujo et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260116159P/abstract). The rapid release of spin-down energy from nascent millisecond magnetars is a leading theoretical engine for extreme transients. A deeper exploration of these objects is provided in [[neutron-star-physics|Neutron Star Physics]]. Beyond the core collapse of massive stars, the Accretion-Induced Collapse of [[ultra-massive-white-dwarfs|massive white dwarfs]] serves as an alternative formation channel for neutron stars.

Conversely, if the neutrino-driven shock fails or if the explosion is too weak to fully unbind the star, the massive [[stellar-remnant|remnant]] will grow via [[fallback-accretion|Fallback accretion]] until it exceeds the [[high-mass-neutron-stars|maximum neutron star mass]], collapsing into a black hole. The exact progenitor mass threshold separating neutron star from black hole formation remains a subject of intense debate, especially given the galactic chemical evolution constraints linking heavy element yields to successful explosions. The relative abundance of these outcomes can be observationally evaluated in dense clusters.

The properties of the resulting [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]] are strongly dependent on progenitor metallicity and mass-loss history. At low metallicities, reduced line-driven stellar winds allow progenitors with initial masses of $90-100 M_\odot$ to retain massive cores. Stellar models incorporating core overshooting suggest these stars can bypass the fundamental [[pair-instability-supernovae|pair-instability]] limit, producing unusually heavy remnants. Because optically dark or "failed" supernovae lack traditional electromagnetic counterparts, multi-messenger astronomy is essential for tracking their evolution. As discussed previously, the abrupt termination of GW emissions offers an unambiguous signature of an event horizon's formation, allowing observers to trace the rapid formation of black holes via fallback without relying on electromagnetic triggers [Powell et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603581P/abstract), [Powell et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025CQGra..42u5002P/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

A major unresolved issue in stellar astrophysics is the mapping of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] to their final compact remnants. While stars with initial masses $\gtrsim 8 M_\odot$ are the standard progenitors for [[type-ii-supernovae|Type II Supernovae]] and [[stripped-envelope-supernovae|Stripped-Envelope Supernovae]], it is actively debated whether there is a strict upper mass limit for successful explosions. Some models predict that stars with masses $\gtrsim 17$--$18 M_\odot$ undergo the [[stellar-death-hypothesis|failed supernova]] process, where they undergo catastrophic [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] directly into [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]], producing no bright electromagnetic transient [Suzuki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...852..101S/abstract). However, this hypothesis creates a severe tension with Galactic chemical evolution models. If a large fraction of these high-mass progenitors fail to explode, the total ejected mass of oxygen falls substantially short of the yields required to explain observed solar and Galactic abundances [Suzuki et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...852..101S/abstract). Consequently, a significant fraction of stars $>17 M_\odot$ must successfully eject their envelopes, leaving the exact physical criteria for black hole formation highly ambiguous.

The diversity of [[explosion-mechanisms|Explosion mechanisms]] also remains an active theoretical frontier. While the delayed neutrino-heating model is the consensus standard, it struggles to completely explain highly energetic or highly asymmetric events. Alternative frameworks, such as the Jittering Jets Explosion Mechanism (JJEM), argue that transient accretion disks launch jets that explode the star, a theory supported by point-symmetric morphologies observed in young supernova remnants [Soker et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Galax..12...29S/abstract), [Soker et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..31S/abstract). Closely tied to the central engine debate is the true magnetar fraction among newborn [[proto-neutron-stars|Proto-neutron stars]]. Recent population synthesis suggests that magnetars may be far more common than historically assumed. The extent to which these ultra-magnetized remnants actively drive the supernova shock versus passively cooling remains an open question.

Resolving these theoretical tensions relies heavily on the future of multi-messenger astronomy. Gravitational waves (GWs) and neutrinos offer unobstructed views of the collapsing core prior to shock breakout. While current GW interferometers are largely restricted to detecting Galactic events, upcoming 3rd generation detectors will be capable of observing GW signals out to the Local Group, and up to megaparsec distances for rapidly rotating progenitors [Abdikamalov et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201004356A/abstract), [Choi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240801525C/abstract). Crucially, these advanced facilities will be uniquely equipped to detect electromagnetically dark events. By analyzing the GW and neutrino memory components, researchers expect to clearly distinguish between obscured successful explosions, failed supernovae, and events characterized by rapid black hole formation [Choi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240801525C/abstract), [Powell et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603581P/abstract), [Powell et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025CQGra..42u5002P/abstract).
