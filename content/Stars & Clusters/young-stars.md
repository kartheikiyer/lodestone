**Young stars** represent the earliest observable phases of stellar evolution, encompassing objects that have recently emerged from their deeply embedded [[stellar-formation-and-protostars|protostellar stage]] but have not yet achieved stable nuclear fusion on the [[zero-age-main-sequence|Zero Age Main Sequence]]. This demographic spans a wide mass range, from [[low-mass-stars|Low-mass stars]] (such as T Tauri stars) and intermediate-mass [[herbig-ae-be-stars|Herbig Ae/Be stars]] to the most [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] ([[o-type-stars|O]] and [[ob-stars|B types]]). For objects below roughly $8 M_\odot$, this phase is characterized by a gradual physical contraction governed by the Kelvin-Helmholtz timescale, tracing specific [[evolutionary-tracks|Evolutionary tracks]]—such as the fully convective [[hayashi-track|Hayashi track]]—across the [[hertzsprung-russell-diagram|Hertzsprung-Russell diagram]]. Physically, these [[young-stellar-objects|Young stellar objects]] are defined by intense magnetospheric activity, actively accreting [[circumstellar-disk-evolution|circumstellar disks]], and the driving of bipolar molecular outflows within dense [[star-forming-regions-and-cores|Star-Forming Regions and Cores]].

Within the broader context of galactic evolution, young stars act as both foundational tracers of recent [[massive-star-formation|star formation]] and primary drivers of interstellar medium dynamics. [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] and their evolved [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]] descendants possess exceptionally short lifetimes but exert a disproportionate influence through intense ionizing radiation and mechanical feedback from supersonic stellar winds [Langer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..107L/abstract). They frequently form in dense, gravitationally bound aggregates known as [[young-massive-clusters|Young massive clusters]], which serve as modern analogs to ancient [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] and provide critical high-pressure laboratories for testing the universality of the initial mass function [Scalo et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986FCPh...11....1S/abstract) [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract). Conversely, the more abundant low-mass population preserves the chemical and physical conditions necessary for [[planetary-and-stellar-system-formation|planetary assembly]], charting the intricate processes of [[circumstellar-disk-evolution|Circumstellar Disk Evolution]] and early-stage stellar flaring. Additionally, near-infrared surveys like the VVV survey have revealed classical Cepheids tracing a thin disk of young stars extending across the [[galactic-bulge-giants|Milky Way's bulge]] [Dékány et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812L..29D/abstract).

This article comprehensively examines the physical mechanisms governing the birth and early evolution of stars. It details their pre-main-sequence contraction, the use of [[stellar-chemical-abundances|surface chemical abundances]] as markers of youth, and their complex kinematics, including the dynamical ejection of [[runaway-stars|Runaway stars]]. Furthermore, it explores the profound effects of [[stellar-populations-by-metallicity|metallicity]] on stellar evolution, highlighting observations of young populations in [[low-metallicity-environments|Low-Metallicity Environments]] such as the Magellanic Clouds. Despite robust observational paradigms, several critical tensions remain at the forefront of the field. Chief among these are the unexpectedly prolonged accretion lifetimes ($>10$-$20$ Myr) observed in metal-poor [[young-stellar-objects|Young stellar objects]], unresolved discrepancies in the [[mass-loss-processes|mass-loss rates]] and theoretical evolutionary pathways of massive stars [Humphreys et al. (1978)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1978ApJS...38..309H/abstract) [Conti et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...274..302C/abstract), and the highly debated origins of apparently "young" alpha-rich stars. Resolving whether these alpha-rich objects represent genuine, localized recent star formation or are merely the masquerading products of [[stellar-mergers|Stellar mergers]] remains a critical open question for models of [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]].

## Pre-Main-Sequence Evolution and Evolutionary Tracks

The transition from an embedded [[stellar-formation-and-protostars|protostar]] to an optically visible pre-main-sequence star begins when the object reaches the stellar birthline. At this stage of late protostellar collapse, the young star has achieved a state of quasi-equilibrium, supported internally by deuterium burning. The subsequent physical contraction toward the [[zero-age-main-sequence|Zero Age Main Sequence]] is governed by the rate at which the star can radiate away its gravitational potential energy. This contraction occurs on the Kelvin-Helmholtz time, defined as:

$$ t_{\rm KH} = \frac{G M_\star^2}{R L} $$

where $M_\star$, $R$, and $L$ are the stellar mass, radius, and luminosity, respectively. The relationship between the formation timescale and $t_{\rm KH}$ fundamentally dictates a star's early evolution, with the dividing line situated at roughly $8 M_\odot$. For [[low-mass-stars|Low-mass stars]] ($< 8 M_\odot$), the protostellar formation time is significantly shorter than $t_{\rm KH}$. Consequently, their early luminosities are accretion-dominated, but they eventually emerge to undergo a distinct, classical pre-main-sequence contraction phase. In contrast, [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] ($> 8 M_\odot$) assemble on timescales longer than their $t_{\rm KH}$. Their luminosities become dominated by core nuclear burning while they are still actively accreting gas, meaning they do not experience a standard, optically visible pre-main-sequence contraction.

For objects below the $8 M_\odot$ threshold, the contraction path is described by [[evolutionary-tracks|Evolutionary tracks]] in the [[hertzsprung-russell-diagram|Hertzsprung-Russell diagram]] (the $T_{\rm eff}$-$L$ plane). Because these young stars have relatively low surface temperatures, their interiors become almost entirely convective. During this phase, the star descends along a vertical [[hayashi-track|Hayashi track]], characterized by a precipitous drop in luminosity while maintaining a nearly constant, cool effective temperature. 

The subsequent evolution along these tracks is strictly mass-dependent. Stars with masses in the range of $0.08 M_\odot \lesssim M_\star \lesssim 0.3 M_\odot$ remain fully convective throughout their entire pre-main-sequence phase. As a result, their [[evolutionary-tracks|Evolutionary tracks]] remain vertical until they ignite core hydrogen and settle onto the [[zero-age-main-sequence|Zero Age Main Sequence]]. 

For stars with $M_\star > 0.3 M_\odot$, the star develops a radiative core before reaching the main sequence. At this juncture, the star departs from its convective [[hayashi-track|Hayashi track]] and undergoes radiative contraction. This late pre-main-sequence stage is characterized by a horizontal leftward movement across the [[hertzsprung-russell-diagram|Hertzsprung-Russell diagram]], indicating an increase in effective temperature at nearly constant luminosity. 

Conversely, for objects at the extreme low-mass end ($M_\star \lesssim 0.08 M_\odot$), core temperatures never reach the threshold required to ignite sustained hydrogen fusion. After their initial deuterium-burning phase on the birthline, these [[sub-stellar-objects|Sub-stellar objects]] merely cool and contract indefinitely, never achieving the thermal equilibrium required to join the [[zero-age-main-sequence|Zero Age Main Sequence]].

## Low and Intermediate-Mass Young Stellar Objects

Low and intermediate-mass [[young-stellar-objects|Young stellar objects]] (YSOs) represent the observable pre-main-sequence phase of stellar evolution, occurring after a protostar has emerged from its deeply embedded envelope but before it reaches the [[zero-age-main-sequence|Zero Age Main Sequence]]. This demographic is broadly divided by mass: [[low-mass-stars|Low-mass stars]] ($M \lesssim 2 M_\odot$) are classified as T Tauri stars, while intermediate-mass objects ($2 M_\odot \lesssim M \lesssim 8 M_\odot$) are known as [[herbig-ae-be-stars|Herbig Ae/Be stars]]. 

A defining physical feature of these systems is the presence of [[circumstellar-disk-evolution|circumstellar accretion disks]]. Material from these disks falls onto the central stellar core, creating surface shocks that dissipate kinetic energy into heat. This active accretion is strongly coupled to the driving of bipolar molecular outflows, which help regulate angular momentum and inject mechanical feedback into the surrounding [[star-forming-regions-and-cores|host molecular clouds]]. Notably, recent nearby discoveries have identified AP Col as the closest known pre-main-sequence star to the Solar System [Riedel et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhDT.......100R/abstract). In the solar neighborhood, the estimated feedback timescale for gas dispersal by YSOs is roughly $1.7$ Myr [Zhou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.1091Z/abstract). 

These young stars are also characterized by intense magnetospheric activity and strong chromospheric emission, frequently exhibiting filled-in Balmer lines [Viana Almeida et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...501..965V/abstract). X-ray and ultraviolet observations reveal extreme magnetic flaring that occurs more frequently in fast-rotating, disk-less YSOs compared to classical T Tauri stars that still retain their accretion disks [Caballero et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AJ....137.5012C/abstract).

Kinematically, these pre-main-sequence populations retain the dynamical signatures of their birth environments and actively trace local spiral structure. Studies of unbound young stellar systems reveal a hierarchical or fractal assembly pattern, directly reflecting the turbulent density structures of their natal molecular clouds [Gouliermis et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASP..130g2001G/abstract). On a larger spatial scale, these young populations are organized into "star complexes" spanning kiloparsecs, which act as the fundamental building blocks of star formation, grouping together diverse clusters and associations [Efremov et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ssfg.book.....E/abstract). Analyses of local YSOs reveal that T Tauri stars possess residual velocity dispersions of $\sigma_{1,2,3} = (9.45, 6.99, 6.61) \pm (0.94, 0.43, 0.32)$ km/s, whereas the intermediate-mass Herbig Ae/Be stars exhibit slightly broader velocity dispersions of $\sigma_{1,2,3} = (13.67, 9.25, 7.26) \pm (2.40, 2.44, 0.88)$ km/s [Bobylev et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200704124B/abstract). In slightly more massive regimes, roughly 12% of B-type stars in the Andromeda galaxy (M31) exhibit H-alpha emission (indicating [[b-type-and-emission-line-stars|Be stars]]). This frequency is lower than that observed in the Milky Way, suggesting that M31 has a higher [[stellar-populations-by-metallicity|metallicity]], as more metal-rich environments drive stronger [[mass-loss-processes|stellar winds]] that deplete angular momentum and suppress the formation of Be stars [Prichard et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.4180P/abstract).

## Massive Young Stars and Wolf-Rayet Populations

[[massive-stars|Massive stars]], primarily corresponding to [[o-type-stars|O-type stars]] and early B-type stars, occupy the uppermost mass range of young stellar populations, with initial masses $M \gtrsim 8 M_\odot$ extending up to $>100 M_\odot$ [Langer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..107L/abstract) [Shenar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241004436S/abstract). Because their nuclear burning timescales are extremely short (with a main-sequence lifetime $t_{\rm MS} \sim 10^7$ yr for a $10 M_\odot$ star), their [[main-sequence-evolution|Main Sequence Evolution]] and post-main-sequence phases occur rapidly. Consequently, they inject massive amounts of energy into the interstellar medium almost simultaneously with their formation.

These young massive stars dominate galactic environments through intense ionizing radiation and powerful mechanical feedback. Their UV continuum flux is responsible for ionizing surrounding gas to form [[giant-h-ii-regions|Giant H II regions]], while the photodissociation of molecular hydrogen clouds by these photons provides a substantial fraction of the neutral H I gas found in galaxy disks [Allen et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..273..183A/abstract). Concurrently, supersonic stellar winds and eventual [[type-ii-supernovae|core-collapse supernovae]] drive a mechanical feedback cycle that regulates local star formation [Langer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..107L/abstract).

The most massive O stars ($M \gtrsim 40 M_\odot$) evolve into [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]] prior to core collapse [Conti et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...274..302C/abstract). These classical Wolf-Rayet objects are hydrogen-depleted, evolved stars characterized by exceedingly high temperatures ($T_{\rm eff} \gtrsim 30,000$ K) and powerful, rapid winds [Shenar et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241004436S/abstract). Within the late-type nitrogen-rich (WN) Wolf-Rayet population, there is a marked structural dichotomy: WN6 and WN7 types are frequently found within binary systems, whereas the cooler WN8 and WN9 stars tend to be more spatially dispersed, highly variable, and are rarely found in binaries [Moffat et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ApJ...347..373M/abstract). While Wolf-Rayet populations dominate mechanical feedback in young clusters, calculating this feedback solely based on their contributions can overestimate the average cluster wind velocity ($\bar{V}_{\rm cl}$) by $500-2000$ km s$^{-1}$ at or above the metallicity of the Large Magellanic Cloud ($Z \ge Z_{\rm LMC}$) [Larkin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251006100L/abstract). At sub-LMC metallicities, integrated cluster wind velocities drop rapidly, with typical values falling to $500-1000$ km s$^{-1}$ [Larkin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251006100L/abstract).

The evolutionary pathways connecting [[o-type-stars|O-type stars]] to their Wolf-Rayet descendants contain several unresolved theoretical tensions. Observational surveys indicate that the ratio of Wolf-Rayet to [[o-type-stars|O-type stars]] is approximately three times higher than predicted by theoretical models assuming continuous star formation [Conti et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...274..302C/abstract). Furthermore, evolutionary models that incorporate stellar rotation frequently fail to explain the observed CNO [[stellar-chemical-abundances|surface abundances]] in Galactic and Magellanic [[ob-stars|OB stars]] [Morel et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009CoAst.158..122M/abstract). It is observationally established, however, that metallicity significantly affects these evolutionary paths: rotation-induced chemical mixing is demonstrably more efficient at lower metallicities, leading to stronger surface nitrogen enrichment in Magellanic Cloud stars compared to their Galactic counterparts [Langer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ARA&A..50..107L/abstract) [Martins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A..31M/abstract). To aid in interpreting these environments, observers have developed empirical infrared excess roadmaps for massive stars in the Magellanic Clouds, providing a crucial tool for analyzing resolved [[stellar-populations|Stellar Populations]] across various evolutionary stages [Bonanos et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011BSRSL..80..346B/abstract).

Finally, magnetic fields play a critical role in the mass loss and angular momentum evolution of a small fraction of massive young stars. Approximately 7% of Galactic OB stars host strong, stable fossil magnetic fields [Bagnulo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...635A.163B/abstract). This has led to the identification of [[of-p-stars|Of?p stars]] as a distinct class of slowly rotating magnetic [[o-type-stars|O-type stars]], where organized magnetic fields successfully confine the supersonic stellar winds into dense, structured magnetospheres [Wade et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..272..220W/abstract). The legacy of such massive stars extends beyond their short lifetimes; surface abundances in some old [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] stars suggest chemical enrichment by a prior generation of fast-rotating, metal-poor massive stars, often termed "spinstars" [Chiappini et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Natur.472..454C/abstract).

## Young Massive Clusters (YMCs) and Dense Systems

[[young-massive-clusters|Young massive clusters]] (YMCs) are exceptionally dense stellar aggregates characterized by masses exceeding $10^4 M_\odot$ and ages younger than $\sim100$ Myr [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract). Recognized as the high-mass analogs of typical [[open-clusters|Open clusters]], YMCs are increasingly viewed as the modern-day precursors to ancient [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] [Maíz-Apellániz et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..315..231M/abstract). Across nearly five orders of magnitude in mass, these systems follow a shallow mass-radius power-law relationship, $R \propto M^\alpha$, with $\alpha \approx 0.2 - 0.33$ [Choksi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.5492C/abstract). The survival chances of YMCs depend heavily on their internal properties, emphasizing that the shape of the initial mass function (IMF) is the key factor dictating their long-term evolution into stable globular clusters [de Grijs et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0706.1865D/abstract). Supporting this evolutionary link, multiple stellar populations (MPs) characterized by light-element variations have been confirmed in clusters as young as 1.7 Gyr, such as the Large Magellanic Cloud cluster NGC 2173. This discovery suggests that YMCs and ancient globular clusters share fundamental formation processes [Kapse et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927L..10K/abstract).

The Milky Way's Galactic Center hosts three of the most massive young clusters in the local universe, notably the [[arches-cluster|Arches cluster]] and the [[quintuplet-cluster|Quintuplet cluster]], which serve as premier laboratories for testing models of massive stellar evolution [Figer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...49F/abstract). The [[arches-cluster|Arches cluster]], with an age of $2.5 \pm 0.5$ Myr, is a uniquely dense environment containing over 200 [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]] and [[o-type-stars|O-type stars]] [Markakis et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1108.4452M/abstract). The slightly older [[quintuplet-cluster|Quintuplet cluster]] contains stars in a wider variety of evolved states, including luminous blue variables such as the Pistol Star [Figer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...49F/abstract). Expanding the census of these extreme systems, infrared surveys have identified VVV CL074 as one of the farthest known young massive clusters, located approximately 10.2 kpc away near the intersection of the Galactic bar and the Norma arm [Martins et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...627A.170M/abstract). Other prominent Galactic laboratories include the star-forming region NGC 6357 (specifically the Pismis 24 cluster), which serves as a prime environment for studying triggered star formation and confirming the presence of Salpeter-like initial mass functions in young Galactic clusters [Massi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...573A..95M/abstract).

The extreme stellar densities within these clusters make them vital testbeds for [[stellar-dynamics-and-kinematics|Stellar Dynamics and Kinematics]]. In such closely packed environments, dynamical interactions and close encounters are frequent. Numerical simulations of these cluster dynamics are thus crucial not only for stellar kinematics but also for understanding the environmental impact on the formation, stability, and survival of nascent [[planetary-and-stellar-system-formation|planetary systems]] [Olczak et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASPC..453..241O/abstract). Over time, these interactions drive mass segregation, a relaxation process by which kinetic energy equipartition causes the most [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] to sink toward the deep gravitational potential well at the cluster core, while lower-mass stars are scattered outward.

A central question in astrophysics is whether the stellar initial mass function (IMF) is strictly universal or dependent on environmental conditions. Observations of the [[arches-cluster|Arches cluster]] suggest an upper stellar mass cutoff of $\sim150 - 200 M_\odot$ [Figer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...49F/abstract). Furthermore, measurements in the Galactic Center indicate a potentially top-heavy IMF, implying that the extreme high-pressure environment preferentially forms massive stars [Figer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322...49F/abstract). However, the literature remains highly conflicting; other studies argue that the IMF is fundamentally universal, and that apparent environmental variations in YMCs may largely stem from stochastic sampling effects or observational biases [Portegies Zwart et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..431P/abstract) [Scalo et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986FCPh...11....1S/abstract). Furthermore, observational identifications of these massive structures require caution; some apparent clusters, such as RSGC4 (Alicante 8), may not be genuine, gravitationally bound aggregates, but rather composite populations of [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]] and [[asymptotic-giant-branch-stars|asymptotic giant branch stars]] fortuitously aligned along the line of sight [Chun et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167..230C/abstract).

Beyond the Local Group, extreme starburst environments host [[super-star-clusters|Super star clusters]] (SSCs), which are even more massive and compact. For example, the [[blue-compact-dwarf-galaxies|blue compact dwarf galaxy]] NGC 5253 contains nascent SSCs with ages of $\sim1$ Myr and radii $< 0.6$ pc [Smith et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...896...84S/abstract). In such exceptionally dense, gas-rich environments, standard stellar feedback mechanisms can fail. Models indicate that global cluster winds may be completely suppressed because stellar feedback is unable to expel the leftover gas, leaving the young massive stars deeply embedded in a dense residual medium [Silich et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944L..32S/abstract). Over time, these compact [[super-star-clusters|Super star clusters]] are expected to eventually merge, acting as the fundamental building blocks that form the dense stellar cusps of nuclear [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] [Smith et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...896...84S/abstract).

## Chemical Markers of Youth and Nucleosynthesis

The elemental abundances of stellar atmospheres serve as a critical fossil record, encoding both the age of individual stars and the broader chemical enrichment history of [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]]. Because different [[chemical-abundances|nucleosynthetic processes]] operate on different timescales, the relative abundances of specific elements act as sensitive chemical clocks. In addition to broad chemical patterns, [[chemically-peculiar-stars|Chemically peculiar stars]] (CP) stars, such as HgMn and [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]], can serve as high-precision age tracers when modeling the integrated light of unresolved star clusters [Worthey et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...580L...5W/abstract).

For [[low-mass-stars|Low-mass stars]], the surface lithium abundance is one of the most robust empirical indicators of youth. Because lithium is destroyed by proton capture at relatively low core temperatures, convective mixing during pre-main-sequence contraction rapidly depletes it. Consequently, the presence of strong lithium absorption lines is a primary marker for [[candidate-pre-main-sequence-stars|Candidate pre-main-sequence stars]]. Observations of late-type stars in nearby kinematic moving groups and associations demonstrate that strong lithium retention typically implies ages spanning $\sim 10$-$200$ Myr [Bowler et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877...60B/abstract). In the Galactic disk, young stars exhibit an initial contemporary abundance of $\log \epsilon(\mathrm{Li}) = 3.2 \pm 0.1$, significantly higher than the primordial "lithium plateau" ($\log \epsilon(\mathrm{Li}) \approx 2.2$) observed in ancient halo stars . However, age-dating via lithium is complex; its depletion rate is strongly dependent on stellar mass, and anomalies such as the "lithium dip" at effective temperatures of $\approx 6600$ K complicate simple age-abundance correlations .

In [[massive-stars|Massive stars]], the CNO cycle dominates nuclear burning and profoundly alters interior elemental ratios. Through rotational mixing, these heavily processed elements are dredged up to the stellar surface. Spectroscopic surveys reveal that this surface chemical enrichment is highly sensitive to the initial metallicity of the star's environment. For instance, massive [[o-type-stars|O-type stars]] in the low-metallicity Magellanic Clouds show a much stronger maximum surface nitrogen enrichment than their Galactic counterparts, an effect predicted by models where rotational mixing is more efficient at lower metallicities [Martins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A..31M/abstract). Despite this general agreement, a significant tension persists in the literature: standard evolutionary models that include rotation frequently fail to fully reproduce the observed CNO properties of early-type stars in both the Galaxy and the Magellanic Clouds [Morel et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009CoAst.158..122M/abstract).

On a galactic scale, the ratio of $\alpha$-elements to iron, $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$, is a fundamental metric for chemical tagging. Because $\alpha$-elements are rapidly injected into the interstellar medium by [[type-ii-supernovae|Type II Supernovae]] from massive stars, while iron is produced over longer timescales by Type Ia supernovae, young stars are generally expected to exhibit lower, solar-like $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ ratios alongside higher overall metallicities. However, the exact metallicity-age relation in the local disk exhibits substantial scatter, indicating complex local enrichment histories driven by gas inflows and outflows. 

This standard paradigm is currently challenged by the discovery of an apparently "young" $\alpha$-rich population. Asteroseismic data from the Kepler and K2 missions have identified stars with high $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ that appear to be anomalously young. The literature remains divided on their origin. Some authors suggest they represent a genuinely young population, implying that star formation or radial migration proceeded highly unevenly in the Galaxy [Warfield et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....161..100W/abstract). Conversely, recent chemical analyses suggest these objects are not genuinely young but are the products of binary stellar mergers or mass accretion. They exhibit chemical and kinematic properties identical to the old, high-$\alpha$ thick disk, except for anomalous $[\mathrm{C}/\mathrm{N}]$ ratios, which point to binary evolution that artificially inflated their asteroseismic masses and, consequently, their derived youth [Grisoni et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.111G/abstract). Additionally, kinematic and chemical analyses have revealed evidence for a distinct third stellar population in the Milky Way's disk, the metal-weak thick disk (MWTD), which exhibits a rotational lag of $\sim 30$ km s$^{-1}$ relative to the canonical thick disk [Carollo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887...22C/abstract). Beyond the local disk, chemical tagging also helps identify distinct populations such as "Small Accreted Stellar System" (SASS) stars. These extremely metal-poor halo stars possess low neutron-capture element abundances and display retrograde motion, indicating an origin in ancient [[accreted-dwarf-galaxies|dwarf galaxies]] that were accreted by the Milky Way [Andales et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.4712A/abstract). For extremely metal-poor populations, accurate classification between carbon-enhanced metal-poor subclasses—specifically distinguishing CEMP-s stars from CEMP-no stars—is critical to disentangling binary mass transfer events from the true primordial nucleosynthetic signatures of the [[first-star-formation|first generation of stars]] [Goswami et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024BSRSL..93..406G/abstract). Further complicating this analysis, some apparent ultra-metal-poor stars may not reflect primordial yields at all; instead, they could be chemically peculiar post-AGB or [[lambda-bootis-stars|Lambda Bootis stars]] whose anomalous abundances result from dust-gas separation in their envelopes [Venn et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...677..572V/abstract).

## Kinematics: Runaway and Hypervelocity Stars

Young stars found far from their birthplaces in [[star-clusters|Star clusters]] or active [[star-forming-regions-and-cores|Star-Forming Regions and Cores]] are primarily classified as [[runaway-stars|Runaway stars]]. The two classical mechanisms for accelerating these stars to high velocities are binary supernova ejections (BSE), where the primary [[massive-stars|massive star]] explodes and disrupts the binary system, and dynamical ejections (DE), which involve multi-body gravitational interactions within dense cluster environments [Irrgang et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711..138I/abstract) [Irrgang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...620A..48I/abstract). These processes efficiently populate the field with massive [[ob-stars|OB stars]] and contribute significantly to the broader distribution of [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]] [Oey et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASPC..465..431O/abstract).

A subset of these objects, termed hypervelocity stars (HVSs), exhibit extreme space motions often exceeding the local Galactic escape velocity. Historically, accelerating a star to such extreme speeds (e.g., $v > 500$ km s$^{-1}$) was presumed to require a tidal disruption event involving a binary star system and the supermassive black hole at the Galactic Center. Indeed, studies suggest that some [[high-velocity-stars|hypervelocity]] hot subdwarfs (sdO stars) may represent exactly this type of ejected object originating from the supermassive black hole [Heber et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AIPC.1314...79H/abstract). However, high-precision astrometry in the Gaia era has revealed considerable tension with this paradigm. Tracing the 6D phase-space trajectories of several unbound or extremely fast young B-type stars demonstrates that many cannot be traced back to the Galactic Center at any confidence level [Irrgang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...620A..48I/abstract) [Marchetti et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490..157M/abstract).

Young stars launched from the Galactic disk at extreme velocities are instead classified as "hyper-runaways." For example, the runaway B star HIP 60350 boasts a Galactic rest-frame velocity of $530 \pm 35$ km s$^{-1}$, boosted by its ejection in the direction of Galactic rotation, rendering it potentially unbound [Irrgang et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711..138I/abstract). The existence of disk-ejected hyper-runaways is theoretically challenging: the ejection velocities of several non-Galactic Center stars significantly exceed the theoretical upper limits predicted for standard BSE and DE mechanisms. This discrepancy indicates that alternative powerful dynamical accelerators, such as interactions with intermediate-mass black holes within massive [[star-clusters|Star clusters]], may operate in the Galactic disk [Irrgang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...620A..48I/abstract).

The literature surrounding extreme velocity stars is further complicated by significant sample contamination. Spectroscopic and chemical tagging analyses have shown that many late-type "hypervelocity" candidates are actually bound, metal-poor [[giant-stars|Giant stars]] ($-2 \leq [\text{Fe}/\text{H}] \leq -1$ dex) representing the high-velocity tail of the standard stellar halo [Hawkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.1028H/abstract) [Nelson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.tmp.1628N/abstract). Additionally, other studies have demonstrated that some of these false-positive candidates are actually bound, low-mass dwarf stars rather than giants [Yeom et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019JKAS...52...57Y/abstract). Together, these samples generally represent standard components of the stellar halo rather than genuinely ejected young stars.

Conversely, some genuinely unbound young stars have proven extragalactic origins. A prominent example is the massive HVS 3, which kinematic tracing has confirmed originated in the Large Magellanic Cloud rather than the Milky Way [Irrgang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...620A..48I/abstract). Furthermore, population studies of unbound stars have identified extreme velocity sources ($v > 700$ km s$^{-1}$) whose orbits do not intersect the Galaxy at all, suggesting they may represent an intergalactic young stellar population or the debris of disrupted satellite galaxies [Marchetti et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490..157M/abstract).

## Stellar Activity and Young Planetary Systems

The high-energy environments of [[young-stellar-objects|Young stellar objects]] are dominated by intense magnetic activity and frequent stellar flares. These processes govern the radiative feedback in nascent star systems and play a critical role in shaping [[circumstellar-disk-evolution|Circumstellar Disk Evolution]] and the atmospheres of newly formed exoplanets.

Magnetic activity in young stars is driven by the interplay between rapid rotation and convective dynamos. For [[low-mass-stars|Low-mass stars]] of spectral types G, K, and M younger than 300 Myr, flare activity is observed to saturate below a Rossby number of $R_0 < 0.14$ [Feinstein et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168...60F/abstract). Data from the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) encompassing over 26,000 flares from more than 3,000 young stars indicate that the flare frequency distribution slope remains remarkably constant at $\alpha \approx -0.5$ over the first 300 Myr of evolution [Feinstein et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168...60F/abstract). The amplitudes of these events can be extreme; X-ray observations of the [[orionis|σ Orionis]] cluster reveal violent flares lasting up to six days and peaking at eight times the quiescent emission level [Caballero et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AJ....137.5012C/abstract).

The near-ultraviolet (NUV) and X-ray fluxes generated by magnetic reconnection events can severely erode and chemically alter the atmospheres of close-in planets. Modeling this irradiation history is complex; the observed NUV luminosity function of early [[m-type-stars|M dwarfs]] is inconsistent with both a constant star-formation rate and a simple two-parameter power-law age-activity relation [Ansdell et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...798...41A/abstract). Curiously, recent demographic studies suggest that planet-hosting stars exhibit systematically lower flare rates compared to the broader population of active young stars, which may significantly influence the atmospheric retention and survival of short-period exoplanets [Feinstein et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168...60F/abstract).

Detecting exoplanets around young stars is inherently difficult, as vigorous magnetic activity and rapid rotation mask planetary transit and radial velocity signals. However, breakthroughs have occurred, such as the discovery of a dense mini-Neptune orbiting the young (~300 Myr) K2 dwarf HD 18599. This planet sits at the edge of the so-called Neptune Desert, characterized by a mass of $M_p = 25.5 \pm 4.6 M_\oplus$ and a radius of $R_p = 2.70 \pm 0.05 R_\oplus$. These parameters yield a remarkably high bulk density of $7.1 \pm 1.4 \text{ g cm}^{-3}$ [Vines et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.2627V/abstract). Structural models imply a composition heavily dominated by rock and iron (approximately 77%), highlighting the distinct physical properties of young mini-Neptunes compared to mature counterparts [Vines et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.2627V/abstract).

The window for planet formation and early migration is dictated by the lifetime of protoplanetary disks. In the solar neighborhood, gas dispersal is rapid, occurring on the order of $\sim 1.7$ Myr [Zhou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.1091Z/abstract). However, this timeline is highly environmentally dependent. The prolonged disk lifetimes observed in [[low-metallicity-environments|Low-Metallicity Environments]]—where YSOs can remain actively accreting for over 10-20 Myr [Biazzo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025spf..confE...5B/abstract)—significantly extend the epoch available for gas accretion. This interplay between metallicity and disk longevity critically regulates the planetary core accretion process, intertwining with the broader observation that [[giant-planets|Giant planets]] are more frequently found around metal-rich stars [Swastik et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AJ....164...60S/abstract). Interestingly, when examining older, chemically separated populations, the incidence of planet-hosting stars appears to be higher among thick disk stars with $[\text{Fe}/\text{H}] \leq -0.3$ dex compared to their thin disk counterparts, adding another dimension to the relationship between metallicity, galactic environment, and planet formation [Adibekyan et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASPC..472..117A/abstract).

## Star Formation in Low-Metallicity Environments

Star formation in [[low-metallicity-environments|Low-Metallicity Environments]] and [[low-metallicity-galaxies|Low-metallicity galaxies]] provides a critical observational window into the astrophysics of the early Universe. Because the [[first-star-formation|first generation of stars]]—often referred to as Population III stars—formed from primordial, metal-free gas, their environments suffered from inefficient radiative cooling. This lack of cooling is theoretically expected to yield a top-heavy initial mass function heavily biased toward [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] (see [[first-star-formation|First star formation]]). To study analogous conditions in the local Universe, astronomers investigate metal-deficient [[accreted-dwarf-galaxies|dwarf galaxies]], ranging from the Magellanic Clouds to extreme local starbursts like SBS 0335-052E [Hatano et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230403726H/abstract), where heavy-element mass fractions span $Z \approx 1/3$ to $1/50\ Z_\odot$ [Izotov et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ASPC..353..349I/abstract). Evidence for primordial (Population III) stars has also been identified in some of the brightest galaxies of the early universe, such as CR7 [James et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Natur.526...46J/abstract). Additionally, high-redshift Lyman-alpha emitters are actively investigated as potentially harboring zero-metallicity stars, a scenario proposed to explain their extreme equivalent widths exceeding 240 Å [Malhotra et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...565L..71M/abstract). However, cosmological simulations, such as TNG50, indicate that low-metallicity star formation is not strictly confined to the early universe; approximately 20% of stars with $Z < 0.1 Z_\odot$ formed after $z=2$, and some pristine, primordial gas continues to fuel star formation in galaxies even at $z=0$ [Pakmor et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.3602P/abstract). 

In the local Universe, far-ultraviolet catalogs of the Small Magellanic Cloud have mapped the morphologies of these young, metal-deficient environments, revealing highly clumpy structures and broken bars in stellar populations younger than 150 Myr [Hota et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168..255H/abstract). Further emphasizing the complex environmental triggers of star formation, young main-sequence stars (aged 10-50 Myr) have been discovered in the far periphery of the Large Magellanic Cloud. While surveys searched up to 30° from the LMC's center, the confirmed young main-sequence stars are projected between 7° and 13° away, likely formed in situ as a result of recent tidal interactions rather than migrating from the main galactic disk [Moni Bidin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466.3077M/abstract). It should also be noted that the stellar mass function within LMC supergiant shells appears bottom-heavy, suggesting that gas compression in these specific structures may be insufficient to trigger the formation of massive stars [Kokusho et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...953..104K/abstract).

The evolution of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] is fundamentally altered by sub-solar metallicity. Reduced opacity in the stellar atmosphere drastically weakens line-driven stellar winds, altering [[mass-loss-processes|Mass Loss Processes]] and integrated feedback. Theoretical models predict that the average cluster wind velocity, $\bar{V}_{\mathrm{cl}}$, drops rapidly at sub-Large Magellanic Cloud metallicities, necessitating downward revisions of assumed mechanical feedback in early-universe environments [Larkin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251006100L/abstract). Furthermore, standard [[evolutionary-tracks|Evolutionary tracks]] that include rotation frequently fail to replicate the observed CNO surface abundances in Magellanic OB stars [Morel et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009CoAst.158..122M/abstract). To address these gaps, the Hubble UV Legacy Library of Young Stars as Essential Standards (ULLYSES) program was designed to uniformly map the UV spectra of [[o-type-stars|O-type stars]] and B-type stars across nearby [[low-metallicity-galaxies|Low-metallicity galaxies]] [Roman-Duval et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250405446R/abstract). Initial ULLYSES results demonstrate that rotational mixing is significantly more efficient at low metallicity. For instance, [[o-type-stars|O-type stars]] in the Small Magellanic Cloud exhibit stronger surface nitrogen enrichment than their Galactic counterparts of similar surface gravity, confirming predictions that angular momentum transport and chemical mixing are enhanced in metal-poor interiors [Martins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A..31M/abstract).

The altered mass-loss rates at low metallicity also impact advanced evolutionary stages, including the formation of [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]]. The theoretical understanding of the Wolf-Rayet population remains in tension with some observations; for example, it has long been noted that the observed ratio of Wolf-Rayet to [[o-type-stars|O-type stars]] can be up to three times higher than predicted by standard continuous star-formation models [Conti et al. (1983)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...274..302C/abstract). This discrepancy highlights an ongoing debate regarding the exact metallicity dependence of eruptive mass loss and the role of binary interactions in stripping massive stellar envelopes.

At the lower mass end, metal-poor conditions uniquely affect the accretion phases of [[young-stellar-objects|Young stellar objects]]. Standard models of [[circumstellar-disk-evolution|Circumstellar Disk Evolution]] dictate that protoplanetary disks dissipate on timescales of a few million years due to photoevaporation and stellar feedback. However, recent spectroscopic surveys of [[young-stellar-objects|Young stellar objects]] in the Small Magellanic Cloud and the outer Milky Way have revealed a stark contradiction with this paradigm. A significant fraction of these low-metallicity candidates, with masses spanning $M = 0.5 - 2.0\ M_\odot$, remain actively accreting at ages exceeding $10 - 20 \text{ Myr}$ [Biazzo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025spf..confE...5B/abstract). This unexpected longevity suggests that the dispersal of circumstellar gas is significantly retarded in [[low-metallicity-environments|Low-Metallicity Environments]], an observation that severely complicates simple age-activity relations and carries profound implications for planet formation timescales in the early Universe.

## Historical Development and Observation Surveys

The systematic study of young stars and their birth environments began in earnest in the mid-20th century. During World War II, Walter Baade utilized the wartime blackout conditions at the Mount Wilson Observatory to resolve the inner [[galactic-stellar-populations|Galactic stellar populations]]. He established the fundamental distinction between the older, metal-poor spheroidal component (**Population II**) and the younger, metal-rich disk component, designated **Population I**. This framework demonstrated that the most recent epochs of star formation are strictly confined to the Galactic disk and are chemically enriched by the nucleosynthetic yields of prior [[stellar-generations|Stellar generations]]. In contrast to these ongoing young populations, volume-complete surveys of nearby solar-type stars estimate that the Milky Way's thick disk represents a massive, single-burst older population with a local normalization of at least 20% [Fuhrmann et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.384..173F/abstract).

While early classifications relied heavily on optical photometry and spectroscopy, modern observational astrophysics tracks [[young-stellar-populations|Young Stellar Populations]] across the entire electromagnetic spectrum. Because nascent stars are frequently enshrouded in natal molecular clouds, infrared observations are critical for separating them from older field stars. Additionally, historical radio observations, such as those conducted by the Parkes telescope, have played a fundamental role in mapping Galactic H II regions and masers intimately associated with massive star formation [Caswell et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1210.0978C/abstract). The *Spitzer Space Telescope* significantly advanced this demographic work; for example, the SAGE survey of the Large Magellanic Cloud identified over $1,000$ new [[young-stellar-objects|Young stellar objects]] (YSOs). These targets were selected based on their [[ir-stars|infrared excess]], a photometric signature indicative of thermal emission from circumstellar disks and infalling envelopes [Whitney et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AJ....136...18W/abstract). 

In the ultraviolet regime, the *Hubble Space Telescope* has undertaken the largest Director's Discretionary program in its history to build the **UV legacy library** of Young Stars as Essential Standards (ULLYSES). Utilizing approximately $1,000$ orbits, this program provides a comprehensive spectroscopic database. For accreting [[low-mass-stars|Low-mass stars]], the UV spectra provide crucial diagnostics of accretion shocks, stellar winds, and warm disk surfaces. For massive [[o-type-stars|O-type stars]] and B-type stars, the library characterizes wind mass-loss rates at low metallicities, offering templates necessary for the synthesis of integrated stellar populations [Roman-Duval et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250405446R/abstract) [Roman-Duval et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985..109R/abstract).

Massive ground-based spectroscopic surveys have scaled these detailed studies to Galactic proportions. The fifth generation of the **Sloan Digital Sky Survey** (SDSS-V) includes the APOGEE and BOSS Young Star Survey (ABYSS), which aims to systematically observe $\sim 10^5$ young stars with ages $< 30$ Myr. To overcome the heavy extinction in the Galactic plane, target selection relies on a homogeneous synthesis of parameters, including infrared excess, photometric variability, and phase-space kinematics [Kounkel et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..266...10K/abstract).

Astrometry has provided the most recent paradigm shift in the field. The precision of the **Gaia DR2** catalog enabled the full 6D phase-space mapping of [[young-stellar-populations|Young Stellar Populations]]. By tracking proper motions and radial velocities, astronomers can trace the dispersal of unbound young associations and determine the residual velocity ellipsoids of [[candidate-pre-main-sequence-stars|Candidate pre-main-sequence stars]], such as T Tauri and [[herbig-ae-be-stars|Herbig Ae/Be stars]] [Bobylev et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200704124B/abstract). Furthermore, subsequent extensive kinematic surveys in the Gaia era have highlighted significant tensions in standard stellar age-dating techniques. For instance, populations of apparently "young" $\alpha$-enhanced stars identified by asteroseismology and Gaia kinematics exhibit abundance ratios (such as [C/N]) suggesting they may actually be older binary merger products or mass-accretion systems. The ambiguity over whether these objects represent genuinely recent star formation or are merely evolutionary impostors currently complicates the interpretation of the Galactic age-metallicity relation [Grisoni et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.111G/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While our understanding of star formation and galactic assembly has grown considerably, several fundamental questions regarding the evolution of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] and the nature of the Universe's earliest stellar populations remain unresolved.

A major theoretical bottleneck involves **mass-loss uncertainties** in the advanced evolutionary stages of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]]. Although mechanical and radiative feedback in [[young-massive-clusters|Young massive clusters]] is frequently assumed to be dominated by [[wolf-rayet-stars|Wolf-Rayet stars]], recent models highlight extreme gaps in our knowledge of [[red-supergiants|Red supergiants]] and other cool supergiants. The mass-loss rates for these cool supergiants are unconstrained and remain uncertain by a factor of $\sim 20$ or more [Larkin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251006100L/abstract). Because stellar winds dictate the mechanical energy injected into the surrounding interstellar medium, varying the mass-loss assumptions for these evolved stars can alter the inferred average cluster wind velocity, $\bar{V}_{\mathrm{cl}}$, by up to $1000$ km s$^{-1}$. This severe uncertainty currently limits the accuracy of cluster-scale feedback models [Larkin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251006100L/abstract).

In the field of Galactic archaeology, the origin of apparently 'young' **alpha-rich stars** constitutes a highly active and conflicting debate. Standard chemical evolution models predict that stars with high $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ ratios should belong exclusively to ancient, early-forming populations. However, asteroseismic surveys frequently identify a subset of these stars with anomalously young derived ages. It is currently disputed whether these represent a genuinely young population—perhaps indicating uneven radial migration or localized recent star formation—or if their apparent youth is an observational artifact. Studies of their $[\mathrm{C}/\mathrm{N}]$ abundance ratios and kinematic properties strongly suggest that many are actually the products of [[stellar-mergers|Stellar mergers]] or binary mass accretion, events which rejuvenate their asteroseismic appearance [Grisoni et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.111G/abstract). Separate from the disk debate, the discovery of intermediate-age (6-9 Gyr) halo stars from the Gaia-Enceladus/Sausage (GES) debris poses a different challenge. These highly radial stars are not stellar merger products; instead, their existence and distinct properties present new challenges for constraining the timing of the GES merger and the overall accretion history of the Milky Way [Horta et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...971..170H/abstract). However, recent advances in asteroseismic age dating of these ex situ stars suggest that the Gaia-Enceladus/Sausage merger occurred approximately 8 to 10 billion years ago [Borre et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.2527B/abstract).

Finally, efforts to understand the first generation of stars (Population III) rely heavily on finding and interpreting the oldest surviving [[extremely-metal-poor-stars|Extremely metal-poor stars]] in the Milky Way [Bonifacio et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250406335B/abstract). A critical parameter in these studies is the primordial **binary fraction**. To accurately reconstruct the initial mass function and explosive yields of the first stars, observers must distinguish between chemical peculiarities caused by primordial nucleosynthesis (such as in CEMP-no stars) and those resulting from localized mass transfer from an [[asymptotic-giant-branch-stars|asymptotic giant companion]] (such as in CEMP-s stars) [Lucey et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.4049L/abstract). High-resolution spectroscopic surveys indicate that the short-period [[primordial-binaries|binary fraction]] among extremely metal-poor stars is at least $10\%$, and potentially as high as $20\%$ if carbon-enhanced targets are predominantly formed in binaries [Aoki et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015AJ....149...39A/abstract). Recent large-scale surveys observing very metal-poor stars with facilities like LAMOST and Subaru are providing crucial findings on the frequency of short-period double-lined spectroscopic binaries among these targets [Aoki et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931..146A/abstract). Constraining this early binary fraction and identifying the precise physical mechanisms responsible for extremely metal-poor sub-classes remain primary directives for [[first-star-formation|First star formation]] research.
