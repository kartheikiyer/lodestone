## Article

**X-ray binaries** (XRBs) are a class of gravitationally bound stellar systems characterized by immense high-energy emission, typically ranging from $10^{35}$ to $10^{39}$ erg s$^{-1}$. These systems consist of a compact remnant—either a neutron star or a [[stellar-mass-black-holes|stellar-mass black hole]]—that accretes matter from a non-degenerate [[donor-stars|companion star]]. The intense X-ray luminosity is powered by the conversion of gravitational potential energy into radiation as material from the companion is captured via Roche lobe overflow or powerful stellar winds. This infalling matter typically forms a rapidly rotating, geometrically thin accretion disk, emitting thermal X-rays from a multi-temperature blackbody. In systems with highly magnetized neutron stars, the accretion flow is truncated and channeled onto the magnetic poles, producing coherent [[x-ray-pulsars|X-ray pulsars]]. As the observable and long-lived endpoints of massive stellar evolution, XRBs serve as crucial astrophysical laboratories for studying extreme accretion physics, general relativity in strong-field regimes, and the star-formation and dynamical histories of their host galaxies.

The taxonomy of X-ray binaries is fundamentally dictated by the mass and evolutionary state of the companion star, which in turn governs the system's accretion mode and galactic environment. [[high-mass-x-ray-binaries|High-mass X-ray binaries]] (HMXBs) feature massive early-type companions ($M \gtrsim 8 M_\odot$) and are heavily concentrated in regions of recent star formation. This class encompasses transient [[galactic-be-x-binaries|Galactic Be/X binaries]] with decretion disks and wind-fed [[supergiant-x-ray-binaries|Supergiant X-ray binaries]], including the highly variable and actively debated [[supergiant-fast-x-ray-transients|Supergiant Fast X-ray Transients]]. Conversely, [[low-mass-x-ray-binaries|Low-mass X-ray binaries]] (LMXBs) possess low-mass companions ($M \lesssim 1 M_\odot$) and are prevalent in older stellar populations. While many LMXBs evolve from isolated primordial binaries in the galactic field, a disproportionately large fraction forms dynamically through multi-body exchange interactions and tidal captures in the dense cores of globular clusters [Verbunt et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987IAUS..125..187V/abstract). Extreme architectural variants also exist at the margins of these classifications, such as [[ultra-compact-x-ray-binaries|Ultra-compact X-ray binaries]] driven by gravitational wave emission in sub-hour orbits, and high-energy [[microquasars|Microquasars]] capable of launching relativistic radio jets.

This article explores the physical architecture, accretion dynamics, and evolutionary pathways of the diverse X-ray binary population. It examines the mechanisms driving canonical spectral state transitions—from jet-producing hard states to disk-dominated [[soft-states|Soft states]]—and addresses the phenomenological extremes observed in [[ultraluminous-x-ray-sources|Ultraluminous X-ray sources]] (ULXs). While historically hypothesized to harbor intermediate-mass black holes, recent discoveries have demonstrated that a significant fraction of ULXs are instead powered by highly magnetized neutron stars sustaining extreme super-Eddington accretion. The text also traces the life cycle of these systems from their origins as [[massive-binary-stars|Massive binary stars]] through precarious common envelope phases to their ultimate fate as [[double-compact-objects|Double compact objects]] [van den Heuvel et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ASIC..377..233V/abstract). Finally, the article reviews the historical progression of major X-ray surveys and highlights pressing open questions in the field, including the elusive "mass gap" between neutron stars and black holes, the true nature of quiescent galactic sources, and the emerging role of X-ray binaries as continuous gravitational-wave and high-energy neutrino targets in the era of multi-messenger astronomy.

## Classification and System Architecture

The primary classification scheme for X-ray binaries is based on the mass and evolutionary state of the companion (or donor) star. This fundamental property dictates the system's accretion mode, physical architecture, and broader galactic context.

### Classification by Donor Mass
**Low-mass X-ray binaries (LMXBs)**
In [[low-mass-x-ray-binaries|Low-mass X-ray binaries]], the donor star has a mass $M \lesssim 1 M_\odot$ and is typically a main-sequence star, red giant, or white dwarf [McClintock et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985ASSL..113...61M/abstract). Because the donor is less massive than the compact remnant, mass transfer proceeds via Roche lobe overflow, leading to the formation of a stable, geometrically thin accretion disk. These systems belong to older stellar populations and are predominantly found in galactic bulges and globular clusters, where a significant fraction forms dynamically through tidal captures and multi-body exchange interactions in dense cluster cores [Verbunt et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987IAUS..125..187V/abstract), [Pooley et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591L.131P/abstract). 

**High-mass X-ray binaries (HMXBs)**
[[high-mass-x-ray-binaries|High-mass X-ray binaries]] feature early-type (O or B) donor stars with masses $M \gtrsim 8 M_\odot$. They are tightly correlated with regions of recent star formation, making them reliable tracers of galactic star formation rates [Gilfanov et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004NuPhS.132..369G/abstract), [Mineo et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.419.2095M/abstract). The HMXB class is phenomenologically divided into three main sub-categories based on the accretion mechanism:
*   **Be/X-ray binaries (BeXRBs)**: The largest HMXB sub-population, these systems contain a rapidly rotating Be star (see [[galactic-be-x-binaries|Galactic Be/X binaries]]) and, almost exclusively, a neutron star accretor. The neutron star typically occupies a wide, highly eccentric orbit, accreting periodically as it passes through the Be star's equatorial decretion disk to produce transient X-ray outbursts [Ziolkowski et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MmSAI..73.1038Z/abstract).
*   **Supergiant X-ray binaries (sgHMXBs)**: In these systems (see [[supergiant-x-ray-binaries|Supergiant X-ray binaries]]), the compact object accretes continuously from the dense, radiatively driven stellar wind of an OB supergiant, resulting in persistent X-ray emission. Surveys by the INTEGRAL observatory have also revealed a vast population of highly obscured sgHMXBs hidden behind immense columns of intrinsic local absorbing material ($N_H > 10^{23}$ cm$^{-2}$) [Walter et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ESASP.552..417W/abstract), [Chaty et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...484..783C/abstract).
*   **Supergiant Fast X-ray Transients (SFXTs)**: A distinct subclass of sgHMXBs (see [[supergiant-fast-x-ray-transients|Supergiant Fast X-ray Transients]]) characterized by extreme, short-lived X-ray flares that can reach $10^{36}$ erg s$^{-1}$ before returning to a faint quiescent state. This rapid variability is debated and currently attributed either to the accretion of massive clumps in the supergiant's wind or to magnetic gating mechanisms at the neutron star magnetosphere [Walter et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007A&A...476..335W/abstract), [Bozzo et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0811.0995B/abstract).

**Intermediate-mass X-ray binaries (IMXBs)**
Systems with donor masses between $1 M_\odot$ and $8 M_\odot$ are observationally rare. Mass transfer from an intermediate-mass star to a lighter compact object is frequently dynamically unstable due to the mass ratio, leading to a rapid common envelope phase that truncates the X-ray active lifespan; however, models indicate that stable configurations do exist and may power a subset of ultraluminous X-ray sources [Misra et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...642A.174M/abstract). 

### Compact Object Architectures
The nature of the compact remnant further defines the observational signatures of the binary:
*   **Neutron Star Binaries**: Accreting neutron stars often exhibit coherent pulsations (see [[x-ray-pulsars|X-ray pulsars]]) if they possess a strong magnetic field ($B \sim 10^{12}$ G) that channels accreted material onto the magnetic poles. Furthermore, in highly magnetized systems, cyclotron resonant scattering can produce distinct energy-dependent phase lags in their observed pulse profiles [Ferrigno et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...532A..76F/abstract). In older LMXB systems where the magnetic field has decayed, the accretion disk can extend to the neutron star surface. These weakly magnetized neutron stars frequently exhibit Type-I thermonuclear [[x-ray-bursts|X-ray bursts]] as accreted material undergoes unstable runaway fusion [Verbunt et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..265..289V/abstract), driving the systematic detection of rare Galactic transients, such as the over a thousand events chronicled in the three-year catalog of 1084 X-ray bursts by the Fermi Gamma-ray Burst Monitor [Jenke et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...826..228J/abstract).
*   **Black Hole Binaries**: Systems containing a [[stellar-mass-black-holes|stellar-mass black hole]] are dynamically confirmed when the measured mass function of the donor star mandates a compact object mass exceeding the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff limit ($\gtrsim 3 M_\odot$) [Tanaka et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000IAUS..195...37T/abstract). Lacking a solid surface, black hole XRBs do not produce classical X-ray bursts or surface pulsations. Mass threshold constraints for compact remnants suggest that progenitor stars with zero-age masses between 40 and 80 solar masses typically terminate as stellar-mass black holes [van den Heuvel et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984Natur.309..598V/abstract). They share accretion-driven behaviors with their neutron star counterparts, such as exhibiting distinct spectral state transitions (see [[soft-states|Soft states]]) and launching relativistic radio jets (see [[microquasars|Microquasars]]) [Migliari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.366...79M/abstract). 

### Extreme System Configurations
At the extremes of binary evolution, several specialized architectures exist:
*   **Ultra-compact X-ray binaries (UCXBs)**: A subset of LMXBs with ultra-short orbital periods under 80 minutes (see [[ultra-compact-x-ray-binaries|Ultra-compact X-ray binaries]]). The Roche lobe in these tight orbits is so small that the donor must be a hydrogen-deficient degenerate or semi-degenerate star, such as a white dwarf or helium star [Belczynski et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...603..690B/abstract), [Wang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.4654W/abstract). 
*   **Ultraluminous X-ray sources (ULXs)**: [[off-nuclear-sources|Off-nuclear point sources]] exceeding the Eddington luminosity for a typical $10 M_\odot$ black hole ($L_X \gtrsim 10^{39}$ erg s$^{-1}$). While historically thought to harbor intermediate-mass black holes, the discovery of pulsations in several ULXs has confirmed that a significant fraction of these systems are highly magnetized neutron stars undergoing sustained super-Eddington accretion, challenging the prior consensus that all such sources were black holes (see [[ultraluminous-x-ray-sources|Ultraluminous X-ray sources]]) [Bachetti et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.514..202B/abstract), [Atapin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018acps.confE..38A/abstract).

## High-Mass X-ray Binaries: Be and Supergiant Systems

[[high-mass-x-ray-binaries|High-mass X-ray binaries]] (HMXBs) consist of a compact object accreting from an early-type massive donor star, typically with a mass $M \gtrsim 10 M_\odot$. Due to the short lifetimes of massive stars, HMXBs are strongly concentrated in regions of recent star formation, making their collective population a reliable observational tracer of a host galaxy's star formation rate [Gilfanov et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004NuPhS.132..369G/abstract), [Mineo et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.419.2095M/abstract). In the Milky Way, their spatial distribution closely tracks spiral arm tangents [Lutovinov et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ESASP.622..241L/abstract) with a vertical scale height of $h \simeq 85$ pc, slightly larger than the general population of young stars [Lutovinov et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431..327L/abstract). Furthermore, the paucity of detectable mid-infrared bow shocks around HMXBs suggests that most of these systems reside within low-density bubbles created by their parent star clusters [Prišegen et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...621A..37P/abstract). HMXBs are broadly divided into two major subclasses based on the evolutionary state and wind properties of the donor: [[galactic-be-x-binaries|Galactic Be/X binaries]] (BeXRBs) and [[supergiant-x-ray-binaries|Supergiant X-ray binaries]] (sgHMXBs).

BeXRBs constitute the majority of the known HMXB population [Coleiro et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...560A.108C/abstract). These systems feature a rapidly rotating Be star that sheds material into an equatorial decretion disk, accompanied by a compact object—almost exclusively a neutron star—on a wide, eccentric orbit [Ziolkowski et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MmSAI..73.1038Z/abstract). Observational constraints on these systems, such as the mass distribution of BeXRBs in the Small Magellanic Cloud, suggest that at least 30% of the mass donated by a neutron star's progenitor is typically accreted by the companion during formation [Vinciguerra et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.4705V/abstract). Accretion in these systems is distinctly transient. X-ray outbursts are primarily triggered when the compact object passes through the decretion disk near periastron (Type I bursts) or during major dynamical instabilities in the disk (Type II bursts) [Ziolkowski et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MmSAI..73.1038Z/abstract). The population of BeXRB [[x-ray-pulsars|X-ray pulsars]] exhibits a strong spin-period bimodality. While historically hypothesized to arise from either two distinct supernova kick channels or different prevailing accretion modes, recent kinematic analyses contradict both of these hypotheses, indicating that a completely new alternative physical explanation is required [Prišegen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...640A..86P/abstract). 

While the HMXB population overall harbors a variety of architectures—such as M33 X-7, which was identified as the first eclipsing [[stellar-mass-black-holes|black hole]] high-mass X-ray binary [Pietsch et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...646..420P/abstract)—a long-standing puzzle regarding BeXRBs specifically is the apparent scarcity of black holes as companions. Binary population synthesis models suggest this ratio naturally strongly favors neutron stars due to the initial mass function and common envelope survival probabilities [Belczynski et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...707..870B/abstract). Conversely, the discovery of MWC 656—a Be star with a dynamically confirmed black hole companion accreting at a radiatively inefficient rate of $< 1.6 \times 10^{-7}$ times the Eddington limit—suggests that Be-black hole binaries do exist but remain largely "X-ray quiet" and hidden from conventional surveys [Casares et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.505..378C/abstract).

In contrast to the transient disk accretion of BeXRBs, sgHMXBs are typically powered by the continuous capture of material from the strong stellar wind of an OB supergiant. The launch of the INTEGRAL observatory revolutionized the census of these systems by detecting a vast, previously hidden population of highly absorbed sgHMXBs enshrouded in dense, cold stellar winds [Walter et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ESASP.552..417W/abstract), [Chaty et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..422..243C/abstract).

INTEGRAL also established the existence of a distinct, enigmatic subclass known as [[supergiant-fast-x-ray-transients|Supergiant Fast X-ray Transients]] (SFXTs) [Walter et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007A&A...476..335W/abstract). SFXTs display extreme and rapid variability, marked by intense X-ray flares reaching $L_X \sim 10^{36}$ erg/s that last only for hours to a few days, separated by extended periods of deep quiescence. The dominant physical mechanism driving SFXT variability remains a subject of active debate in the literature. One leading hypothesis attributes the flaring to accretion from a highly structured, clumpy stellar wind, where the compact object intercepts discrete wind clumps with masses of $\sim 10^{22}-10^{23}$ g [Walter et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007A&A...476..335W/abstract). Conversely, competing models argue that the variability is regulated by a magnetospheric "gate"; in this scenario, the centrifugal or magnetic barrier of a slowly spinning, highly magnetized neutron star halts steady accretion (magnetic arrest) until the barrier is intermittently breached [Bozzo et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0811.0995B/abstract), [Lutovinov et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431..327L/abstract).

## Low-Mass X-ray Binaries and Ultracompact Systems

[[low-mass-x-ray-binaries|Low-mass X-ray binaries]] (LMXBs) are systems in which a compact object—either a neutron star or a black hole—accretes matter from a companion star with a mass typically $\lesssim 1 M_\odot$. They are strongly associated with older stellar populations, and their galactic abundance scales tightly with the integrated stellar mass of the host galaxy.

Globular clusters are highly efficient LMXB factories due to their high stellar densities, which catalyze binary formation via dynamical processes such as tidal capture and exchange interactions [Verbunt et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987IAUS..125..187V/abstract), [Pooley et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591L.131P/abstract). Observations consistently show that LMXBs are overabundant in globular clusters compared to the galactic field, with metal-rich (red) clusters hosting these systems roughly three times more frequently than metal-poor ones [Sivakoff et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0806.0626S/abstract), [D'Ago et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...567A...2D/abstract). However, the origin of the field LMXB population remains an active area of debate. While some field LMXBs are undoubtedly ejected from globular clusters, studies of early-type galaxies suggest that a substantial fraction must have evolved *in situ* from primordial binaries to account for the observed distribution and lack of correlation with cluster specific frequencies in some environments [Irwin et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...631..511I/abstract), [Fragos et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...683..346F/abstract).

The [[x-ray-luminosity-function|X-ray luminosity function]] (XLF) of LMXBs exhibits a characteristic break near $L_X \sim 2 \times 10^{37}$ erg/s. This flattening at lower luminosities is generally interpreted as a transition in the dominant mass-transferring population. The break separates systems with unevolved main-sequence [[donor-stars|Donor stars]] at the faint end from those with giant donors at higher luminosities, where mass transfer proceeds much faster due to the expansion of the giant companion [Revnivtsev et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...526A..94R/abstract). However, this shape is environmentally dependent; for instance, young post-merger elliptical galaxies host a larger fraction of highly luminous LMXBs ($L_X > 5 \times 10^{38}$ erg s$^{-1}$) compared to older elliptical galaxies, largely lacking this prominent high-luminosity break [Kim et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...721.1523K/abstract).

A distinct and extreme sub-population of LMXBs are the [[ultra-compact-x-ray-binaries|Ultra-compact X-ray binaries]] (UCXBs). These systems are characterized by an ultrashort orbital period, typically less than one hour. At such tight orbital separations, a standard hydrogen-rich main-sequence star cannot fit within its Roche lobe. Consequently, a UCXB must harbor a hydrogen-deficient, degenerate or semi-degenerate white dwarf donor or a helium star [Chen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...900L...8C/abstract), [Wang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.4654W/abstract). Mass transfer in these systems is driven primarily by orbital decay due to the continuous emission of gravitational radiation. Population synthesis models suggest multiple formation channels for UCXBs, including the survival of primordial binaries through multiple common-envelope phases, the accretion-induced collapse of an oxygen-neon-magnesium white dwarf to a neutron star [Belczynski et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...603..690B/abstract), and dynamical formation in globular clusters. Recently, wide triples undergoing the eccentric Kozai-Lidov mechanism have also been proposed as a pathway to dynamically form mass-transferring black hole-white dwarf systems in the galactic field [Xuan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250813264X/abstract).

Because of their tight orbits, UCXBs are critical targets for multi-messenger astronomy. They are prominent sources of continuous, low-frequency ($10^{-4}$ to $10^{-2}$ Hz) gravitational waves. Many known Galactic UCXBs are expected to serve as "verification binaries" for the upcoming LISA mission [Kilic et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASPC..467...47K/abstract), [Chen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...900L...8C/abstract). While neutron star UCXBs are expected to dominate the detections, theoretical models predict a smaller, yet detectable, population of systems containing [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]]. The detection of these systems by LISA would provide unprecedented constraints on the initial parameter space of extreme mass-ratio inspirals and the stability of mass transfer from degenerate donors [Qin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...83Q/abstract).

## Accretion Physics and Spectral State Transitions

X-ray binaries are powered by the conversion of gravitational potential energy into radiation as matter from a companion star falls into the deep potential well of a compact object. This infalling material typically forms an optically thick, geometrically thin accretion disk. In standard disk models, the thermal emission from the inner radii is described by a multi-color blackbody, frequently peaking at an inner disk temperature $kT_{\text{in}} \sim 0.7-1.7$ keV and following a bolometric luminosity relation of $L_X \propto T^4_{\text{in}}$ [Fabbiano et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...725.1824F/abstract). Furthermore, a robust correlation exists between X-ray luminosity, orbital period, and absolute near-infrared magnitude for persistent LMXBs, likely due to X-ray reprocessing in the disk [Revnivtsev et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421.2846R/abstract).

The spectral output of these systems is highly variable and characterized by distinct accretion regimes. State transitions are most prominently observed in [[galactic-black-hole-transients|Galactic black hole transients]], which migrate between regimes driven by variations in the mass accretion rate, $\dot{M}$. This hysteretic evolution during outbursts is standardly visualized using a hardness-intensity diagram, often referred to as a "q diagram" due to its characteristic shape [Agarwal et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220907166A/abstract). In the hard state, the X-ray spectrum is dominated by a non-thermal power-law spectrum (typically with a photon index $\Gamma \sim 1.7 - 2.0$) produced by inverse Compton scattering of soft disk photons in a hot, optically thin corona [Feng et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...650L..75F/abstract), [Cheng et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250712876C/abstract). This state is characteristically associated with the launching of steady relativistic radio jets [Migliari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.366...79M/abstract). Furthermore, multi-wavelength observations have revealed that stellar-mass black hole binaries follow a millimetre fundamental plane of accretion, demonstrating a tight correlation between their millimetre luminosity, X-ray luminosity, and compact object mass [Elford et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260104007E/abstract). Conversely, during high-accretion episodes, systems transition into [[soft-states|Soft states]] (or thermal-dominant states), where the thermal emission of the accretion disk dominates the X-ray flux, the high-energy Compton tail weakens or steepens ($\Gamma \gtrsim 2.5$), and radio jets are strongly quenched [Feng et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...650L..75F/abstract), [Kaaret et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770...20K/abstract), [Poutanen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.445.3987P/abstract). Extreme jet quenching and a purely thermal response are observed in the so-called "hypersoft" state of some [[microquasars|Microquasars]] [Koljonen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..307K/abstract), [Koljonen et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...612A..27K/abstract).

The definitions of these canonical states, however, are ambiguous and frequently break down when applied to the extreme luminosities of [[ultraluminous-x-ray-sources|Ultraluminous X-ray sources]]. The literature shows that standard thin disk models are insufficient for many of these sources. Instead, they exhibit novel spectral states, such as the "broadened disc" or "hard/soft ultraluminous" regimes, which are indicative of super-Eddington accretion rates and optically thick outflows originating in the inner accretion disk [Pintore et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.4271P/abstract), [Salvaggio et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.1814S/abstract).

Accretion dynamics also produce complex temporal variability. Power density spectra of the X-ray light curves often reveal a broken power law at low accretion rates that transitions into a simple power law at higher rates [Barnard et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..8284B/abstract). Embedded within this broadband noise are quasi-periodic oscillations (QPOs) that operate on timescales ranging from hundreds of seconds to milliseconds [Belloni et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0802.0376B/abstract). Low-frequency QPOs (e.g., $20-300$ mHz) are frequently detected in super-Eddington systems and faint transients, offering direct probes of the inner disk geometry and relativistic precession [El Byad et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250516921E/abstract), [Cheng et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250712876C/abstract). In accreting neutron stars, particularly the highly luminous [[z-source|Z source]] class of [[low-mass-x-ray-binaries|Low-mass X-ray binaries]], the evolution of rapid QPO frequencies (varying from $1.3$ to $35$ Hz) is tightly correlated with the source's spectral migration along specific kinematic branches in color-color diagrams [Shirey et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998PhDT........31S/abstract).

## Ultraluminous X-ray Sources (ULXs) and Super-Eddington Accretion

[[ultraluminous-x-ray-sources|Ultraluminous X-ray sources]] (ULXs) are defined as extragalactic, off-nuclear point sources with X-ray luminosities $L_X > 10^{39}$ erg s$^{-1}$, a threshold that ostensibly exceeds the isotropic Eddington limit for a standard $10 M_\odot$ [[stellar-mass-black-holes|stellar-mass black hole]]. Because their extreme luminosities challenge standard accretion theory, ULXs have historically been posited as candidate intermediate-mass black holes (IMBHs) with masses $M \sim 10^2 - 10^4 M_\odot$. Broadly, the ULX population is likely a mixture of systems undergoing thermal-time-scale mass transfer in HMXBs and long-lasting transient outbursts in LMXBs [King et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.335L..13K/abstract). While the subset of hyper-luminous X-ray sources (HLXs) with $L_X > 10^{41}$ erg s$^{-1}$, such as ESO 243-49 HLX-1, remain strong IMBH candidates [Farrell et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AN....332..392F/abstract), [Webb et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012sf2a.conf..631W/abstract), the consensus for the majority of the ULX population has firmly shifted toward super-Eddington accretion onto stellar-mass compact objects.

A major paradigm shift occurred with the detection of coherent X-ray pulsations from the ULX M82 X-2, unambiguously identifying the accretor as a highly magnetized neutron star [Bachetti et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.514..202B/abstract). Reaching peak luminosities of $L_X \approx 1.8 \times 10^{40}$ erg s$^{-1}$, this system accretes at roughly 100 times the Eddington limit for a $1.4 M_\odot$ neutron star. The discovery of further pulsating ULXs established that neutron stars can power a significant fraction of the ULX population, profoundly challenging standard thin-disk accretion models and highlighting the role of strong magnetic fields in modifying the local Eddington limit [Bachetti et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.514..202B/abstract). Additionally, brief, intense ultraluminous X-ray bursts reaching $10^{40}$ erg s$^{-1}$ have been detected in old stellar populations of nearby elliptical galaxies, possibly originating from neutron stars or black holes in ultracompact companion systems [Irwin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.538..356I/abstract).

For the broader ULX population, broad-band X-ray spectroscopy has revealed that their spectral states differ fundamentally from the canonical [[soft-states|Soft states]] and hard states seen in sub-Eddington [[galactic-black-hole-transients|Galactic black hole transients]]. High-quality data from XMM-Newton and NuSTAR show pronounced spectral curvature and a high-energy cutoff above 5 keV, which cannot be accurately modeled by a standard multicolor thin disk [Pintore et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.4271P/abstract). Instead, these sources are typically classified into "broadened disk" or "ultraluminous" spectral states. These states are interpreted as the signature of a super-Eddington accretion flow where a geometrically thick inner disk launches powerful, optically thick outflows, which may geometrically beam the central emission toward the observer [Pintore et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.4271P/abstract), [Salvaggio et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.1814S/abstract). 

The galactic environment plays a crucial role in the formation of ULXs, exhibiting a strong dependence on host metallicity. Multi-wavelength surveys indicate that ULXs are predominantly associated with young stellar populations and are significantly more abundant in low-metallicity, star-forming dwarf galaxies [Linden et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...725.1984L/abstract), [Lehmer et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...907...17L/abstract). This metallicity dependence is largely attributed to the reduced efficiency of radiation-driven stellar winds in metal-poor environments. Weaker winds allow massive progenitor stars to retain a larger fraction of their mass prior to core collapse, enabling the formation of more massive stellar-mass black holes ($M \gtrsim 25 M_\odot$). These heavier black holes require less extreme violations of the Eddington limit to reach ULX luminosities [Mapelli et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1302.2238M/abstract). Consequently, the elevated number of ULXs in low-metallicity galaxies significantly boosts the bright end of the [[high-mass-x-ray-binaries|high-mass X-ray binary]] luminosity function [Lehmer et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...907...17L/abstract). This population likely served as an important source of X-ray feedback during the Epoch of Reionization, where X-ray binaries can effectively heat the intergalactic gas without necessarily expelling it, acting as early as 100 Myr after the onset of a starburst [Justham et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.1641J/abstract), [Brorby et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.448.3374B/abstract), [Sazonov et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.2530S/abstract). Theoretical models and cosmological simulations further demonstrate that black hole X-ray binaries provide significant kinetic feedback in these early low-metallicity galaxies, helping to heat the interstellar medium without expelling large gas fractions [Artale et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.448.3071A/abstract).

## Formation Dynamics in Globular Clusters versus Galactic Fields

The formation and evolutionary pathways of [[low-mass-x-ray-binaries|Low-mass X-ray binaries]] (LMXBs) are strongly dictated by their local stellar environments. In particular, a sharp dichotomy exists between systems that form from primordial binaries in the galactic field and those assembled via dynamical processes in the dense cores of globular clusters.

In the galactic field, LMXBs evolve from isolated, [[binary-systems-and-evolution|primordial binary systems]]. For a field binary to reach the X-ray emitting stage, it must survive several precarious evolutionary phases, including a common envelope phase and the supernova explosion that forms the compact object [Kalogera et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997PASP..109.1394K/abstract). The stringent requirements for survival, particularly the necessity of the binary remaining bound after the natal kick imparted during the supernova, naturally lead to a relatively low formation efficiency for primordial LMXBs. 

In stark contrast, globular clusters are remarkably efficient factories for X-ray binaries. Although globular clusters account for a very small fraction of a galaxy's total stellar mass, they harbor a disproportionately large fraction of its LMXB population [Angelini et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...557L..35A/abstract). This overabundance is driven by the extreme stellar densities found in cluster cores, where the formation of close binaries is dominated by dynamical interactions rather than primordial stellar evolution. The primary mechanisms for dynamical formation include tidal capture—where a compact object dissipates orbital energy through tidal interactions during a close encounter with a main-sequence star—and exchange interactions, wherein a compact object replaces a lighter star in a pre-existing binary [Verbunt et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987IAUS..125..187V/abstract). This process is further enhanced by mass segregation, which causes heavier compact objects, such as neutron stars and [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]], to sink toward the high-density cluster core via dynamical friction. This effectively maximizes their interaction rates with potential [[donor-stars|Donor stars]] [Cheng et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904..198C/abstract). Notably, the overall X-ray emissivity of a stellar population is observed to increase significantly only above stellar densities of $10^4 M_\odot$ pc$^{-3}$ due to this rapid dynamical production; below this critical density threshold, the emissivity is substantially lower and relies more heavily on the underlying metallicity and primordial binary fraction [Heinke et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.5684H/abstract).

Observational evidence strongly supports the dynamical formation scenario. The abundance of LMXBs in a globular cluster scales robustly with its stellar encounter rate [Pooley et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591L.131P/abstract). Furthermore, there is a pronounced metallicity dependence: metal-rich (red) globular clusters are approximately three times more likely to host LMXBs than metal-poor (blue) clusters of similar mass and structural properties [Irwin et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...510L..21I/abstract), [Sivakoff et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0806.0626S/abstract), [D'Ago et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...567A...2D/abstract). 

The divergence in formation channels is also reflected in the [[x-ray-luminosity-function|X-ray luminosity function]] (XLF) of the respective populations. The XLF of globular cluster LMXBs often exhibits a break or flattening at low luminosities, typically near $L_X \sim 10^{37} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$ [Revnivtsev et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...526A..94R/abstract), [D'Ago et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...567A...2D/abstract). This shape differs from the field XLF, prompting debate over the origins of the field population. While some models propose that a fraction of field LMXBs were dynamically formed in globular clusters and subsequently ejected, population models demonstrate that these escapees cannot account for the entirety of the field population [Kremer et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180204895K/abstract). Indeed, studies comparing the total LMXB X-ray luminosity against the specific globular cluster frequency in early-type galaxies reveal that a substantial, independent primordial field population must exist [Irwin et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...631..511I/abstract). Deep X-ray surveys of elliptical galaxies such as NGC 3379 show a distinct lack of low-luminosity cluster LMXBs relative to the field, reinforcing the conclusion that primordial field LMXBs represent a parallel and distinct formation pathway [Fabbiano et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0710.5126F/abstract), [Fragos et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...683..346F/abstract).

## Non-thermal Emission and Relativistic Jets: Microquasars

[[microquasars|Microquasars]] are a specialized class of accreting binaries characterized by the production of highly collimated, relativistic jets. The fundamental connection between the accretion disk and these non-thermal outflows is observationally codified by the radio-X-ray correlation. Black hole systems typically exhibit a non-linear relationship between their radio and X-ray luminosities, suggesting radiatively inefficient accretion flows. In contrast, neutron star binaries are generally less radio-loud at a given $L_X$ and display a steeper correlation slope, providing a quantitative metric to distinguish the underlying accretion physics between different classes of compact objects [Migliari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.366...79M/abstract).

Jet production is intimately tied to the spectral states of the binary. Steady jets are ubiquitous when sources are in the hard state, but they are typically suppressed when the system transitions to disk-dominated [[soft-states|Soft states]]. A prime example of this disk-jet coupling is observed in the [[wolf-rayet-binaries|Wolf-Rayet binary]] Cygnus X-3, where the radio and hard X-ray fluxes are dramatically suppressed during its "hypersoft" state. The quenching of the jet pressure during this state allows the heavy stellar wind of the companion to refill the inner accretion zone [Koljonen et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406..307K/abstract), [Koljonen et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...612A..27K/abstract). In the most extreme microquasars, such as [[ss-433|SS 433]] or the extragalactic candidate S2 in M83, the kinetic power injected into the surrounding interstellar medium by these jets can reach $\sim 10^{40} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$ [Soria et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888..103S/abstract).

A rare, high-energy extension of this phenomenology is found in gamma-ray binaries. The total Galactic [[gamma-ray-binary-population|Gamma-ray binary population]] is estimated at approximately 100 systems [Dubus et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...608A..59D/abstract). These systems are defined by spectral energy distributions that peak in the GeV or TeV regimes. Their high-energy emission is strictly modulated by the orbital period, as demonstrated by the 16.6-day period of 1FGL J1018.6-5856 [Fermi LAT Collaboration et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Sci...335..189F/abstract) and the 10.3-day period of the first extragalactic gamma-ray binary discovered in the Large Magellanic Cloud. In the latter, the radio and X-ray fluxes are observed to vary in anti-phase with the gamma-ray modulation [Corbet et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...829..105C/abstract).

The precise mechanisms driving particle acceleration in gamma-ray binaries remain a subject of active debate in the literature. While the emission is generally attributed to either particle acceleration within a microquasar jet or the collision between a young pulsar wind and the massive companion's stellar wind, theoretical tensions exist when modeling their broadband spectra. For example, the GeV spectrum of LS I +61 303 shows an exponential cutoff that cannot be explained by simple $\gamma\gamma$ absorption, implying that the GeV and TeV emissions may originate from entirely different physical sites, such as the unshocked versus shocked regions of a pulsar wind [Cerutti et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0912.3722C/abstract). Furthermore, strong gamma-ray absorption in the dense radiation field of the massive primary star inevitably triggers $e^+e^-$ pair cascades; these secondary pairs subsequently generate a hard spectrum of synchrotron emission that heavily influences the system's overall radio morphology and variability [Bosch-Ramon et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IJMPD..19..741B/abstract). Recent observations by very-high-energy observatories indicate that microquasars and gamma-ray binaries may also act as Galactic PeVatrons, accelerating particles to $\sim \text{PeV}$ energies and significantly contributing to the structure of the cosmic ray spectral knee [Yue et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241213889Y/abstract).

## Evolutionary Pathways and Double Compact Object Progenitors

The life cycle of an X-ray binary (XRB) represents a transitional phase in the evolution of [[massive-binary-stars|Massive binary stars]] toward their ultimate fate as [[double-compact-objects|Double compact objects]] (DCOs). The pathway from a pair of main-sequence stars to a merging DCO is governed by a sequence of complex interactions, primarily driven by stellar wind mass loss, Roche-lobe overflow, common envelope (CE) evolution, and supernova explosions.

The formation of the first compact object requires the primary star to undergo core collapse. The survival of the binary depends heavily on the mass ejected during the explosion and the specific supernova channels that dictate the magnitude of the natal kicks imparted to the newborn compact object. Observational evidence suggests a dichotomy in kick velocities: while neutron stars (NSs) typically receive large natal kicks ($v \sim 300\mathrm{\,km\,s^{-1}}$), a distinct population of [[high-mass-x-ray-binaries|High-mass X-ray binaries]] (HMXBs) with long orbital periods and low eccentricities implies that some NSs receive kicks of $\lesssim 50\mathrm{\,km\,s^{-1}}$ [Pfahl et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...574..364P/abstract). Furthermore, black holes (BHs) are generally inferred to receive smaller kicks than NSs, with many potentially forming through direct collapse (failed supernovae) [Wang et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457.1015W/abstract). The immediate aftermath of these formations can occasionally be observed directly; for instance, [[circinus-x-1|Circinus X-1]] has been identified as the youngest known X-ray binary by its association with a still-visible natal supernova remnant [Heinz et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...779..171H/abstract). This mechanism helps explain the retention of massive binaries in bound orbits and the existence of BH [[low-mass-x-ray-binaries|low-mass X-ray binaries]] (LMXBs), whose formation is otherwise difficult to reconcile with standard CE theories.

During the X-ray binary phase, the system's orbital architecture is significantly modified by mass transfer from the secondary star. Furthermore, irradiation-driven mass loss from the donor star and the accretion disk can significantly accelerate orbital evolution, reducing X-ray active lifetimes to $10^6-10^7$ years and resolving historical discrepancies with millisecond pulsar populations [Tavani et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991Natur.351...39T/abstract). When the donor expands to overfill its Roche lobe, the mass transfer can become dynamically unstable, leading to a common envelope phase. The drag forces exerted on the compact object as it spirals through the donor's envelope act to drastically shrink the orbital separation, expelling the envelope in the process. The CE phase is critical for producing tightly bound systems capable of merging within a Hubble time. However, in some BH-HMXBs with radiative donor envelopes, stable mass transfer can efficiently strip the donor without entering a CE phase, naturally producing short-period [[wolf-rayet-binaries|Wolf-Rayet binaries]] that may later evolve into close double black holes [van den Heuvel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471.4256V/abstract). 

Alternative evolutionary channels can also dictate the XRB's fate. For instance, an accreting NS in an intermediate- or low-mass X-ray binary may undergo super-Eddington accretion, rapidly gaining mass until it experiences an accretion-induced collapse into a "mass-gap" BH [Gao et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.1054G/abstract). Additionally, systems that successfully expel their hydrogen envelopes and achieve ultra-short orbital periods can form [[ultra-compact-x-ray-binaries|Ultra-compact X-ray binaries]] (UCXBs) driven by gravitational wave radiation, typically involving helium star or white dwarf donors [Wang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.4654W/abstract). In a related regime, [[supersoft-x-ray-sources|Supersoft X-ray sources]], consisting of massive white dwarfs with sustained nuclear burning of accreted matter, represent a major potential progenitor channel for Type Ia supernovae [Li et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997A&A...322L...9L/abstract).

Following the core collapse of the secondary star, the binary—if it is not disrupted by the second supernova—emerges as a DCO. Binary population synthesis models show that only a small fraction of observed XRBs will successfully navigate these evolutionary bottlenecks. For example, simulations of the XRB population in the spiral galaxy M83 suggest that only $1\%-2\%$ of active XRBs will eventually form merging BH-BH, BH-NS, or NS-NS systems within a Hubble time, primarily due to binary disruptions during supernovae or premature mergers during the CE phase [Kotko et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A..85K/abstract). Nevertheless, population synthesis models suggest that the XRBs that do survive may have contributed a small subset (e.g., $\approx 2-5$ events) of the binary black hole mergers currently detected by gravitational-wave observatories [Romero-Shaw et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524..245R/abstract). The ultimate merger of these compact binary progenitors is also deeply connected to short gamma-ray bursts, such as the case of GRB 050509B, which was observed in association with an older, non-star-forming elliptical galaxy [Gehrels et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005Natur.437..851G/abstract).

## Historical Development of X-ray Binary Surveys

The systematic observation of [[stellar-x-ray-binaries|Stellar X-ray binaries]] has evolved dramatically over the past half-century, driven by rapid advancements in space-based X-ray telescope technology. Early all-sky monitors, such as the Uhuru satellite and the Einstein Observatory, established the foundational catalog of bright Galactic sources and initially extended X-ray astronomy into the Local Group [Helfand et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984PASP...96..913H/abstract). In the 1990s, the ROSAT mission provided a highly sensitive soft X-ray ($0.1-2.4$ keV) sky survey, which was instrumental in identifying faint active binaries, cataclysmic variables, and luminous transients in the Magellanic Clouds and M31 [Haberl et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000A&AS..142...41H/abstract), [Pietsch et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.11022P/abstract).

The launches of Chandra and XMM-Newton marked a paradigm shift in extragalactic and crowded-field X-ray astronomy. Chandra’s sub-arcsecond spatial resolution allowed astronomers to definitively isolate individual point sources in dense environments, such as the cores of globular clusters, proving that the abundance of [[low-mass-x-ray-binaries|Low-mass X-ray binaries]] (LMXBs) scales with the stellar encounter rate [Pooley et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...591L.131P/abstract). At the same time, XMM-Newton provided unprecedented high-throughput spectroscopy, allowing detailed studies of accretion state transitions [Fabbiano et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019cxro.book....7F/abstract). Combined, these observatories executed deep Legacy surveys of nearby galaxies, which resulted in the formulation of the universal [[x-ray-luminosity-function|X-ray luminosity function]] (XLF). These surveys conclusively demonstrated that [[high-mass-x-ray-binaries|High-mass X-ray binaries]] (HMXBs) scale linearly with the host galaxy's star formation rate, while LMXBs scale with the total integrated stellar mass [Gilfanov et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004NuPhS.132..369G/abstract), [Inoue et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASJ...73.1315I/abstract). In this context, it was also established that the old stellar populations of elliptical galaxies can be characterized by a universal X-ray emissivity per unit stellar mass, primarily driven by unresolved populations of cataclysmic variables and coronally active binaries [Revnivtsev et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...490...37R/abstract). XMM-Newton also provided critical spectral data that characterized the super-Eddington "broadened disc" states of many [[ultraluminous-x-ray-sources|Ultraluminous X-ray sources]] [Feng et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...650L..75F/abstract), [Pintore et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.4271P/abstract).

In the hard X-ray regime ($>20$ keV), the INTEGRAL observatory fundamentally redefined the Galactic HMXB census. Through repeated deep scans of the Galactic plane, INTEGRAL discovered a vast, previously hidden population of highly obscured [[supergiant-x-ray-binaries|Supergiant X-ray binaries]] whose soft X-ray emission was completely attenuated by dense local stellar winds [Walter et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ESASP.552..417W/abstract), [Walter et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&ARv..23....2W/abstract). Furthermore, INTEGRAL revealed the existence of [[supergiant-fast-x-ray-transients|Supergiant Fast X-ray Transients]] (SFXTs), a distinct subclass characterized by brief, extreme flares ($L_X \sim 10^{36} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$) driven by compact objects interacting with clumpy winds or magnetospheric barriers [Walter et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007A&A...476..335W/abstract), [Bozzo et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0811.0995B/abstract). Beyond HMXBs, INTEGRAL's continuous monitoring of the Galactic bulge constrained the faint end of the LMXB luminosity function down to $\sim 10^{35} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$ [Revnivtsev et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...491..209R/abstract), [Sazonov et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NewAR..8801536S/abstract).

More recently, NuSTAR revolutionized hard X-ray astronomy by deploying focusing optics in the $3-79$ keV band. Extragalactic Legacy surveys utilizing NuSTAR achieved the first spatially resolved hard X-ray maps of external star-forming galaxies, such as M83 and M33, enabling the separation of intermediate-state black hole binaries from background active galactic nuclei, and revealing the nature of specific sources like M33 X-8 [Yukita et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...824..107Y/abstract), [Krivonos et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.2357K/abstract). NuSTAR has also provided strict constraints on the nature of the diffuse X-ray background in the Galactic center, confirming that a significant fraction of unresolved hard emission originates from populations of accreting white dwarfs and quiescent binaries [Kuznetsova et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ASPC..528..371K/abstract). 

Today, the continuous accumulation of multi-epoch data from Chandra, XMM-Newton, and NuSTAR, combined with wide-field scanning from missions like eROSITA [Liu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250510478L/abstract), provides a comprehensive, multi-wavelength view of X-ray binary evolution, spanning from local Galactic transients to primordial populations in the high-redshift universe. This includes the continued development of comprehensive, cross-matched catalogues of Galactic HMXBs that integrate legacy data from the INTEGRAL mission with precise astrometry from the Gaia era [Fortin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...671A.149F/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite decades of extensive multi-wavelength study, several fundamental uncertainties remain regarding the formation pathways of X-ray binaries, the mass distribution of their compact objects, and their potential as multi-messenger astrophysical sources.

**The Mass Gap and Quiescent Black Holes**
Observations of compact objects reveal a dearth of systems in the $2.5-5~M_{\odot}$ "mass gap" between the heaviest neutron stars and the lightest [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]]. However, recent theoretical work suggests that this gap might be populated via the accretion-induced collapse of neutron stars undergoing super-Eddington accretion, a channel that could explain low-mass black holes in systems like GRO J0422+32 [Gao et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.1054G/abstract). 

There is also an ongoing search for "hidden" black hole binaries. Standard [[binary-systems-and-evolution|binary evolution models]] struggle to explain the extreme scarcity of black hole companions in [[galactic-be-x-binaries|Galactic Be/X binaries]]. The discovery of the Be-star/black-hole binary MWC 656 suggests that such systems do exist but are predominantly X-ray quiet; their accretion flows are severely truncated and radiatively inefficient, yielding luminosities $\lesssim 10^{-7} L_{\rm Edd}$ [Casares et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.505..378C/abstract). 

Similarly, the census of isolated accreting black holes in the Galactic field remains strongly constrained. Deep X-ray surveys limit their surface density, implying a combined Bondi-Hoyle mass capture and radiative accretion efficiency of less than $10^{-3}$ [Motch et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MmSAI..83..415M/abstract). However, the recent identification of sources like VLA J213002.08+120904 outside of a globular cluster suggests a large, hidden population of quiescent black hole binaries in the Galaxy that remain undetected by traditional X-ray surveys [Tetarenko et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...825...10T/abstract). In the dense environment of the Galactic Center, Chandra observations have revealed a spatial density cusp of quiescent X-ray sources initially interpreted as a large population of black hole [[low-mass-x-ray-binaries|Low-mass X-ray binaries]] [Hailey et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.556...70H/abstract). Conflicting analyses argued that the emission could alternately arise from a population of transient neutron star binaries with recurrence times exceeding a decade, or from millisecond pulsars [Maccarone et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.2365M/abstract). However, this alternative hypothesis remains highly contested, with subsequent rebuttals defending the original interpretation that these sources are indeed quiescent black holes [Mori et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220409812M/abstract). Furthermore, the unresolved population of Galactic LMXBs, specifically those harboring neutron stars, has been proposed as a potential explanation for both the Fermi Galactic center excess and the enigmatic 511 keV positron-annihilation signal, though definitively attributing these phenomena to binaries remains an active area of investigation [Bartels et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.3826B/abstract).

**ULXs and IMBH Candidates**
The physical nature of the accretors powering the most luminous [[ultraluminous-x-ray-sources|Ultraluminous X-ray sources]] (ULXs) is another major open question. While the discovery of pulsating ULXs definitively proved that highly magnetized neutron stars can sustain accretion at factors of $10-100$ above the Eddington limit [Bachetti et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.514..202B/abstract), the existence of intermediate-mass black holes (IMBHs) within the ULX population is actively debated. Hyper-luminous sources ($L_{X} > 10^{41}$ erg s$^{-1}$) such as HLX-1 in ESO 243-49 provide the strongest current evidence for IMBHs, requiring a central mass $>500~M_{\odot}$ to explain their luminosity without invoking extreme super-Eddington beaming [Farrell et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AN....332..392F/abstract). Quasi-periodic oscillations observed in sources like M82 X-1 further support intermediate-mass accretors [El Byad et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250516921E/abstract), though establishing definitive dynamical masses remains a critical observational hurdle.

**Multi-Messenger Prospects**
As the era of multi-messenger astronomy matures, X-ray binaries are emerging as critical targets. In the gravitational-wave regime, [[ultra-compact-x-ray-binaries|Ultra-compact X-ray binaries]] (UCXBs)—particularly those with helium star or white dwarf donors—are prime guaranteed verification sources for the upcoming Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA) [Chen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...900L...8C/abstract). Population synthesis models predict that LISA may also be capable of detecting a small subset of UCXBs harboring black hole accretors [Qin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...83Q/abstract). 

In the high-energy neutrino sector, [[microquasars|Microquasars]] and gamma-ray binaries are hypothesized to be sites of hadronic cosmic-ray acceleration. Current IceCube observations have placed stringent upper limits on high-energy neutrino emission from Galactic X-ray binaries, but next-generation observatories like IceCube-Gen2 are expected to finally probe the underlying hadronic acceleration physics and jet-wind interactions in these systems with unprecedented sensitivity [Abbasi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930L..24A/abstract). 

Finally, understanding the earliest epochs of X-ray binary formation remains a profound challenge. Future cosmological studies, particularly upcoming 21-cm signal observations, hold the potential to constrain the mass distribution of the first stars (Population III) and the subsequent populations of primordial X-ray binaries that profoundly influenced the intergalactic medium [Gessey-Jones et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1268G/abstract).
