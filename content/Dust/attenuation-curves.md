In extragalactic astrophysics, **attenuation curves** describe the wavelength-dependent reduction of a galaxy's integrated starlight caused by the absorption and scattering of photons by interstellar dust. Unlike a simple [[extinction-law|Extinction law]]—such as the Milky Way-derived [[cardelli-extinction-law|Cardelli extinction law]] or the [[monotonic-smc-extinction-curve|Monotonic SMC extinction curve]]—which quantifies the loss of light along a single, infinitesimally narrow line of sight to a point source, attenuation is an emergent macroscopic property. It accounts for the complex, three-dimensional spatial geometry between heterogeneous stellar populations and the multiphase interstellar medium. Because light originating from embedded birth clouds experiences vastly different levels of obscuration and multiple scattering compared to light from older, diffusely distributed stars, simple [[line-of-sight-corrections|Line-of-sight corrections]] are fundamentally insufficient for modeling whole galaxies. Consequently, the observed shape of an attenuation curve is often dictated more by the macroscopic star-dust geometry and total [[optical-depth|Optical depth]] than by the intrinsic optical properties of individual dust grains.

Accurately characterizing these curves is essential for modeling galaxy spectral energy distributions (SEDs) and deriving fundamental physical properties, such as total stellar mass and obscured star formation rates. Historically, empirical templates like the Calzetti starburst law—which was derived empirically by Calzetti et al. and later shown to be theoretically reproducible using radiative transfer models of clumpy media [Witt et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528..799W/abstract)—served as a universal standard, featuring a shallower ultraviolet (UV) slope than local extinction laws and assuming the absence of the prominent 2175 Å carbonaceous absorption bump. However, modern multi-wavelength surveys have revealed a profound diversity in dust obscuration across different galaxy populations, driving a shift toward flexible [[variable-dust-attenuation|Variable dust attenuation]] models. The exact shape of a galaxy's attenuation curve—parameterized largely by its UV slope and the strength of the 2175 Å bump—is now known to correlate strongly with parameters such as stellar mass, specific star formation rate, and disk inclination [Conroy et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...718..184C/abstract), [Wild et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.417.1760W/abstract), [Kriek et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...775L..16K/abstract). This structural diversity is also the primary driver of scatter in the infrared-to-ultraviolet luminosity ratio (the IRX-$\beta$ relation) [Salim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...23S/abstract), a vital diagnostic that enforces the energy balance between UV starlight absorption and thermal re-emission by dust in the infrared [Jonsson et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...637..255J/abstract).

Today, the study of attenuation curves extends across cosmic time, propelled by next-generation observatories like JWST and ALMA. Recent observations have detected complex dust features, including the 2175 Å bump, in galaxies deep into the epoch of reionization ($z > 7$), challenging traditional timelines for the early enrichment of the interstellar medium. Despite these empirical advances, several critical open questions remain. A central theoretical and computational challenge is breaking the profound degeneracies between stellar population age, metallicity, and dust reddening in spatially unresolved spectra. Furthermore, researchers continue to debate the extent to which the observed evolution of attenuation curves is driven purely by macroscopic geometric effects versus intrinsic physical changes in grain size distributions—such as dust shattering or accretion—in extreme, high-redshift environments.

## Introduction to Dust Attenuation

The wavelength-dependent reduction of the integrated light of a galaxy is termed dust attenuation. Unlike a simple [[extinction-law|Extinction law]], which describes the removal of photons from a single line of sight toward a point source via dust absorption and scattering out of the beam, attenuation curves describe the net loss of photons from a distributed, composite stellar energy distribution. In the context of the interstellar medium (ISM), attenuation incorporates not only the intrinsic optical properties of the dust grains but also the complex spatial distribution of stars relative to dust clouds and the phenomenon of multiple scattering back into the observer's line of sight [Witt et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528..799W/abstract), [Chevallard et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.432.2061C/abstract). Observationally, empirical proxies such as sodium absorption (Na I D) are sometimes employed to trace this dust extinction, with typical Galactic sightlines crossing roughly three distinct dust clouds [Poznanski et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.1465P/abstract).

A fundamental consensus in the literature is that geometry and radiative transfer effects often dominate over intrinsic grain properties in shaping the emergent attenuation curve [Narayanan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869...70N/abstract), [Duarte et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250304906D/abstract). Historically, astronomers sometimes applied a uniform dust screen model using a standard [[cardelli-extinction-law|Cardelli extinction law]] or a [[monotonic-smc-extinction-curve|Monotonic SMC extinction curve]] to correct integrated galaxy spectra. However, this assumption is fundamentally insufficient for spatially unresolved galaxies [Conroy et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...718..184C/abstract), [Lower et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931...14L/abstract). Because of the geometry of the ISM, a single intrinsic extinction law—characterized by a specific total-to-[[selective-extinction|Selective extinction]] ratio, such as $R_V \approx 3.1$—can produce a wide diversity of macroscopic attenuation slopes. Depending entirely on the star-dust geometry and the total [[optical-depth|Optical depth]], effective $R_V$ values for the integrated galaxy can range from $0.5$ to $7$ [Duarte et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250304906D/abstract). 

The architecture of the ISM is typically modeled as dense "birth clouds" embedded in a more diffuse medium, meaning that stellar populations of different ages experience varying levels of obscuration [Chevallard et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.432.2061C/abstract), [Narayanan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869...70N/abstract). Young, UV-bright O and B stars are heavily embedded in optically thick molecular clouds, whereas older stellar populations reside in regions with lower diffuse dust column densities. Furthermore, multiple scattering within clumpy media can lead to geometric saturation, sometimes producing "gray" (wavelength-independent) attenuation where light preferentially escapes through low-opacity holes in the dust distribution [Witt et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528..799W/abstract). This complex topology necessitates the use of [[variable-dust-attenuation|Variable dust attenuation]] models rather than strict [[line-of-sight-corrections|Line-of-sight corrections]] when fitting the integrated photometry or spectroscopy of galaxies.

Consequently, the resulting attenuation curve of a galaxy is a macroscopic, emergent property. While some ongoing debate exists regarding the exact contribution of intrinsic grain size evolution (e.g., via shattering or accretion) across cosmic time [Li et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160303497L/abstract), the spatial decoupling of stars and dust remains the primary driver of attenuation diversity. Accurately characterizing this curve is critical for modern astrophysics; failing to account for the correct attenuation shape and features (such as the 2175 Å UV bump) can systematically bias derived physical parameters, such as overestimating stellar masses by up to $0.4 \text{ dex}$ [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250110541F/abstract).

## Historical Development and Canonical Laws

Historically, the frameworks used to describe dust obscuration in extragalactic sources were directly adapted from Local Group [[extinction-law|Extinction law]]s, which characterize the loss of light along a single line of sight to a point source. The Milky Way (MW) extinction curve is most famously codified by the Cardelli-Clayton-Mathis (CCM) parameterization, or the [[cardelli-extinction-law|Cardelli extinction law]], which successfully describes approximately 93% of sampled Galactic sightlines [Valencic et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...616..912V/abstract). The CCM law is parameterized primarily by the total-to-selective extinction ratio:
$$ R_V = \frac{A_V}{E(B-V)} $$
where $A_V$ is the total visual extinction and $E(B-V)$ is the [[selective-extinction|Selective extinction]]. Early deep surveys, such as the ANS photometric survey, were among the first to indicate that this Galactic extinction law varies significantly from place to place [Papaj et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992AcA....42..211P/abstract). To facilitate comparisons with other galaxies, early efforts attempted to derive a "Mean Galactic" curve based on observations of very distant stars [Krelowski et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992AcA....42..233K/abstract). The Milky Way curve is characterized by a prominent absorption feature at 2175 Å, generally attributed to carbonaceous grains or polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs). More recently, JWST NIRSpec measurements of the Milky Way extinction law toward Westerlund 2 have further refined our understanding at longer wavelengths, confirming a power-law index $\alpha \approx 1.98$ in the infrared [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964L...3W/abstract).

In contrast to the Milky Way, the [[monotonic-smc-extinction-curve|Monotonic SMC extinction curve]] is steeper in the ultraviolet (UV) and lacks the Milky Way 2175 Å feature entirely [Zafar et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...532A.143Z/abstract). Even within a single galaxy, intrinsic point-source extinction can be highly heterogeneous. For example, Swift/UVOT observations reveal significant spatial variation in both the extinction curve slope and the 2175 Å bump strength even within local star-forming galaxies like the SMC and M33 [Hagen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150406635H/abstract). Recent HST observations of the solar-metallicity galaxy M31 reveal that UV extinction curves can vary significantly along different sightlines, often resembling the 30 Doradus region of the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC) regardless of global metallicity gradients [Clayton et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250708612C/abstract). This includes substantial diversity in the 2175 Å bump strength across at least 20 mapped sightlines, revealing instances where the bump is entirely absent [Yan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251223958Y/abstract).

As extragalactic observations improved, it became evident that applying these point-source extinction laws to integrated galaxy spectra was fundamentally insufficient. Because galaxies contain complex, clumpy dust geometries, the emergent light is heavily modified by multiple scattering and geometric saturation [Witt et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528..799W/abstract), [Narayanan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869...70N/abstract). Early Monte Carlo simulations of high-redshift spheroidal galaxies, or extremely red objects (EROs), corroborated this by demonstrating that the emergent strength of the 2200 Å feature depends strongly on both the total [[optical-depth|Optical depth]] and the specific dust geometry [Cimatti et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997MNRAS.290L..43C/abstract). 

To address this macroscopic complexity, empirical relations were developed specifically for integrated galaxy light. The most canonical of these is the Calzetti law, derived empirically by Calzetti et al. from local UV-selected starburst galaxies. Witt & Gordon (2000) later demonstrated that theoretical Monte Carlo radiative transfer models of clumpy starburst environments can reproduce this empirical law [Witt et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528..799W/abstract). The Calzetti law yields a shallower, grayer UV slope than the MW and SMC extinction laws and intrinsically lacks a 2175 Å bump. For decades, the Calzetti law served as the "universal" canonical template for correcting dust attenuation in high-redshift and star-forming galaxies.

However, the consensus of a universal attenuation law has since broken down. The Calzetti starburst law is frequently found to be a poor fit for normal star-forming galaxies, disk-dominated systems, and many high-redshift populations [Conroy et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...718..184C/abstract), [Wild et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.417.1760W/abstract), [Kriek et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...775L..16K/abstract). Furthermore, the Milky Way 2175 Å feature—once thought to be largely absent in extragalactic integrated light—has now been securely detected in the attenuation curves of low-redshift disk galaxies [Conroy et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...718..184C/abstract), intermediate-redshift star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 0.8$ [Barišić et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903..146B/abstract), high-redshift galaxies at $z > 2$ [Scoville et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800..108S/abstract), and even in early systems at $z \sim 7-8$ observed by JWST [Markov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...679A..12M/abstract). 

Consequently, the literature exhibits tension regarding the exact evolutionary sequence of these curves, but definitively rejects the application of a single static template. Modern spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting has largely moved toward [[variable-dust-attenuation|Variable dust attenuation]] models. These flexible empirical relations treat the UV slope and the 2175 Å bump strength as free parameters, recognizing that attenuation curves are dynamically shaped by the global optical depth, stellar mass, and inclination of the galaxy [Kriek et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...775L..16K/abstract), [Salim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...23S/abstract), [Lower et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931...14L/abstract).

## Physical Drivers: Geometry and Orientation

Because galaxy attenuation integrates over a complex spatial arrangement of stars and dust—incorporating the effects of photons scattering back into the observer's line of sight—radiative transfer and macroscopic geometry often dominate over the intrinsic optical properties of individual dust grains in shaping the emergent curve [Chevallard et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.432.2061C/abstract), [Narayanan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869...70N/abstract). 

Theoretical radiative transfer models demonstrate the extreme sensitivity of the attenuation curve to geometry. A single intrinsic dust [[extinction-law|Extinction law]] (e.g., one with a total-to-selective extinction ratio $R_V \approx 3.1$) can produce effective attenuation slopes ranging from $R_{V,\mathrm{eff}} = 0.5$ to $7$ purely through variations in dust density and spatial distribution [Duarte et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250304906D/abstract). In highly clumpy media, multiple scattering and geometric saturation can even produce "gray" (wavelength-independent) attenuation, severely complicating the interpretation of rest-frame ultraviolet fluxes [Witt et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528..799W/abstract). 

To properly model this spatial complexity, modern spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting typically discards the assumption of a uniform dust screen in favor of two-component models. These models separate the attenuation affecting dense, optically thick stellar birth clouds (which harbor young OB stars) from the more extended diffuse ISM (which attenuates older, lower-mass stars) [Wild et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.417.1760W/abstract), [Lower et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931...14L/abstract). In support of this physical picture, observations provide evidence for an optically thin dust component within H II regions (birth clouds) that selectively attenuates Balmer emission lines, an effect particularly notable in high-mass galaxies [van der Giessen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...662A..26V/abstract). The macroscopic distribution of these populations tightly regulates the curve's shape: an increasing fraction of unobscured young stars effectively flattens the normalized attenuation curve, whereas a high fraction of unobscured old stars steepens it [Narayanan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869...70N/abstract). 

Observationally, galaxy inclination serves as the most direct proxy for line-of-sight geometry. Empirical studies consistently demonstrate that highly inclined (edge-on) disk galaxies exhibit significantly flatter UV-optical attenuation curves compared to their face-on counterparts [Conroy et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...718..184C/abstract), [Wild et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.417.1760W/abstract), [Battisti et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...851...90B/abstract). This flattening is a direct consequence of the increased [[optical-depth|Optical depth]] ($\tau$) through the disk. As $\tau$ increases, the densest central dust clouds become entirely opaque; the emergent flux is instead dominated by less-attenuated stars residing in the outer disk or above the midplane, mimicking a shallower attenuation law [Barišić et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903..146B/abstract), [Nagaraj et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932...54N/abstract). 

Despite the strong consensus that geometry is the primary driver of [[variable-dust-attenuation|Variable dust attenuation]], there remains an active debate regarding the relative contribution of intrinsic dust evolution. While geometric models successfully reproduce the scatter in observed attenuation slopes, some studies argue that physical processes modifying the intrinsic grain size distribution—such as dust shattering enriching the ISM with small grains at high redshifts or in massive galaxies—are required to fully explain the observed steepening of certain curves [Li et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160303497L/abstract), [Lu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250516858L/abstract). Additionally, dynamic processes such as shocks and grain-grain collisions in the ISM can increase the small-to-large grain ratio, leading to "anomalous" extinction curves characterized by large UV deviations [Mazzei et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...527A..34M/abstract). Consequently, some researchers suggest that extinction curves respond time-dependently to local physical conditions—such as intense UV radiation fields and varying gas densities—rather than being strictly fixed by global galactic parameters like metallicity [Cecchi-Pestellini et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...788..100C/abstract). Disentangling this degeneracy between geometric configuration and intrinsic grain properties remains a central challenge in interpreting galaxy spectra.

## Morphological and Environmental Dependencies

The shape and normalization of dust attenuation curves vary significantly across different galaxy populations, reflecting diverse star-dust geometries and intrinsic grain properties. Unlike a simple [[extinction-law|Extinction law]] derived from single sightlines, global galaxy attenuation is a complex function of environmental and morphological factors, including stellar mass, star formation rate (SFR), metallicity, and galaxy inclination.

**Stellar Mass, SFR, and Optical Depth**
Observational constraints show that the overall V-band attenuation ($A_V$) strongly correlates with both stellar mass and SFR [Battisti et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...818...13B/abstract), [Nagaraj et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932...54N/abstract). As the effective [[optical-depth|Optical depth]] increases, attenuation curves generally become shallower (flatter) due to geometric saturation [Salim et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...23S/abstract), [Battisti et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888..108B/abstract). In highly optically thick environments, much of the deeply embedded light is entirely absorbed; the emergent flux is instead dominated by light escaping through low-opacity sightlines or from unattenuated stars in the outer regions. This produces a "greyer" (wavelength-independent) attenuation profile that effectively flattens the curve [Witt et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528..799W/abstract), [Narayanan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869...70N/abstract). 

Beyond specific spectral features, broadband photometric colors also tightly trace these physical properties; for instance, observations from the ZFOURGE survey reveal a tight linear relation between rest-frame UVJ colors and dust attenuation in star-forming galaxies at $z=1-3$ [Forrest et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...818L..26F/abstract). However, there remains an active debate regarding the precise scaling of the ultraviolet (UV) slope with galaxy mass. Some studies report that the UV slope steepens (exhibiting a lower total-to-[[selective-extinction|Selective extinction]] ratio, $R_V$) in more massive galaxies [Zeimann et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...814..162Z/abstract), [Lu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250516858L/abstract). In contrast, other work finds no significant mass dependence at a fixed epoch [Li et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160303497L/abstract), or suggests that stellar mass and star formation rate (SFR) surface densities are more fundamental drivers of the slope than global mass [Maheson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250415346M/abstract), [Sommovigo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990..114S/abstract). 

**Specific Star Formation Rate and Age**
The specific star formation rate (sSFR) and mass-weighted stellar age also dictate curve shapes. Galaxies with higher sSFRs or younger stellar populations tend to exhibit shallower dust curves and weaker 2175 Å UV bumps [Kriek et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...775L..16K/abstract). This flattening may result from intense local radiation fields in young, bursty environments that preferentially destroy small dust grains, thereby shifting the size distribution toward larger grains and producing a greyer attenuation profile [Markov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250412378M/abstract), [Markov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...702A..33M/abstract).

**Metallicity and Gas-to-Dust Ratio**
Variations in gas-phase metallicity and the corresponding gas-to-dust ratio strongly influence the attenuation curve. Metal-rich regions typically show steeper attenuation curves [Duffy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526..904D/abstract). Furthermore, the strength of the 2175 Å UV bump—frequently attributed to carbonaceous grains or polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons—exhibits a positive correlation with oxygen abundance, quantified as $$12 + \log(\text{O/H})$$ [Markov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250412378M/abstract), [Markov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...702A..33M/abstract). This correlation points to a metallicity-driven evolution in the underlying dust composition.

**Morphology and Inclination**
Galaxy inclination is a primary, geometry-driven determinant of attenuation. Highly inclined (edge-on) disk galaxies consistently exhibit flatter UV attenuation curves compared to their face-on counterparts [Wild et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.417.1760W/abstract), [Battisti et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...851...90B/abstract), [Barišić et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903..146B/abstract). Detailed studies of local systems further support this geometric dependence. For instance, the DustKING project expanded UV coverage for nearby galaxies, finding that median attenuation curves in face-on systems like NGC 628 are notably steep with prominent 2175 Å bumps, a shape driven largely by local variations in star-dust geometry [Decleir et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhDT.......129D/abstract). In face-on systems generally, the measured curves are often significantly steeper than the standard [[cardelli-extinction-law|Cardelli extinction law]] derived for the Milky Way. Bulge-dominated galaxies also present steeper optical attenuation slopes than the Milky Way average [Wild et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.417.1760W/abstract). To systematically capture these morphological dependencies, global intrinsic attenuation in spirals can be effectively modeled using principal components based on stellar mass, H I content, and infrared surface brightness [Kourkchi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884...82K/abstract).

**The Dust-Metallicity-Age Degeneracy**
Disentangling these environmental dependencies is complicated by the dust-metallicity-age degeneracy inherent in integrated spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting [Nagaraj et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932...54N/abstract). The scatter in the well-known IRX-$\beta$ relation (infrared excess versus UV slope) is driven almost entirely by the diversity of attenuation curve slopes across different galaxy environments [Salim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...23S/abstract). Consequently, assuming a universal attenuation law can introduce severe systematic errors. For instance, neglecting the presence of the 2175 Å bump or failing to account for [[variable-dust-attenuation|Variable dust attenuation]] slopes can lead to an overestimation of stellar masses by up to $0.4 \text{ dex}$ [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250110541F/abstract), [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.539..109F/abstract).

## Key Spectral Features: The UV Slope and the 2175 Å Bump

The shape of a galaxy's attenuation curve in the ultraviolet (UV) is primarily characterized by the UV spectral slope and the strength of the 2175 Å absorption feature. Together, these elements dictate the conversion of rest-frame UV photometry into physical parameters like the star formation rate (SFR) via the infrared excess (IRX)-$\beta$ relation [Mao et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757...52M/abstract), [Salim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...23S/abstract). 

The UV spectral slope is frequently parameterized as a power law relative to the visual attenuation:
$$ A_\lambda = A_V \left( \frac{\lambda}{\lambda_V} \right)^\delta $$
where $\delta$ is the power-law index governing the steepness of the curve. Average attenuation curves derived from large local samples, such as SDSS, are generally consistent with a $\lambda^{-0.7}$ power law, exhibiting minimal variation in this global slope across different stellar mass ranges [Johnson et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJS..173..392J/abstract). However, when exploring diverse individual systems, the UV slope proves to be highly sensitive to both the overall [[optical-depth|Optical depth]] and the star-dust geometry. Galaxies with high specific SFRs or edge-on inclinations generally exhibit higher optical depths, which flattens the attenuation curve ($\delta \to 0$) due to geometric saturation effects [Wild et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.417.1760W/abstract), [Barišić et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903..146B/abstract). Standard Milky Way [[extinction-law|Extinction law]]s, which feature steep slopes and prominent features, are similarly often disfavored for early-type galaxies, where the observed UV emission is likely dominated by evolved stellar populations rather than young, dust-enshrouded stars [Johnson et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJS..173..377J/abstract). Furthermore, the UV slope exhibits measurable redshift evolution. Attenuation curves generally appear flatter at $z > 1.4$, while grain shattering at later cosmic epochs ($z < 1.4$) is thought to enrich the small-to-large grain ratio, leading to the steeper attenuation curves characteristic of many local star-forming galaxies [Li et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160303497L/abstract).

Superimposed on the UV continuum is the 2175 Å bump, a broad absorption feature traditionally associated with complex carbonaceous dust species such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and small graphite grains [Scoville et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800..108S/abstract), [Markov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...679A..12M/abstract). While prominently featured in the Milky Way extinction law, the bump was historically modeled as absent in starburst attenuation laws (e.g., the Calzetti curve). However, modern multi-wavelength surveys have confirmed its ubiquitous presence across a wide range of environments, from local disk-dominated galaxies [Conroy et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...718..184C/abstract) to high-redshift systems in the epoch of reionization ($z \sim 7-8$) observed by JWST [Markov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...679A..12M/abstract), [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250110541F/abstract).

In extragalactic attenuation curves, the amplitude of the 2175 Å bump is typically weaker than in the Milky Way, averaging approximately $33\%$ of the Galactic value [Battisti et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888..108B/abstract). Its strength strongly correlates with the UV spectral slope: galaxies with steeper attenuation curves tend to exhibit stronger UV bumps, while flatter curves show weaker or absent features [Kriek et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...775L..16K/abstract), [Narayanan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869...70N/abstract). Properly accounting for this feature is critical in spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting; omitting the bump can artificially bias the inferred UV continuum slope and lead to the overestimation of stellar masses by up to $0.4$ dex [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250110541F/abstract). The bump's distinct spectral shape can even be utilized as a redshifting signature in broadband photometry, helping to cleanly disentangle the effects of dust from underlying stellar population ages, as demonstrated in DEEP2 galaxy samples at $z \sim 1$ [Conroy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.404..247C/abstract).

Despite these general trends, the literature remains somewhat conflicting regarding the direct correlation between the 2175 Å bump strength and global macroscopic parameters. Some studies report clear detections preferentially in face-on, lower-mass galaxies with low overall attenuation [Barišić et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903..146B/abstract), while others find the feature strongly in highly inclined disks [Conroy et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...718..184C/abstract), [Battisti et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...851...90B/abstract). A leading hypothesis to explain environmental variations in the bump amplitude is the preferential destruction of PAHs and small carbonaceous carriers by intense radiation fields in UV-bright star-forming regions, which would simultaneously weaken the bump and flatten the overall UV slope [Battisti et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...851...90B/abstract), [Markov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250412378M/abstract).

## The IRX-β Relation and Energy Balance

The impact of dust obscuration on the cosmic energy budget is profound: integrated over all galaxy types, only approximately 11% of $0.1 \, \mu\text{m}$ (UV) photons escape their host galaxies, whereas this escape fraction rises to 87% in the near-infrared at $2.1 \, \mu\text{m}$ [Driver et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...678L.101D/abstract). On a cosmological scale, semi-analytic models (SAMs) of the extragalactic background light (EBL) rely on this dust reprocessing to accurately predict the density of infrared photons. The galaxy emissivities modeled in these SAMs are fundamentally linked to the resulting [[optical-depth|Optical depth]] from electron-positron pair production, serving as a powerful constraint on the total history of starlight [Primack et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AIPC.1085...71P/abstract), [Gilmore et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.3189G/abstract). Furthermore, the non-attenuation of gamma rays from active galactic nuclei (AGN) and gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) provides vital upper limits on the EBL, which help 'close the loop' for cosmological galaxy formation models [Primack et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AIPC.1381...72P/abstract). 

The relationship between the infrared excess (IRX) and the ultraviolet (UV) spectral slope ($\beta$) serves as a cornerstone diagnostic for characterizing this dust obscuration in star-forming galaxies. The infrared excess is formally defined as the ratio of total infrared to ultraviolet bolometric luminosity:
$$ {\rm IRX} \equiv \frac{L_{\rm IR}}{L_{\rm UV}} $$
This relationship is fundamentally grounded in the principle of energy balance: interstellar dust absorbs short-wavelength stellar radiation and thermally re-emits the absorbed energy at infrared wavelengths. By explicitly enforcing this energy conservation, modern spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting codes can tightly constrain both the obscured stellar populations and the overall energy budget of a galaxy [Lo Faro et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.1372L/abstract). In extreme environments, such as major galaxy mergers or intense starbursts, dust can absorb and re-process up to $90\%$ of a system's intrinsic bolometric luminosity, with the thermal re-emission by dust in the infrared often modeled as a [[modified-blackbody|Modified blackbody]] [Jonsson et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...637..255J/abstract). Phenomenological SED models of this re-emission indicate that the 20-42 $\mu\text{m}$ mid-infrared continuum acts as the most sensitive tracer of both star formation and general activity levels in normal galaxies [Dale et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...549..215D/abstract).

Originally calibrated using local starbursts, the nominal IRX-$\beta$ relation provides a prevalent empirical recipe to estimate total dust attenuation relying solely on the rest-frame UV slope. This is highly advantageous for high-redshift studies where far-infrared observations are often unavailable. However, normal star-forming galaxies frequently exhibit considerable dispersion around this standard relation, complicating its widespread use [Mao et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757...52M/abstract). 

While earlier studies suggested that variations in star formation histories and stellar population ages contributed significantly to this scatter by altering the intrinsic UV slope, more recent comprehensive analyses indicate that the scatter is driven almost entirely by the diversity of dust attenuation curve slopes. When the attenuation curve slope and the strength of the 2175 Å UV bump are mathematically fixed, the scatter in the IRX-$\beta$ plane effectively vanishes [Salim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...23S/abstract). The dominant physical driver of the curve's slope—and consequently a galaxy's position in the IRX-$\beta$ diagram—is the effective optical depth of the dust column, which is governed by the total dust mass and the complex star-dust geometry [Salim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...23S/abstract).

Furthermore, deviations in the IRX-$\beta$ space are systematic across different galactic environments. For instance, heavily obscured dusty star-forming galaxies (DSFGs) with star formation rates exceeding $50 \, M_\odot \, {\rm yr}^{-1}$ systematically diverge from the nominal local relation. Both locally and at high redshift, these galaxies exhibit bluer rest-frame UV slopes than expected for their substantial infrared luminosities [Casey et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...796...95C/abstract). This behavior suggests that the UV continuum in these extreme systems is dominated by young stars that are only partially enshrouded by the thick dust cocoons responsible for the bulk of the IR emission, highlighting a decoupling between the spatial origins of the UV and IR light.

Despite the inherent diversity in galaxy dust properties, the underlying concept of energy balance remains a robust theoretical tool. Incorporating flexible attenuation curves into SED models that strictly enforce energy balance allows researchers to accurately trace dust re-emission. Doing so is critical for disentangling the complex degeneracies between stellar age, metallicity, and dust obscuration, thereby preventing the systematic misestimation of stellar masses that occurs when an inflexible attenuation law is applied to diverse galaxy populations [Lo Faro et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.1372L/abstract).

## Computational Modeling and SED Fitting

Deriving attenuation curves from integrated galaxy light requires disentangling the intrinsic stellar emission from the modifying effects of dust. This is predominantly achieved through Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) fitting. Historically, SED models assumed a universal attenuation curve (e.g., the Calzetti law), but modern approaches recognize that attenuation shapes vary significantly across galaxy populations, necessitating flexible models [Kriek et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...775L..16K/abstract). Contemporary SED fitting relies on parameterizations that fit the attenuation slope and the strength of the 2175 Å UV bump simultaneously with the star formation history and metallicity. 

Beyond standard photometric SED fitting, alternative spectroscopic reddening measures have been developed. For instance, a three-line approach utilizing the combination of Balmer (H$\alpha$/H$\beta$) and Paschen-$\beta$ emission lines provides a promising technique to independently measure both the slope and the normalization of dust attenuation laws in individual galaxies; however, it currently faces practical limitations, such as differing slit losses, resulting in many galaxies being best fit by unusually steep or shallow slopes, thus requiring highly consistent observational strategies [Prescott et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928...71P/abstract). Similarly, utilizing stellar wind lines like the He II $\lambda 1640/\lambda 4686$ ratio from Wolf-Rayet stars provides a novel method to probe the attenuation of the youngest stellar populations independently of nebular emission lines [Maschmann et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961..159M/abstract). 

Meanwhile, advanced SED modeling codes, such as CIGALE, Prospector, and BAGPIPES, employ flexible prescriptions to capture broader attenuation diversity. CIGALE, which enforces energy balance between ultraviolet (UV) absorption and infrared (IR) emission, has been used to demonstrate that scatter in the IRX-$\beta$ relation is driven almost entirely by variations in the attenuation curve slopes rather than just measurement noise or age differences [Salim et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872...23S/abstract). In high-redshift studies, modified versions of BAGPIPES have fit JWST NIRSpec data at $z \sim 7-8$, demonstrating that the choice of attenuation curve can alter derived stellar masses by up to 0.4 dex [Markov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...679A..12M/abstract), [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250110541F/abstract). Prospector frameworks frequently implement non-parametric star-formation histories paired with two-component dust models—separating dense birth clouds from the diffuse interstellar medium—or non-uniform screen models. Allowing a fraction of stellar light to remain unobscured decouples the UV and optical attenuation slopes, significantly reducing systemic offsets in derived star formation rates [Lower et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...931...14L/abstract), [Maheson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250415346M/abstract).

A fundamental challenge in SED fitting is the degeneracy between stellar population age, metallicity, and dust attenuation. To rigorously account for these correlated variables and non-Gaussian error distributions, recent studies have developed hierarchical Bayesian population models. For example, the empirical "DustE" framework utilizes a Bayesian population model to map dust attenuation parameters as a 5D function of stellar mass, star formation rate, metallicity, redshift, and inclination [Nagaraj et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932...54N/abstract). These population-level approaches reveal that as [[optical-depth|Optical depth]] ($\tau$) increases, attenuation curves generally become shallower due to geometric saturation, though the exact relationship with stellar mass remains highly complex [Nagaraj et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932...54N/abstract). By fitting thousands of galaxies simultaneously, such models provide physically motivated priors that better reproduce the observed distributions of dusty star-forming galaxies than simple independent fits [Nagaraj et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...939...29N/abstract). This is further enhanced by extracting physically motivated priors from large-scale cosmological simulations; for example, synthetic attenuation libraries generated from simulations like IllustrisTNG directly link the expected attenuation curve shape to parameters like the star formation rate surface density [Sommovigo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250213240S/abstract).

Theoretical validation of these empirical SED fits relies heavily on 3D Monte Carlo simulations of dust radiative transfer. Codes like SKIRT, SUNRISE, and DIRTY track photon packets through clumpy, multiphase environments to compute the effects of multiple scattering and complex geometries [Witt et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528..799W/abstract), [Pierini et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...617.1022P/abstract), [Jonsson et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...637..255J/abstract), [Duarte et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250304906D/abstract). These models consistently show that the effective attenuation of a galaxy is fundamentally distinct from the intrinsic [[extinction-law|Extinction law]] of a point source [Duarte et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250304906D/abstract). Depending on the star-dust geometry and viewing inclination, an intrinsic Milky Way-like dust population can yield observed effective $R_V$ values ranging from 0.5 to 7 [Duarte et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250304906D/abstract). Furthermore, Monte Carlo simulations confirm that the slope of the normalized attenuation curve is highly sensitive to the fraction of unobscured young versus old stars, underscoring that [[variable-dust-attenuation|Variable dust attenuation]] is driven as much by spatial configurations as by the physical evolution of dust grains [Narayanan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869...70N/abstract). 

Finally, properly modeling the colors of high-redshift galaxies requires accounting for external absorption. Dedicated tools like IGMtransmission allow researchers to implement Monte Carlo simulations of stochastic intergalactic neutral hydrogen absorption, cleanly separating IGM effects from internal dust attenuation during the SED fitting process [Harrison et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1105.6208H/abstract).

## Evolution Across Cosmic Time

The advent of next-generation observatories, particularly ALMA and JWST, has extended the study of attenuation curves into the epoch of reionization ($z > 6$) and Cosmic Noon ($1 \lesssim z \lesssim 3$). Observations of high-redshift galaxies consistently demonstrate that local, universal templates (such as the Calzetti law) are often insufficient to capture the diversity of dust attenuation in the early Universe. 

At Cosmic Noon, attenuation laws exhibit significant structural diversity. Studies of massive star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 1.2-2.4$ demonstrate that these systems often host LMC-like extinction curves featuring significant UV bumps [Noll et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASPC..380..461N/abstract). Furthermore, studies of emission line galaxies at $z \sim 2$ show that ultraviolet (UV) attenuation slopes steepen systematically with increasing stellar mass [Zeimann et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...814..162Z/abstract). Theoretical models of grain evolution suggest this mass- and redshift-dependence may be driven by timescale effects: early stellar dust production initially yields flatter curves, while at later epochs ($z \lesssim 1.4$), interstellar dust shattering increases the abundance of small grains, naturally steepening the attenuation slope [Li et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160303497L/abstract), [Hou et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469..870H/abstract). Furthermore, high-resolution imaging from surveys such as Blue Jay ($1.7 < z < 3.5$) reveals that macroscopic dust-star geometry strongly modulates these curves, inducing wavelength-dependent size gradients where massive galaxies appear up to 30% larger in the rest-optical than the rest-NIR due to centralized dust obscuration [Maheson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250415346M/abstract).

Deeper into the reionization era, JWST NIRSpec observations have provided direct rest-frame UV-to-optical spectroscopy of the first generations of galaxies. By employing a "progressive fitting method" to link the average cosmic star formation rate density (SFRD) and dust attenuation up to $z \sim 8$, researchers have found that the cosmic peak in the SFRD occurs at a notably higher redshift than the corresponding peak in global dust attenuation [Khaire et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...805...33K/abstract). The ALMA REBELS survey, followed up with JWST IFU observations, analyzed a sample of massive Lyman-break galaxies ($9 < \log(M_\star/M_\odot) < 10$) at $z = 6.5-7.7$. These galaxies display moderate [[optical-depth|Optical depth]] ($A_V \approx 0.1-0.6$ mag) and attenuation curve slopes ($-0.41 \le \delta \le 0.09$) that are, on average, flatter than those of local galaxies of comparable mass [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250110541F/abstract), [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.539..109F/abstract). Analysis of broader samples up to $z \sim 11.5$ corroborates this flattening, suggesting that the intense radiation fields in young, highly star-forming environments may preferentially destroy small dust grains, effectively shifting the grain size distribution [Markov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250412378M/abstract).

A critical tension in high-redshift literature revolves around the 2175 Å UV bump, a feature typically associated with carbonaceous grains or polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs). While the bump is notably absent in many high-$z$ spectra and the standard Calzetti law [Zeimann et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...814..162Z/abstract), [Sanders et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...989..209S/abstract), recent JWST observations have yielded high-confidence ($>4\sigma$) detections of the feature in galaxies at $z \sim 7-8$ [Markov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...679A..12M/abstract), [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250110541F/abstract), [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.539..109F/abstract). This implies a surprisingly rapid early enrichment of complex carbonaceous dust in some environments. Properly accounting for this feature is highly consequential; ignoring the bump during spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting can artificially inflate derived stellar masses by up to $0.4$ dex [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250110541F/abstract).

Despite these advancements, the literature remains ambiguous regarding whether a characteristic high-redshift attenuation curve exists. For example, the AURORA survey's detailed mapping of a galaxy at $z=4.41$ derived a nebular attenuation curve with a steeper long-wavelength slope ($\lambda > 5000$ Å) than local templates and a complete lack of a UV bump [Sanders et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...989..209S/abstract). The emerging consensus is that while intrinsic grain evolution is significant, the observed variance across cosmic time is predominantly driven by geometry and radiative transfer effects. Localized optical depth, metallicity, and the complex arrangement of young stars relative to diffuse dust appear to dictate the shape of the observed attenuation curve far more than fundamental shifts in the underlying [[extinction-law|Extinction law]] [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250110541F/abstract), [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.539..109F/abstract), [Markov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250412378M/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

A fundamental challenge in extragalactic astrophysics is determining the extent to which variations in attenuation curves are driven by the intrinsic optical properties of dust (the underlying [[extinction-law|Extinction law]]) versus macroscopic dust-star geometry. Theoretical models of radiative transfer demonstrate that complex geometries and clumpiness can produce effective attenuation curves with $R_V$ values ranging anywhere from $0.5$ to $7$ from a single intrinsic extinction law [Duarte et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250304906D/abstract), [Narayanan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...869...70N/abstract). Conversely, other studies argue that physical changes to the grain size distribution—such as the enrichment of small grains via dust shattering at $z < 1.4$—are primarily responsible for the steepening of attenuation slopes over cosmic time [Li et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160303497L/abstract). 

Resolving this tension requires breaking the severe degeneracies between stellar age, metallicity, and dust attenuation in integrated spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting. The literature remains conflicted on the exact mechanisms driving curve diversity. For example, some evidence suggests that strong radiation fields in young, bursty galaxies systematically flatten the attenuation curve by selectively destroying small dust grains [Markov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250412378M/abstract). Other analyses contend that macroscopic variations in [[optical-depth|Optical depth]] and galaxy inclination are entirely sufficient to explain these scaling relations without invoking changes to intrinsic dust chemistry [Barišić et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903..146B/abstract).

To overcome the limitations of global SED fitting, the field is increasingly focused on mapping spatially resolved attenuation. Utilizing IFU spectroscopy, observers can trace interstellar matter on sub-kiloparsec scales, directly probing local variations in dust geometry and composition [Viaene et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.1286V/abstract). High-resolution instruments like JWST's NIRSpec have extended these IFU spectroscopy studies to high-redshift environments ($z \simeq 7$), revealing that spatial variations in dust attenuation can significantly alter observed galaxy morphologies and size gradients [Fisher et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250110541F/abstract), [Maheson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250415346M/abstract). Additionally, observations of backlit or occulting galaxy pairs offer "clean" independent sightlines to map dust distributions without the systematic confusion of mixed stellar populations [Holwerda et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUFM..29B.173H/abstract), [Robertson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....167..263R/abstract). Early studies of backlit pairs, such as NGC 2207, demonstrated that gray attenuation laws can be produced by the macroscopic dust distribution alone, without requiring the differential escape of stars [Keel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....147...44K/abstract). More recently, observations of the backlit galaxy VV 191 using JWST/NIRCam have traced dust attenuation behavior out to $1.5 \, \mu\text{m}$, finding a stronger reddening behavior in the extended outer disks than what is typically seen in starburst models [Keel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....165..166K/abstract).

Finally, as observational frontiers push deeper into the epoch of reionization, properly isolating internal dust attenuation from the intergalactic medium (IGM) has emerged as a critical hurdle. Stochastic neutral hydrogen absorption in the IGM along random lines of sight can mimic the reddening effects of internal dust, leading to photometric redshift underestimates of up to $\Delta z = 0.3$ at $z > 3$ [Tepper-García et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..235..438T/abstract). Accurately modeling IGM transmission is essential to lift the degeneracy between the intrinsic galaxy spectrum and external absorption [Byrohl et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...642L..16B/abstract), enabling more robust constraints on the cosmic evolution of dust.
