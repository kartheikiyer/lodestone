The **Cosmic Dawn (CoDa) project** is a series of massive, fully coupled radiation-hydrodynamics [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] designed to model the Epoch of Reionization (EoR)—the period during the first billion years of the universe when radiation from the first generations of stars ionized the surrounding intergalactic medium (IGM). Simulating this era requires bridging the large-scale, inhomogeneous topology of the IGM with the sub-grid physics of individual star-forming galaxies. Because solving the radiative transfer of ionizing photons on the fly is computationally bottlenecked by the speed of light, CoDa employs a unique hybrid CPU-GPU architecture. Utilizing the RAMSES-CUDATON code, the project computes fluid dynamics and dark matter gravity on standard CPUs, while offloading computationally expensive radiative transfer and chemical ionization rate-solvers to graphics processing units. This allows the simulation to execute hundreds of radiation timesteps for every single hydro-gravity step [Ocvirk et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.1462O/abstract).

Within the broader landscape of [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]], CoDa distinguishes itself by moving beyond post-processed radiative transfer to capture dynamic, history-dependent feedback. A foundational result of the CoDa suite is demonstrating how photoionization heating during local reionization events effectively raises the Jeans mass of the surrounding gas, severely suppressing star formation in low-mass dark matter haloes (below $\sim 2 \times 10^9 \text{ M}_\odot$) [Ocvirk et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.1462O/abstract) [Ocvirk et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496.4087O/abstract). This environment-dependent quenching directly shapes the faint end of the high-redshift ultraviolet luminosity function (UVLF). Furthermore, unlike statistically random unconstrained models, early iterations of CoDa utilized [[clues-project|constrained initial conditions]] designed to reproduce the modern local universe. These models successfully mapped the specific reionization histories of the Milky Way and Andromeda, revealing that our Local Group progenitors evolved as insulated "islands of reionization" driven primarily by their own internal stellar sources [Aubert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...856L..22A/abstract).

Through successive iterations (CoDa I, II, and III), the project has continually refined its sub-grid algorithms to align with stringent observational constraints, such as the cosmic microwave background optical depth and quasar spectra. Today, a primary focus of the CoDa collaboration is forward-modeling multi-wavelength synthetic observables to guide upcoming missions, including 21cm signal maps, Fast Radio Burst (FRB) dispersion measures, and H$\alpha$ line intensity forecasts [Ziegler et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102699Z/abstract) [Lee et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025RNAAS...9...96L/abstract).

## Numerical Methodology and GPU Acceleration

Modeling the EoR with fully coupled radiation-hydrodynamics (RHD) poses a severe computational challenge for [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]]. Unlike post-processed radiative transfer schemes, which apply radiation fields to pre-computed density outputs, the CoDa project employs fully coupled RHD to capture the dynamic, history-dependent nature of the early universe. The primary bottleneck in this approach is the vast disparity between characteristic signal speeds: the speed of light driving radiative transfer (RT) is orders of magnitude larger than the sound and fluid velocities governing hydrodynamics. Consequently, the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) stability condition demands dramatically smaller timesteps for the radiation field than for the baryonic gas and dark matter. 

To circumvent this limitation, the CoDa project employs a highly optimized hybrid computing architecture. At the core of the CoDa I and CoDa II simulations is the RAMSES-CUDATON code. This framework integrates hydrodynamics and dark matter gravity on standard CPUs, while completely offloading the RT and non-equilibrium chemical ionization rate-solvers to graphics processing units via CUDA [Ocvirk et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.1462O/abstract). The radiation field is evolved using a moment-based method utilizing the M1 closure relation, which approximates the Eddington tensor to track the transport of ionizing photons [Aubert et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724..244A/abstract). Early benchmarking demonstrated that porting the ATON radiative transfer code to GPUs achieved an acceleration factor of $\sim 100$ over CPU-only implementations [Aubert et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...724..244A/abstract). By leveraging this hardware acceleration, RAMSES-CUDATON self-consistently performs hundreds of RT timesteps on the GPUs for every single hydro-gravity timestep executed on the CPUs [Ocvirk et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.1462O/abstract).

In addition to uniform grid applications, the CoDa project utilizes Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR) to further resolve the highly inhomogeneous, multi-scale topology of the EoR. Simulations such as CoDa I-AMR deploy the EMMA (Electromagnétisme et Mécanique sur Maille Adaptative) CPU-GPU code. For instance, CoDa I-AMR initialized with an unrefined grid of $2048^3$ cells and particles, which were then adaptively refined to dynamically increase spatial resolution at the locations of dense cosmic web structures and sharp ionization fronts [Aubert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...856L..22A/abstract). Finally, these immense RHD runs are paired with [[matter-only-simulations|dark-matter-only companion models]] tracing the exact same initial conditions—such as the CoDa I-DM2048 simulation run with [[gadget-simulation-code|GADGET (simulation code)]]—to confidently bridge high-redshift reionization fields to present-day ($z=0$) structures [Aubert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...856L..22A/abstract). Ensuring sufficient resolution is fundamental for accurately capturing sub-grid physics in these components; to avoid systematic biases in property measurements, [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] must generally resolve individual dark matter haloes with approximately 400 particles [Trenti et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711.1198T/abstract).

## Generations of the CoDa Suite

The Cosmic Dawn project has evolved through three primary generations, progressively refining its numerical techniques and sub-grid physics to align with increasingly stringent observational constraints.

* **CoDa I:** The inaugural iteration was a landmark fully coupled RHD simulation evolving $4096^3$ particles and cells within a $(91 \, \mathrm{Mpc})^3$ comoving volume. Operating on the Titan supercomputer, it scaled efficiently across 8,192 CPU cores and 8,192 GPUs [Ocvirk et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.1462O/abstract). While it successfully demonstrated the mechanisms of photoionization feedback, a major limitation was its prediction for the global end of reionization, which occurred later than observed and required an artificial temporal rescaling to reconcile with the cosmic microwave background (CMB) electron-scattering optical depth [Ocvirk et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.1462O/abstract).
* **CoDa II:** To address the timing discrepancies of the first generation, CoDa II simulated a slightly larger $94 \, \mathrm{Mpc}$ volume using a newly recalibrated sub-grid star formation algorithm [Ocvirk et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496.4087O/abstract). By modifying the threshold and efficiency of early star formation, CoDa II successfully shifted the completion of reionization to an earlier epoch ($z \sim 6.1$), producing a self-consistent match to the intergalactic Lyman-$\alpha$ opacity measured from high-redshift quasar spectra and the updated Planck CMB fluctuations [Ocvirk et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496.4087O/abstract).
* **CoDa III:** The most recent iteration extends the physical fidelity of the simulation suite by incorporating a dynamical dust model. This addition allows the simulation to directly address recent ALMA and JWST observations regarding dust and UV morphology in massive, high-redshift galaxies [Ocvirk et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240905946O/abstract) [Ocvirk et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...703A..98O/abstract). CoDa III is also heavily utilized for generating mock lightcones for upcoming Line Intensity Mapping (LIM) experiments [Lee et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025RNAAS...9...96L/abstract).

The evolution of the CoDa suite highlights a specific strategy within modern [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]]: prioritizing the high-resolution, on-the-fly coupling of radiative transfer and hydrodynamics to study reionization patchiness. In contrast, parallel simulation efforts have prioritized expanding the background cosmological parameter space; for example, the [[astrid-simulations|ASTRID simulations]] model enormous volumes to track inhomogeneous reionization while concurrently incorporating beyond-standard-model physics, such as massive neutrinos [Bird et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.3703B/abstract). Another highly complementary modern effort is the [[thesan|THESAN project]], a radiation-magnetohydrodynamic simulation suite that pairs a high-resolution RHD solver (AREPO-RT) with the IllustrisTNG galaxy formation model to explore escape fractions and 21cm signatures [Kannan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.4005K/abstract). Comparing the heavily calibrated, small-scale RHD physics of the CoDa iterations with these parallel approaches represents an active frontier in precision computational astrophysics.

## Major Scientific Results

### Feedback and Star Formation Suppression
Because the propagation of ionizing starlight directly alters the thermal and chemical state of the IGM, solving RHD on-the-fly is necessary to consistently model the history-dependent feedback loop between galaxy formation and the environment. As the first massive stars form, they emit extreme ultraviolet radiation that sweeps through the IGM, driving photoheating that raises the gas temperature to $\sim 10^4$ K. This sudden heating increases the local Jeans mass, preventing intergalactic gas from efficiently accreting onto and cooling within shallow dark matter potential wells. 

The severity of this photo-suppression is highly mass-dependent. CoDa simulations consistently demonstrate that photoheating primarily quenches star formation in haloes with masses below $\sim 2 \times 10^9 \, \mathrm{M}_\odot$ [Ocvirk et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.1462O/abstract) [Ocvirk et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496.4087O/abstract). For the lowest-mass haloes ($M < 10^9 \, \mathrm{M}_\odot$), the star formation rate (SFR) remains steady prior to reionization but experiences a sharp decline immediately following the reionization of the local neighborhood. This is accompanied by a rapid drop in the baryonic gas-to-dark matter ratio, $M_{\mathrm{gas}}/M_{\mathrm{DM}}$ [Dawoodbhoy et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.1740D/abstract). Conversely, massive haloes ($M > 10^{10} \, \mathrm{M}_\odot$) are largely impervious to the UV background; their deep potential wells allow star formation to continue and increase unabated. Haloes in the intermediate regime ($10^9 < M < 10^{10} \, \mathrm{M}_\odot$) exhibit a transitional behavior, where the SFR increases modestly during local reionization before eventually declining [Dawoodbhoy et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.1740D/abstract).

Crucially, because reionization proceeds in a patchy, inside-out manner, the timing of star formation suppression is strongly governed by the local environment. Overdense regions, which host the earliest massive galaxy progenitors, can reionize up to $500 \, \mathrm{Myr}$ earlier than the global average [Aubert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...856L..22A/abstract). Consequently, the local UV background halts dwarf galaxy formation in overdense regions hundreds of millions of years earlier than in underdense cosmic voids, directly tying a galaxy's star formation history to its local reionization time rather than the global state of the universe [Dawoodbhoy et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.1740D/abstract) [Ocvirk et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496.4087O/abstract).

However, photoheating is not the exclusive mechanism capable of hindering dwarf galaxies in the early universe. Studies of "cosmic web stripping" demonstrate that ram pressure can effectively remove gas from low-mass haloes as they move rapidly through cosmic filaments, providing a non-feedback alternative for dwarf galaxy suppression in dense environments like the Local Group [Benítez-Llambay et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763L..41B/abstract). 

Despite the severe quenching of low-mass systems, this suppression does not imprint a distinct drop in the global cosmic star formation history, as the total SFR during the EoR is heavily dominated by unaffected massive haloes [Ocvirk et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.1462O/abstract). However, the suppression fundamentally alters the faint end of the UV Luminosity Function (UVLF). As local regions reionize, their UVLF flattens, and by the end of reionization, the global UVLF exhibits a pronounced roll-over at $M_{\mathrm{UV}} \gtrsim -17$ [Dawoodbhoy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.6231D/abstract). 

### Constrained Realizations of the Local Universe
A distinguishing feature of the first two CoDa iterations is their use of constrained initial conditions to model the specific cosmography of our local universe. Unlike standard unconstrained models, which produce statistically representative but random distributions of cosmic structure, CoDa initialized its density fields using constraints derived from galaxy survey data to force primordial density fluctuations to evolve into well-known large-scale structures observed today, such as the Local Group and the Virgo Cluster [Ocvirk et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463.1462O/abstract) [Ocvirk et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496.4087O/abstract).

By simulating a [[clues-project|constrained realization of the local volume]], CoDa bridges the gap between global models of patchy reionization and the specific environmental histories of Local Group galaxies. A key finding from the CoDa II simulation is that the progenitors of the Milky Way and Andromeda (M31) evolved as "islands of reionization." Despite being embedded in the broader cosmic web, these galaxies were reionized predominantly by their own internal stellar sources rather than by external ionizing flux radiating from the nearby, massive Virgo Cluster [Ocvirk et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496.4087O/abstract).

Quantitative analysis using CoDa I tracked these Milky Way progenitors down to $z=0$ to establish their specific reionization redshifts, $z_R$, defined as the epoch when the local gas became 50% ionized. The simulations reveal that both the Milky Way and M31 reionized earlier than the globally averaged reionization redshift of $\langle z_R \rangle = 7.8$. The most massive individual progenitors of the Milky Way and M31 at $z > 6$ reached 50% ionization at $z_R = 9.8$ and $z_R = 11.0$, respectively. When integrated over all branches of their merger trees, both galaxies experienced an aggregate reionization redshift of $z_R = 8.2$ [Aubert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...856L..22A/abstract). These constrained models underscore the strong environmental dependence of reionization; the Local Group's intermediate environment dictated that the Milky Way and M31 were largely insulated from external reionization fronts [Aubert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...856L..22A/abstract) [Ocvirk et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496.4087O/abstract). These findings are highly complementary to other targeted constrained simulation efforts, such as the HESTIA project, which utilizes the AREPO code and AURIGA model to specifically focus on the Local Group's cosmographic context and assembly history [Libeskind et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.2968L/abstract).

### Multi-Wavelength Observational Predictions
The CoDa project generates high-fidelity synthetic observables to connect the complex physics of the EoR with current and future observational campaigns. 

**Lyman-$\alpha$ Forest Transmissivity:** Using CoDa II, studies of the IGM highlight the complex nature of Lyman-$\alpha$ forest transmission at $z > 6$. Gravitational infall motion in the IGM extends resonance absorption to the red side of the Ly$\alpha$ line [Park et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922..263P/abstract). The simulations reveal that damping-wing opacity strongly correlates with both the global neutral fraction and the UV magnitude of the source galaxy. Because reionization is highly inhomogeneous, the surrounding H II bubbles are non-spherical, leading to extreme sight-line-to-sight-line variations. Consequently, CoDa II predicts that constraining the average Ly$\alpha$ transmission to $10\%$ accuracy will require observational samples of over 100 galaxies at $z \ge 7$ [Park et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922..263P/abstract).

**21cm Signal and Line Intensity Mapping:** Using a separate, lower-resolution (1 Mpc) coupled hydro-radiative transfer simulation framework that employs sub-grid star formation models calibrated against the high-resolution CoDa II results, researchers map the 21cm brightness temperature global signal and its spatial fluctuations, providing templates for next-generation radio telescopes [Gillet et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210303061G/abstract) [Gillet et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.3179G/abstract). Furthermore, CoDa III has been utilized to forecast Line Intensity Mapping signals, specifically H$\alpha$ emission originating from radiative recombination following UV ionization by massive stars. These mock data, which compute mean intensities and voxel intensity distributions down to $z=4.6$, are designed to guide upcoming missions such as NASA's SPHEREx [Lee et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025RNAAS...9...96L/abstract).

**Dispersion Measures of Fast Radio Bursts:** The simulations provide unique quantitative constraints on Fast Radio Burst (FRB) dispersion measures as probes of EoR patchiness. Combining CoDa II reionization fields with [[matter-only-simulations|dark-matter-only density extensions]] for the post-reionization universe, the mean FRB dispersion measure and its standard deviation are shown to increase with redshift until plateauing at $z \gtrsim 8$. The asymptotic mean dispersion measure is predicted to be:
$$ \mathcal{DM}_{\mathrm{max}} \approx 5900\ \mathrm{pc\ cm^{-3}} $$
with a maximal standard deviation of $\sigma_{\mathrm{DM, max}} \approx 497\ \mathrm{pc\ cm^{-3}}$ driven by large-scale cosmic inhomogeneities [Ziegler et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102699Z/abstract) [Ziegler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.542.1518Z/abstract). 

**Dust-UV Spatial Offsets:** CoDa III predicts observable spatial disparities between dust continuum and UV emission in massive, high-redshift galaxies ($M_{\mathrm{DM}} > 10^{11.5} \, \mathrm{M}_\odot$). The simulation naturally reproduces spatial offsets of up to $\sim 2\ \mathrm{pkpc}$, matching recent ALMA and JWST observations. The simulation indicates that these offsets do not arise from a physical separation of the dust and stellar mass, but rather from severe dust extinction in the galactic centers obscuring the central UV light [Ocvirk et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240905946O/abstract) [Ocvirk et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...703A..98O/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the CoDa project has established a robust framework for modeling the EoR, several theoretical and observational tensions remain, driving the next phase of fully coupled radiation-hydrodynamics research. 

A primary frontier involves reconciling theoretical predictions with recent JWST and ALMA observations of the aforementioned dust-UV offsets. While CoDa III successfully reproduces the spatial disparities caused by core obscuration, a significant tension persists: to successfully match the observed bright-end UV luminosity function, CoDa III requires the effective dust attenuation to be artificially suppressed, utilizing only $\sim 7.5\%$ of the simulated SMC-like dust mass [Ocvirk et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...703A..98O/abstract). This indicates that current models may either overpredict early dust production or mischaracterize its optical properties.

Another active debate centers on the exact severity of star formation suppression at the faint end of the UVLF. While CoDa demonstrates a clear roll-over at $M_{\mathrm{UV}} \gtrsim -17$, the true abundance of ultra-faint dwarfs ($M_{\mathrm{UV}} \approx -12.5$) remains ambiguous due to the small effective volumes of deep lensing surveys. It is currently unresolved whether potential discrepancies require further sub-grid refinement of the star formation algorithms or are entirely explained by high cosmic variance, which previous analytic models may have underestimated by factors of 2 to 4 [Dawoodbhoy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.6231D/abstract). 

Ultimately, overcoming current limitations will require a transition to [[next-generation-cosmological-simulations|exascale computing]]. To simultaneously resolve the atomic cooling limit and capture the gigaparsec-scale volumes necessary to properly sample rare, massive structures and large-scale ionizing radiation fields, [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] must scale to the exaflop regime [Norman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018FrASS...5...34N/abstract). Harnessing next-generation hybrid CPU-GPU architectures will be critical for advancing the coupled RHD framework pioneered by CoDa, allowing researchers to model the early universe with unprecedented fidelity.
