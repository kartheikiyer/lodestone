## Article

In computational astrophysics, **subgrid physics models** are numerical algorithms used in [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] to represent physical processes that occur on spatial and mass scales smaller than the explicitly resolved computational grid cells or particles. Modeling the evolution of the Universe requires spanning an immense dynamic range, from megaparsec-scale large-scale structure down to the sub-parsec environments of molecular cloud collapse and black hole accretion. Because finite [[resolution-simulations|Resolution (simulations)]] and limited [[mass-resolution|Mass resolution]] make it computationally prohibitive to track this entire hierarchy simultaneously from first principles, simulations rely on [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|subgrid prescriptions]]. These models act as [[heuristic-models|heuristic]] or physically motivated bridges, utilizing resolved macroscopic fluid variables—such as gas density, temperature, and metallicity—to approximate the microscopic phenomena that govern galaxy evolution.

The most critical applications of subgrid models involve tracking star formation, [[sn-feedback-model|stellar feedback from supernovae]], and the growth of supermassive black holes alongside their associated active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback. Without these [[multi-scale-feedback-processes|Multi-scale feedback processes]], simulated galaxies suffer from catastrophic overcooling, where gas rapidly loses pressure support and fragments into unphysically dense, hyper-compact clumps. Historically, simulations countered this by employing simple density-threshold "Schmidt laws" alongside purely phenomenological energy injections [Pfenniger et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Ap&SS.284..445P/abstract) [Baugh et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006RPPh...69.3101B/abstract). However, modern approaches increasingly leverage isolated, [[high-resolution-simulations|high-resolution interstellar medium (ISM) simulations]] to derive physically grounded, pressure-regulated frameworks that capture complex multiphase gas dynamics [Naab et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55...59N/abstract). Because the exact efficiencies of unresolved physical mechanisms retain some inherent arbitrariness, standard practice involves calibrating free parameters—such as wind mass-loading factors and AGN coupling efficiencies—to match robust $z=0$ observational benchmarks, like the galaxy stellar mass function and cluster gas fractions [Schaye et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446..521S/abstract).

Rigorous code comparison initiatives, such as the [[agora-simulation|AGORA simulation]] project, have demonstrated a pivotal consensus: macroscopic galaxy properties are largely more sensitive to the precise formulation and calibration of these subgrid choices than to the underlying numerical hydrodynamics solvers (e.g., SPH versus AMR) [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract) [Somerville et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ARA&A..53...51S/abstract). Moving forward, the field is expanding beyond basic thermal and kinetic feedback to incorporate advanced non-thermal microphysics, including cosmic rays, magnetic fields, and explicit radiative transfer. Despite this progress, significant open questions remain. Researchers are actively working to reconcile $z=0$ calibrated models with the unexpectedly abundant population of massive, quiescent galaxies observed at $z>6$ by JWST. Furthermore, mitigating the $\sim 10-25\%$ intrinsic stochasticity inherent in discrete subgrid modules and leveraging machine learning for scientific equation discovery to replace purely [[heuristic-models|Heuristic models]] represent critical frontiers as the community transitions toward exascale computing.

## Introduction to Subgrid Physics in Cosmology

The accurate modeling of galaxy formation within a cosmological context represents one of the most formidable computational challenges in modern astrophysics. To simulate representative volumes of the Universe, [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] must span an enormous spatial and mass dynamic range. While gravity and large-scale hydrodynamics can be explicitly computed on megaparsec scales, the fundamental baryonic physics that drives galaxy evolution—such as the collapse of molecular clouds, star formation, and the accretion onto supermassive black holes—unfolds on parsec or sub-parsec scales. Because it is computationally infeasible to capture this entire hierarchy of scales simultaneously, global simulations inevitably encounter a resolution limit. 

To bridge this gap, [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] rely on numerical prescriptions known as [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|subgrid models]]. These heuristic frameworks parameterize unresolved physical processes as functions of the resolved, macroscopic fluid variables (e.g., gas density, temperature, and metallicity) within the computational cells or particles. Crucially, instead of modeling individual supernova remnants or black hole accretion disks from first principles, subgrid models inject thermal or kinetic energy directly into the resolved medium to approximate the macroscopic effects of [[multi-scale-feedback-processes|Multi-scale feedback processes]] [Crain et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ARA&A..61..473C/abstract). 

Because the exact efficiencies of these unresolved processes cannot be derived from fundamental theory, subgrid prescriptions inherently introduce arbitrariness into the simulations. Consequently, it has become standard practice to calibrate the free parameters of these models to reproduce robust observational benchmarks, such as the $z=0$ galaxy stellar mass function, galaxy sizes, and cluster hot gas fractions [Schaye et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446..521S/abstract) [McCarthy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2936M/abstract). 

Extensive code-comparison projects, such as the [[agora-simulation|AGORA initiative]], have demonstrated a critical consensus regarding these techniques: while inherent algorithmic differences exist between smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) and mesh-based solvers, the global properties of simulated galaxies are vastly more sensitive to the choice and calibration of subgrid physics than to the underlying hydrodynamics solver itself [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract). 

Despite their success in reproducing realistic global galaxy populations, subgrid models remain a major source of systematic uncertainty. In the era of precision cosmology, baryonic feedback driven by these subgrid modules significantly redistributes gas, leading to a strong, model-dependent suppression of the matter power spectrum $P(k)$ at non-linear scales ($k > 1 \, h/\mathrm{Mpc}$) [Salcido et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.2247S/abstract) [Gebhardt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4896G/abstract). Furthermore, the literature exhibits tension regarding the predictive power of these models for individual objects and early epochs. The inherent stochasticity required to discretize continuous feedback processes introduces an intrinsic variability of $\sim 10-25\%$ in the predicted properties of individual galaxies [Borrow et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.2441B/abstract) [Pakmor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543.1761P/abstract), and there is an ongoing debate over whether models tuned to match the local Universe can successfully capture the star formation regulation required to explain the abundant population of massive, high-redshift ($z > 6$) galaxies recently observed by JWST [Chaikin et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115207C/abstract).

## Historical Development and Evolution of Models

The modeling of galaxy formation within the hierarchical paradigm has undergone a continuous evolution, driven by the need to bridge vast cosmological scales with the microphysics of the interstellar medium (ISM). Early theoretical efforts largely relied on [[semi-analytic-modeling|Semi-analytic modeling]] [Baugh et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006RPPh...69.3101B/abstract). These heuristic models utilized merger trees extracted from dark matter-only N-body simulations, applying simplified, analytic prescriptions to track gas cooling, star formation, and subsequent feedback. While computationally efficient, these models could not self-consistently capture the non-linear gas dynamics and spatially varying processes of the ISM. Today, public semi-analytical models like A-SLOTH continue to complement full hydrodynamics by computationally efficiently tracing the formation of the first stars across large merger trees [Magg et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JOSS....7.4417M/abstract). Furthermore, hybrid approaches now combine the cosmological background of semi-analytic merger trees with the high resolution of hydrodynamic multi-merger simulations to model individual galaxy evolution efficiently [Moster et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.437.1027M/abstract).

As computational power increased, the field transitioned toward [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]] that directly integrated hydrodynamics. However, because resolving the multiphase ISM was computationally prohibitive in cosmologically representative volumes, simulators introduced subgrid physics to approximate processes occurring below the grid scale. A foundational milestone in this era was the Springel-Hernquist model, which treated the ISM as a co-spatial mixture of cold, dense clouds and a hot, diffuse medium. To circumvent catastrophic artificial cooling—a major numerical hurdle in early codes—supernova energy was injected directly into the hot phase. Star formation in these early models was typically governed by an empirical density-threshold "Schmidt law" combined with a stiff effective equation of state (EOS) to provide artificial pressure support against runaway gravitational collapse. Building upon these early milestones, subsequent advancements like the [[mufasa-simulation|MUFASA suite]] introduced meshless finite mass (MFM) hydrodynamics alongside H2-based star formation, achieving an unprecedented match to the galaxy stellar mass function from $z=4$ to $0$ [Davé et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.3265D/abstract).

Because these early implementations relied on highly parametrized, phenomenological prescriptions, their validity remained inherently uncertain. To mitigate this arbitrariness, it became standard practice to calibrate subgrid models against empirical observations, such as the $z=0$ galaxy stellar mass function and cluster gas fractions, before employing them for predictive cosmology [Schaye et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446..521S/abstract), [McCarthy et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2936M/abstract). This robust calibration approach became the backbone of [[next-generation-cosmological-simulations|next-generation simulations]], including the [[bahamas-simulations|BAHAMAS simulations]] and EAGLE, allowing hydrodynamic models to reproduce global galaxy population statistics across massive volumes [Crain et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ARA&A..61..473C/abstract). Pushing these boundaries further, the next-generation COLIBRE project supersedes the EAGLE suite by incorporating explicit non-equilibrium chemistry for hydrogen and helium alongside a comprehensive three-species dust model [Schaye et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250821126S/abstract).

Currently, the field is systematically replacing ad hoc threshold models with physically grounded prescriptions derived from highly resolved ISM simulations [Naab et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55...59N/abstract). Modern [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] are shifting away from stiff EOS formulations toward pressure-regulated, feedback-modulated (PRFM) models. Frameworks calibrated on local, parsec-scale ISM simulations (e.g., the TIGRESS suite) demonstrate that star formation is primarily governed by vertical dynamical equilibrium—a competition between gravity, turbulence, and radiative dissipation [Hassan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..151H/abstract). These PRFM subgrid formulations yield more realistic, thinner galactic discs than older prescriptions [Burger et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250213244B/abstract). Similarly, advanced treatments now employ hybrid thermal-kinetic supernova injections [Chaikin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3709C/abstract) and explicit stellar feedback that naturally drives galactic winds without relying on tuned, pre-determined mass-loading factors [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract).

## Star Formation and Interstellar Medium (ISM) Prescriptions

In [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]], finite [[resolution-simulations|Resolution (simulations)]] prevents the explicit resolution of molecular cloud collapse, necessitating subgrid models to approximate the conversion of gas into stars. Historically, these prescriptions have relied on empirical scaling relations applied within a multi-phase ISM framework. In these classical models, the interstellar medium is treated as a co-spatial mixture of cold, dense star-forming clouds embedded in a hot, diffuse medium. To prevent spurious artificial fragmentation at the resolution limit, simulations typically implement a stiff effective equation of state to provide a stabilizing pressure floor. Star formation is then triggered when gas exceeds a critical density threshold, and the local star formation rate is scaled to reproduce the macroscopic Kennicutt-Schmidt relation. This conversion rate is parameterized by the star formation efficiency per free-fall time, $\epsilon_{\rm ff}$, which directly dictates the speed of molecular gas consumption and the resulting spatial distribution of giant molecular clouds [Li et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.5862L/abstract).

Recent years have seen a paradigm shift away from purely empirical density thresholds toward physically grounded, pressure-regulated prescriptions. These next-generation models assert that star formation is governed by the dynamical equilibrium between self-gravity, radiative dissipation, and the pressure support driven by stellar feedback and turbulence. Calibrated against [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] of the ISM (such as the TIGRESS framework), these Pressure-Regulated Feedback-Modulated (PRFM) models replace traditional stiff effective equations of state with analytic formulations evaluated using local fluid quantities [Hassan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..151H/abstract). By predicting a much softer effective equation of state, PRFM models naturally produce more realistic, substantially thinner galactic discs than older heuristic formulations [Burger et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250213244B/abstract). Furthermore, they predict significantly shorter gas depletion times at the high densities characteristic of high-redshift environments [Hassan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..151H/abstract).

Modern implementations increasingly account for the complex physics of the multi-phase ISM by incorporating [[subgrid-scale-turbulence|Subgrid-scale turbulence]] and non-thermal pressure. For example, tracking the decay of numerical dissipation into subgrid kinetic energy allows for locally variable star formation efficiencies that respond directly to local gas compression rates [Semenov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241023339S/abstract). Similarly, coupling magnetic pressure into the star formation criteria successfully reproduces the observed anti-correlation between local magnetic field strength and the star formation rate, an effect missed by purely thermal prescriptions [Batziou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240503765B/abstract).

Despite these methodological advances, subgrid criteria choices—such as density thresholds versus turbulence-inspired models—can yield fundamentally distinct thermodynamic states in the ISM. Even when these variant models produce identical final stellar masses, they can predict vastly different nebular emission line ratios, indicating that the subgrid "feedback landscape" remains a major source of systematic uncertainty in understanding high-redshift galaxy evolution [Katz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241107282K/abstract). To further address resolution limitations, particularly in the circumgalactic medium, recent frameworks like the Cold Gas Subgrid Model (CGSM) introduce a two-fluid approach that explicitly tracks unresolved cold gas as a secondary continuous phase alongside the standard hot medium [Butsky et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.535.1672B/abstract).

## Stellar Feedback and Galactic Outflows

[[sn-feedback-model|Stellar feedback]] is an essential component of [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]], responsible for regulating star formation and preventing the unphysical runaway collapse of galaxies. Without explicit subgrid models for stellar feedback, simulated star formation rates are systematically overpredicted by factors of $10$ to $100$, and galactic discs collapse into ultra-thin, unphysically dense clumps [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract). To counteract this, simulations employ a variety of numerical mechanisms to inject the energy, momentum, and heavy elements generated by massive stars and supernovae (SNe) into the surrounding interstellar medium (ISM).

**Thermal Heating and Kinetic Outflows**
Subgrid supernova feedback models broadly rely on two primary channels: **thermal heating** and **kinetic outflows**. Early attempts to inject thermal energy directly into the simulated ISM suffered from the "overcooling" problem, where finite [[resolution-simulations|Resolution (simulations)]] caused the injected thermal energy to be artificially and rapidly radiated away before it could do mechanical work. To mitigate this, modern codes often utilize stochastic thermal feedback, which accumulates SN energy and injects it in large discrete events, ensuring that the local gas heating time-scale briefly exceeds the cooling time-scale [Schaye et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446..521S/abstract), [Chaikin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3709C/abstract). Notably, the energy required to ionize the surrounding neutral gas can account for up to $50\%$ of the total SN feedback budget; neglecting this potential energy reservoir can drastically overestimate the efficiency of thermal heating [Vandenbroucke et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...771...36V/abstract).

To bypass the overcooling problem entirely, many models utilize kinetic feedback. This approach directly imparts momentum to the gas, physically displacing it from star-forming regions to drive large-scale galactic winds. Recent implementations refine this by "kicking" gas particles in opposite pairs, which rigorously conserves energy, linear momentum, and angular momentum while remaining statistically isotropic [Chaikin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3709C/abstract). The efficiency of these winds is commonly parameterized by the mass loading factor, defined as the ratio of the mass outflow rate to the star formation rate:
$$ \eta = \frac{\dot{M}_{\rm out}}{\dot{M}_*} $$
Hybrid models that combine both thermal and kinetic channels have proven highly successful. In these implementations, large thermal energy injections generate a volume-filling hot phase of the ISM, while low-energy kinetic kicks drive the turbulent velocity dispersion of the cold, neutral gas [Chaikin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3709C/abstract). Because different feedback prescriptions sculpt the large-scale structure of gas distinctly, the rotational asymmetry and morphology of H I discs in $L^*$ galaxies serve as sensitive observational probes to differentiate between various star formation efficiencies and feedback models [Gensior et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.1158G/abstract). The ARCHITECTS I project demonstrates that even when subgrid models are calibrated to produce the same stellar mass, the resulting circumgalactic medium (CGM) properties vary wildly depending on whether the feedback is primarily ejective or preventive [Rey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543...12R/abstract). Furthermore, as an alternative to particle-scale injections, the "galcon" approach in the Enzo code provides a novel subgrid framework by modeling galaxies as extended analytic constructs to systematically follow the propagation of feedback into the intracluster medium [Arieli et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...716..918A/abstract).

Moreover, the temporal nature of these feedback injections profoundly impacts dark matter distributions. Bursty star formation histories driven by subgrid feedback generate rapid fluctuations in the gravitational potential, which can transform central dark matter cusps into cores in dwarf galaxies, with the core formation efficiency peaking at specific stellar-to-halo mass fractions [Azartash-Namin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...40A/abstract).

**Chemical Enrichment**
**Chemical enrichment** is inextricably tied to stellar feedback. Detailed subgrid models track the multi-element yields from core-collapse SNe, Type Ia SNe, and asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stars [Côté et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777..107C/abstract). To mitigate uncertainties in sub-Milky Way mass galaxy predictions, recent frameworks like the FIRE-3 project provide updated, plug-and-play stellar evolution tracks and mass-dependent yields [Hopkins et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.3154H/abstract). Because the cooling rate of the ISM is highly sensitive to its metal content, chemical enrichment acts as a critical feedback loop. The dynamical balance between cosmological inflows and metal-enriched SN outflows naturally establishes the observed mass-metallicity relation [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.1354D/abstract). However, variations in the strength of explosive feedback can eject excess metals into the circumgalactic medium, leading to a lower ISM metallicity at a fixed stellar mass—making the mass-metallicity relation a stringent test for feedback calibrations [Agertz et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.1656A/abstract). Furthermore, accurately modeling early epochs is essential; incorporating metallicity-dependent Pop III feedback is often required to produce gas-rich dwarf galaxies at low redshifts that correctly align with the baryonic Tully-Fisher relation [Vandenbroucke et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.458..912V/abstract).

## Black Hole Growth and AGN Feedback

In [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]], the inclusion of supermassive black holes (SMBHs) and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback is strictly required to prevent catastrophic cooling and to quench star formation in massive galaxies and galaxy clusters [Scannapieco et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...686..927S/abstract) [Le Brun et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441.1270L/abstract). Because the physical scales of black hole accretion and jet formation are orders of magnitude smaller than the typical [[resolution-simulations|Resolution (simulations)]] of galaxy-scale runs, these processes must be approximated using [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]].

### Black Hole Dynamics and Accretion
Most models parameterize gas accretion onto SMBHs using the Bondi-Hoyle-Lyttleton approximation:
$$ \dot{M}_{\rm BHL} = \frac{4\pi G^2 M_{\rm BH}^2 \rho}{(c_s^2 + v^2)^{3/2}} $$
where $M_{\rm BH}$ is the black hole mass, $\rho$ is the local gas density, $c_s$ is the sound speed, and $v$ is the relative velocity between the black hole and the gas. Because the Bondi radius is typically unresolved, bare implementations often severely underestimate the true accretion rate. This is traditionally circumvented by applying artificial boost factors, though modern approaches employ super-Lagrangian refinement to explicitly resolve the Bondi radius on the fly [Curtis et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.3445C/abstract). 

Accurate orbital tracking is equally challenging. Because of finite resolution and discrete particle masses, standard treatments often fail to capture dynamical friction correctly, leading to artificially inflated merger rates and spurious stochastic heating for low-mass black holes ($M_{\rm BH} \le 5 M_{\rm DM}$) [Sutter et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...723.1308S/abstract) [Genina et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240508870G/abstract). Recent suites, such as the [[romulus-cosmological-simulations|ROMULUS cosmological simulations]], have prioritized physically motivated subgrid corrections for dynamical friction to accurately follow SMBH orbital decay [Tremmel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.1121T/abstract). 

The maximal accretion rate is typically capped at the Eddington limit. However, there is ongoing debate regarding early BH growth: recent studies argue that brief periods of super-Eddington accretion are strictly necessary to reproduce the abundance of massive quiescent galaxies at $z > 6$ discovered by JWST [Chaikin et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115207C/abstract). Additionally, code comparisons evaluating black hole growth between SPH codes (like GASOLINE) and AMR codes (like RAMSES) reveal that while overall dynamical behaviors remain consistent, significant quantitative differences in accretion bursts and corresponding star formation emerge due to their distinct subgrid fueling and thermodynamics models [Gabor et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...592A..62G/abstract).

### AGN Feedback and Quenching
Energy and momentum injected by AGN are critical for regulating host galaxies. Feedback is commonly split into two regimes: a high-accretion "quasar mode" (often modeled via thermal injection) and a low-accretion "radio mode" (modeled via kinetic or mechanical momentum injection). Momentum-driven AGN feedback allows for self-regulated black hole growth even without generating massive global outflows [Debuhr et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406L..55D/abstract). In the intracluster medium, radio-mode jets inflate underdense, hot bubbles. When coupled with subgrid turbulence models, simulations demonstrate that these bubbles mix with their surroundings via Rayleigh-Taylor instabilities, regulating entropy and metal distributions in cluster cores [Scannapieco et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...686..927S/abstract). Conversely, AGN jets are not exclusively destructive; they can also drive "positive feedback" by over-pressurizing the interstellar medium, triggering starbursts of up to $\sim 10^{10} M_\odot$ in gas-rich discs [Gaibler et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425..438G/abstract).

### Current Tensions and Observational Constraints
Despite its successes, AGN subgrid modeling exhibits persistent tensions with observations. Hydrodynamical models often over-predict the stellar mass of Brightest Cluster Galaxies (BCGs) and yield overly large effective radii, suggesting that while current feedback models adequately suppress global star formation, their energy distribution at the galactic center remains imperfect [Ragone-Figueroa et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.1750R/abstract). 

Furthermore, the exact parameterization of AGN feedback drives massive variations in the distribution of circumgalactic and intergalactic baryons. This efficiently suppresses the matter power spectrum on non-linear scales ($k \gtrsim 1 h/\text{Mpc}$), complicating precision cosmology [Gebhardt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4896G/abstract). Because different [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]] produce identical stellar masses but vastly different gas fractions, observations of the circumgalactic medium are required to break model degeneracies. Recent measurements of the kinetic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (kSZ) effect—which probes the momentum of free electrons—strongly disfavor weak feedback implementations, instead benchmarking toward models with highly efficient, expulsive AGN feedback [Bigwood et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251015822B/abstract).

## Advanced Microphysics: Magnetic Fields, Cosmic Rays, and Radiation

As computational resources expand, [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] increasingly move beyond ideal hydrodynamics to incorporate magnetic fields, cosmic rays (CRs), and explicit radiative transfer (RT). These non-thermal and radiative components are proving crucial for accurately modeling the multiphase structure of the interstellar medium (ISM) and the circumgalactic medium (CGM).

**Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD)**
The inclusion of magnetic fields fundamentally alters the dynamics of the ISM and CGM. In massive haloes, amplification via turbulent dynamos and shear flows can drive magnetic field strengths to $10$--$100\ \mu\mathrm{G}$, consistent with observations of galaxy clusters [Marinacci et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.3999M/abstract). The necessity of MHD for regulating global star formation remains debated. Some [[high-resolution-simulations|high-resolution models]] suggest that magnetic fields, conduction, and viscosity alter global star formation rates (SFR) by only $\sim 10\%$, remaining largely sub-dominant to explicit stellar feedback [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract). However, on local scales, integrating magnetic pressure into subgrid star formation models successfully reproduces the observed anti-correlation between local magnetic field strength and SFR [Batziou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240503765B/abstract). Furthermore, MHD effects dramatically influence the morphology of galaxy merger remnants, fostering the survival of extended, star-forming discs rather than the compact ring structures typically produced by pure hydrodynamics [Whittingham et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506..229W/abstract).

**Cosmic Ray Feedback**
Cosmic rays provide a vital source of non-thermal pressure support. Because CRs possess relatively long cooling times and couple dynamically to the background plasma, they can efficiently drive large-scale galactic winds and quench cooling flows in massive haloes [Ruszkowski et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&ARv..31....4R/abstract). For dwarf galaxies, hydrodynamical models relying solely on supernova feedback often produce overly compact or unphysically quenched systems. Incorporating CRs alongside radiative feedback yields more realistic, extended, and rotationally supported dwarf galaxies [Martin-Alvarez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.3806M/abstract). Current theoretical modeling is rapidly evolving, transitioning from simplified Fokker-Planck or leaky-box diffusion approximations to rigorous CR-MHD formulations that natively bridge micro-scale gyro-orbits and meso-scale mean-free-paths [Hopkins et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250907104H/abstract).

**Radiative Transfer and Plasma Microphysics**
Explicit RT is critical for capturing the thermal state of the ISM and the large-scale timing of cosmic reionization, an effort pioneered by large-volume suites like [[thesan|THESAN]] [Garaldi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.3765G/abstract). In low-mass haloes ($M_{\rm halo} \sim 10^9\ \mathrm{M}_\odot$), the inclusion of on-the-fly RT fundamentally shifts the mode of galactic self-regulation. Instead of relying entirely on violent starburst-driven outflows, stellar photoheating maintains the gas at $T \sim 10^4\ \mathrm{K}$, gently suppressing star formation even prior to cosmic reionization [Agertz et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.1656A/abstract).

Furthermore, resolving the ionization state of the gas introduces a massive potential energy reservoir. The energy required to strip electrons from atoms can absorb up to $50\%$ of the energy injected by a supernova event. Failing to account for this ionization sink artificially inflates the local temperature and exaggerates the macroscopic efficiency of thermal feedback in [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] [Vandenbroucke et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...771...36V/abstract). To directly connect these microphysical processes to modern JWST observations, state-of-the-art subgrid models now couple RT with non-equilibrium chemistry networks tracking over 80 chemical species. These approaches demonstrate that resolving radiation and the multiphase ISM structure is strictly necessary to reproduce the diverse nebular emission line ratios observed in high-redshift galaxies [Katz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241107282K/abstract).

Finally, research is rapidly expanding into alternative gravity frameworks. Hydrodynamical simulations executed within Milgromian gravity (MOND) demonstrate that standard subgrid physics implementations can successfully produce exponential discs and flat rotation curves even without dark matter halos, underscoring the generalizability of these advanced baryonic prescriptions [Nagesh et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.5128N/abstract).

## Numerical Convergence and Code Comparison

The robustness of [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]] is routinely evaluated through large-scale code comparison initiatives and rigorous numerical convergence tests. A consistent finding across collaborative efforts, such as the [[agora-simulation|AGORA simulation]] project and the nIFTy cluster comparison, is that macroscopic simulation outcomes are significantly more sensitive to the parametrization of subgrid models than to the underlying hydrodynamics solver [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract), [Cui et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.458.4052C/abstract). The nIFTy cluster comparison further showed that while dark-matter-only subhalo distributions are remarkably robust across varying architectures, the inclusion of subgrid feedback physics can drive up to a factor of 4 difference in the total number of resolved galaxies produced by different codes [Elahi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.458.1096E/abstract).

In isolated disk tests, fundamental properties—such as rotation curves, velocity dispersions, and global star formation rates—agree across different code architectures (e.g., [[gadget-simulation-code|GADGET (simulation code)]], [[ramses-simulation-code|Ramses (simulation code)]]) to within a few tens of percent [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract). However, substantial variations emerge in the small-scale multiphase structure; for instance, newly formed stellar clump mass functions can differ by up to a factor of 3. Differences between solvers are most pronounced in the treatment of hydrodynamical instabilities and turbulent mixing. Classic smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) codes often fail to produce entropy cores in clusters and require modern implementations with artificial conduction and viscosity limiters to properly capture energetic feedback events [Beck et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2110B/abstract), [Borrow et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.2367B/abstract).

Demonstrating strict numerical convergence remains a persistent challenge in [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]]. [[finite-resolution-effects|Finite resolution effects]] dictate that subgrid processes must be explicitly coupled to the simulation's resolution limits. For example, the choice of gravitational softening length sets a minimum resolved escape speed, $V_\epsilon$. If parameters are chosen such that $V_\epsilon \gtrsim 10 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, the thermal effects of photoionized gas are artificially suppressed because the particle binding energy exceed the sound speed of the ionized gas. Separately, if the softening length drops below a critical value $\epsilon_{eFB}$, subgrid feedback models suffer from severe numerical overcooling, causing injected energy to be unphysically radiated away before it can do mechanical work [Ludlow et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.2926L/abstract). Furthermore, energy equipartition driven by two-body scattering between unequal-mass species (e.g., massive dark matter and lighter stellar particles) can lead to spurious energy transfer. This numerical heating artificially inflates galaxy sizes, causing the projected half-mass radius, $R_{50}$, to undergo spurious growth until it exceeds a critical fraction of the mean interparticle separation, $l$:
$$ R_{50} \gtrsim 0.05 l $$
This effect poses strict limits on the stellar-to-dark matter mass ratios permissible in simulations [Ludlow et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488L.123L/abstract), [Ludlow et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.2926L/abstract). In some modern frameworks, rather than merely suppressing numerical artifacts, researchers dynamically track local numerical dissipation to model unresolved subgrid-scale turbulence, effectively linking truncation errors to physical turbulent heating cascades [Semenov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241023339S/abstract).

Finally, the discrete nature of subgrid physics introduces an irreducible level of stochasticity into simulation outcomes. Because physical rates are applied probabilistically to discrete resolution elements, re-running the exact same [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] with identical initial conditions but different random number seeds yields measurable divergence in evolutionary paths. While properties aggregated over large cosmological volumes remain robust, the bursty nature of black hole feedback and exact merger timings drive an intrinsic variability of $10\% - 25\%$ in the final stellar mass, star formation rate, and morphology of individual galaxies [Borrow et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.2441B/abstract), [Pakmor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543.1761P/abstract). Because changing the [[mass-resolution|Mass resolution]] by a factor of 8 induces systematic shifts that exceed this intrinsic scatter, performing exact convergence tests across varying resolution tiers is highly complex [Pakmor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543.1761P/abstract). Moreover, the [[agora-simulation|AGORA simulation]] project's recent expansion to the high-redshift frontier ($z \ge 10$) reveals that while various simulations can match the bulk properties of massive JWST galaxies, internal properties like metallicity and exact morphologies remain highly sensitive to specific feedback implementations [Kim et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251104435K/abstract). Consequently, researchers are advised to treat "single-object" case studies with caution, necessitating the simulation of statistical ensembles to draw reliable conclusions about subgrid physics.

## Model Calibration and Cosmological Observables

Because [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] lack the spatial and mass resolution required to capture the full spectrum of interstellar and intergalactic gas dynamics from first principles, they rely heavily on parameterized [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]]. The macroscopic efficiencies of these processes—such as the mass-loading factors of supernova-driven winds or the fraction of accretion energy coupled to the gas by active galactic nuclei (AGN)—cannot be strictly derived from fundamental theory. Consequently, state-of-the-art models must empirically calibrate their subgrid parameters to match well-characterized cosmological observables in the local Universe. 

The primary calibration target for almost all modern [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] is the $z \approx 0$ **stellar mass function** (GSMF). Without explicit and properly tuned stellar feedback, simulated star formation rates are overpredicted by factors of 10 to 100, leading to the runaway collapse of gas into unphysically dense clumps [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract). By adjusting feedback efficiencies, projects like the EAGLE suite are able to reproduce the observed GSMF to within $\lesssim 0.2$ dex over a widely resolved mass range of $10^8 < M_*/\text{M}_\odot \lesssim 10^{11}$, alongside matching the amplitude of the galaxy-central black hole mass relation and characteristic galaxy sizes [Schaye et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446..521S/abstract).

In addition to the GSMF, large-scale structure models place stringent constraints on macroscopic **scaling relations**, particularly the gas fractions of massive halos. The [[bahamas-simulations|BAHAMAS simulations]] were explicitly designed to calibrate subgrid stellar and AGN feedback models simultaneously to the present-day GSMF and the hot gas mass fractions of groups and clusters of galaxies ($10^{13} \lesssim M_{500}/\text{M}_\odot \lesssim 10^{15}$) [McCarthy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2936M/abstract). Similarly, the FABLE simulations utilize the AREPO code with updated AGN and supernova feedback models to simultaneously match the galaxy stellar mass function alongside the gas mass fractions of galaxy clusters, ensuring proper large-scale baryon distributions [Henden et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.5385H/abstract). Tuning to these extended gas profiles is critical because energetic AGN feedback redistributes baryons over vast distances, significantly suppressing the non-linear matter power spectrum $P(k)$ at scales $k \gtrsim 1 \, h/\text{Mpc}$ [Salcido et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.2247S/abstract). Accurately reproducing these cluster gas fractions mitigates a primary systematic uncertainty, allowing hydrodynamical simulations to serve as precision benchmarks for upcoming large-scale structure surveys. Building on these calibration strategies, modern flagship suites like the MillenniumTNG (MTNG) project combine the robust subgrid physics of IllustrisTNG with the vast cosmological volume of the Millennium simulation to provide high-precision predictions for matter clustering and halo statistics [Hernández-Aguayo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2556H/abstract). Further evaluations show that the galaxy size-to-halo spin relation varies significantly between prominent suites like IllustrisTNG and EAGLE, suggesting that resulting disc sizes act as highly sensitive probes of both the underlying hydrodynamics solvers and the specific subgrid choices employed [Yang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.5253Y/abstract).

Calibrated subgrid models also provide a physically grounded framework for evaluating the **halo occupation distribution** (HOD). The HOD specifies the probability $P(N|M)$ that a dark matter halo of virial mass $M$ hosts $N$ galaxies of a given type. Both full hydrodynamical simulations and computationally lighter approaches like [[semi-analytic-modeling|Semi-analytic modeling]] yield converging predictions for the non-linear mean occupation $N_{\text{avg}}(M)$ and its sub-Poisson variance, which allows observers to map complex baryonic physics robustly onto the underlying dark matter scaffold [Weinberg et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..283....3W/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite the widespread successes of modern [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]], subgrid models remain fundamentally limited by finite resolution and inherent numerical stochasticity. Re-simulations of individual galaxies demonstrate that probabilistic variations within subgrid modules can induce up to a $25\%$ intrinsic variability in macroscopic properties, thereby placing fundamental limits on the predictive reliability of single-object studies [Borrow et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.2441B/abstract), [Pakmor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543.1761P/abstract). Furthermore, existing models that have been tightly calibrated to $z=0$ scaling relations are being aggressively tested by recent JWST observations of unexpectedly massive, quiescent galaxies at $z > 6$. Reconciling this high-redshift tension—potentially through new subgrid channels like super-Eddington accretion—remains an active area of research [Chaikin et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115207C/abstract). A major complication in predicting these evolutionary pathways is that stellar and AGN feedback modes are highly interrelated; stellar feedback efficiency significantly impacts black hole growth, thereby determining the subsequent timing and intensity of AGN feedback activation [Medlock et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016361M/abstract).

A critical open question in the field is how to break the physical degeneracies between baryonic feedback and physics beyond the Standard Model. Variations in subgrid feedback implementations can mimic the suppression of the non-linear matter power spectrum, $P(k)$, expected from warm dark matter or massive neutrinos [Stafford et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497.3809S/abstract). To directly address the interplay between baryonic processes and dark matter physics, initiatives like the AIDA-TNG project are exploring the simultaneous impact of baryonic subgrid physics and alternative dark matter models—such as warm (WDM) and self-interacting dark matter (SIDM)—on galaxy populations [Despali et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250112439D/abstract). Moreover, gravitational lensing anomalies in Einstein rings offer a promising independent avenue to potentially distinguish between standard particle dark matter and wave-like (axion) dark matter models [Amruth et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7..736A/abstract). Crucially, these degeneracies can be broken: higher-order clustering statistics, such as the halo bispectrum, successfully separate the effects of massive neutrinos from baryonic feedback, because neutrinos suppress large scales up to the free-streaming length while baryons dominate small scales [Yankelevich et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1448Y/abstract). Furthermore, new analytical frameworks are being developed to detect parity-violating trispectra in galaxy surveys, which could serve as unique probes of high-energy inflationary physics once baryonic effects are accurately marginalized [Bao et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250402931B/abstract). To constrain feedback directly, researchers are turning to multi-wavelength observations of the circumgalactic medium (CGM) and the kinetic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (kSZ) effect. Recent comparisons with DESI and ACT data demonstrate that kSZ measurements serve as a stringent benchmark for AGN feedback models, strongly constraining the radial extent of gas expulsion and favoring models with powerful AGN-driven winds [Bigwood et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251015822B/abstract), [Crain et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ARA&A..61..473C/abstract).

To navigate the vast and computationally expensive parameter spaces of these models, the field is increasingly leveraging machine learning. Advanced emulators can map complex "feedback landscapes" into low-dimensional latent spaces, enabling the marginalization of baryonic uncertainties during cosmological inference [Lin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250901881L/abstract). Moreover, machine learning bridges the gap between different simulation approaches; graph neural networks trained on rapid [[semi-analytic-modeling|Semi-analytic modeling]] catalogs can successfully extrapolate matter density parameter ($\Omega_m$) predictions to match full hydrodynamical simulations [de Santi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251210222D/abstract). For instance, recent developments within the HACC framework utilize high-fidelity emulators to efficiently calibrate complex subgrid AGN feedback parameters against cluster gas fractions and density profiles [Ramachandra et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107306R/abstract). More fundamentally, machine learning is driving new methods for scientific equation discovery. Frameworks such as Neural Infalling Cloud Equations (NICE) employ neural ODEs to capture unresolved multiphase gas dynamics directly from high-resolution idealized runs. By applying symbolic regression to these learned networks, researchers can extract interpretable, physically grounded differential equations to replace heuristic subgrid models [Tan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240810387T/abstract). Additionally, cross-disciplinary foundation models (e.g., OmniCosmos), initially trained on particle physics data, are being adapted to generalize baryonic predictions across disparate simulation suites [Mikuni et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251224422M/abstract).

Finally, the transition to exascale simulations represents a major hardware-driven frontier. Next-generation [[numerical-simulation-codes|Numerical Simulation Codes]] are being aggressively refactored for GPU-accelerated architectures to simultaneously simulate survey-scale cosmological volumes while preserving robust, multi-channel subgrid physics [Frontiere et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251121921F/abstract), [Norman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018FrASS...5...34N/abstract). This leap in computational capability is essential for self-consistently coupling micro-scale feedback mechanisms with the macro-scale evolution of the universe, ultimately bridging the gap between localized multiphase ISM dynamics and global large-scale structure.
