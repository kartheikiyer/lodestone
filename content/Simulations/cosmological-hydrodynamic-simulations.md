## Full draft


**Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations** are large-scale computational frameworks designed to model the formation and evolution of galaxies and large-scale structure within an expanding universe. Unlike pure N-body models that solely track the gravitational collapse of collisionless dark matter via the [[collisionless-boltzmann-equation|Collisionless Boltzmann equation]], these simulations couple N-body gravity with the fluid equations of baryonic gas [[astrophysical-fluid-dynamics-and-turbulence|Astrophysical Fluid Dynamics and Turbulence]]. By explicitly modeling the dissipative nature of baryons—which, unlike dark matter, can lose kinetic energy through radiative cooling—these simulations track how gas breaks the self-similar scaling of gravitational collapse and condenses into the centers of dark matter halos to form stars [Owen et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..5097O/abstract). The foundational development of these coupled solvers in the 1990s marked a critical transition from studying isolated systems to modeling the highly non-linear, multi-phase physics of the observable universe [Katz et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJS..105...19K/abstract).

A fundamental challenge in [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]] is the immense dynamic range required to bridge the megaparsec scales of the cosmic web with the parsec and sub-parsec scales of the interstellar medium. Because the resolution of cosmological volumes cannot capture individual star formation or black hole accretion *ab initio*, these frameworks rely on heuristic [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] to approximate unresolved astrophysical processes. Historically, early hydrodynamic models suffered severely from the [[overcooling-problem|Overcooling problem]], where an excess of dense, rapidly cooling gas yielded unrealistically massive, highly compact galaxies that failed to form extended spiral disks [Piontek et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0909.4156P/abstract). Modern models mitigate this by implementing phenomenological feedback from supernovae and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]], which inject thermal and kinetic energy back into the surrounding gas to regulate star formation, drive massive outflows, and successfully truncate the high-mass end of the galaxy luminosity function. 

Today, flagship large-volume suites (such as Illustris, EAGLE, and [[mufasa-simulation|MUFASA (simulation)]]) successfully reproduce macroscopic observables, including the spatial clustering of galaxies, stellar mass functions, and fundamental scaling relations like the Tully-Fisher correlation [Vogelsberger et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.1518V/abstract), [Davé et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.3265D/abstract). This article details the underlying physical principles of these models and the major numerical methodologies—spanning Eulerian adaptive mesh refinement (AMR), Lagrangian smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH), and hybrid meshless schemes—used to solve the Euler-Poisson equations. It further explores how these computational laboratories are applied across cosmic history, from tracking the patchy thermodynamic signatures of the Epoch of Reionization to employing [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] to resolve the detailed assembly of Milky Way analogues and the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]].

Despite profound successes in matching galaxy stellar populations, significant theoretical challenges and open questions remain. Because simulations rely on the calibration of internal parameters, distinct [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]] can yield nearly identical galaxies while predicting drastically different thermodynamic states and mass flow rates in the circumgalactic medium (CGM). Furthermore, persistent parameter degeneracies make it difficult to disentangle whether structural anomalies on small scales—such as the flattening of dark matter [[density-profiles|Density profiles]]—are driven by baryonic feedback or by alternative fundamental physics, such as self-interacting dark matter. Overcoming these degeneracies is driving the transition toward exascale computing and [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] that seek to replace tuned heuristic models with first-principles ISM physics and complex non-thermal components, including magnetic fields and cosmic rays.

## Fundamentals of Cosmological Hydrodynamics

At the core of [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]] incorporating baryonic physics is the need to simultaneously evolve two fundamentally distinct mass components within an expanding Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) universe: a collisionless dark matter fluid and a collisional [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]].

**Dark Matter Dynamics**
Cold dark matter, along with stars formed later in the simulation, behaves as a pressureless dust where local particle diffusion is negligible on the macroscopic scales of interest. It is governed by the [[collisionless-boltzmann-equation|Collisionless Boltzmann equation]] (often referred to interchangeably with the [[vlasov-poisson-equation|Vlasov-Poisson equation]] in this context), which describes the conservation of the [[phase-space-density|Phase space density]] $f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{p}, t)$:
$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{\mathbf{p}}{m a^2} \cdot \nabla f - m \nabla \Phi \cdot \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{p}} = 0 $$
where $a(t)$ is the cosmological scale factor, $m$ is the particle mass, $\mathbf{p} = m a^2 \dot{\mathbf{x}}$ is the canonical momentum in comoving coordinates $\mathbf{x}$, and $\Phi$ is the peculiar gravitational potential. Because the dimensionality of phase space is too large for direct Eulerian integration on a grid, this equation is overwhelmingly solved using N-body particle-mesh or tree algorithms.

**Baryonic Gas Dynamics**
For regions much smaller than the Hubble radius, the intergalactic and interstellar media are adequately described as a Newtonian fluid. Frequent collisions among the particles establish local thermal equilibrium, validating a continuum fluid description [[astrophysical-fluid-dynamics-and-turbulence|Astrophysical Fluid Dynamics and Turbulence]]. The evolution of the baryonic density $\rho_b$, peculiar velocity $\mathbf{v}$, and specific internal energy $e$ is dictated by the Euler equations, modified to account for cosmological expansion:
$$ \frac{\partial \rho_b}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{a} \nabla \cdot (\rho_b \mathbf{v}) + 3 H \rho_b = 0 $$
$$ \frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{a} (\mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla) \mathbf{v} + H \mathbf{v} = -\frac{1}{a \rho_b} \nabla P - \frac{1}{a} \nabla \Phi $$
$$ \frac{\partial e}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{a} \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla e + 2 H e = -\frac{P}{a \rho_b} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} + \frac{\mathcal{H} - \mathcal{C}}{\rho_b} $$
Here, $H = \dot{a}/a$ is the Hubble parameter, $P$ is the thermal pressure (closed by an equation of state, typically an ideal gas), and $\mathcal{H}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ represent the volumetric heating and radiative cooling rates, respectively. The inclusion of radiative cooling ($\mathcal{C}$) is a critical step, as it breaks the self-similar scaling of pure gravitational collapse, allowing baryons to dissipate thermal energy and condense into dense, star-forming systems [Owen et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..5097O/abstract).

**The Euler-Poisson Coupling**
The dark matter and baryonic fluids are dynamically coupled through the Poisson equation, which dictates the evolution of the peculiar gravitational potential $\Phi$:
$$ \nabla^2 \Phi = 4 \pi G a^2 \left( \rho_{\rm dm} + \rho_b - \bar{\rho} \right) $$
where $\bar{\rho}$ is the mean cosmic matter density. While baryons and dark matter are expected to be well mixed in the linear regime of perturbations, they decouple on scales smaller than the baryonic Jeans length due to the pressure gradient term $\nabla P$ acting exclusively on the gas. Recognizing the limitations of purely collisionless [[matter-only-simulations|Matter-only simulations]] approaches has proven crucial, as these highly non-linear baryonic effects can significantly alter dark matter [[density-profiles|Density profiles]] within halos [Kuhlen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PDU.....1...50K/abstract).

## Historical Development and the Overcooling Problem

The transition from purely collisionless $N$-body models to modern [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] required the inclusion of gas dynamics and radiative processes. Under the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], cosmic structures grow "bottom-up" as small dark matter halos collapse and subsequently merge. To model the luminous components of galaxies within these halos, early numerical efforts sought to simulate the dissipational collapse of baryons. Pioneering work by Katz & Gunn (1991) and Katz (1992) represented some of the first applications of smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) to galaxy formation. These early simulations modeled the collapse of uniformly rotating, expanding spheres consisting of an admixture of dark matter and gas. While these early models successfully demonstrated that gas could dissipate kinetic energy and settle into a centrifugally supported disk under idealized conditions, they also revealed that incorporating realistic cosmological irregularities led to fundamental physical discrepancies, such as premature cooling in subclumps. Subsequent refinement of these numerical techniques, however, enabled successful implementations of SPH in larger Cold Dark Matter models, yielding stable baryonic mass functions that were largely insensitive to specific star formation assumptions [Katz et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJS..105...19K/abstract).

As computational resolution improved, a severe limitation emerged, known widely as the **[[overcooling-problem|Overcooling problem]]**. Radiative cooling in dark matter halos is dominated by two-body collisional processes, meaning the cooling rate is highly sensitive to gas density, scaling as $\rho^2$. In the absence of substantial non-gravitational heating, gas in the simulations rapidly lost its thermal pressure, condensed into the centers of dark matter potential wells, and converted nearly all available baryons into stars. Consequently, these early simulations systematically overpredicted the stellar mass fraction of the universe and failed to reproduce the sharp upper-mass cutoff of the galaxy luminosity function [Thoul et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...442..480T/abstract). A further challenge to these early hierarchical models was the historical mismatch between the kinematics predicted by early SPH simulations and observed Damped Lyman Alpha (DLA) systems at high redshift, highlighting the glaring limitations of models lacking sufficient feedback [Prochaska et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...560L..33P/abstract).

The overcooling problem directly triggered a secondary, equally problematic effect known as the **angular momentum catastrophe**. In a hierarchical universe, overly efficient cooling at high redshifts causes gas to prematurely condense into dense clumps within low-mass progenitor halos. As these subhalos undergo mergers, they spiral into the center of the primary dark matter halo via dynamical friction. During this orbital decay, the dense, locked-up baryonic clumps transfer the vast majority of their orbital angular momentum to the extended, collisionless dark matter halo [Piontek et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0909.4156P/abstract). As a result, the simulated galaxies were far too compact and heavily bulge-dominated, failing entirely to form the extended, rotationally supported disks characteristic of observed spiral galaxies.

It quickly became apparent that gravitational physics and radiative cooling alone were insufficient to govern galaxy formation. To resolve both the overcooling problem and the angular momentum catastrophe, models required physical mechanisms to reheat cold gas and regulate star formation. The historical consensus shifted to recognize that energetic [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]]—specifically supernova feedback in low-mass halos and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] in massive systems—are strictly necessary. By injecting thermal and kinetic energy into the interstellar medium, these feedback mechanisms suppress early star formation, prevent the premature formation of massive bulges, and allow gas to retain the angular momentum required to form realistic disk galaxies [Piontek et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0909.4156P/abstract).

## Numerical Methodologies: Eulerian, Lagrangian, and Meshless Schemes

To self-consistently evolve the baryonic component of the Universe alongside dark matter, [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]] must solve the Euler-Poisson equations governing gas dynamics and gravity [Teyssier et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251013129T/abstract). Because the dynamic range of galaxy formation spans many orders of magnitude, accurately capturing the fluid dynamics of the intergalactic and interstellar media remains a formidable computational challenge. Over the past few decades, three primary numerical methodologies have emerged: Lagrangian, Eulerian, and hybrid moving-mesh/meshless schemes.

**Lagrangian Schemes: Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH)**
In Lagrangian approaches, the fluid is discretized into a finite number of mass elements (particles) that move with the flow. Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH) is the most widely utilized Lagrangian method in astrophysics, serving as the foundation for codes like [[gadget-simulation-code|GADGET (simulation code)]] and GASOLINE. The primary advantage of SPH is its strict Galilean invariance and its natural adaptivity; particles naturally cluster in high-density collapsing regions, providing high spatial resolution where star formation occurs. However, traditional SPH relies on artificial viscosity to handle shocks, which can lead to excessive entropy generation and poor performance in capturing fluid instabilities (e.g., Kelvin-Helmholtz) and phase mixing [O'Shea et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJS..160....1O/abstract). 

**Eulerian Schemes: Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR)**
Eulerian methods compute gas dynamics across a stationary spatial grid. To achieve the resolution necessary for tracking galaxy formation within large cosmological volumes, these codes—such as [[ramses-simulation-code|Ramses (simulation code)]], Enzo, and ART—utilize Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR), where the grid subdivides dynamically in regions of high density or steep gradients. Eulerian schemes generally employ Riemann solvers (e.g., the Piecewise Parabolic Method, or PPM), which provide superior shock-capturing capabilities compared to standard SPH and seamlessly resolve the diffuse, low-density gas of the cosmic web [O'Shea et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJS..160....1O/abstract). The primary drawbacks of Eulerian grids are their lack of Galilean invariance and their susceptibility to numerical diffusion, particularly when gas moves at high velocities relative to the grid axes [Teyssier et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251013129T/abstract). Pushing the limits of Eulerian AMR, models like the [[newhorizon|NEWHORIZON]] project demonstrate the capability of running high-resolution (34 pc) simulations over intermediate cosmological volumes, effectively capturing the multi-phase ISM and clumpy star formation dynamically [Dubois et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...651A.109D/abstract).

**Moving-Mesh and Meshless Finite Mass (MFM) Schemes**
To bridge the gap between Eulerian shock-capturing and Lagrangian adaptivity, recent generation codes have developed hybrid techniques. Moving-mesh codes, such as AREPO, utilize a Voronoi tessellation that continuously deforms and moves with the local fluid velocity, minimizing advection errors while employing an exact Riemann solver across cell interfaces. Similarly, Meshless Finite Mass (MFM) schemes, implemented in [[gizmo-simulation-code|GIZMO (simulation code)]], solve the fluid equations via a Riemann solver operating between interacting particle pairs without requiring a formal grid. These hybrid approaches have been successfully deployed in modern large-scale suites, with moving-mesh driving the Illustris and [[auriga-simulations|AURIGA simulations]] projects, and MFM powering the [[mufasa-simulation|MUFASA (simulation)]] suite [Davé et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.3265D/abstract).

**Code Convergence and Comparison**
Extensive community efforts, most notably the [[agora-simulation|AGORA simulation]] High-resolution Galaxy Simulations Comparison Project, have systematically tested these methodologies against one another. By running isolated and cosmological disk simulations with standardized initial conditions and identical cooling/feedback packages, AGORA demonstrated that different numerical platforms converge remarkably well on macroscopic properties such as rotation curves, [[galaxy-dm-velocity-dispersions|Galaxy-DM velocity dispersions]], and the mass-metallicity relation [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract), [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..125R/abstract). While systematic differences persist—mesh-based and particle-based codes still deviate in poorly resolved low-density voids and at the extreme high-density tail of the gas distribution—these intrinsic numerical discrepancies are generally small. Overall, the prevailing consensus is that the physical properties of simulated galaxies are vastly more sensitive to the chosen [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]] (such as supernovae and AGN feedback) than to the underlying hydrodynamics solver [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract), [Davé et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.3265D/abstract).

## Subgrid Physics and the Baryon Cycle

In [[cosmological-and-hydrodynamical-simulations|Cosmological and Hydrodynamical Simulations]], the immense dynamic range required to bridge large-scale structure formation and local interstellar medium (ISM) physics remains computationally prohibitive. Consequently, astrophysical processes occurring below the spatial and mass resolution limits of the simulation—such as star formation, chemical enrichment, and energy injection—must be approximated using heuristic [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]]. This necessity reflects a broader convergence of physical processes—such as gas accretion, stellar winds, and black hole feedback—across both [[semi-analytic-modeling|Semi-analytic modeling]] and hydrodynamic modelling frameworks [Somerville et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ARA&A..53...51S/abstract).

The baryon cycle begins with the [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]] and subsequent radiative cooling of gas within dark matter halos. As gas dissipates its thermal and kinetic energy, it collapses to form dense cold clouds. The conversion of this gas into collisionless [[sink-particles|Sink particles]] representing stellar populations is traditionally modeled using an empirical Schmidt law based on the local cold gas density, $\rho_{\rm gas}$. However, modern simulation suites are increasingly shifting toward pressure-regulated, feedback-modulated (PRFM) models derived from high-resolution, multiphase ISM calibrations rather than relying solely on empirically tuned density thresholds [Hassan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..151H/abstract). At the faint end of the luminosity function, subgrid prescriptions for the ultraviolet radiation background are critical; photoionization heats the gas and effectively suppresses star formation in low-mass halos with $v_{\rm circ} \lesssim 30-50 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Thoul et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...465..608T/abstract).

Early [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]] universally suffered from the [[overcooling-problem|Overcooling problem]]. Because radiative cooling rates scale as $\rho_{\rm gas}^2$, gas rapidly cooled and collapsed to the centers of halos, leading to overly massive, overly compact galaxies [Piontek et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0909.4156P/abstract). To counter this, simulations must inject energy, momentum, and metals from stellar winds and supernovae back into the surrounding gas, driving [[superwinds|Superwinds]] that regulate further star formation. Phenomenological implementations of these [[multi-scale-feedback-processes|Multi-scale feedback processes]] are indispensable for extending galactic disks, matching the Tully-Fisher relation, and reproducing the observed stellar mass-to-halo mass relationships [Stinson et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..129S/abstract). While the interplay between baryonic condensation and feedback has led to historical debate over whether these processes drive adiabatic halo contraction or [[halo-expansion|Halo expansion]], specific studies using state-of-the-art simulations argue strongly that the inner dark matter density increases, concluding that the halo contraction effect is real and must be included in modeling [Gnedin et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1108.5736G/abstract).

While stellar feedback successfully regulates low- to intermediate-mass galaxies, it is insufficient to prevent overcooling in massive galaxies and galaxy clusters. To achieve the observed quenching of massive galaxies, [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] must be included. To track the growth of supermassive black holes, different numerical approaches have been developed. For instance, simulations must often compare standard modified Bondi-Hoyle accretion prescriptions with localized methods like the Accretion Disc Particle (ADP) method to accurately model black hole mass growth [Muldrew et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.4327M/abstract). Subgrid AGN feedback injects massive amounts of thermal or kinetic energy into the intra-cluster medium, which is required to reproduce the exponential cutoff at the bright end of the galaxy luminosity function and to correct systematically biased hydrostatic cluster mass estimates [Short et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704..915S/abstract), [Vogelsberger et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.1518V/abstract). 

Ultimately, these interacting subgrid processes dictate the global baryon cycle. Currently, macroscopic observables like the cosmic star formation rate and stellar mass functions can be successfully reproduced across different codes by calibrating ill-constrained subgrid parameters. However, the literature is clear that models with radically different feedback implementations can yield identical stellar populations while predicting vastly different gas flow rates and circumgalactic medium (CGM) thermodynamic profiles [Crain et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ARA&A..61..473C/abstract). Thus, the exact physical formulation of subgrid feedback remains phenomenological in many flagship runs, with CGM observations increasingly serving as the critical discriminant for validating the accuracy of these heuristic models.

## Galaxy Assembly and Scaling Relations

Modeling the assembly of galaxies within a fully cosmological context requires capturing the non-linear collapse of dark matter halos alongside complex baryonic physics. Having largely overcome historical hurdles like the [[overcooling-problem|Overcooling problem]] and the angular momentum catastrophe [Piontek et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0909.4156P/abstract), modern [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] can now reproduce realistic disk morphologies through the inclusion of energetically viable [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]]. It is now a consensus that stellar feedback from supernovae is required to regulate star formation in low-mass halos, while [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] is essential to quench massive systems [Naab et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55...59N/abstract). By calibrating these feedback channels, flagship runs such as the Illustris project and the [[mufasa-simulation|MUFASA (simulation)]] suite can self-consistently reproduce the observed $z=0$ [[mass-distribution-and-functions|Mass Distribution and Functions]] across several decades in mass [Vogelsberger et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.1518V/abstract), [Davé et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.3265D/abstract). 

Achieving this represents a major historical step forward, as early models faced the long-standing "redness problem": although simulations could successfully match the mass scales of massive galaxies at $z=1-3$, they struggled to reproduce the colors of Extremely Red Objects (EROs) without invoking artificially high dust extinction [Nagamine et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...627..608N/abstract). Furthermore, state-of-the-art models like the COLIBRE project are now pushing structural boundaries even further by employing high-resolution supersampling of dark matter alongside non-equilibrium hydrogen and helium tracking, allowing them to accurately resolve the multi-phase ISM without needing an artificial pressure floor [Schaye et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250821126S/abstract). Feedback and baryonic condensation additionally influence spatial alignment within the halos; simulations reveal that the inner dark matter halo tends to align its minor axis with the disk axis of the central galaxy, while the outer halo orientation remains largely uncorrelated [Bailin et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...627L..17B/abstract).

The emergence of realistic galaxy morphology is highly sensitive to the angular momentum of accreted material and the timing of feedback. Pre-supernova early stellar feedback expands the dense gas distribution, preventing premature central star formation and yielding exponential disks with flat rotation curves [Stinson et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..129S/abstract). High-resolution [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]], such as the [[auriga-simulations|AURIGA simulations]] and [[nihao-simulations|NIHAO simulations]], demonstrate that quiet merger histories and high specific angular momentum halos naturally yield extended, star-forming disks. Conversely, [[galaxy-merger-physics|Galaxy Merger Physics]] and strong AGN feedback typically drive morphological transformations into quiescent spheroids by destroying pre-existing disks and suppressing subsequent gas accretion [Grand et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467..179G/abstract), [Choi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837...68C/abstract). Moreover, simulations reveal that high-velocity collisions between gas-rich dwarfs can physically separate the collisionless dark matter from the collisional gas, offering a natural origin for the formation of dark matter deficient galaxies (DMDGs) [Shin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...25S/abstract).

Current large-volume models also successfully capture fundamental structural and chemical correlations. The Tully-Fisher relation between stellar (or baryonic) mass and rotation velocity is now routinely matched by simulations that appropriately distribute feedback energy without overly disrupting the disk structure [Vogelsberger et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.1518V/abstract). Similarly, the mass-metallicity relation provides a critical constraint on galactic outflow processes. Simulations show that models leveraging momentum-driven winds—where the outflow velocity scales as the local escape velocity—yield the correct slope and amplitude for the $M_*-Z$ correlation at $z \sim 2$ and broadly match local universe observations [Daveé et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..8537D/abstract). Furthermore, suites like the [[magneticum-simulations|Magneticum (simulations)]] demonstrate strong agreement with the [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]] and specific angular momentum profiles for both early- and late-type galaxies [Remus et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..309..145R/abstract). Interestingly, high-resolution $N$-body re-simulations of massive dark matter halos demonstrate that hierarchical merging alone, even in the absence of gas dissipation, can naturally produce scaling relations on cluster scales—such as the Fundamental Plane—that are remarkably similar to those of observed clusters [Lanzoni et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MSAIS...1..145L/abstract).

Despite these successes, the literature explicitly notes that reproducing scaling relations does not guarantee a unique physical solution. Distinct [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]] can yield virtually identical stellar populations and morphological mixes while predicting drastically different circumgalactic gas flows, mass loading factors, and thermodynamic states [Crain et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ARA&A..61..473C/abstract). Consequently, while the reproduction of global scaling laws is a necessary benchmark, mapping the diffuse multi-phase gas remains critical to breaking degeneracies between competing theoretical models.

## Large-Scale Structure and the Intergalactic Medium

Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations naturally produce the cosmic web, a vast network of dark matter and baryonic filaments, sheets, and voids. On these largest scales, suites like the [[bahamas-simulations|BAHAMAS simulations]] project specifically calibrate feedback processes to ensure accurate matter clustering, providing an indispensable theoretical baseline for large-scale structure cosmology and precision weak lensing surveys [McCarthy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2936M/abstract). Historically, foundational efforts like the [[millenniumtng|MillenniumTNG]] highlighted how acoustic features induced by baryons in the early universe are subsequently reflected in the distorted low-redshift galaxy distribution, serving as a powerful cosmological probe [Springel et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005Natur.435..629S/abstract). Furthermore, large-scale models are employed to isolate the effects of non-baryonic components; for instance, the [[massivenus|MassiveNuS]] simulation suite explicitly incorporates the role of massive neutrinos, demonstrating their effect in suppressing the growth of cosmic structure on small scales [Liu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JCAP...03..049L/abstract). In these vast environments, the diffuse intergalactic medium (IGM) is subjected to photoionization and heating by the cosmic ultraviolet background. High-resolution radiation-hydrodynamic simulations have demonstrated that patchy cosmic reionization seeds large-scale temperature fluctuations and spatially varying pressure smoothing in the IGM [Puchwein et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.6162P/abstract). These thermodynamic variations persist well after reionization, detectably altering the flux power spectrum and absorption profiles of the Lyman-alpha forest at $z > 4$ by inducing features such as flat-bottom or double-dip absorption lines from photoheated structures [Weinberg et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..148...21W/abstract), [Petkova et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412..935P/abstract), [Puchwein et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.6162P/abstract).

At the interface between the IGM and galaxies lies the circumgalactic medium (CGM). The thermodynamic state of the CGM is closely tied to [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]] and regulates how galaxies acquire their gas. Simulations reveal a broadly bimodal accretion pattern: [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] residing in halos of $M_{\rm halo} \lesssim 10^{11.4} M_\odot$ predominantly grow via "cold mode" accretion ($T < 10^5$ K) that channels gas along cosmic filaments, whereas more [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] shock-heat accreting gas to near their virial temperatures in a spherical "hot mode" [Keres et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007PhDT........11K/abstract). Additionally, gravitational shock heating driven by the collapse of large-scale structure is instrumental in cosmic downsizing; as matter collapses, galaxies are embedded in high-entropy environments where gas cannot efficiently cool, suppressing continued star formation globally [Cen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741...99C/abstract). Because modern [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] can often reproduce the same empirical stellar mass functions using disparate [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]], the CGM has emerged as a critical discriminant for theory. Different feedback implementations predict markedly different inflow and outflow rates, making CGM column densities and UV absorption line ratios (such as N V/O VI) a stringent test of a model's physical fidelity [Cen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160903583C/abstract), [Crain et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ARA&A..61..473C/abstract).

Bridging the gap between individual galaxies and the most massive scales, galaxy groups serve as complex laboratories for testing baryonic redistribution, and their accurate modelling significantly impacts precision cosmological parameter estimation [Oppenheimer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Univ....7..209O/abstract). On the largest virialized scales, simulations model the hot intracluster medium (ICM) of massive galaxy clusters. To systematically study these extreme environments, efforts like [[thethreehundred-project|TheThreeHundred Project]] model large samples (e.g., 324 clusters) with full hydrodynamics to enable robust statistical analyses of cluster scaling relations and internal physics [Cui et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480.2898C/abstract). A persistent consensus from numerical models is that the assumption of hydrostatic equilibrium (HSE)—routinely used to estimate cluster masses from X-ray and Sunyaev-Zel'dovich observations—is systematically biased. Simulations show that non-thermal pressure support from internal gas motions, turbulence, and gas acceleration accounts for $10\% - 30\%$ of the total pressure support; consequently, HSE-derived estimates systematically underestimate true cluster masses by a commensurate fraction [Suto et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...767...79S/abstract), [Nelson et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...792...25N/abstract). 

Furthermore, to prevent the [[overcooling-problem|Overcooling problem]] and reproduce the high-entropy cores of the ICM, simulations require energetic feedback from [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] [Short et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...704..915S/abstract). Despite the inclusion of these mechanisms, a persistent tension remains between observed entropy profiles in groups and clusters and the systematically higher entropy predicted by modern hydrodynamic simulations [Altamura et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhDT.........8A/abstract). Within the ICM itself, accurately reproducing thermodynamic profiles remains an active challenge. However, models demonstrate that incorporating artificial thermal diffusion alongside AGN feedback allows simulations to successfully reproduce the observed diversity of cool-core and non-cool-core clusters, as well as their iron profiles [Rasia et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...813L..17R/abstract). When examining specific AGN feedback mechanisms, numerical simulations reveal that rising buoyant bubbles in cluster cooling flows require dynamically important magnetic fields to maintain their structural integrity against fluid instabilities [Robinson et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...601..621R/abstract).

The environmental impact of clusters on galaxy evolution also begins well outside the cluster core. Before even entering the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|Virial Theorem and Parameters]] of a massive cluster, a significant fraction of infalling galaxies undergo "preprocessing", wherein their gas reservoirs are severely depleted within smaller group halos [Jung et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...865..156J/abstract). Beyond the hot gas, hydrodynamic simulations resolve the origin of the diffuse stellar component, or Intracluster Light (ICL), in group and cluster environments. Tracking the hierarchical assembly of these systems reveals that the ICL is primarily produced by the tidal destruction of intermediate-mass galaxies ($M_* \sim 6 \times 10^8 - 3 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$) rather than the disruption of low-mass dwarfs [Martel et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...757...48M/abstract). The spatial and kinematic distributions of this diffuse component correlate strongly with the dynamical state of the host cluster, providing a reliable observable tracer of the cluster's recent merger and assembly history [Remus et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017Galax...5...49R/abstract).

## Reionization and High-Redshift Frontiers

The Epoch of Reionization represents the last major phase transition of the Universe, driven by the emergence of the first luminous sources. Modeling this era computationally requires [[multi-dimensional-radiation-hydrodynamic-simulations|Multi-dimensional radiation-hydrodynamic simulations]], which couple the equations of gas dynamics with radiative transfer to track the propagation of ionizing photons through the intergalactic medium (IGM). While early numerical approaches relied on post-processing [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]], modern efforts self-consistently solve radiation hydrodynamics on the fly to capture the dynamic interplay between photoheating, gas expansion, and star formation [Petkova et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412..935P/abstract). However, even early [[three-dimensional-radiation-hydrodynamical-simulations|Three-dimensional radiation hydrodynamical simulations]] were pivotal in demonstrating that the topology of reionization is highly complex, revealing that cosmic voids are not necessarily ionized before overdense regions [Razoumov et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...572..695R/abstract).

The timeline of cosmic dawn begins with the formation of metal-free Pop III stars in primordial minihalos. High-resolution simulation frameworks, such as the MEGATRON and Renaissance suites, explicitly model these early epochs by capturing non-equilibrium [[chemodynamical-simulation|Chemodynamical simulation]] and reaching parsec-scale resolution to predict the spectral properties of the earliest galaxies [O'Shea et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...807L..12O/abstract), [Katz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251005201K/abstract). A consensus result from these radiation-hydrodynamic models is that the ultraviolet background established during reionization heats the surrounding gas, effectively suppressing accretion and star formation in low-mass halos ($v_{\rm circ} < 30-50$ km/s) [Thoul et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...465..608T/abstract), [Petkova et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412..935P/abstract). Consequently, the faint end of the ultraviolet luminosity function is predicted to be flat rather than steep, with star formation dropping to zero in halos below $\simeq 7 \times 10^6 \, M_\odot$ [O'Shea et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...807L..12O/abstract).

To capture the global topology of reionization while sufficiently resolving the source galaxies, large-volume radiation-hydrodynamic suites have been developed. A premier example is [[thesan|THESAN]] [Kannan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.4005K/abstract), which spans a comoving volume of $(95.5 \text{ cMpc})^3$. THESAN couples the AREPO-RT radiative transfer solver with the [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]] and dust prescriptions. Achieving baryonic mass resolutions of $5.8 \times 10^5 \, M_\odot$ and spatial softening down to $\sim 10$ pc at $z = 5.5$, THESAN successfully reproduces the observed evolution of the global neutral hydrogen fraction and the electron scattering optical depth. These results cement the consensus that star-forming galaxies were the primary drivers of hydrogen reionization by $z \sim 6$ [Kannan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.4005K/abstract).

The patchy nature of reionization leaves lasting thermodynamic and observational signatures on the IGM. Radiation-hydrodynamic models like the Sherwood-Relics suite demonstrate that photoheating generates large-scale temperature fluctuations and spatially varying pressure smoothing that persist down to $z \sim 4$, measurably altering the flux power spectrum of the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest [Puchwein et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.6162P/abstract). Furthermore, the varied bubble size distributions generated during the Epoch of Reionization imprint unique topological signatures on 21 cm emission. Specifically, the slope of the 21 cm power spectrum at large spatial scales is highly sensitive to the ionizing escape fractions and the nature of the sources, providing a crucial theoretical template for current and upcoming radio interferometry experiments aiming to characterize the sources that dominated the ionizing photon budget [Kannan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.4005K/abstract).

## Zoom Simulations and Local Group Cosmography

To overcome the computational limits of uniform-volume [[cosmological-and-hydrodynamical-simulations|Cosmological and Hydrodynamical Simulations]], researchers employ the "zoom-in" technique (see [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]]). This method allows for parsec-scale resolution of individual galaxies or specific environments within a larger, lower-resolution cosmological volume. By tracing the particles of a selected target halo back to the initial density field, the region of interest is re-populated with high-resolution dark matter and baryonic particles. The surrounding large-scale structure is sampled coarsely, which computationally preserves the correct cosmological tidal torques and continuous gas accretion without resolving the entire box at prohibitive cost. 

Zoom simulations have been instrumental in modeling Milky Way analogues. Suites such as the [[auriga-simulations|AURIGA simulations]] and the [[nihao-simulations|NIHAO simulations]] achieve the necessary resolution to self-consistently form two-component, disc-dominated galaxies. These models successfully reproduce empirical rotation curves, stellar mass-size relations, and specific morphological features such as the X-shaped bulge of the Milky Way [Grand et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467..179G/abstract), [Buck et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..334..209B/abstract). Furthermore, these [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]]—including the FIRE-2 suite—explicitly resolve the multiphase interstellar medium and implement direct stellar feedback mechanisms, demonstrating that the structural evolution of galactic discs is highly dependent on both the halo's angular momentum and the implemented [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]] [Grand et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467..179G/abstract), [Wetzel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...44W/abstract).

Beyond isolated Milky Way analogues, zoom simulations are uniquely suited for cosmography of the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]]. The ELVIS (Exploring the Local Volume in Simulations) project modeled Local Group-like environments, contrasting isolated Milky Way-mass halos with paired Milky Way-Andromeda (M31) analogues [Garrison-Kimmel et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.2578G/abstract). Although ELVIS utilized dissipationless dark matter simulations, it revealed that paired configurations possess kinematically hotter velocity fields and host nearly twice as many satellite companions on megaparsec scales compared to isolated halos. Abundance matching applied to these results predicts a large population of undiscovered [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] with stellar masses $M_* > 10^3 M_\odot$ and neutral hydrogen masses $H I > 10^5 M_\odot$ remaining in the local volume [Garrison-Kimmel et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.2578G/abstract).

Moving to fully hydrodynamic models of our specific cosmological neighborhood, the HESTIA project employs constrained initial conditions derived from the observed peculiar velocities of nearby galaxies [Libeskind et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.2968L/abstract). By embedding a 3-5 Mpc high-resolution region within a correct cosmographic landscape, HESTIA yields simulated Milky Way and M31 analogues that are in excellent agreement with observations. These constrained zoom simulations successfully reproduce the masses, mass ratios, bulge-to-disc morphologies, and satellite radial distributions of the Local Group, occasionally even producing massive satellite analogues akin to the Magellanic Clouds [Libeskind et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.2968L/abstract).

## Current Challenges and Future Directions

Despite the remarkable success of modern [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] in reproducing the macroscopic properties of the observable Universe, significant theoretical and numerical challenges remain. A primary limitation is the persistent degeneracy in [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]]. While state-of-the-art simulation suites, such as Illustris, EAGLE, and [[mufasa-simulation|MUFASA (simulation)]], successfully match the $z=0$ [[mass-distribution-and-functions|Mass Distribution and Functions]] and basic scaling relations, they often predict markedly different circumgalactic gas flow rates [Crain et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ARA&A..61..473C/abstract). Because these macroscopic successes are achieved by calibrating phenomenological feedback parameters, distinguishing between competing models requires moving beyond stellar properties to accurately probe the complex, multi-phase circumgalactic medium.

As a prerequisite for validating any of these models, rigorous [[convergence-study|Convergence study]] testing must be employed. Despite significant community alignment on macroscopic variables, systematic differences persist between numerical schemes; properties of individual simulated halos, such as spin and central [[density-profiles|Density profiles]], can suffer from severe numerical biases if resolved by fewer than $N \sim 100$ particles [Trenti et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711.1198T/abstract).

Another critical challenge is the tension and degeneracy between baryonic physics and fundamental cosmological parameters on sub-galactic scales. For example, the feedback-driven expansion of dark matter halos—often invoked to solve the [[core-halo-structure|Core-halo structure]] problem—can mimic the structural signatures of alternative dark matter paradigms, such as warm or self-interacting dark matter [Despali et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250112439D/abstract), [Silvestrini et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107543S/abstract). Alternative approaches invoke the collisional Boltzmann equation to model self-interacting dark matter (SIDM), demonstrating that such interactions can naturally produce soft cores in halos across a wide mass range [Firmani et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.10497F/abstract). Breaking these degeneracies requires [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] that minimize *ad hoc* assumptions. Moreover, the "back-reaction" of baryons on dark matter remains a profound systematic uncertainty; suites such as the CAMELS simulations show that feedback-driven gas ejection indirectly redistributes dark matter, potentially altering the total matter clustering power spectrum by up to 20% [Gebhardt et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260106258G/abstract). 

In the realm of fundamental physics beyond standard General Relativity, complementary computational approaches are essential. Structure formation simulations are used to test scalar-field-coupled dark matter models, exploring scenarios where a 'fifth force' significantly modifies the matter power spectrum on small scales [Zhao et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...712L.179Z/abstract). While hydrodynamic models map baryonic effects, $N$-body simulations and approximate analytic models are explicitly utilized to test Modified Gravity models (such as nDGP or K-mouflage) by comparing their predicted modifications to the matter power spectrum against standard gravitational frameworks [Bose et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240613667B/abstract). 

To address existing limitations, the field is moving toward multi-physics, first-principles modeling. Historically, simulations modeled interstellar medium (ISM) pressure using simplified effective equations of state. Future directions focus on implementing pressure-regulated, feedback-modulated (PRFM) models calibrated directly from sub-parsec ISM simulations [Hassan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..151H/abstract), [Burger et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250213244B/abstract). Furthermore, models are increasingly incorporating non-thermal components—specifically magnetic fields and cosmic rays—which provide critical pressure support, regulate local star formation rates, and alter the dynamics of galactic outflows [Naab et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55...59N/abstract), [Batziou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240503765B/abstract). 

The realization of these advanced, multi-scale models relies heavily on the transition to exascale computing. [[next-generation-cosmological-simulations|Next-generation cosmological simulations]] GPU-accelerated frameworks are now capable of coupling cosmological hydrodynamics with extensive subgrid models across massive volumes, optimized for modern exascale architectures [Frontiere et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251121921F/abstract). As the computational barrier recedes, cosmological hydrodynamic simulations are poised to transition from phenomenologically tuned models to fully predictive laboratories for testing the interplay of dark matter and baryonic physics across cosmic time.
