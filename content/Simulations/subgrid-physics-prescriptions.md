In [[computational-astrophysics|Computational astrophysics]], **subgrid physics prescriptions** are effective analytical and numerical models used to represent baryonic processes that occur below the spatial [[resolution-simulations|Resolution (simulations)]] or [[mass-resolution|Mass resolution]] limits of a simulation. [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] must track the evolution of large-scale structure across hundreds of megaparsecs, yet the astrophysical drivers of galaxy formation—such as [[molecular-cloud-dynamics|Molecular Cloud Dynamics]], star formation, and supermassive black hole accretion—unfold on sub-parsec scales. Because capturing this vast dynamic range from first principles is computationally prohibitive, subgrid models are required to bridge the gap. They act as macroscopic approximations, transferring mass, momentum, and energy between the unresolved microphysics and the resolved macroscopic fluid governed by the simulation's grid or [[sink-particles|Sink particles]] [Naab et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55...59N/abstract).

The inclusion of subgrid physics is essential to reproduce realistic galaxy populations and avoid catastrophic numerical artifacts. Early foundational works introduced [[heuristic-models|Heuristic models]] for stellar feedback within semi-analytic hierarchical models, recognizing its role in regulating star formation [Cole et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994MNRAS.271..781C/abstract). Subsequently, explicit hydrodynamical implementations for both stellar and active galactic nuclei (AGN) feedback demonstrated that injecting energy and momentum directly back into the surrounding gas is critical for driving large-scale galactic outflows [Vogelsberger et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3031V/abstract). Today, subgrid recipes govern the entirety of the simulated baryon cycle in modern [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]], spanning from the non-equilibrium thermochemistry of molecular gas to the kinetic jet modes of AGN feedback that quench massive elliptical galaxies.

While earlier textbook treatments often cautioned against the arbitrariness of these heavily tuned recipes, modern frameworks are increasingly rigorous and physically grounded. This article details the historical evolution of feedback models and examines contemporary [[multi-scale-multi-science-systems|Multi-scale multi-science systems]] that calibrate effective equations of state against small-scale [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] and observational datasets. It also explores efficient hybrid approaches like [[semi-analytic-modeling|Semi-analytic modeling]], as well as emerging techniques utilizing machine learning and neural ODEs for data-driven equation discovery. Finally, the article outlines persistent challenges and open questions in the field. Despite vast improvements, varying subgrid implementations remain the dominant source of systematic uncertainty in code comparisons [Scannapieco et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.1726S/abstract), [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract). Ongoing debates center on the transport mechanisms of cosmic rays, the geometric coupling of sub-parsec AGN accretion to large-scale feedback, and resolving the stark structural diversity of high-redshift dwarf galaxies now being unveiled in the JWST era.

## Foundations and the Resolution Gap

[[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] of galaxy formation must simultaneously capture the growth of large-scale structure—spanning volumes of hundreds of megaparsecs to gigaparsecs—while accounting for baryonic processes that occur on sub-parsec scales [Kuhlen et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PDU.....1...50K/abstract). This vast dynamic range presents a fundamental barrier in [[computational-astrophysics|Computational astrophysics]]. Because computational resources are finite, a strict compromise must be made between the simulated volume and the numerical [[resolution-simulations|Resolution (simulations)]]. Consequently, the internal structure of the interstellar medium, individual star-forming clouds, and the accretion discs around supermassive black holes remain orders of magnitude below the resolution limit of large-volume runs [Hobbs et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JPhCS.372a2003H/abstract), [Naab et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ARA&A..55...59N/abstract).

At the [[length-scale-astrophysics|Length scale (astrophysics)]] and [[mass-resolution|Mass resolution]] accessible to typical [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] (where spatial softening is often $\gtrsim 100 \text{ pc}$), the macroscopic fluid equations cannot self-consistently capture the microphysics governing gas collapse. For instance, radiative cooling is dominated by two-body processes, meaning the cooling rate scales with the square of the gas density, $\Lambda \propto \rho^2$. As gas cools and condenses, it rapidly collapses down to the scale of the gravitational softening length or the minimum grid cell size. Historically, this inability to resolve the multi-phase interstellar medium and the subsequent stellar feedback loop led to severe numerical artifacts. Most notable were the "overcooling problem" (where thermal energy radiated away too efficiently) and the associated "angular momentum catastrophe," where simulated galaxies rapidly lost angular momentum, becoming unphysically compact and overly massive.

To bridge this resolution gap, simulators employ subgrid physics prescriptions. These function as an effective model that approximates the macroscopic consequences of unresolved physics on the resolved scales. Instead of tracking individual supernova remnants or resolving the exact accretion radius of a black hole, subgrid models use analytic or [[heuristic-models|Heuristic models]] to transfer mass, momentum, and energy between the unresolved and resolved domains. For example, star formation is frequently modeled by converting dense gas into [[sink-particles|Sink particles]]—representing simple stellar populations—at a rate determined by the local resolved density, while the ensuing feedback is injected via artificially broadened thermal or kinetic channels to prevent immediate numerical dissipation [Vogelsberger et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3031V/abstract).

Early textbook treatments frequently cautioned that the "arbitrariness" of subgrid implementations made their physical validity highly uncertain. Indeed, comparative studies have consistently shown that variations in subgrid parameterizations are the dominant source of uncertainty in predicting observables like the galaxy stellar mass function. These subgrid variations vastly outweigh the intrinsic differences between the underlying numerical hydrodynamics solvers, such as smoothed-particle hydrodynamics versus adaptive mesh refinement [Scannapieco et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.1726S/abstract), [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract). 

However, modern simulation suites have significantly mitigated this arbitrariness. Current methodologies increasingly favor a [[multi-scale-multi-science-systems|Multi-scale multi-science systems]] approach, replacing heavily tuned phenomenological formulations with effective models systematically derived from or calibrated against small-scale, [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] of the interstellar medium [Hassan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..151H/abstract). For instance, the foundational IllustrisTNG physical model introduced revised galactic wind energy scalings and black-hole-driven kinetic feedback at low accretion rates to better regulate massive galaxies across cosmic time [Pillepich et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.4077P/abstract). By physically grounding these subgrid equations of state and utilizing multi-channel feedback injections, modern effective models successfully regulate the baryon cycle. Alternatively, some frameworks bypass the need for an equation of state entirely by explicitly resolving the multiphase interstellar medium and stellar feedback on small scales, a method which also naturally yields realistic, rotationally supported galactic structures without succumbing to the historical overcooling problem [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract).

## Historical Evolution of Feedback Recipes

In early [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]], the inclusion of stellar feedback was recognized as essential to prevent the overproduction of faint galaxies [Cole et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994MNRAS.271..781C/abstract). However, early implementations typically relied on a simple "thermal dump" approach, where the energy from supernovae (SNe) was injected purely as thermal energy into neighboring gas cells. Because star-forming gas is inherently dense, the injected thermal energy radiated away almost immediately before it could perform any mechanical work, exacerbating the aforementioned overcooling and angular momentum catastrophes [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract). 

To circumvent this, numerical recipes in the 2000s and early 2010s shifted toward phenomenological, macroscopic fixes. One popular approach was *delayed cooling* (or adiabatic feedback), where radiative cooling in heated gas particles is temporarily disabled, allowing the gas to expand adiabatically and drive outflows. While effective at suppressing star formation, this pressurizes the gas *ad hoc*, artificially altering its thermo-chemical state and suppressing molecular hydrogen ($\text{H}_2$) formation even in dense regimes [Lupi et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.1687L/abstract). Alternatively, many models adopted kinetic feedback, explicitly injecting momentum to kick gas particles out of star-forming regions. This bypasses the cooling calculation entirely and successfully generates galactic winds with realistic mass-loading factors [Vogelsberger et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3031V/abstract). In particular, cosmological models utilizing energy-driven outflows, where the wind mass loading increasingly scales toward lower-mass galaxies, were shown to be essential for accurately matching the low-mass end of the galaxy stellar mass function [Puchwein et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.2966P/abstract). Over time, state-of-the-art codes began employing hybrid thermal-kinetic subgrid models, stochastically injecting large quanta of thermal energy alongside momentum kicks to explicitly conserve energy, linear momentum, and angular momentum [Chaikin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3709C/abstract).

The modern paradigm of [[self-consistent-numerical-simulations|Self-consistent numerical simulations]] has transitioned from these macroscopic interventions toward multi-phase, explicitly resolved treatments. A critical breakthrough was the recognition that *pre-supernova* feedback—such as stellar winds and photoionizing radiation—must be included to disperse dense molecular clouds before the first SNe explode. By incorporating these early feedback channels, the local density of the interstellar medium (ISM) is pre-conditioned, naturally reducing the cooling rate when SNe eventually detonate [Emerick et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...865L..22E/abstract), [Fujimoto et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.1717F/abstract). Explicit treatments of ionizing radiation are necessary to produce the cloud-scale decorrelation between gas and stars observed in the local Universe [Emerick et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...865L..22E/abstract), [Fujimoto et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.1717F/abstract).

Concurrently, the philosophy of subgrid calibration has evolved. Rather than tuning heuristic parameters solely to match $z=0$ global scaling relations, the field is moving toward a [[multi-scale-multi-science-systems|Multi-scale multi-science systems]] approach. Modern subgrid prescriptions are increasingly derived from or calibrated against [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] of the parsec-scale ISM. For example, recent models implement star formation as a pressure-regulated, feedback-modulated (PRFM) dynamical equilibrium, replacing arbitrary density thresholds with effective equations of state grounded in first-principles ISM physics [Hassan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..151H/abstract). This historical shift underscores a broader movement in computational astrophysics: transitioning from empirical, grid-scale interventions to physically motivated, multi-channel feedback models.

## Stellar Feedback and ISM Physics

Without explicit stellar feedback, numerical models of galaxy formation consistently overpredict star formation rates by factors of 10 to 100, failing to produce a multiphase ISM or drive galactic outflows [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract). To regulate global star formation efficiencies, modern [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] employ the modified subgrid recipes for supernova feedback discussed above. For example, injecting supernova energy stochastically in large thermal dumps minimizes spurious radiative losses, while simultaneous kinetic kicks reliably produce realistic mass loading factors in galactic winds and elevate the turbulent velocity dispersion of the neutral ISM [Chaikin et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3709C/abstract).

While supernova feedback regulates global gas consumption, pre-supernova feedback—encompassing photoionization, radiation pressure, and stellar winds—is critical for regulating the local cloud-scale ISM. By clearing high-density gas around young star clusters, early feedback prevents subsequent supernovae from exploding in highly dissipative environments. This pre-clearing is strictly necessary to drive significant large-scale outflows, particularly in low-mass dwarf galaxies, where ionizing radiation alone can reduce star formation rates by over a factor of 5 [Emerick et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...865L..22E/abstract). [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] like those from the FIRE project, which track feedback on a star-by-star basis, demonstrate that incorporating these processes yields a realistic, turbulent multiphase medium rather than an unphysically thin gas disc [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract). Furthermore, the strength of pre-supernova feedback can be tested observationally via the spatial decorrelation between molecular gas and young stars on $\lesssim 100 \text{ pc}$ scales; subgrid models with insufficient early feedback frequently fail to reproduce this observed cloud-scale "uncertainty principle" [Fujimoto et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.1717F/abstract). Additionally, the rotational asymmetry and smoothness of H I discs provide a unique diagnostic for the underlying feedback physics and star formation efficiency laws [Gensior et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.1158G/abstract).

Despite broad consensus on the necessity of early feedback, the specific efficiency of individual physical mechanisms remains actively debated. While some studies find radiation pressure to be a critical driver of outflows, explicit radiation-hydrodynamical simulations frequently suggest that its dynamical impact is relatively weak compared to photoionization. In these models, photoheating suppresses star formation primarily by smoothing and thickening the gas disc to prevent the formation of dense clouds, rather than by driving violent mechanical winds [Rosdahl et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451...34R/abstract).

To reduce the arbitrariness of these implementations, the field is increasingly adopting multi-scale approaches. Next-generation [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] calibrate effective equations of state and gas depletion timescales directly against resolved parsec-scale ISM simulations. Frameworks such as the PRFM model derive these parameters from the dynamical equilibrium between gravitational collapse and feedback-driven turbulence, offering a predictive and physically grounded foundation for cosmological volumes [Hassan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..151H/abstract). The EMP-Pathfinder suite demonstrates that the tidal shock-driven disruption of stellar clusters is highly sensitive to the graininess of the cold ISM, further emphasizing the need for robust small-scale multiphase modeling in these formulations [Reina-Campos et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.3144R/abstract).

## Supermassive Black Hole Accretion and AGN Feedback

To reproduce the sharp truncation at the high-mass end of the galaxy stellar mass function, [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] must incorporate feedback from active galactic nuclei (AGN) [Booth et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398...53B/abstract), [Vogelsberger et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3031V/abstract). Without it, massive galaxies continue to artificially overcool gas, yielding unphysically high stellar masses.

**Black Hole Seeding and Accretion**
Supermassive black holes (SMBHs) are typically introduced into simulations as collisionless [[sink-particles|Sink particles]] seeded within the potential wells of massive halos. These particles then grow via BH-BH mergers and the accretion of surrounding gas. Because the Bondi radius is generally unresolved in large-scale volumes, simulations often estimate the accretion rate using the Bondi-Hoyle-Lyttleton formula:

$$ \dot{M}_{\rm BHL} = \frac{4\pi G^2 M_{\rm BH}^2 \rho}{(c_s^2 + v^2)^{3/2}} $$

However, because the smooth, large-scale gas density $\rho$ resolved by the simulation systematically underestimates the true density of the unresolved multiphase ISM, the theoretical Bondi rate is frequently multiplied by a large, ad-hoc numerical boost factor to allow BHs to grow efficiently [Booth et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398...53B/abstract). To avoid the arbitrariness of this boost factor, some models adopt alternative approaches. For instance, the SIMBA simulation utilizes a torque-limited accretion model to calculate BH growth from cold gas based on gravitational torques, while retaining the un-boosted Bondi prescription purely for hot gas [Davé et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.2827D/abstract).

**AGN Feedback Modes**
The energy liberated by BH accretion is injected back into the surrounding gas. This feedback is commonly divided into two regimes based on the Eddington ratio:
*   **Quasar Mode:** At high accretion rates, feedback is typically modeled by continuously injecting thermal energy into the surrounding gas [Booth et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398...53B/abstract), [Vogelsberger et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3031V/abstract).
*   **Radio Mode (Jet Feedback):** At low accretion rates, feedback is primarily implemented mechanically. Studies consistently show that kinetic jet feedback is the most effective mechanism for quenching star formation in massive galaxies at late times [Davé et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.2827D/abstract), [Scharré et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240407252S/abstract). In SIMBA, this is modeled via bipolar outflows with ejection velocities up to $\sim 8000 \ {\rm km \ s^{-1}}$ at Eddington ratios below $2\%$ [Davé et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.2827D/abstract). Additionally, explicit X-ray feedback is sometimes included alongside jets to quench residual star formation in the dense central regions of galaxies [Scharré et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240407252S/abstract). While AGN jets are primarily known for quenching, they can also trigger "positive feedback." In gas-rich environments, jets can compress the clumpy interstellar medium, inducing a burst of star formation that manifests as a ring- or disc-shaped population of young stars [Gaibler et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425..438G/abstract). Recent 3D enhancements in models like MACER3D resolve gas dynamics from the Bondi radius to halo scales, showing that synergistic supernova and AGN feedback are required to reproduce observed AGN duty cycles [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...985..178Z/abstract). Furthermore, to succinctly capture the macroscopic consequences of these varied energy injection mechanisms, simulators have developed universal fitting formulas derived from the Simba suite that describe gas density profiles. These analytic forms reliably capture the profound impact of different AGN feedback modes on the halo gas distribution across a vast range of masses and redshifts [Sorini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E.115S/abstract).

**Tensions and Geometric Dependencies**
The literature contains significant tension regarding the optimal geometry and numerical implementation of these energy injections. While early models often deposited energy spherically, injecting momentum or thermal energy in a bipolar or conical geometry yields a more complex, physically realistic central gas structure and avoids artificially clearing the entire nucleus [Zubovas et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457..496Z/abstract), [Debuhr et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406L..55D/abstract). Recent stochastic bipolar momentum deposition models, such as MISTRAL, have shown success in regulating massive galaxies without relying on explicit, SMBH-mass dependent AGN feedback scalings [Farcy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250408041F/abstract). Furthermore, code comparison studies show that even with identical subgrid prescriptions, the morphology and mixing of AGN jets vary between smoothed particle hydrodynamics, moving-mesh, and grid codes due to differences in hydrodynamical solvers [Maragkakis et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251018293M/abstract).

Ultimately, while conflicting subgrid choices—such as Bondi versus torque-limited accretion, or thermal versus kinetic feedback—can often be calibrated to reproduce similar macroscopic scaling relations at redshift zero, they predict markedly different internal thermodynamic states, outflow rates, and gas structures within the circumgalactic medium [Crain et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ARA&A..61..473C/abstract). Resolving these ambiguities requires comparing simulated mock observables against detailed multi-wavelength data of the circumgalactic environment.

## Thermochemistry and Molecular Cloud Evolution

To accurately model the multi-phase interstellar medium within [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]], recent approaches have shifted from relying solely on total cold gas density thresholds to linking star formation directly to the local abundance of molecular hydrogen (${\rm H_2}$). Subgrid models tracking non-equilibrium chemistry, often utilizing specialized libraries such as KROME [Capelo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.3283C/abstract), [Lupi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.1687L/abstract), solve coupled reaction networks to determine the ${\rm H_2}$ fraction within coarse simulation cells. Implementing ${\rm H_2}$-regulated star formation limits early stellar assembly in metal-poor dark matter haloes where molecule formation is inefficient. This physically motivated approach yields better agreement with the observed Kennicutt-Schmidt relation and has been shown to reduce the number of luminous satellites around Milky Way-mass galaxies by $10{-}30\%$ [Tomassetti et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.3330T/abstract).

A persistent challenge in these [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] is the precise calculation of radiative cooling rates. While many older models assume collisional ionization equilibrium, non-equilibrium metal cooling alters the thermodynamic state of the gas. Simulations incorporating non-equilibrium metal networks alongside dust physics generally produce warmer gas and smoother galactic discs [Capelo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.3283C/abstract). Furthermore, correctly accounting for the physics of a partially ionized gas introduces a crucial ionization potential energy reservoir. This reservoir can absorb up to $50\%$ of injected supernova feedback energy, substantially altering the thermodynamic state of the ISM and potentially reducing the dynamical effects of thermal feedback [Vandenbroucke et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...771...36V/abstract). Dust grains act as crucial catalysts for ${\rm H_2}$ formation. Although the exact choice of formation rates on dust is less critical at near-solar metallicities, it strongly dictates the molecular gas fraction at low metallicities [Capelo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.3283C/abstract). To properly model these regimes, recent efforts involve explicit subgrid models for ${\rm H_2}$ formation through dust grain condensation coupled with its destruction via photodissociation; incorporating these effects demonstrates that such detailed microphysical processes directly impact and delay the onset time of star formation in assembling galaxies [Lozano et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251000959L/abstract). Consequently, cutting-edge frameworks, such as the COLIBRE project, are introducing explicit tracking of multiple dust grain species and sizes coupled directly to the non-equilibrium chemical network [Schaye et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250821126S/abstract). However, there remains active debate in the literature regarding the macroscopic impact of non-equilibrium thermochemistry on global galaxy morphology; the extent to which non-equilibrium cooling alters large-scale structure compared to the dominant energetic injections from a [[sn-feedback-model|SN feedback model]] is still under investigation.

Resolving the lifecycle of giant molecular clouds (GMCs) remains computationally prohibitive in large cosmological volumes. The predicted mass and spatial distributions of GMCs are highly sensitive to the chosen subgrid star formation efficiency per free-fall time, $\epsilon_{\rm ff}$, and the stellar feedback channels [Li et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.5862L/abstract). [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] of isolated clouds demonstrate that $\epsilon_{\rm ff}$ is not a universal constant; while it is $\sim 1\%$ for typical Milky Way GMC surface densities ($\sim 10^2 \, \mathrm{M_\odot \, pc^{-2}}$), it can saturate toward $\sim 1$ in ultraluminous infrared galaxy environments ($\sim 10^4 \, \mathrm{M_\odot \, pc^{-2}}$) [Grudić et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.3511G/abstract). Without effective feedback, simulations overpredict GMC masses, producing a shallower power-law mass function. Furthermore, incorporating detailed feedback leaves distinct spatial imprints: radiative feedback strongly suppresses the GMC two-point correlation function on scales $< 0.2 \, \mathrm{kpc}$, whereas supernova feedback diminishes clustering on scales $> 0.2 \, \mathrm{kpc}$ [Li et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.5862L/abstract). To better capture these unresolved structures, recent methods like the Cold Gas Subgrid Model (CGSM) utilize a two-fluid framework that explicitly tracks the mass and momentum of unresolved cold gas using prescriptions derived from cloud-crushing and thermal instability simulations [Butsky et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240203419B/abstract). In this context, modeling [[molecular-cloud-dynamics|Molecular Cloud Dynamics]] remains central to understanding the transition from diffuse gas to star-forming regions.

## Non-thermal Physics: Cosmic Rays and Magnetic Fields

While early [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] relied primarily on thermal and kinetic energy injection to model feedback, contemporary frameworks increasingly incorporate non-thermal physics—specifically magnetic fields (MHD) and cosmic rays (CRs)—to provide additional pressure support and drive galactic outflows.

**Magnetic Fields and the Galactic Dynamo**
The inclusion of magnetohydrodynamics in [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] allows for the tracking of magnetic field amplification via galactic turbulent dynamos. In the absence of strong radiative cooling and feedback, magnetic field amplification largely follows the adiabatic flux-freezing scaling 
$$B \propto \rho^{2/3}$$
where $\rho$ is the gas density. However, feedback physics dramatically alters this picture [Marinacci et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.3999M/abstract). Modern simulations show that magnetic fields are amplified through three distinct evolutionary phases: an initial collapse phase, an accretion-driven phase, and a feedback-driven phase [Martin-Alvarez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.3343M/abstract). In massive haloes, the resulting magnetic fields can be amplified from primordial seed values to intensities of $10-100\,\mu\text{G}$, bringing them into agreement with observations of galaxy clusters [Marinacci et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.3999M/abstract). When subgrid star formation models are modified to self-consistently include magnetic pressure as an additional source of regulation, the simulations successfully reproduce local structural properties, such as the observed anti-correlation between local magnetic field strength and the star formation rate [Batziou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240503765B/abstract). Complementing these advanced numerical simulations, specialized theoretical models have also been developed to derive explicit magnetic field scaling relations for both random and [[mean-field-approximation|mean components]], linking these fields directly to fundamental observable properties like galaxy rotation curves and global star formation rates [Chamandy et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966...78C/abstract).

**Cosmic Ray Transport and Feedback**
Cosmic rays, accelerated primarily by supernovae and active galactic nuclei (AGN), can approach energy equipartition with the thermal and magnetic components of the interstellar medium and provide dominant pressure support in the circumgalactic medium [Ruszkowski et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&ARv..31....4R/abstract). Because explicitly resolving CR-MHD interactions on microscopic scales is computationally prohibitive, simulations utilize simplified subgrid prescriptions for CR transport. These models typically parametrize CR diffusion and streaming speeds, interpolating between limits where CRs escape galaxies with negligible energy loss and where they catastrophically lose energy in dense starburst environments [Hopkins et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.2936H/abstract).

The injection of CRs has profound effects on galaxy evolution from dwarf to L* galaxies. The additional non-thermal pressure support suppresses gas accretion onto haloes, weakening galactic outflows and inhibiting the growth of supermassive black holes by lowering central gas densities [Ramesh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240918238R/abstract). Furthermore, on cluster scales, the efficiency with which CRs are accelerated by structure formation shocks is a critical parameter. Recent constraints derived from $\gamma$-ray emission suggest that the acceleration efficiency at cluster shocks may be significantly lower than traditionally assumed, averaging $\le 10^{-3}$ [Vazza et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459...70V/abstract).

**Ambiguities in the Literature**
The relative energetic dominance of non-thermal physics remains a subject of active debate in the literature. Some high-resolution studies argue that the effects of magnetic fields, anisotropic conduction, and metal diffusion are sub-dominant ($\sim 10\%$ level) compared to the macroscopic regulation provided by explicit stellar feedback prescriptions [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract). In this view, galactic star formation is governed overwhelmingly by subgrid-scale turbulence and supernova feedback, with non-thermal components playing only a secondary role. Conversely, other recent models indicate that CRs and magnetic fields are critical for reshaping the cosmic star formation rate density and the stellar mass function, primarily because they provide long-lived pressure support in regions where thermal energy is rapidly radiated away [Ramesh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240918238R/abstract).

## Numerical Implementation and Convergence Challenges

Incorporating subgrid prescriptions into macroscopic hydrodynamic solvers introduces profound numerical challenges. Large-scale comparison efforts, such as the [[agora-simulation|AGORA project]] [Kim et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..210...14K/abstract) and the nIFTy project, have systematically evaluated how varying numerical architectures interact with unresolved physics. A key consensus across the literature is that intrinsic differences between numerical schemes (e.g., smoothed particle hydrodynamics versus adaptive mesh refinement) are generally smaller than the variations induced by differing subgrid feedback parameters [Scannapieco et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.1726S/abstract), [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract). In the nIFTy project, non-radiative cluster simulations exhibited excellent agreement across eleven different codes. However, the introduction of full subgrid feedback physics led to severe divergences: the predicted number of galaxies with stellar masses $10^9 \ h^{-1} \text{M}_\odot \lesssim M_* \lesssim 10^{12} \ h^{-1} \text{M}_\odot$ varied by a factor of 4, and individual galaxy stellar masses scattered by $\sim 0.5 \text{ dex}$ across different codes [Elahi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.458.1096E/abstract). 

Achieving rigorous numerical convergence is fundamentally complicated by finite [[mass-resolution|Mass resolution]] and the necessity of force softening. When simulating multiple collisionless species, energy equipartition driven by two-body scattering can transfer kinetic energy from heavier dark matter particles to lighter stellar particles. If the dark matter particle mass exceeds the stellar particle mass, this scattering artificially inflates the projected half-mass radii ($R_{50}$) of galaxies over time, producing spuriously large galaxies [Ludlow et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488L.123L/abstract). In addition to stellar scattering, the dynamics of massive black holes during galaxy mergers are highly susceptible to numerical perturbations. To prevent spurious orbits and ensure accurate sink particle trajectories, simulations often require specialized, density-independent refinement criteria that enforce maximum spatial resolution around these massive objects [Lupi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.1765L/abstract). Furthermore, hydrodynamical runs are highly sensitive to the choice of gravitational softening. Softening imposes a minimum resolved escape speed, $v_\varepsilon$. If the softening length is too small, yielding $v_\varepsilon \gtrsim 10 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ (comparable to the sound speed in ionized $\sim 10^4 \text{ K}$ gas), the physical efficacy of photoheating is artificially reduced. Conversely, setting the softening below a critical threshold $\varepsilon_{\rm eFB}$ can exacerbate numerical overcooling during feedback injection, highlighting that softening lengths cannot be arbitrarily reduced without breaking subgrid assumptions [Ludlow et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.2926L/abstract). In addition to standard hydrodynamics, explicitly capturing magnetohydrodynamic turbulence presents its own formidable numerical obstacles. High-resolution MHD turbulence simulations are deeply sensitive to the implicit and explicit roles of numerical viscosity and numerical resistivity, which strictly dictate the physical dissipation scales and complicate efforts to achieve true convergence in turbulent environments [Federrath et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251012203F/abstract).

Because physical processes such as star formation and black hole accretion occur on sub-parsec scales, [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] typically discretize their associated feedback using probabilistic sampling. This stochasticity introduces fundamental limits on the predictive power of simulations for individual objects. Random variability across re-simulations of the exact same initial conditions can alter the final stellar mass of well-resolved individual galaxies by up to $25\%$ [Borrow et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.2441B/abstract). Consequently, while cumulative statistical distributions (e.g., the galaxy stellar mass function) remain robust, the detailed evolutionary trajectories of individual halos are subject to significant scatter driven by bursty, stochastic physics. Beyond power spectra, the halo bispectrum on small scales is highly sensitive to the specific efficiency of subgrid star formation and feedback models [Yankelevich et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1448Y/abstract). To empirically constrain the numerical thresholds used in these implementations, simulators have turned to specific observational metrics. For example, the two-point correlation function of young stars (stellar clustering) serves as a sensitive test for star formation prescriptions, with observations strongly favoring models that employ high star formation density thresholds ($n > 10 \text{ cm}^{-3}$) [Buck et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.1481B/abstract).

Beyond the baryonic sector, subgrid feedback choices also profoundly impact the underlying collisionless structures within the simulation. Varying the efficiency of stellar and AGN feedback models induces distinct, measurable modifications to the dark matter density profile and the corresponding velocity distribution within host halos [Nuñez-Castiñeyra et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...05..012N/abstract). Furthermore, these strong baryonic influences can often mimic the signatures of modified cosmologies, introducing significant degeneracies between baryonic physics prescriptions and structural modifications caused by extensions to the standard cosmological model. For instance, disentangling whether the suppression of satellite galaxy abundance is caused by warm dark matter or by potent baryonic feedback remains a significant challenge for interpreting small-scale structure observables [Stafford et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497.3809S/abstract).

Ultimately, strict [[resolution-simulations|resolution]] independence remains an active challenge in [[computational-astrophysics|Computational astrophysics]]. Historically, radiative cooling was inherently sensitive to resolution, systematically leading to overcooling as higher resolutions resolved higher density peaks. While explicit [[multi-dimensional-radiation-hydrodynamic-simulations|Multi-dimensional radiation-hydrodynamic simulations]] and non-equilibrium chemistry mitigate some of this sensitivity by providing more physically grounded cooling and heating rates [Tomassetti et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.3330T/abstract), most contemporary subgrid models still require explicit re-calibration of their efficiency parameters at different resolution tiers to maintain agreement with observational scaling relations. Despite these ongoing challenges, advances in numerical implementation continue to push the boundaries of large-scale hydrodynamics. The MillenniumTNG project (MTNG740), for instance, has successfully produced realistic galaxy cluster scaling relations within unprecedented, large-volume hydrodynamical environments, demonstrating that modern subgrid implementations can remain robust across immense cosmological scales [Pakmor et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2539P/abstract).

## Hybrid Approaches: Semi-Analytic Models and Baryonification

To bridge the massive dynamical range between sub-parsec feedback injection and gigaparsec cosmological volumes, researchers frequently employ hybrid approaches that bypass explicit hydrodynamics. These techniques rely on $N$-body gravity solvers supplemented by phenomenological or parametric models to trace the baryonic component. 

[[semi-analytic-modeling|Semi-analytic modeling]] provides a computationally efficient alternative to full [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] by applying simplified analytic equations for gas cooling, star formation, and feedback directly to dark matter halo merger trees extracted from $N$-body simulations [Somerville et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ARA&A..53...51S/abstract). Modern semi-analytic models can successfully reproduce a wide range of global observables over large volumes. For instance, the GalICS 2.0 model calculates galaxy rotation speeds, $v_{\rm rot}$, derived from the full gravitational potential—incorporating both dark matter and baryons—which allows it to simultaneously reproduce the galaxy stellar mass function and the Tully-Fisher relation [Cattaneo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471.1401C/abstract). Recent iterations of models like L-Galaxies match global scaling relations up to $z\simeq 10$ [Vani et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240800824V/abstract), though they still struggle significantly to reproduce the abundance and sizes of massive quenched galaxies at high redshifts (e.g., underpredicting them by a factor of 60 at $z\simeq 3$). Note that the SAGE codebase provides a modular and customizable SAM framework for modeling baryons across various N-body simulations including Millennium and Bolshoi [Croton et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..222...22C/abstract). Meanwhile, other models such as FEGA have successfully incorporated positive active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback (AGN-induced starbursts) alongside standard quenching mechanisms [Contini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240510544C/abstract). Despite these successes, an acknowledged limitation remains: these models inherently fail to capture the detailed, multi-phase internal structure of the interstellar and circumgalactic mediums that explicit hydrodynamical solvers resolve.

To populate massive simulation volumes for mock catalogs and parameter estimation, statistical frameworks like the Halo occupation distribution (HOD) are frequently employed. Analyses of magneto-hydrodynamic runs have demonstrated that the redshift evolution of the HOD is primarily governed by the underlying cosmological hierarchical growth of structure, rather than specific baryonic physics prescriptions [Contreras et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.4257C/abstract). When coupled with machine learning emulators, HOD models can predict fully non-linear galaxy-galaxy auto- and cross-power spectra to an accuracy of $\sim 1-2\%$ [Kwan et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...810...35K/abstract).

An increasingly vital hybrid approach for large-scale structure cosmology is Baryonification. This technique applies physically motivated, parametric spatial corrections to the particle distributions of gravity-only $N$-body simulations to mimic gas expulsion and heating. Baryonic feedback—particularly from AGN jets—is known to suppress the total matter power spectrum, $P(k)$, by up to $\approx 20\%$ on small scales [Schaye et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.4978S/abstract). Baryonification algorithms accurately model this effect, reproducing the matter power spectrum suppression to $1\%$ accuracy across a variety of AGN feedback strengths. Furthermore, extended baryonification models can simultaneously fit the electron pressure auto- and cross-power spectra to within $10\%$ accuracy, allowing for self-consistent, rapid modeling of the thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect and weak gravitational lensing down to small scales [Aricò et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A.188A/abstract).

Finally, the rich phase-space data generated by these hybrid approaches is increasingly leveraged by advanced machine learning techniques. For example, graph neural networks trained purely on the 3D positions and radial velocities of galaxies generated via semi-analytic models can estimate the matter density parameter, $\Omega_{\rm m}$, to a precision of $\approx 10\%$. Remarkably, this predictive capability is robust across entirely different hydrodynamical and semi-analytic codes, suggesting that the phase-space encoding of cosmology is largely independent of the specific subgrid physical prescriptions chosen [de Santi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251210222D/abstract).

## Emerging Techniques: Machine Learning and Neural ODEs

In the context of [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]], traditional subgrid physics heavily relies on phenomenological prescriptions that must be manually tuned to match a specific set of macroscopic observables. As the complexity of [[computational-astrophysics|Computational astrophysics]] grows, the field is increasingly integrating machine learning and data-driven techniques. These emerging tools are utilized both to systematically calibrate existing multiparameter models and to discover entirely new governing equations directly from small-scale fluid dynamics data.

**Machine Learning for Subgrid Calibration**
One major application of machine learning is the use of emulators to explore vast subgrid parameter spaces efficiently. By running smaller-volume simulations spanning a Latin hypercube of parameter values, researchers train emulators (often utilizing Gaussian processes) to predict large-scale observables as continuous functions of the subgrid parameters. This method replaces manual tuning with robust statistical inference, allowing simulators to formally match targets such as the $z=0$ galaxy stellar mass function, galaxy sizes, and cluster gas fractions. This emulator-based calibration has been successfully deployed to constrain stellar and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback efficiencies in the FLAMINGO [Schaye et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.4978S/abstract) and COLIBRE [Chaikin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250904067C/abstract) simulation suites. Similarly, high-fidelity emulators have been utilized to infer constraints on AGN kinetic feedback modes by simultaneously fitting radial gas density profiles and overall cluster gas fractions [Ramachandra et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107306R/abstract).

**Scientific Discovery via Neural ODEs and Symbolic Regression**
Beyond parameter calibration, machine learning is now being employed for *scientific equation discovery*. In multi-phase astrophysical environments, such as the interaction between cold gas clouds and a hot circumgalactic medium, the exchange of mass, momentum, and energy is governed by complex, small-scale processes like [[subgrid-scale-turbulence|Subgrid-scale turbulence]] and radiative cooling. Accurately capturing these interactions is notoriously difficult using standard [[heuristic-models|Heuristic models]].

To address this, recent frameworks utilize Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Neural ODEs). In a traditional subgrid prescription, the time evolution of a localized state vector $\mathbf{y}$ (representing, for example, cloud mass, velocity, and thermal energy) is dictated by an assumed, closed-form analytic function. In a Neural ODE framework, the uncertain or unresolved physics is instead modeled by an artificial neural network with weights $\mathbf{\theta}$:
$$ \frac{d\mathbf{y}}{dt} = f_{\text{known}}(\mathbf{y}, t) + \text{NN}_{\mathbf{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}, t) $$
The neural network term is trained on precise data derived from [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] or targeted [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] to capture the exact non-linear dynamics of the unresolved scales. 

Once the network accurately predicts the phase evolution, *symbolic regression* is applied to the trained neural network model. Symbolic regression algorithms search the space of mathematical expressions to translate the numerical weights of the neural network back into human-interpretable, analytic equations. The Neural Infalling Cloud Equations (NICE) framework has successfully demonstrated this pipeline, proving that coupling Neural ODEs with symbolic regression can extract the governing equations for infalling cold clouds directly from simulation data [Tan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240810387T/abstract). By systematically identifying these underlying mathematical relations, this approach enhances the accuracy, generality, and physical interpretability of subgrid models, representing a significant paradigm shift from empirical parameter tuning toward rigorous, data-driven theoretical discovery.

## Observational Calibration and Future Directions

Historically, subgrid models for [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] have been calibrated primarily against $z=0$ observables, such as the galaxy stellar mass function (GSMF). However, models employing fundamentally different feedback implementations can often reproduce similar stellar populations while predicting markedly different gas flow rates into and out of galactic halos [Crain et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ARA&A..61..473C/abstract). To break these degeneracies, the focus of observational calibration has shifted to the circumgalactic medium (CGM). Modern calibration strategies frequently employ machine learning and emulators trained on Latin hypercube samples of the subgrid parameter space. This allows for rigorous, simultaneous calibration against both the GSMF and X-ray or thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich observations of cluster gas fractions. Tools like POWDERDAY allow for the creation of synthetic spectral energy distributions by post-processing simulations with dust radiative transfer and stellar population synthesis [Narayanan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..252...12N/abstract), directly bridging the gap between physical models and complex telescope datasets. This multi-observable strategy was pioneered by efforts such as the [[bahamas-simulations|BAHAMAS project]], which calibrated AGN feedback explicitly to match both the galaxy stellar mass function and the hot gas mass fractions of clusters, ensuring realistic matter distributions on large scales [McCarthy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2936M/abstract). This approach has been further refined by modern suites like FLAMINGO and COLIBRE [Schaye et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.4978S/abstract), [Chaikin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250904067C/abstract). Additionally, the kinetic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (kSZ) effect has recently emerged as a crucial benchmark for evaluating the radial extent of gas expulsion and directly testing the physical efficacy of differing AGN feedback prescriptions [Bigwood et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251015822B/abstract).

At high redshifts, early data from JWST is providing unprecedented constraints on the physics of star formation and feedback. The structural diversity, stellar masses, and interstellar medium (ISM) properties of galaxies at $z \gtrsim 6$ serve as stringent tests for early feedback mechanisms. Recent models have demonstrated that modern subgrid prescriptions can successfully reproduce the observed abundance of high-redshift massive quiescent galaxies without requiring deviations from the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology or variable stellar initial mass functions [Chaikin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250907960C/abstract). Furthermore, rest-frame ultraviolet and optical strong-line diagnostics—such as O32, C43, and N43—have been shown to be highly sensitive to the feedback-modulated density and temperature structure of the ISM. These nebular emission line ratios act as direct constraints on the ionizing radiation and feedback energy budget in the early Universe [Katz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241107282K/abstract), [Choustikov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251006347C/abstract). In addition to these rest-frame optical diagnostics, $\text{Ly}\alpha$ emission maps and their detailed spectral parameters function as sensitive probes of the feedback-regulated escape of ionizing Lyman-continuum (LyC) photons from high-redshift dwarf galaxies [Yuan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.3643Y/abstract). The [[thesan|THESAN]] project specifically targets this regime, coupling radiation hydrodynamics with subgrid galaxy formation to study cosmic reionization and directly match JWST observations of the Epoch of Reionization [Kannan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.4005K/abstract), [Garaldi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.3765G/abstract). Furthermore, the inclusion of binary stars in stellar population models, as seen in the SPHINX simulations, can significantly accelerate reionization by increasing ionizing escape fractions [Rosdahl et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479..994R/abstract). 

For low-mass systems, the EDGE project has demonstrated that the stellar mass-metallicity relation of dwarf galaxies provides a unique, highly sensitive constraint for differentiating between competing feedback models, which often yield degenerate predictions for the total stellar mass alone [Agertz et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.1656A/abstract). Comparative studies of TNG50 and [[newhorizon|NewHorizon]] reveal that dwarf galaxy morphologies are extremely sensitive to ISM and feedback prescriptions, with models often sitting at opposite extremes of observed structural trends [Martin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.541.1831M/abstract). At the opposite extreme of the mass scale, model-independent assessments using spectral variance in the optical window suggest that current [[heuristic-models|AGN feedback]] and black hole seeding implementations may still struggle to mimic real quiescent galaxy populations accurately [Sharbaf et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108945S/abstract).

Looking forward, the field is undergoing a paradigm shift away from purely [[heuristic-models|Heuristic models]] toward first-principles calibrations derived from [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] of the multiphase ISM. Traditional effective equation of state models are progressively being supplanted by pressure-regulated, feedback-modulated (PRFM) frameworks. These advanced models extract subgrid formulae—such as the effective velocity dispersion and gas depletion time—directly from parsec-scale ISM simulations (e.g., the TIGRESS framework) and apply them to large-scale [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]] [Hassan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..151H/abstract), [Burger et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250213244B/abstract). As the community transitions into the era of exascale computing [Norman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018FrASS...5...34N/abstract), these [[multi-scale-multi-science-systems|Multi-scale multi-science systems]] will increasingly bridge the gap between sub-parsec star formation physics and gigaparsec cosmological volumes [Hopkins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..18H/abstract), a central goal of modern [[computational-astrophysics|Computational astrophysics]].
