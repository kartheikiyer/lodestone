In modern extragalactic astrophysics, **forward modeling** is the computational technique of translating the physical outputs of cosmological and hydrodynamical simulations—such as gas densities, stellar masses, and metallicities—into synthetic observables. Traditionally, validating theoretical models relied on the "inverse problem," where observers mathematically invert spectral energy distributions (SEDs) to derive intrinsic galaxy properties. However, as standard textbook theory notes, this inverse problem is highly degenerate. Relying on simplistic analytic assumptions, such as uniform foreground dust screens, frequently biases inferred physical properties like the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]] or star formation rates. Forward modeling circumvents these inferential bottlenecks by constructing a "virtual observatory." By processing simulated galaxies purely from the observer's perspective, this approach enables direct, apples-to-apples comparisons between theoretical models and real multiwavelength data from facilities like JWST and ALMA [Silva et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph..3350S/abstract), [Overzier et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..778O/abstract), [Fortuni et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...677A.102F/abstract).

The forward modeling pipeline requires a series of complex physical and instrumental transformations. First, the discrete star particles within a simulation are mapped to continuous rest-frame spectra using [[bruzual-charlot-stellar-population-synthesis-models|Bruzual-Charlot stellar population synthesis models]] or other libraries of [[composite-astrophysical-models-and-populations|Composite Astrophysical Models and Populations]] [Conroy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...708...58C/abstract), [Conroy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ARA&A..51..393C/abstract). Because the inhomogeneous 3D geometry of the interstellar medium profoundly alters escaping radiation, state-of-the-art pipelines utilize 3D Monte Carlo radiative transfer to rigorously simulate the absorption, scattering, and thermal re-emission of starlight by dust grains. These frameworks are augmented by sub-grid recipes for nebular emission, which predict how local ionized gas alters line ratios and shifts simulated galaxies across [[traditional-bpt-diagrams|Traditional BPT diagrams]]. Finally, to guarantee an "even-handed" comparison, the resulting ideal SEDs and data cubes are degraded with telescope-specific instrumental effects, including filter throughput convolutions, point spread function (PSF) blurring, and background noise injection.

Generating realistic mock images and catalogs has revolutionized both the study of galaxy evolution and cosmological parameter estimation. By producing synthetic surveys that emulate specific observational selection functions—such as those targeting the [[sdss-luminous-red-galaxy|SDSS luminous red galaxy]] population—researchers can utilize simulation-based inference (SBI) to extract stringent cosmological constraints from complex, non-linear clustering scales. Because executing high-fidelity radiative transfer across massive cosmological volumes is computationally prohibitive, the methodology is currently undergoing a major shift. The field is rapidly adopting differentiable programming frameworks and GPU-accelerated machine learning emulators to bypass traditional processing bottlenecks [Hearin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1741H/abstract), [Çakır et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241208265C/abstract). These advanced computational architectures allow for gradient-based optimization and expansive likelihood analyses that were previously impossible.

Despite these rapid advancements, forward modeling continues to expose critical open questions and tensions between simulated universes and observational reality. For instance, complex star-dust geometries introduce severe degeneracies; current simulations often struggle to simultaneously reproduce ultraviolet continuum slopes and sub-millimeter dust emission at high redshifts ($z > 5$). Furthermore, comparing mock catalogs to real data highlights persistent macroscopic tensions, such as discrepancies in the predicted [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]]. Resolving these limitations to match the unprecedented fidelity of next-generation space telescopes will require pushing future hydrodynamical simulations to exascale, star-by-star resolutions while continuously refining the sub-grid physics of the virtual observatory.

## Conceptual Framework of Forward Modeling

Modern cosmological simulations integrate gravity, gas dynamics, and various feedback mechanisms to provide a foundational model of the complex processes governing galaxy evolution [Feldmann et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250708925F/abstract). Traditionally, validating these galaxy formation simulations relied on an indirect, "backward" approach. Observers would estimate physical parameters—such as stellar mass, star-formation rate (SFR), or metallicity—from real data by mathematically inverting the highly degenerate physics of galaxy emission. These derived parameters were then compared to the intrinsic outputs of cosmological simulations. However, this inverse problem is fraught with systematic uncertainties. Extracting physical quantities from spectral energy distributions (SEDs) requires making restrictive, simplifying assumptions about the stellar initial mass function, star-dust geometry, and chemical enrichment histories [Conroy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...708...58C/abstract). 

When applied to mock data where the "ground truth" is known, standard observational inference techniques often fail to accurately recover the underlying physical properties of the simulated galaxies. Simplifications in SED fitting, such as assuming a uniform foreground dust screen or idealized star formation histories, can systematically bias inferred stellar masses, photometric redshifts, and star formation rates compared to the known ground truth [Hahn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240919054H/abstract), [Faucher et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..220F/abstract). Systematic differences arising from the choice of mass-weighted versus luminosity-weighted quantities further compound the disconnect between theory and observations [Guidi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.2381G/abstract).

To bypass these inferential bottlenecks, the field has increasingly adopted a direct forward modeling framework. In this paradigm, theoretical models are viewed purely from the *observer's perspective* [Overzier et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..778O/abstract). Rather than attempting to translate observational data into physical space, simulations are rigorously translated into the observational plane. This is achieved by constructing a *virtual telescope* or "virtual observatory" pipeline that processes hydrodynamical simulation snapshots into *synthetic observables*—such as multi-band images, integral field unit (IFU) data cubes, and mock catalogs—that precisely mimic the specific instrumental characteristics of target surveys like the Hubble Space Telescope, JWST, or SDSS [Overzier et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..778O/abstract), [Fortuni et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...677A.102F/abstract).

The forward modeling process can be generalized mathematically as applying a complex observational transfer function, $\mathcal{H}$, to the intrinsic simulated physical state $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{sim}}$. The resulting synthetic data vector $\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mock}}$ is given by:
$$ \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mock}} = \mathcal{H}(\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{sim}}) + \mathcal{N} $$
where $\mathcal{N}$ represents the instrumental and environmental noise. The operator $\mathcal{H}$ encapsulates several sequential physical and instrumental transformations: the assignment of stellar spectra using [[bruzual-charlot-stellar-population-synthesis-models|Bruzual-Charlot stellar population synthesis models]] or other [[composite-astrophysical-models-and-populations|Composite Astrophysical Models and Populations]]; 3D radiative transfer to simulate the scattering, absorption, and thermal re-emission of light by the interstellar medium; cosmological redshift effects; and telescope optics, including point spread function (PSF) blurring and filter convolutions [Fagioli et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JCAP...11..015F/abstract), [Fortuni et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...677A.102F/abstract).

By applying the exact same source extraction and data reduction pipelines to these mock images as to real telescope data, theorists and observers can make a truly *direct comparison* [Overzier et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..778O/abstract), [Bottrell et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.2821B/abstract). This "even-handed" approach avoids the pitfalls of the inversion process, allowing for robust, apples-to-apples tests of subgrid physics and cosmological models based strictly on the photons that would theoretically reach an observer's detector.

The conceptual utility of forward modeling also extends far beyond pure galaxy evolution to other astrophysical populations and challenges. For example, researchers employ kinematic forward modeling of double neutron star progenitors to infer natal kick velocities and inspiral times based on the observed spatial offsets of short gamma-ray bursts [Zevin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904..190Z/abstract). Additionally, forward modeling addresses mixed stellar populations and observational bias when interpreting the Kepler stellar rotation period distributions [van Saders et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872..128V/abstract), and hybrid methods of estimating dimensionless halo spin in gas-rich dwarf barred galaxies have been developed by forward modeling observed baryonic disk properties [Ansar et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230709952A/abstract).

## Historical Development and Key Simulations

The practice of forward modeling theoretical simulations into observer-space has evolved from rudimentary geometric projections into highly sophisticated, multi-physics pipelines. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, efforts to bridge N-body and Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH) codes with observational data relied on discrete post-processing, often coupling semi-analytic models of galaxy formation with early spectro-photometric tools like GRASIL [Silva et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph..3350S/abstract), [Gelato et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..117..380G/abstract). During this period, the early use of dissipationless simulations and non-parametric models successfully linked halo circular velocity to galaxy luminosity [Tasitsiomi et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007idm..conf..596T/abstract), while early 21st-century simulations established the missing link between cosmic initial conditions and the galaxy/quasar distributions observed in SDSS [Suto et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph.11575S/abstract). In the same era, forward modeling facilitated large multiwavelength census projects like the Subaru/XMM-Newton Deep Survey (SXDS) to investigate [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|mass assembly history]] over cosmic time [Sekiguchi et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005mmgf.conf...82S/abstract). 

Simultaneously, mock catalogs became essential for diverse multiwavelength physics: the GEMS survey used mock group catalogs from LCDM simulations to study galaxy group evolution across X-ray and radio wavelengths [Forbes et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PASA...23...38F/abstract), high-resolution cluster simulations were used to study the regularity of the intracluster medium (ICM) and X-ray observable-mass relations outside cluster cores [Nagai et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007hvcg.conf..358N/abstract), and detailed modeling of the Galactic magnetic field utilized Faraday rotation measures of extragalactic sources to identify reversals in spiral arm structures [Van Eck et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...728...97V/abstract).

A major paradigm shift occurred with the introduction of the **Millennium Run Observatory** (MRObs) [Overzier et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..778O/abstract). Serving as a theoretical virtual observatory, MRObs ingested semi-analytic outputs based on the Millennium dark matter simulations to produce highly realistic synthetic images and catalogs. By emulating the precise filter sets, point spread functions, and noise characteristics of instruments used in surveys like SDSS and CANDELS, MRObs allowed theorists to apply standard observational source extraction software to mock data. Beyond simple luminosity functions, the Millennium Simulations have also been used to determine the alignment profiles of cluster satellites, helping to break degeneracies between matter density and fluctuation amplitudes [Song et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748...98S/abstract). Modern expansions of these techniques include the Mentari pipeline, which integrates semi-analytic model (SAM) histories with stellar population synthesis to predict panchromatic galaxy evolution across massive simulated volumes [Triani et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..341..119T/abstract). For spectroscopic data, forward-modeling tools like USPEC were developed to robustly emulate the selection functions and instrumental responses for the [[sdss-luminous-red-galaxy|SDSS luminous red galaxy]] sample [Fagioli et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JCAP...11..015F/abstract).

During the 2010s, the field transitioned toward post-processing full cosmological hydrodynamical suites with 3D radiative transfer (RT) codes. Early efforts successfully coupled SPH simulations with the GRASIL code to predict infrared luminosity functions for Spitzer comparisons [Nagamine et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1007.2018N/abstract). Later, the **EAGLE project** released a massive public database of panchromatic fluxes—from the far-ultraviolet to the sub-millimeter—by applying the SKIRT RT code to hundreds of thousands of simulated galaxies with stellar masses $>10^{8.5} M_\odot$ [Camps et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJS..234...20C/abstract). Concurrently, the **IllustrisTNG** suite became a standard testbed for synthetic observations. Tools like FORECAST generated emulated JWST CEERS and HST deep fields from TNG snapshots [Fortuni et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...677A.102F/abstract), while the TNG50-SKIRT Atlas provided $100$-pc spatial resolution synthetic images to investigate how complex dust geometries impact structural properties and the UVJ diagram [Baes et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.181B/abstract). Furthermore, mock images have been extensively used to study the origin of diffuse light in galaxy groups and clusters, demonstrating that such intra-cluster light predominantly arises from the stellar tidal stripping of intermediate-mass satellites [Tang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...959..104T/abstract). For zoom-in simulations of specific galaxy types, the **Auriga project** produced synthetic multi-observer images that provide redshift-zero benchmarks for Milky Way-type galaxies using SKIRT radiative transfer [Kapoor et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.5703K/abstract). Similarly, the **ARTEMIS project** provides synthetic dust-aware observations for Milky Way-mass simulated galaxies, serving as a vital resource to test scaling relations and structural morphological trends [Camps et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.2728C/abstract).

While large-volume simulations must rely on sub-grid scaling relations to approximate the interstellar medium (ISM) [Cochrane et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.5522C/abstract), high-resolution zoom-in suites like the **FIRE simulations** explicitly resolve multiphase gas and stellar feedback. These simulations have been instrumental in forward-modeling resolved populations; for example, the `ananke` framework converts FIRE phase-space outputs into synthetic *Gaia* surveys, generating mock catalogs that incorporate realistically mapped dust extinction [Sanderson et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..246....6S/abstract), [Thob et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JOSS....9.6234T/abstract). However, a persistent tension in the literature remains: standardized benchmarking efforts, such as the public release of the AGORA CosmoRun data—which provides raw hydrodynamical simulation outputs to the community for standardized forward modeling benchmarks—demonstrate that synthetic galaxy observables are often more sensitive to uncertain sub-grid physical recipes (e.g., supernova feedback efficiencies) than to the underlying numerical hydrodynamics schemes [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract), [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240800432R/abstract). Furthermore, isolated dynamical frameworks using tools like [[galactics|Galactics]] have shown that capturing accurate 6D distribution functions is critical when forward-modeling integral field unit (IFU) kinematics [Taranu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...850...70T/abstract).

Modern efforts are simultaneously pushing the boundaries of volume and resolution. Large-volume suites like the **Magneticum simulations** self-consistently model expansive cosmological scales to compare morphological and thermal footprints (such as X-ray emission and the Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect in the CMB) directly to multiwavelength observations [Dolag et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUGA..2250156D/abstract). To support Stage IV cosmological surveys, GPU-accelerated frameworks like CRK-HACC are scaling hydrodynamical models with fully calibrated baryonic physics to exascale supercomputers [Frontiere et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251121921F/abstract). At the opposite extreme, the field is undergoing a transition from treating macro-particles as massive stellar populations toward a star-by-star modeling paradigm. The AEOS simulations, achieving $1$ pc resolution, explicitly model star-by-star chemical yields and feedback to track chemical abundances and capture the stochastic transition from Population III to Population II stars in the early universe [Brauer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016366B/abstract). This explicit resolution of early chemical enrichment provides critical theoretical links to the [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] observed by modern infrared telescopes.

## Stellar Population Synthesis and Spectral Libraries

In cosmological and zoom-in simulations, predicting panchromatic observables requires translating the simulated physical properties into theoretical spectral energy distributions (SEDs). This foundational step is governed by stellar population synthesis (SPS) models, which map the mass, age, and metal enrichment history of simulated star particles into continuous spectra [Conroy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ARA&A..51..393C/abstract). In standard implementations, each star particle—typically representing a mass of $10^4 - 10^6 M_{\odot}$—is treated as a Simple Stellar Population (SSP). Using pre-computed spectral libraries such as the [[bruzual-charlot-stellar-population-synthesis-models|Bruzual-Charlot stellar population synthesis models]], the physical state of the particle is mapped to a composite spectrum by integrating over an assumed initial stellar mass function.

The accuracy of the predicted galaxy SED is fundamentally bottlenecked by the underlying stellar evolution prescriptions. Forward modeling reveals that uncertainties in SPS models—particularly the logarithmic slope of the initial stellar mass function above $1 M_{\odot}$, the specific dust attenuation law, and the treatment of advanced evolutionary phases like thermally pulsating asymptotic giant branch (TP-AGB) stars—propagate into substantial variations in ultraviolet, optical, and near-infrared synthetic colors [Conroy et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...708...58C/abstract). A critical review of uncertainties in stellar atmosphere and evolution models underscores this effect, emphasizing their profound impact on panchromatic luminosity from the UV to the near-IR [Leitherer et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005AIPC..761...39L/abstract). These variations are often systematic and can exceed the statistical errors of modern observational surveys. While textbook methodologies frequently assume that stellar masses and current star formation rates can be robustly derived from SPS-generated templates, forward modeling exposes significant tensions. When SPS is combined with overly simplistic parameterizations of star formation histories or dust, it systematically biases the inferred [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]] and stellar mass, and can underestimate SFRs by factors of 2 to 3 compared to the intrinsic values of the simulations [Faucher et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..220F/abstract). 

To overcome the computational bottleneck of generating millions of mock SEDs while avoiding discrete grid interpolation errors, the field is undergoing a transition toward machine-learning accelerated and auto-differentiable frameworks. Notably, the DSPS (Differentiable Stellar Population Synthesis) package natively implements SPS in JAX, allowing for gradient-based optimization and expansive likelihood analyses [Hearin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1741H/abstract). By evaluating on modern GPUs, DSPS achieves speed-ups of a factor of 300 to 400 over traditional discrete codes. Simultaneously, the mathematical parameterization of [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] and star formation histories (SFHs) within SPS is being refined. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) models utilizing the first five eigen-histories, combined with a step function for the most recent $0 - 0.3\ \mathrm{Gyr}$, have been shown to drastically outperform traditional parametric forms (e.g., $\tau$ or $\Gamma$ models) in matching simulated spectra [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250218561Z/abstract).

When applied to resolved nearby galaxies, tools like the SMART model generate synthetic color-magnitude diagrams (CMDs) to self-consistently derive star formation and chemical enrichment histories directly from resolved stellar populations [Yuk et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...668..876Y/abstract). Finally, as simulations push to extreme resolutions, the SSP assumption itself is being relaxed. Next-generation frameworks targeting the epoch of reionization and the transition from Population III to Population II stars now resolve spatial scales of $\sim 1\ \mathrm{pc}$, modeling chemical yields and specific SEDs on a star-by-star basis [Brauer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016366B/abstract). This explicit resolution bypasses traditional SPS aggregate uncertainties, accurately capturing the stochastic nature of early chemical enrichment and stellar feedback.

## 3D Dust Radiative Transfer and Star-Dust Geometry

A comprehensive overview of recent developments in dust modeling techniques within both hydrodynamic and semi-analytic simulations over the past decade highlights the sheer complexity of this endeavor [Parente et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250410585P/abstract). To accurately forward-model panchromatic spectral energy distributions (SEDs) from hydrodynamical simulations, it is necessary to move beyond idealized 1D models (such as uniform foreground screens) and account for the complex 3D distribution of the interstellar medium. State-of-the-art forward modeling relies on 3D Monte Carlo radiative transfer (RT) codes—such as SKIRT [Camps et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJS..234...20C/abstract), POWDERDAY [Narayanan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..252...12N/abstract), GRASIL-3D [Domínguez-Tenreiro et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.439.3868D/abstract), and RADMC-3D [Sinigaglia et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241208609S/abstract). These tools propagate photon packets through the simulated gas and dust, rigorously computing the geometry-dependent absorption and scattering of starlight. Crucially, high-fidelity RT must explicitly model the stochastic heating of small dust grains. Unlike large grains in thermal equilibrium, small grains undergo massive temperature spikes following single photon absorption events, a process that fundamentally shapes the mid-infrared continuum [Camps et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJS..234...20C/abstract), [Narayanan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..252...12N/abstract).

Because cosmological simulations often lack the resolution to self-consistently resolve the multiphase ISM, 3D RT pipelines typically require sub-grid prescriptions to partition dust into dense molecular birth clouds and the diffuse galactic cirrus [Domínguez-Tenreiro et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.439.3868D/abstract). The resulting star-dust geometry dictates the wavelength-dependent attenuation curve. Young, UV-bright stars are preferentially embedded in optically thick birth clouds, leading to inhomogeneous [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]] distributions. Detailed RT post-processing demonstrates that this non-linear star-dust geometry heavily modifies the spatial distribution of nebular lines, shifting synthetic observations away from their intrinsic, physical values on [[traditional-bpt-diagrams|Traditional BPT diagrams]] [Vijayan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.7337V/abstract).

Forward modeling reveals severe limitations in standard observational inference techniques that assume simple star-dust geometries. For instance, RT post-processing of the NIHAO simulations shows that the common assumption of strict energy balance between UV/optical attenuation and far-infrared (FIR) re-emission can be violated by up to a factor of 3 along specific sightlines [Faucher et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957....7F/abstract). Relying on the uniform dust screen approximation systematically biases photometric redshifts and underestimates stellar masses by up to $0.4\ \mathrm{dex}$ for highly inclined or edge-on galaxies [Hahn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240919054H/abstract). Furthermore, full spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting techniques can underestimate true SFRs by factors of 2 to 3 when applied to systems with complex geometries [Faucher et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..220F/abstract). Conversely, some studies suggest robustness in specific regimes; numerical experiments using MAGPHYS on synthetic isolated and merging galaxies demonstrate that most physical parameters are recovered well regardless of viewing angle, provided the correct dust extinction curve is assumed [Hayward et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.1512H/abstract).

At high redshifts ($z > 5$), 3D RT is critical for interpreting the infrared excess (IRX), defined quantitatively as $\mathrm{IRX} = \log_{10}(L_{\mathrm{IR}} / L_{\mathrm{UV}})$, and its empirical relation to the UV continuum slope, $\beta$. Forward models successfully recover the broad locus of the IRX-$\beta$ relation but highlight a persistent tension in current simulations: producing enough dust to match observed sub-millimeter fluxes often results in excessive UV attenuation if the simulated star-dust geometry is overly symmetric [Esmerian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..113E/abstract), [Vijayan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.4999V/abstract). This indicates that resolving the precise geometrical distribution of dust—and its subsequent clearing by stellar feedback—is just as important as the bulk dust mass when linking simulated [[composite-astrophysical-models-and-populations|Composite Astrophysical Models and Populations]] to multiwavelength observables.

## Modeling the Interstellar Medium and Nebular Emission

To generate realistic synthetic observables from hydrodynamic simulations, forward models must accurately capture the fine-scale structure of the interstellar medium (ISM). Because large-volume cosmological simulations typically lack the spatial resolution to directly resolve individual molecular clouds and H II regions, they rely on sub-grid recipes and post-processing frameworks to model ionized gas and predict nebular emission lines. 

A standard approach involves coupling stellar population synthesis codes—such as [[bruzual-charlot-stellar-population-synthesis-models|Bruzual-Charlot stellar population synthesis models]]—with sub-grid ISM prescriptions. Frameworks like SÍGAME utilize probability distribution functions (PDFs) of gas density, calibrated against high-resolution zoom-in simulations, to populate unresolved scales [Olsen et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...922...88O/abstract). These density PDFs are then passed to photoionization codes such as CLOUDY to predict far-infrared (FIR) fine-structure lines and molecular transitions like $[\text{O I}]$ and $\text{CO}$. More recent methodologies employ machine learning to map the properties of H II regions from high-resolution simulations (e.g., the FIRE suite) onto the stellar particles of lower-resolution cosmological volumes (e.g., IllustrisTNG) [Yang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986..225Y/abstract). This allows for the efficient prediction of rest-frame optical lines, such as $[\text{O II}]$, $[\text{O III}]$, and the Balmer series, while preserving the underlying ISM physics.

Accurate modeling of nebular emission is particularly critical when forward modeling high-redshift ($z > 5$) galaxies, where strong nebular emission lines passing through broadband filters can dominate the photometric colors observed by JWST [Wilkins et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.3227W/abstract). Notably, the historical role of NGST (JWST predecessor) simulations was instrumental in establishing emission-line diagnostics for probing star formation and metallicities at high redshift decades before the telescope flew [Kennicutt et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ESASP.429...81K/abstract). For instance, the MEGATRON suite utilizes on-the-fly multifrequency radiation transport and thermochemistry to faithfully reproduce the sheer diversity of high-redshift galaxy spectra observed by JWST [Katz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251005201K/abstract). Full radiation hydrodynamics models, such as the SPHINX$^{20}$ simulations, compute resonant line radiative transfer to track up to 52 nebular emission lines, including $\text{Ly}\alpha$ [Katz et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..44K/abstract), demonstrating that emission-line strengths and equivalent widths vary significantly depending on local gas mass fractions and formation histories [Barrow et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.4863B/abstract). At these high redshifts, forward-modeled predictions from the IllustrisTNG simulations for Balmer breaks and UV continuum spectral indices generally match observations, though current models may lack heavily attenuated UV-red galaxies [Shen et al. These findings highlight that robust sub-grid modeling of both the emitting gas and the obscuring dust is required to correctly interpret line ratios and spectroscopic metallicities in survey data.

## Instrumental Effects and Mock Survey Realism

Comparing physical quantities directly derived from observations against raw simulation outputs frequently introduces severe systematic biases. To perform an "even-handed" comparison between theory and observation, the entire observing process must be simulated to account for detection limits and measurement errors [Overzier et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..778O/abstract), [Fortuni et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...677A.102F/abstract). 

To translate physical models into synthetic observations, cosmological simulations are typically first projected into mock light cones that preserve the spatial and redshift distributions of the simulated universe [Overzier et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..778O/abstract). Radiative transfer codes and stellar population synthesis models are then used to produce "ideal," high-resolution spectral energy distributions or data cubes. 

Emulating realistic survey data requires applying instrumental constraints to these ideal outputs. Telescope-specific filter throughput curves are integrated over the rest-frame or redshifted spectra to compute mock photometric fluxes. Spatial degradation is then achieved via PSF blurring—convolving the high-resolution images with an instrument-specific point spread function—and subsequent down-sampling to the detector's pixel scale [Massey et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004MNRAS.348..214M/abstract), [Zhou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250615060Z/abstract). Finally, representative background noise, encompassing sky emission, dark current, and read noise, is injected to match the signal-to-noise ($S/N$) limits of the targeted survey [Fortuni et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...677A.102F/abstract).

The resulting degraded mock images mimic real observations closely enough to be processed by standard observational reduction pipelines. For example, the Millennium Run Observatory (MRObs) generates images spanning 40 filters to emulate surveys like CANDELS and GOODS; these images are then passed through standard source detection software such as Source Extractor to produce mock catalogs [Overzier et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..778O/abstract). Expanding on these capabilities, PyMGal introduces a flexible tool for projecting simulation properties directly into 2D optical mock observations with highly customizable filters and scalable resolution setups [Janulewicz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250700123J/abstract). To prepare for specific modern missions, phenomenological models are increasingly utilized; the JAGUAR (JAdes extraGalactic Ultradeep Artificial Realizations) package, for instance, models galaxy populations from the UV through the near-IR over 13 billion years of cosmic history to optimize and simulate deep JWST surveys [Williams et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJS..236...33W/abstract). Similarly, forward modeling is being heavily leveraged for upcoming missions like SPHEREx to predict spectrophotometric redshift performance and quantify the impact of emission lines on low-resolution spectra across the entire sky [Feder et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...972...68F/abstract). This end-to-end processing captures vital selection effects—such as surface brightness dimming, dust obscuration, and source blending—which naturally bias inferred parameters like the [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]]. Forward modeling frameworks like Obiwan have demonstrated that varying observing conditions (e.g., imaging depth and PSF size) significantly perturb the recovered density of targets such as [[sdss-luminous-red-galaxy|luminous red galaxies]] targeted by Stage-IV surveys like DESI. These observational systematics can only be quantified by explicitly injecting mock galaxies into the image plane [Kong et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...01..146K/abstract).

Beyond steady-state galaxy properties, forward modeling has also been extended to time-domain transients. For instance, researchers forward-model Tidal Disruption Flare (TDF) populations to understand how survey selection effects fundamentally influence the detected flare rates and host galaxy distributions [Roth et al. Beyond broadband photometry, spectroscopic surveys require the emulation of fiber collisions, slit losses, and spectral resolution limits. Tools like USPEC model the 1D instrumental responses of the SDSS/BOSS spectrographs to recreate realistic spectral selection cuts [Fagioli et al. In studies observing the complex kinematics of the Milky Way, the role of MHD modeling has been critical in creating synthetic Galactic ISM observations, specifically illustrating how velocity crowding affects observed longitude-velocity diagrams [Gómez et al. ## Simulation-Based Inference and Cosmological Constraints

Standard cosmological constraints derived from galaxy clustering typically rely on analytic models of the matter power spectrum, which are restricted to linear or quasi-linear scales (roughly $k \lesssim 0.25\,h/{\rm Mpc}$). To extract the wealth of cosmological information embedded in non-linear scales, the field has increasingly turned to simulation-based inference (SBI) powered by forward modeling. By generating massive suites of simulated galaxy catalogs that mimic survey selection functions and observational systematics, SBI bypasses the need for explicit analytic likelihood functions, enabling robust constraints on parameters like the matter density $\Omega_m$ and the amplitude of density fluctuations $\sigma_8$.

A prominent application of this approach is the SIMBIG framework, which forward models galaxy clustering down to non-linear scales [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221100723H/abstract). SIMBIG leverages high-fidelity $N$-body simulations populated with galaxies using frameworks akin to Halotools [Hearin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AJ....154..190H/abstract) to construct tens of thousands of mock catalogs. By training density estimators, specifically normalizing flows, on the summary statistics of these mocks, the framework maps simulated observables directly to the multidimensional posterior distribution of $\Lambda$CDM parameters. When applied to the BOSS CMASS sample (an [[sdss-luminous-red-galaxy|SDSS luminous red galaxy]] analogue), SIMBIG pushed the maximum wavenumber to $k_{\rm max}=0.5\,h/{\rm Mpc}$ and yielded constraints on $\sigma_8$ that were $27\%$ more precise than standard analytic approaches. This information gain from small scales is statistically equivalent to increasing the survey volume by $\sim 60\%$ [Hahn et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221100723H/abstract). To facilitate this immense computational requirement, there has been significant development of parametric models for fast mock catalog generation, enabling researchers to efficiently test the dependence of non-linear clustering on multiple cosmological parameters simultaneously [Yapici et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190412241Y/abstract).

Advances in field-level inference further refine this process. Forward models based on the Effective Field Theory of Large Scale Structure (EFT of LSS) provide computationally efficient and perturbatively convergent predictions of the galaxy density field [Stadler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...04..089S/abstract). When extending these models to include redshift space distortions (RSDs), field-level forward modeling can recover the linear growth rate $f$ with $<1\%$ statistical and $<1.5\%$ systematic error, successfully marginalizing over unknown bias coefficients and noise amplitudes [Stadler et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241104513S/abstract).

Forward modeling is equally critical for controlling observational systematics in Stage IV surveys. Inferring the true redshift distribution $N(z)$ of photometric galaxy samples typically relies on incomplete spectroscopic calibration. Forward modeling the physical characteristics and observing process of galaxy populations allows researchers to predict tomographic redshift distributions directly, bypassing problematic spectroscopic reweighting [Alsing et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..264...29A/abstract). While early applications achieved biases as low as $\Delta z \lesssim 0.003$ for surveys like GAMA [Alsing et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..264...29A/abstract), recent analyses targeting deeper samples like KiDS-1000 reveal that varying the underlying galaxy formation models introduces systematic shifts of $\Delta z \sim 0.05-0.1$ [Leistedt et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260203935L/abstract). To validate these photometric redshift posteriors, tools like normalizing flows (e.g., PZFlow) are employed for the probabilistic forward modeling of galaxy catalogs [Crenshaw et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AJ....168...80C/abstract). Furthermore, image-level forward modeling pipelines injected with synthetic targets can successfully map false fluctuations in target density caused by varying survey depth, while also revealing systematic contamination in Galactic extinction maps when simulated predictions diverge from observational data [Kong et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...01..146K/abstract).

Despite these successes, a persistent tension exists in the literature regarding the robustness of non-linear scales. Extracting precise constraints from field-level forward models involves handling general modeling uncertainties in the relation between galaxies and the underlying matter distribution [Stadler et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...04..089S/abstract). This complicates the direct translation of small-scale clustering into pure cosmological constraints.

Beyond large-scale structure clustering, forward modeling has also become essential for standard candles. The Pantheon+ analysis applies forward modeling methods (such as Dust2Dust) to the dust and intrinsic color distributions of Type Ia supernovae, meticulously quantifying their impact on dark energy and cosmological parameter estimation [Popovic et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945...84P/abstract).

## Recent Advances: Differentiability and Machine Learning

Historically, coupling hydrodynamic simulation outputs to 3D radiative transfer (RT) codes relied on discrete, forward-pass calculations. Because these high-fidelity calculations are computationally expensive, they have traditionally created a bottleneck that prohibits expansive likelihood analyses and simulation-based inference. Recently, however, the field has undergone a paradigm shift from discrete post-processing to high-speed, auto-differentiable pipelines and machine learning emulators that enable gradient-based parameter optimization.

**Differentiable Programming and GPU Acceleration**
The transition to differentiable programming frameworks, particularly JAX, has allowed researchers to compute exact gradients of synthetic observables with respect to input physical parameters. For instance, the Differentiable Stellar Population Synthesis (DSPS) package natively implements stellar population models—conceptually similar to the standard [[bruzual-charlot-stellar-population-synthesis-models|Bruzual-Charlot stellar population synthesis models]]—directly in JAX [Hearin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1741H/abstract). This allows for the analytical computation of gradients for spectral energy distributions (SEDs) with respect to a galaxy's star formation history (SFH), stellar metallicity, and dust attenuation. By running on modern GPUs, DSPS accelerates forward modeling by a factor of $300\text{--}400$ compared to standard CPU-based codes [Hearin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1741H/abstract). 

Extending these capabilities to spatially resolved integral field spectroscopy, the RUBIX pipeline provides a JAX-based framework for the forward modeling of IFU data cubes [Çakır et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241208265C/abstract). By automatically parallelizing computations across multiple GPUs, RUBIX achieves a $600$-fold speed improvement over previous state-of-the-art codes, reducing compute times from hours to seconds while allowing gradient-based inference to flow through the entire observational pipeline [Çakır et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241208265C/abstract). Broadening these modern capabilities, the Synthesizer software package offers a modular, extensible platform for generating various synthetic astronomical observables—including mock images and IFU data—across diverse physical models [Lovell et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025OJAp....8E.152L/abstract).

**Deep Learning Emulators and Surrogate Modeling**
In tandem with differentiable frameworks, deep neural networks are increasingly being utilized to bypass the computational cost of full radiative transfer. STARDUSTER is a prominent supervised deep-learning model designed to emulate the output of the SKIRT RT code [Qiu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930...66Q/abstract). By taking galaxy geometry and SFH as inputs, STARDUSTER predicts the multiwavelength SED with exceptional fidelity, introducing errors of only $\sim 0.005$ mag in dust attenuation and $0.1\text{--}0.2$ mag for dust emission when compared to direct SKIRT simulations [Qiu et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930...66Q/abstract). Radiative transfer emulators are also critical for testing observational parameter recovery. The SE3D modeling framework utilizes such emulators to test the recovery of bulk stellar and dust properties on mock-observed toy models and simulated galaxies, thoroughly investigating the impact of star formation history mismatches [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251119614Z/abstract). 

Surrogate modeling is also being deployed to accelerate the underlying subgrid physics of the simulations themselves. The ASURA-FDPS-ML framework utilizes machine learning to emulate the inhomogeneous expansion of core-collapse supernova feedback, reducing the computational cost of star-by-star galaxy integrations by approximately $75\%$ while bridging multi-scale physical environments [Hirashima et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241023346H/abstract). Furthermore, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) techniques are being applied to SFH modeling. Utilizing PCA-based eigen-histories allows researchers to accurately reconstruct the diverse simulated spectra and study [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]], significantly outperforming rigid parametric SFH models (e.g., $\tau$-models or exponential bursts) [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250218561Z/abstract). Advanced computational frameworks also facilitate the integration of previously missing physics in SED fitting; for example, explicitly including AGN torus emission in panchromatic SED-fitting frameworks like Prospector has proven essential for avoiding biases in stellar ages and SFRs, which can otherwise be misestimated by a factor of 10 if hot dust is ignored [Leja et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...854...62L/abstract).

Despite these rapid algorithmic advances, the literature highlights an ongoing tension regarding the training data used for these models. Machine learning emulators must be rigorously trained and validated to ensure they do not simply inherit and obscure the systematic biases of the underlying simulations. If a hydrodynamic model fundamentally misrepresents the subgrid star-dust geometry, any surrogate model or emulator trained upon it will seamlessly propagate those same physical biases into downstream cosmological constraints.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite rapid advancements in forward modeling, significant open questions remain regarding **model mismatch** and the degeneracy of physical parameters extracted from mock observations. A primary limitation lies in **dust systematics** and the complex spatial geometry of the interstellar medium. Theoretical textbook approaches often assume that ultraviolet (UV) and far-infrared luminosities serve as reliable, straightforward diagnostics for star formation rates. However, forward modeling reveals that analytical assumptions, such as uniform foreground dust screens, introduce severe systematic biases that drastically alter inferred stellar masses and star formation rates [Faucher et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..220F/abstract), [Lower et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...965..123L/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240919054H/abstract), critically affecting the derived [[galaxy-mass-to-light-ratio|Galaxy mass-to-light ratio]]. Furthermore, realistic 3D radiative transfer demonstrates that inhomogeneous star-dust geometries can violate the assumed energy balance between dust attenuation and re-emission by up to a factor of 3, depending strongly on the observer's viewing angle [Faucher et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957....7F/abstract).

These dust-geometry degeneracies are especially pronounced at high redshifts ($z > 5$). Current cosmological simulations struggle to simultaneously reproduce the observed UV continuum slopes ($\beta$) and the total dust mass or infrared luminosity functions derived from ALMA and JWST observations [Esmerian et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..113E/abstract), [Vijayan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.4999V/abstract). This unresolved tension suggests underlying flaws in current sub-grid models for early dust production, grain growth, and stellar feedback [Lower et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...965..123L/abstract).

At macroscopic scales, forward modeling continues to expose tensions between simulated galaxy populations and survey data. A persistent issue often noted in the literature is the apparent "overquenching" of satellite galaxies, where simulations seemingly produce a satellite population that is significantly redder and more passive than observed. However, recent forward-modeling efforts show that this tension is greatly reduced when empirically motivated observational errors and mock group finders are applied, demonstrating that proper mock comparisons are essential to avoid misdiagnosing sub-grid physics [Bravo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497.3026B/abstract). Similarly, the rigorous use of SPH simulations with chemophotometric implementation continues to be vital to track how major and minor mergers reproduce peculiar UV morphologies and gas quenching in early-type galaxies [Mazzei et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...885..165M/abstract). Other unresolved macroscopic tensions include the failure of current simulations to accurately reproduce observed Sunyaev-Zeldovich pressure profiles in galaxy clusters [Moser et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230710919M/abstract).

Addressing these limitations requires bridging the gap between cosmological survey volumes and **sub-grid resolution**. Matching the unprecedented fidelity of next-generation facilities necessitates **exascale computing** to self-consistently couple survey-scale hydrodynamics with detailed multiphase gas and AGN feedback models [Frontiere et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251121921F/abstract). Future directions are increasingly focused on achieving star-by-star resolution ($\sim 1 \text{ pc}$) within a cosmological context to directly model early chemical enrichment and stochastic feedback, bypassing the limitations of traditional [[composite-astrophysical-models-and-populations|Composite Astrophysical Models and Populations]] [Brauer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016366B/abstract), [Hirashima et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241023346H/abstract). To make the computational cost of such high-fidelity radiative transfer tractable, the field is rapidly adopting auto-differentiable frameworks and machine-learning surrogate models. GPU-accelerated pipelines can now evaluate integral field unit (IFU) data cubes and panchromatic SEDs hundreds of times faster than traditional methods [Çakır et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241208265C/abstract), [Hearin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.1741H/abstract), paving the way for rigorous, gradient-based field-level inference of galaxy evolution physics.
