## Article

The **Cosmology and Astrophysics with MachinE Learning Simulations (CAMELS)** project is a massive ensemble of over 4,000 cosmological $N$-body and hydrodynamic simulations explicitly designed to interface with modern machine learning techniques [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...915...71V/abstract), [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...54V/abstract). Moving beyond the traditional paradigm of single "flagship" runs, CAMELS operates as a comprehensive suite of [[multi-cosmology-simulations|Multi-cosmology simulations]] that systematically varies fundamental cosmological parameters (such as the matter density $\Omega_m$ and clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$) alongside sub-grid astrophysical prescriptions governing supernova (SN) and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback.

Collectively, the core simulations follow the evolution of more than 100 billion resolution elements across a combined effective volume of $(400\, h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc})^3$, with each individual run modeling a comoving volume of $(25\, h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc})^3$ [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...915...71V/abstract). To ensure that machine learning algorithms learn generalized physical relationships rather than the numerical artifacts of a single code, CAMELS employs multiple distinct sub-grid physics models, primarily IllustrisTNG, SIMBA, and ASTRID. The fundamental objective of the project is to bridge the gap between large-scale dark matter structure and the highly non-linear, small-scale physics of galaxy formation. By providing a standardized, densely sampled theoretical dataset, CAMELS enables the training of advanced neural networks to perform field-level, likelihood-free inference. A foundational result of the suite is the demonstration that these algorithms can accurately infer cosmological parameters directly from sparse galaxy distributions, or even the internal properties of a single simulated galaxy, by effectively marginalizing over complex astrophysical uncertainties [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929..132V/abstract).

## Evolution of Cosmological Simulation Strategies

The historical development of theoretical predictions for large-scale structure relied heavily on massive, dark matter-only $N$-body simulations, which modeled cosmic evolution with fixed cosmological parameters over gigaparsec volumes [Riebe et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1109.0003R/abstract). As the field advanced, full-physics hydrodynamic solvers were introduced to track the complex evolution of cosmic gas and galaxies [Kereš et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.2027K/abstract), [Zhu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831...52Z/abstract). For years, progress in the field was largely driven by individual, high-resolution "flagship" hydrodynamic simulations constructed with a single, fixed set of sub-grid physics parameters calibrated to match local Universe observables.

However, as observational precision increased, it became clear that baryonic processes—particularly feedback from AGN and SN—fundamentally alter the distribution of matter. These processes suppress the matter power spectrum by up to $\approx 20\%$ on non-linear scales ($k \gtrsim 0.4\,h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$) [Schaye et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.4978S/abstract), [Delgado et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.5306D/abstract). Because feedback operates below the resolution limit of cosmological grids, codes must rely on sub-grid prescriptions. Different simulation suites produce significantly different matter clustering properties despite yielding similar macroscopic galaxy property distributions [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...915...71V/abstract). Consequently, baryonic feedback emerged as the primary source of uncertainty in small-scale matter clustering, often overshadowing variations induced by cosmology.

This bottleneck motivated a paradigm shift in simulation design. To disentangle the highly non-linear degeneracies between cosmological parameters and astrophysical feedback, it is necessary to explore a high-dimensional parameter space. The field transitioned toward [[multi-cosmology-simulations|Multi-cosmology simulations]] that prioritize generating a high-volume statistical sample over maximizing individual simulation size. The CAMELS project exemplifies this modern strategy. Rather than producing a single massive universe, CAMELS trades extreme volume for dense parameter-space coverage, providing the necessary data to construct neural network emulators and robustly estimate covariance matrices across diverse sub-grid models [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...915...71V/abstract), [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929..132V/abstract).

## Design and Numerical Methodology

Because full (magneto)hydrodynamic simulations are computationally expensive, it is practically impossible to run large-volume simulations across a dense grid of parameters. Instead, CAMELS achieves broad parameter coverage by generating its [[multi-cosmology-simulations|ensemble of small-volume simulations]] using highly structured statistical and numerical methodologies.

**Initial Conditions and Paired-Fixed Fields**
A fundamental limitation of small-volume simulations is the overwhelming presence of sample variance, which can obscure the subtle signatures of cosmological and astrophysical parameter variations. To suppress this variance, the CAMELS framework generates initial conditions using a "paired-fixed" strategy [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...867..137V/abstract). 

In standard cosmological simulations, initial modes are drawn from a Gaussian distribution, introducing intrinsic scatter in the fluctuation amplitudes. In a fixed field, the amplitudes of the Fourier modes, $\delta(\mathbf{k})$, are constrained exactly to the variance dictated by the input theoretical power spectrum, $P(k)$, entirely removing the Rayleigh-distributed amplitude scatter. Furthermore, these fixed fields are generated in pairs with exactly opposite Fourier phases. Averaging the statistics over a paired set cancels out leading-order phase correlations. This technique has been shown to reduce the variance of clustering statistics by factors up to $10^6$ [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...867..137V/abstract), maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio of parameter variations within the limited $(25\, h^{-1} {\rm Mpc})^3$ volumes.

**Latin Hypercube Sampling**
To effectively map the interplay between cosmology and galaxy formation physics, CAMELS relies on Latin Hypercube sampling. This statistical method efficiently covers vast parameter spaces by ensuring that points are maximally stratified across all one-dimensional projections. The core CAMELS suites systematically sample variations in $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$ alongside four astrophysical parameters governing the efficiency and energy yield of SN and AGN feedback [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...915...71V/abstract). Subsequent extensions to the suite utilize Latin Hypercube sampling over much broader 28-parameter spaces to capture non-linear parameter couplings [Ni et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...959..136N/abstract).

**Gaussian Process Emulators and Tools**
By providing dense grids of dark matter, gas, and stellar fields, the suite enables the construction of high-precision surrogate models. Gaussian Process Emulators are frequently trained on the Latin Hypercube samples to perform fast, non-linear interpolations of global summary statistics—such as the galaxy stellar mass function, halo baryon fractions, and the non-linear matter power spectrum—across the continuous parameter space [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...915...71V/abstract). Recent advancements have sought to synthesize these diverse models into a unified 2D latent representation of baryonic feedback across thousands of CAMELS simulations, which successfully disentangles the distinct impacts of SN and AGN feedback on the matter power spectrum [Lin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250901881L/abstract). To extend this predictive power to higher resolutions, the CARPoolGP method leverages high-dimensional parameter space correlations to generate low-variance emulations for massive halos in zoomed-in CAMELS simulations [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968...11L/abstract). Furthermore, while analyzing these massive multi-code datasets is complex, specific post-processing tools aid in property extraction; for instance, the SOAP Python package enables the efficient, parallelized calculation of halo and galaxy properties specifically from SWIFT simulations [McGibbon et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JOSS...10.8252M/abstract). To address numerical artifacts that arise when comparing models, a merger-tree-based matching technique has been developed to allow for galaxy-by-galaxy comparisons across different resolutions in IllustrisTNG, enabling the training of machine learning correction mappings to mitigate resolution bias [Jung et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...965..156J/abstract).

## Sub-grid Physics and Baryonic Imprints

A primary objective of the CAMELS project is to quantify how astrophysical processes modify the underlying distribution of matter—a dominant source of theoretical uncertainty for current and upcoming weak-lensing and galaxy clustering surveys. As baryonic feedback suppresses matter clustering on non-linear scales, the magnitude of this suppression is deeply tied to the physical expulsion of gas from halo centers. This has been formalized in CAMELS through the "baryon spread" metric, which measures the displacement of baryons relative to their initial neighboring dark matter distribution [Gebhardt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4896G/abstract). 

Comparisons between [[multi-cosmology-simulations|different hydrodynamical codes]] within CAMELS reveal stark conflicts regarding the severity of this spread. For instance, the kinetic AGN feedback model employed in SIMBA is highly efficient at evacuating the circumgalactic medium, spreading approximately $40\%$ of baryons to distances $> 1 \ \mathrm{Mpc}$ from their initial dark matter neighbors. Conversely, the thermal feedback models in IllustrisTNG and ASTRID yield a much weaker baryon spread of roughly $10\%$ [Gebhardt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4896G/abstract). As a result, SIMBA predicts significantly lower halo baryon fractions than IllustrisTNG, despite both models being calibrated to yield similar macroscopic galaxy properties [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...915...71V/abstract), [Medlock et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...61M/abstract).

Furthermore, CAMELS highlights a highly non-linear coupling between stellar and AGN feedback. While it might be assumed that increasing both SN and AGN feedback efficiencies would monotonically decrease the halo baryon fraction, aggressive stellar feedback can actually suppress the accretion of gas onto central supermassive black holes. By stunting black hole growth, overly efficient SN feedback delays the onset of massive AGN-driven outflows, paradoxically resulting in a weaker global suppression of the matter power spectrum [Delgado et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.5306D/abstract), [Medlock et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...61M/abstract). Additionally, the stellar mass buildup in CAMELS is found to be more sensitive to the speed of galactic winds than the total energy injected per unit of star formation, with SIMBA's black hole feedback showing a more pronounced impact on galaxies than IllustrisTNG's model [McGibbon et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.5583M/abstract). Beyond the parameter variations within CAMELS, comparisons across different, fixed "flagship" cosmological simulations (such as Illustris, TNG100, EAGLE, and SIMBA) reveal that sub-grid model choices significantly impact the linearity and normalization of the black hole mass-stellar mass relation, particularly at the low-mass end [Habouzit et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.1940H/abstract). Environmental density also significantly modulates gas fractions and star formation rates, with satellite galaxies showing suppression in overdense regions across all CAMELS models [Sims et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260106290S/abstract). Using normalizing flows, researchers investigating the kinematics of simulated galaxies have revealed that central galaxy spatial and velocity biases are sensitive to halo concentration and spin, while satellite distributions consist of distinct orbiting and infalling populations [Kwon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976...76K/abstract). Analyses of intrinsic galaxy alignments show that alignment amplitudes are significantly influenced by supernova feedback, particularly in quiescent populations [Bilsborrow et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251121881B/abstract).

The redistribution of gas also induces a gravitational "back-reaction" on the collisionless dark matter. Comparing full-physics halos to their dark matter-only counterparts, aggressive baryon ejection systematically decreases total virial masses and reshapes dark matter density profiles. Radiative cooling tends to contract the central dark matter distribution—increasing the central density at $r < 0.01 R_{\rm vir}$ by up to $450\%$ in some SIMBA models—while powerful AGN feedback puffs up the outer halo [Gebhardt et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260106258G/abstract). These complex processes imprint distinct, model-dependent signatures on the halo concentration-mass relation [Shao et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.523.3258S/abstract).

To break the degeneracies between cosmology, SN feedback, and AGN feedback, recent research has focused on novel multi-wavelength observables. For example, the dispersion measures of Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) have proven highly sensitive to the spatial distribution of "missing" baryons in the circumgalactic and intergalactic mediums, offering a promising avenue to constrain feedback models independent of local galaxy properties [Medlock et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...967...32M/abstract). To capture this, researchers have implemented neural networks to emulate the complex effects of feedback directly on the probability distribution of cosmic dispersion measures for FRBs [Guo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540..289G/abstract). FRB statistics also show a strong correlation with the matter power spectrum suppression, providing a way to use future FRB populations to enhance weak-lensing measurements [Medlock et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...983...46M/abstract). In the X-ray and microwave regimes, symbolic regression has helped discover a self-similar multi-wavelength mass proxy for low-mass clusters by replacing the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich (SZ) flux with a corrected term accounting for stellar and gas mass fractions [Wadekar et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.2628W/abstract). Additionally, the kinetic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (kSZ) effect has emerged as a critical benchmark, with DESI and ACT data favoring models with powerful AGN feedback like SIMBA over many fiducial prescriptions [Bigwood et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251015822B/abstract). Despite these advances, standard feedback recipes in CAMELS still struggle to reproduce certain multi-wavelength observations, such as the thermal SZ signals for massive BOSS galaxies, highlighting ongoing limitations in sub-grid modeling [Moser et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933..133M/abstract). To overcome these limitations, new physically-motivated star formation prescriptions, such as the pressure-regulated feedback-modulated (PRFM) model, are being calibrated against high-resolution TNG50 data to eventually replace empirical sub-grid models with first-principles physics [Hassan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..151H/abstract).

## Machine Learning for Parameter Inference

The [[multi-cosmology-simulations|CAMELS dataset]] is explicitly tailored for training machine learning algorithms to perform likelihood-free inference. By moving beyond traditional global summary statistics, neural networks—particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)—can extract cosmological parameters directly from highly non-linear, small-scale structures in continuous fields or sparse galaxy catalogs.

GNNs are naturally suited for the irregular and sparse data of dark matter halo and galaxy catalogs. Models trained on CAMELS can perform parameter estimation for the matter density $\Omega_m$ with approximately $12\%$ to $13\%$ precision using only the 3D positions of $\sim 1000$ galaxies within a $(25\,h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc})^3$ volume at $z=0$ [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract). When internal galaxy properties—such as stellar mass, stellar metallicity, and stellar radius—are included as node features, the precision of $\Omega_m$ constraints improves to $4\%$--$8\%$. In doing so, the networks effectively marginalize over the highly non-linear astrophysical feedback processes modeled in the simulations. In addition to global parameters, CAMELS data has been used to train GNNs to infer the masses of individual dark matter halos from the properties of their member galaxies with $\sim 0.2$ dex accuracy [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935...30V/abstract).

A striking paradigm shift enabled by CAMELS is the demonstration that even the internal properties of a *single* randomly selected galaxy contain sufficient information to constrain $\Omega_m$ to $\sim 10\%$ precision [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929..132V/abstract). Autoencoder analyses reveal that this predictability arises because variations in $\Omega_m$ physically shift galaxies off their fiducial "galaxy property manifold," creating unsampled properties. In contrast, variations in the clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$ merely shift galaxies along the manifold, mimicking natural scatter and leaving $\sigma_8$ poorly constrained by individual galaxies alone [Lue et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986..133L/abstract), [Contardo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...988...54C/abstract). Breaking this degeneracy requires multiple galaxies; applying neural density estimation and hierarchical population inference to the mock photometry of thousands of galaxies yields tight, simultaneous constraints on both $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$ [Chawak et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...969..105C/abstract), [Hahn et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966L..18H/abstract).

Further refining inference techniques, the CASCO series studies physical processes in local galaxies. The CASCO I study found that IllustrisTNG models with lower outflow wind energy per unit star formation better reproduce the observed size-mass and dark matter fraction relations in star-forming galaxies [Busillo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525.6191B/abstract). Subsequent CASCO works comparing early- and late-type galaxies in CAMELS to observational datasets like SPIDER and MaNGA find that feedback constraints differ significantly between galaxy morphologies [Busillo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...693A.112B/abstract). Additionally, in cluster cosmology, researchers have bypassed traditional empirical scaling relations entirely by utilizing Lagrangian Deep Learning to model cluster number counts directly from simulations [Cerardi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.110C/abstract). Recent work has also expanded the use of machine learning to cosmic voids, showing that emulators for void density profiles can recover $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$ to $\sim 10\%$ precision even in the presence of baryons [Lehman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205262L/abstract).

Beyond direct point-estimate predictions, CAMELS data facilitates Implicit Likelihood Inference (ILI) to derive full multi-dimensional posterior distributions. By training neural network emulators on summary observables like the cosmic star formation rate density and stellar mass functions, ILI can calibrate both cosmological parameters and sub-grid astrophysical parameters directly against observational data [Jo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...67J/abstract). In this context, analyses utilizing Fisher information and ILI on CAMELS stellar mass functions emphasize that properly accounting for the full covariance of uncertainties is critical for obtaining tighter cosmological constraints and avoiding biased posteriors [Jo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241021722J/abstract). Furthermore, using the SYNTHESIZER code, researchers have performed likelihood-free inference directly from galaxy luminosity functions and color distributions across the CAMELS suites, demonstrating that mock photometry successfully encodes clustering information like $\sigma_8$ through complex star formation histories [Lovell et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.544.3949L/abstract). However, while ILI succeeds with certain observables like the star formation rate density, it currently struggles to consistently reproduce observed Stellar Mass Functions, highlighting ongoing limitations in the underlying galaxy formation modeling and calibration framework [Jo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944...67J/abstract). The standardized LtU-ILI framework has been released to facilitate Likelihood-Free Inference across these simulation suites, enabling consistent cross-model validation and metric assessments [Ho et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..54H/abstract).

## Cross-Code Robustness and Generalization

A persistent challenge in applying simulation-trained machine learning to real observational data is model robustness: neural networks trained on a specific [[multi-cosmology-simulations|suite of simulations]] (e.g., IllustrisTNG) often suffer severe performance degradation when evaluated on data from a different suite (e.g., SIMBA) [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...929..132V/abstract), [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..115V/abstract), [Gluck et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.52710038G/abstract). 

The root cause of this cross-code fragility lies in the distinct sub-grid physics implementations utilized by different hydrodynamical codes, which imprint unique priors onto the simulated galaxy populations. Because different feedback prescriptions fundamentally alter the underlying galaxy property manifold, standard neural networks easily become confused by these simulation-specific features [Lue et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...986..133L/abstract). To rigorously assess these modeling disparities at the level of flagship simulations, researchers have employed spectral variance and principal component analysis to evaluate how accurately models like EAGLE and IllustrisTNG100 mimic real galaxy spectra and star formation histories [Sharbaf et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.539.1480S/abstract).

Despite these challenges, certain observables and algorithmic strategies have proven resilient. Research demonstrates that field-level inference relying strictly on the 3D positions and radial velocities of galaxies can achieve robust constraints on $\Omega_m$. GNNs trained solely on this phase-space information successfully generalize across five disparate sub-grid models, implying that phase-space clustering retains a fundamental physical dependence on $\Omega_m$ that is largely independent of galaxy formation physics down to scales of $\sim 10\,h^{-1}\mathrm{kpc}$ [de Santi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952...69D/abstract). Furthermore, by applying symbolic regression to these GNN architectures, researchers have extracted a universal analytic equation capable of predicting $\Omega_m$ directly from generic halo and phase-space catalogs [Shao et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956..149S/abstract).

For observables that are more deeply coupled to baryonic physics—such as stellar mass and metallicity—researchers have developed specialized frameworks to force cross-simulation generalization. One approach utilizes Domain Adaptive GNNs, which employ unsupervised domain adaptation via the Maximum Mean Discrepancy metric to force the network to extract domain-invariant features [Roncoli et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231101588R/abstract). Another technique, the Model-Insensitive ESTimator (MIEST), explicitly blends latent variables across multiple training suites to remove code-specific features, improving parameter estimation on unseen simulation codes by approximately $17\%$ for $\Omega_m$ and $38\%$ for $\sigma_8$ [Jo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991..120J/abstract). Additionally, implementing strict outlier rejection can substantially recover cross-code accuracy for models that otherwise fail on a small subset of non-conforming galaxies [Echeverri-Rojas et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954..125E/abstract). To further safeguard against applying biased models to observational data, novel algorithms using deep compact clustering can detect 'out-of-domain' data, providing a crucial confidence metric to determine if observed galaxy cluster properties are genuinely consistent with the simulation training set [Tregidga et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251109660T/abstract).

## Recent Extensions: CAMELS-SAM and DREAMS

While the original CAMELS suites revolutionized the use of machine learning in cosmological hydrodynamics, their relatively small volumes and strict adherence to the standard Cold Dark Matter (CDM) paradigm restricted their application. To overcome these limitations, the project has expanded into a broader ecosystem of [[multi-cosmology-simulations|Multi-cosmology simulations]] through two major extensions.

**CAMELS-SAM: Scaling to Larger Volumes**
To study large-scale structure and spatial clustering, the CAMELS framework was extended via the CAMELS-SAM suite. This extension consists of 1,000 dark-matter-only $N$-body simulations run in larger $(100\ h^{-1}\ \mathrm{Mpc})^3$ periodic volumes [Perez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954...11P/abstract). Instead of computationally expensive hydrodynamics, CAMELS-SAM populates these larger volumes with billions of simulated galaxies using the Santa Cruz semi-analytic model (SC-SAM) [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...54V/abstract). Neural networks trained on CAMELS-SAM data have successfully demonstrated that it is possible to marginalize over highly uncertain astrophysical feedback to constrain $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$ to $3\%-8\%$ precision across spatial scales of $0.68 < R < 27\ h^{-1}\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ [Perez et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954...11P/abstract). To further bridge the gap between pure $N$-body simulations and computationally expensive hydrodynamics, recent works focus on machine learning alternatives. The NeHOD framework utilizes diffusion models and point clouds to paint galaxies and subhalos onto dark matter structures with the accuracy of full hydrodynamics at a fraction of the cost [Nguyen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240902980N/abstract). Similarly, the CHARM method employs neural spline flows to rapidly generate mock halo catalogs directly from the large-scale dark matter density field [Pandey et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240909124P/abstract). Additionally, conditional score-based models are being utilized to learn complex mappings that 'inpaint' observable galaxy distributions directly onto $N$-body dark matter density fields across the expansive CAMELS parameter space [Bourdin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240800839B/abstract).

**The DREAMS Project: Probing Warm Dark Matter**
The DaRk mattEr and Astrophysics with Machine learning and Simulations (DREAMS) project extends the CAMELS methodology into the regime of alternative dark matter models, specifically Warm Dark Matter (WDM) [Rose et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982...68R/abstract). Because WDM free-streaming and aggressive supernova feedback can both suppress the abundance of low-mass dwarf galaxies, breaking this degeneracy is critical. DREAMS currently comprises two suites of WDM simulations, each containing 1,024 runs: a set of $(25\ h^{-1}\ \mathrm{Mpc})^3$ uniform-volume boxes and a set of high-resolution Milky Way-mass zoom-in simulations. Across these runs, the WDM particle mass is systematically varied alongside standard cosmological and feedback parameters. Recent joint analyses suggest that WDM induces measurable, independent shifts in the galaxy stellar mass function at masses below $M_* \lesssim 10^8\ M_\odot$, as well as subtle deviations in the dark matter fractions of satellite galaxies, ensuring that future constraints on the particle nature of dark matter are robust against astrophysical uncertainties [Rose et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982...68R/abstract), [Silvestrini et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107543S/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

A primary limitation of the [[multi-cosmology-simulations|core CAMELS suite]] is its restricted simulation volume of $(25\, h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc})^3$. While sufficient for probing the internal properties of galaxies and small-scale clustering, these volumes cannot generate mock catalogs that match the scale of next-generation galaxy surveys like DESI and the Rubin Observatory. Scaling the high-dimensional parameter exploration to much larger volumes represents a significant computational hurdle that will eventually require exascale computing resources [Norman et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018FrASS...5...34N/abstract). In the interim, researchers are exploring hybrid approaches. For instance, the 'Galactification' method utilizes a transformer-based point cloud model to rapidly paint galaxies onto dark-matter-only simulations, capturing the complex conditional dependencies of hydrodynamical galaxy properties [Pandey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251108438P/abstract). Another approach utilizes statistical control variates to combine small-volume hydrodynamical runs (like MillenniumTNG and FLAMINGO) with large-volume $N$-body simulations [Doytcheva et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..184D/abstract). To address resolution constraints, AI-assisted super-resolution emulators are also being developed to enhance low-resolution snapshots into high-fidelity realizations that deterministically reproduce the small-scale structure of target $N$-body simulations [Zhang et al. **Observational Bias and Real-World Application**
Even if machine learning models can be made robust across different simulation codes, real-world application introduces observational bias. Factors including survey masking, redshift-space distortions, and complex selection effects collectively entangle radial positions and velocities, degrading the precision of simulation-trained models [de Santi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...01..082D/abstract). Developing models that reliably overcome these systematic observational effects remains a highly active area of research. Additionally, field-level reconstruction of unobserved dark matter and baryon density distributions from galaxy data is increasingly achievable through hybrid GNN-CNN architectures, offering new ways to map observations back to their underlying physical fields [Krywonos et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250712530K/abstract). Building on this, diffusion generative models have been successfully trained on CAMELS to perform probabilistic reconstruction of unbiased dark matter density fields directly from stellar density maps, showing remarkable generalization capabilities across different simulation codes [Ono et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..174O/abstract).

**Improving Interpretability and Unresolved Physics**
The remarkable success of neural networks in extracting cosmological information has highlighted the urgent need for interpretability. To understand *why* these 'black-box' models work, researchers increasingly rely on tools like autoencoders to study the underlying galaxy property manifold and symbolic regression to distill network outputs into analytic equations. Finally, fundamental discrepancies remain between simulated observables and real-universe measurements. Current standard AGN feedback recipes still struggle to reproduce multi-wavelength observations like the thermal SZ signal for specific massive galaxy samples [Moser et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933..133M/abstract). Disentangling the non-linear coupling between cosmological parameters and the aggressive baryonic feedback required to match observations remains a central open question for future simulation suites.
