## Article

**[[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]]** are sophisticated numerical frameworks designed to model the non-linear evolution of the Universe, bridging the gap between primordial density fluctuations and the complex, observed cosmic web. At their mathematical core, these models solve the coupled Vlasov-Poisson equations for collisionless dark matter alongside the Euler equations for dissipative baryonic gas, all within an expanding $\Lambda$CDM metric. By tracking the hierarchical gravitational collapse of matter, simulations provide the theoretical bedrock for interpreting large-scale structure, [[galaxy-models|galaxy formation]], and the distribution of multiphase gas. The historical evolution of the field—from early, gravity-only N-body calculations [Bertschinger et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ComPh...5..164B/abstract) to the pioneering integration of smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) [Katz et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJS..105...19K/abstract)—has culminated in modern, multi-physics frameworks capable of capturing a vast dynamic range of astrophysical phenomena [Vogelsberger et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatRP...2...42V/abstract).

To model both the macroscopic cosmic web and microscopic galactic environments, modern [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]] employ hybrid gravitational solvers, typically combining Tree and Particle-Mesh (TreePM) algorithms, paired with diverse fluid dynamics solvers like Eulerian Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR), Lagrangian SPH, or Meshless Finite Mass (MFM) schemes [O'Shea et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJS..160....1O/abstract), . Because computational constraints prohibit explicitly resolving individual stars or black holes within cosmologically representative volumes, [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] rely heavily on phenomenological [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]]. These models govern unresolved baryonic processes, specifically radiative cooling, star formation, and feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei (AGN). Feedback is strictly necessary to prevent the artificial "over-cooling" of gas, thereby regulating star formation to successfully reproduce the observed galaxy stellar mass functions and structural scaling relations [Puchwein et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.2966P/abstract), . 

The design of a cosmological simulation generally navigates a fundamental trade-off between spatial resolution and simulated volume. High-resolution [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] circumvent this by artificially refining a small target region—such as a Milky Way analog—within a larger, low-resolution box, allowing for the detailed study of galactic kinematics and the circumgalactic medium. Conversely, flagship large-volume projects like the [[millenniumtng|MillenniumTNG]] and the [[uchuu-simulation|Uchuu simulation]] prioritize the statistical power required for precision cosmology. By extracting spatial clustering statistics, [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|halo mass functions]], and synthetic light-cones from these vast volumes, researchers generate [[mock-galaxy-surveys-and-numerical-modeling|Mock Galaxy Surveys and Numerical Modeling]] pipelines to directly compare theoretical $\Lambda$CDM predictions against observations from telescopes such as JWST, Gaia, and LSST. 

Despite their immense predictive power, several theoretical tensions and computational frontiers remain unresolved. A paramount open question is the origin of the "small-scale controversies" of the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm, such as the cusp-core and missing satellite problems. The literature remains heavily divided on whether these discrepancies arise from under-resolved, violent baryonic feedback or if they constitute evidence for alternative dark sector physics, such as Warm or Self-Interacting Dark Matter [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract). As the field transitions into the exascale computing era, researchers are increasingly adopting variance suppression techniques and developing machine-learning emulators to bypass the extreme costs of full hydrodynamics. A critical ongoing challenge is ensuring these emulators and statistical tools remain robust to the systemic uncertainties inherent in competing [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]].

## Fundamental Framework and Physical Principles

At the mathematical core of modern cosmology, simulations trace the non-linear evolution of density perturbations within an expanding Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric. This requires solving the coupled dynamical equations for two primary cosmic components: a collisionless dark matter fluid and a collisional, dissipative baryonic gas.

### The Vlasov-Poisson Equations
Cold dark matter is modeled as a collisionless fluid whose phase-space distribution function, $f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{p}, t)$, is governed by the collisionless [[boltzmann-equation|Boltzmann equation]], also known as the Vlasov equation. In comoving coordinates $\mathbf{x}$ with canonical momentum $\mathbf{p} = m a^2 \dot{\mathbf{x}}$ and cosmic scale factor $a(t)$, the Vlasov equation is given by:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{\mathbf{p}}{m a^2} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} f - m \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \Phi \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{p}} f = 0 $$

The gravitational potential $\Phi$ arises from the local matter distribution via the comoving Poisson equation:

$$ \nabla^2 \Phi = 4 \pi G \bar{\rho} a^2 \delta $$

where $\bar{\rho}$ is the mean background density and $\delta = (\rho - \bar{\rho})/\bar{\rho}$ is the local density contrast. Direct integration of the 6D Vlasov-Poisson equations is highly computationally restrictive—though it has been achieved for specific applications like tracking massive relic neutrinos [Yoshikawa et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904..159Y/abstract). Building on this, massive neutrino simulations ([[massivenus|MassiveNuS]]) demonstrate that relic neutrino clustering in response to non-linear dark matter evolution significantly suppresses structure on [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|small scales]] [Liu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JCAP...03..049L/abstract). Consequently, standard [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]] discretize the phase-space fluid using $N$-body macro-particles to approximate the collisionless flow.

### Hydrodynamics and the Euler-Poisson Equations
To model the baryonic component, [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] couple gravity to the equations of fluid dynamics (the Euler-Poisson system). The gas obeys conservation laws for mass, momentum, and energy. Under the regime of adiabatic gas dynamics—before the inclusion of radiative cooling and phenomenological [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]]—the fluid responds exclusively to gravity, pressure gradients, and shocks.

Solvers for the baryonic fluid generally fall into discrete classes: Lagrangian Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH, e.g., GADGET), Eulerian Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR, e.g., Enzo), and modern meshless or moving-mesh methods (e.g., GIZMO, AREPO) [O'Shea et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJS..160....1O/abstract), , [Pakmor et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2539P/abstract). While all seek to resolve the non-linear growth of cosmic structures, tensions persist regarding their numerical artifacts. For instance, classic SPH and AMR schemes can yield differing results in fluid shock-capturing and entropy generation, with mesh-based methods generally performing better in low-density intergalactic regions but occasionally producing unphysical heating in preshock regions, while SPH schemes can suffer from excess entropy generation [O'Shea et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJS..160....1O/abstract). 

### Initial Conditions
The accuracy of non-linear structure formation relies fundamentally on the precise generation of initial conditions, which map the primordial power spectrum onto the initial particle or grid distribution. To achieve the 1% precision required by modern cosmological surveys for the [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|halo mass function]] and matter power spectrum, simulations must employ second-order Lagrangian perturbation theory (2LPT). Furthermore, the starting epoch must be carefully calibrated; initializing the simulation between 10 and 50 expansion factors before the target epoch is necessary to ensure convergence and prevent the artificial suppression of small-scale structure [Reed et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431.1866R/abstract). Additionally, utilizing scale-free initial conditions can preserve self-similarity in simulations with radiative cooling by ensuring the cooling time remains a fixed fraction of the Hubble time [Owen et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..5097O/abstract).

To maximize the statistical utility of finite computational volumes, recent projects have adopted "fixed-and-paired" initial conditions. By forcing the initial Fourier mode amplitudes to match the exact ensemble average power spectrum and running paired simulations with exactly inverted initial phases, this technique dramatically suppresses large-scale cosmic variance [Angulo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462L...1A/abstract). This approach allows smaller simulation boxes to yield highly precise clustering statistics, a methodology utilized extensively in large suites like the [[millenniumtng|MillenniumTNG]] project [Hernández-Aguayo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2556H/abstract).

## Historical Development of Cosmological Codes

The historical evolution of [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]] is characterized by a continuous expansion in computational scale and algorithmic complexity, driven by the need to capture a vast dynamic range of physical processes. Early efforts were restricted to collisionless, gravity-only $N$-body calculations that modeled the universe as a fluid of interacting dark matter particles [Aarseth et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980ApJ...236...43A/abstract). To initialize these simulations, pioneers relied on the Zel'dovich approximation to map the linear density fluctuation field onto particle positions and velocities, establishing the standard framework for cosmological initial conditions [Bertschinger et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ComPh...5..164B/abstract). In these early models, the Newtonian gravitational force was typically modified by a softening length $\epsilon$ to prevent unphysical two-body relaxation, yielding the modified force equation:

$$ \mathbf{F}_i = \sum_{j \neq i} \frac{G m_i m_j (\mathbf{r}_j - \mathbf{r}_i)}{(|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j|^2 + \epsilon^2)^{3/2}} $$

By the early 1990s, supercomputers enabled particle-mesh simulations utilizing over two million particles, though limited force resolution and the computational cost of resolving highly dense structures remained significant challenges [Bertschinger et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ComPh...5..164B/abstract).

A major paradigm shift occurred with the realization that baryonic physics—specifically gas cooling and dissipation—was essential to reproduce the internal structure of galaxies. The first [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] to model a cosmologically representative fluid mixture with gas cooling and star formation successfully demonstrated that gas could settle into centrifugally supported disks resembling observed spiral galaxies. This methodology was formalized in the TreeSPH code, which combined Lagrangian smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) with hierarchical tree-based gravity solvers. The Lagrangian nature of SPH, alongside individual particle time steps, provided the large dynamic range necessary to trace both large-scale cosmological collapse and kpc-scale galactic objects simultaneously [Katz et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJS..105...19K/abstract).

As computational power grew, an ongoing debate emerged regarding the optimal numerical schemes for hydrodynamics, specifically between SPH codes (e.g., GADGET) and Eulerian AMR codes (e.g., Enzo) [O'Shea et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJS..160....1O/abstract). Traditional SPH methods excelled in conserving angular momentum and tracking Galilean-invariant flows but struggled with artificial viscosity and capturing fluid shocks. Conversely, AMR methods accurately modeled shocks and entropy generation but suffered from over-mixing and grid-alignment artifacts. Comparisons highlighted that while structural properties of highly overdense regions agreed broadly between methods, significant ambiguities and differences persisted in the temperature and entropy distributions of the lower-density intergalactic medium [O'Shea et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJS..160....1O/abstract).

Modern [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]] have largely bridged this divide through the development of hybrid and high-order methods. Techniques such as the moving-mesh approach (e.g., AREPO) and meshless finite mass (MFM) schemes—used in the GIZMO code for the [[mufasa-simulation|MUFASA (simulation)]] and SIMBA projects , [Davé et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.2827D/abstract)—combine the high force resolution and continuous adaptivity of Lagrangian methods with the accurate shock-capturing of Eulerian grids. Today, these multi-physics frameworks deploy sophisticated [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] to account for unresolved phenomena like active galactic nuclei and supernova feedback. Historically, balancing resolution against the simulated volume was a severe limitation. However, contemporary projects such as [[millenniumtng|MillenniumTNG]] demonstrate that it is now possible to simulate $(740 \, \mathrm{Mpc})^3$ volumes at baryonic mass resolutions of $\sim 3 \times 10^7 \, \mathrm{M}_\odot$ [Pakmor et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2539P/abstract), successfully resolving internal galaxy dynamics within a cosmologically representative environment [Vogelsberger et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatRP...2...42V/abstract).

## Numerical Methodologies: N-Body and Hydrodynamics

The accurate modeling of structure formation requires solving the coupled equations of collisionless dynamics for dark matter and fluid dynamics for baryons. Modern [[cosmological-and-hydrodynamical-simulations|Cosmological and Hydrodynamical Simulations]] employ sophisticated hybrid algorithms to tackle the vast dynamic range of these astrophysical processes [Vogelsberger et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020NatRP...2...42V/abstract), [Valentini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206954V/abstract).

### Gravitational Solvers: TreePM
Dark matter is treated as a collisionless fluid governed by the Vlasov-Poisson equations, typically sampled using $N$-body particle techniques. Pure Particle-Mesh (PM) algorithms assign particle masses to a spatial grid to solve the Poisson equation via Fast Fourier Transforms. While highly efficient on large scales, PM methods lack the short-range force resolution required to resolve dense galactic halos. Conversely, hierarchical Tree algorithms compute pairwise forces efficiently but become computationally expensive when evaluating long-range interactions across large cosmological volumes. Most modern [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]] employ a hybrid TreePM approach, combining the long-range speed of the PM method with the high spatial resolution of the Tree algorithm for short-range interactions. Advanced implementations, such as Adaptive TreePM (ATreePM), further refine force softening lengths dynamically in high-density regions to preserve collisionless evolution without sacrificing resolution [Bagla et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.2211B/abstract).

### Hydrodynamic Solvers: SPH, AMR, and MFM
Baryonic gas is governed by the Euler equations, with sub-resolution astrophysics handled via [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]]. The numerical implementation of hydrodynamics broadly divides into Lagrangian and Eulerian schemes.

*Lagrangian methods*, such as Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH), discretize mass into particles that advect with the fluid flow (e.g., GADGET, GASOLINE). SPH guarantees Galilean invariance and naturally concentrates computational resolution in high-density regions, making it highly efficient for tracking collapsing structures. However, traditional SPH relies on artificial viscosity to capture shocks, which can lead to spurious entropy generation and the unphysical dampening of fluid instabilities.

*Eulerian methods* discretize space rather than mass. Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR) codes (e.g., Enzo, [[ramses-simulation-code|Ramses (simulation code)]]) employ stationary grids that dynamically subdivide in regions of high density or steep gradients. AMR schemes, particularly those utilizing the Piecewise Parabolic Method (PPM), excel at capturing shocks and resolving complex phase boundaries in the circumgalactic medium. A historical drawback of AMR in a cosmological context is that hierarchical grid-based gravity solvers tend to offer lower force resolution at early times prior to extensive mesh refinement, occasionally suppressing the abundance of low-mass dark matter halos compared to Tree methods. Furthermore, Eulerian simulations of cosmological shocks can uniquely characterize the efficiency of cosmic ray acceleration in different environments, comparing theoretical results against cluster observations [Vazza et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MSAIS..13..151V/abstract). High-resolution AMR simulations of Milky Way-sized galaxies also consistently reproduce the observed Tully-Fisher and gas-to-stellar mass correlations, showing that gas velocity dispersions are strongly driven by stellar kinetic energy injection [Colín et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...829...98C/abstract).

To disentangle numerical artifacts from physical effects, standardized benchmarks like the [[agora-simulation|AGORA simulation]] High-resolution Galaxy Simulations Comparison Project evaluate code-independent results across these diverse numerical platforms [Kim et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..210...14K/abstract).

To bridge the divide between SPH and AMR, modern codes have developed hybrid fluid solvers. Moving-mesh codes (e.g., AREPO, used in [[millenniumtng|MillenniumTNG]]) utilize an unstructured Voronoi tessellation that moves with the fluid flow, combining Lagrangian adaptivity with the precise shock-capturing of Eulerian Riemann solvers. Alternatively, Meshless Finite Mass (MFM) methods, implemented in codes like GIZMO and utilized in the [[mufasa-simulation|MUFASA (simulation)]], partition the volume among particles without a rigid mesh, directly solving the Riemann problem between interacting particles. These advanced schemes demonstrate that the choice of hydrodynamics methodology alone can alter predictions for critical observables, such as the galaxy stellar mass function, by factors of up to $\sim 2$, even when underlying feedback parameters remain constant .

Beyond pure hydrodynamics, modeling early galaxy evolution also requires resolving magnetic fields; for example, [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] demonstrate that initial seed magnetic fields can be rapidly amplified via small-scale dynamos driven by supernova-induced turbulence in early, feedback-dominated galaxies [Rieder et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.4368R/abstract).

### Structure Identification and Merger Trees
Beyond the solvers themselves, extracting physical insights requires identifying bound structures and tracking their evolution. New implementations of tools like the VELOCIRAPTOR galaxy finder utilize tree reordering to achieve ideal scaling in high-resolution, dense environments [Rhee et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...927..129R/abstract). To map evolutionary histories, core-tracking algorithms for [[halo-merger-trees|Halo merger trees]]—such as those applied to the massive Q Continuum simulation—allow for the efficient tracking of billions of substructures across hundreds of snapshots [Rangel et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200808519R/abstract).

## Subgrid Physics and Baryonic Feedback Recipes

Because of the vast dynamic range involved in cosmological structure formation, processes occurring below the spatial or mass resolution limits of [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] must be treated using phenomenological [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]]. These recipes are essential for capturing the baryonic physics that govern galaxy evolution, primarily radiative cooling, star formation, and feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei (AGN).

### Radiative Cooling and Star Formation
Gas cooling in simulations is fundamentally calculated assuming an optically thin, primordial composition gas [Katz et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJS..105...19K/abstract), a framework that modern codes have since augmented with sophisticated metal-line cooling networks. In these networks, the local density, temperature, and metallicity dictate the energy loss rates required for gas to collapse into galactic potential wells. Once gas becomes cold and dense, star formation is modeled on a sub-resolution level, frequently utilizing a Schmidt-type law based on the local cold gas density. Advanced star formation recipes now investigate the consistent coupling of magnetic pressure as an additional regulator of gas depletion rates within the multi-phase interstellar medium [Batziou et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240503765B/abstract). Without further regulation, this cooling and star formation leads to the "over-cooling problem," generating unphysically compact galaxies with overly peaked central rotation curves and excessive bulge-to-total mass ratios [Stinson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.408..812S/abstract). Ultimately, this interplay between cooling, star formation, and feedback drives the "two-phase" character of [[galaxy-models|galaxy formation]], where rapid early in situ star formation within the primary halo is subsequently followed by an extended, lower-redshift phase dominated by the accretion of satellite stellar systems [Oser et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...725.2312O/abstract).

### Stellar Feedback
To prevent over-cooling, simulations employ stellar feedback. In low-mass halos, supernova-driven winds are the dominant regulatory mechanism, successfully truncating the low-mass end of the galaxy stellar mass function to match observations [Puchwein et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.2966P/abstract), . Implementations include kinetic wind ejections and delayed thermal energy injection. Additionally, "early" stellar feedback, such as radiation pressure from young massive stars, is often required to suppress early starbursts and match high-redshift galaxy abundances [Stinson et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..129S/abstract), [Moody et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.1389M/abstract). Recent advances incorporate cosmic ray (CR) diffusion into stellar feedback models. CR pressure gradients can support gas against gravity, driving extended outflows and maintaining flat rotation curves in dwarf galaxies more effectively than purely thermal feedback [Salem et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...797L..18S/abstract), [Chen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.3335C/abstract), [Hopkins et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.3465H/abstract). Under the influence of these mechanisms, low-mass galaxies exhibit higher specific star formation rates and delayed stellar assembly relative to their dark matter halos compared to massive counterparts [De Rossi et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.2736D/abstract).

### AGN Feedback
While stellar feedback regulates dwarfs and Milky Way-mass systems, AGN feedback is essential for quenching star formation in massive galaxies, establishing the observed bimodal color distribution, and reproducing the high-mass exponential cutoff in the stellar mass function [Cattaneo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.377...63C/abstract), [Booth et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398...53B/abstract). Energy from supermassive black holes—growing via Bondi-Hoyle accretion—is coupled to the surrounding medium either thermally or via kinetic bipolar jets [Booth et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398...53B/abstract), [Davé et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.2827D/abstract). Systematic studies of these subgrid models show that the choice of seed mass and merger criteria dramatically impacts predicted black hole merger rates and the overall cosmic mass density [Sutter et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...723.1308S/abstract). AGN feedback dramatically alters the large-scale baryon distribution, entraining gas far beyond the host halo and establishing a baryonic "closure radius" of $1.5-2.5 R_{200c}$ where the cosmic baryon fraction is finally recovered [Ayromlou et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5391A/abstract). This large-scale expulsion causes a back-reaction on the dark matter, suppressing the total matter power spectrum by up to 20% at $k \approx 10 \, h \, \mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$ [Springel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475..676S/abstract), [Gebhardt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4896G/abstract).

### Tensions and Future Directions
The literature exhibits significant tension regarding the physical realism and uniqueness of current [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]]. Different feedback implementations can reproduce the same global scaling relations but yield conflicting internal structures or fail to match independent observables. For instance, models frequently struggle to reproduce the multiphase structure and specific column densities (e.g., O VI) observed in the circumgalactic medium [Hummels et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.430.1548H/abstract) or present discrepancies when evaluated against the spectral variance of real galaxies [Sharbaf et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108945S/abstract). To address the arbitrariness of empirically tuned models, the field is increasingly moving toward subgrid algorithms calibrated directly from first-principles, [[high-resolution-simulations|high-resolution]] interstellar medium simulations, such as the pressure-regulated, feedback-modulated (PRFM) star formation framework [Hassan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..151H/abstract), [Burger et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250213244B/abstract).

## The Galaxy-Halo Connection and Large-Scale Structure

Cosmological simulations are indispensable for predicting the non-linear evolution of large-scale structure and establishing the statistical properties of the galaxy-halo connection. To constrain fundamental cosmological parameters such as $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$ using upcoming large-scale surveys, theoretical predictions of the spatial distribution of matter must reach unprecedented levels of precision. 

### Halo Mass Functions and the Power Spectrum
Achieving $1\%$ accuracy in the theoretical [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|halo mass function]] requires rigorous control over numerical artifacts. High-resolution gravity-only simulations can reach this precision provided that numerical artifacts from initial conditions and finite mass resolution are tightly controlled [Reed et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431.1866R/abstract). Flagship runs such as the [[uchuu-simulation|Uchuu simulation]] and [[millenniumtng|MillenniumTNG]] demonstrate that the $\Lambda$CDM model consistently reproduces the observed power spectrum and the abundance of massive clusters on large scales [Ishiyama et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.4210I/abstract), [Hernández-Aguayo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2556H/abstract).

However, baryonic physics significantly modifies the total matter power spectrum. Processes such as active galactic nuclei (AGN) and supernova feedback redistribute gas on [[sub-megaparsec-scales|Sub-megaparsec scales]], which can suppress or enhance the power spectrum by up to $20\%$ at wave numbers $k \sim 10 \, h \, \mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$ [Springel et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475..676S/abstract), [Gebhardt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4896G/abstract). The literature exhibits clear tensions regarding the exact magnitude of this back-reaction, as it is highly sensitive to the choice of [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] and the extent to which gas is expelled from its initial Lagrangian regions [Borrow et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.6102B/abstract), [Gebhardt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4896G/abstract). This theoretical ambiguity requires that baryonic effects be carefully marginalized over in precision cosmological inference.

### Matter Bispectrum and Higher-Order Statistics
While the power spectrum captures the Gaussian variance of the density field, higher-order statistics like the matter bispectrum are essential for breaking parameter degeneracies and extracting non-Gaussian information. For example, the cosmological genus statistic provides a topological measure of large-scale structure that can dynamically delineate the effects of non-linear evolution and primordial non-Gaussianity [James et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...751...40J/abstract). Extracting the matter bispectrum from fast approximate solvers (e.g., particle-mesh or 2LPT codes) reveals a systematic lack of small-scale power compared to full N-body calculations. Empirical "boosting" techniques are often required to correct the power spectrum and bispectrum when utilizing these fast codes for mock catalog generation [Hung et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190201830H/abstract).

### Galaxy Clustering and Halo Occupation
Simulations provide a physically motivated framework for modeling [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|Dark Matter Halo Properties and Statistics]] alongside their resident galaxy populations. In massive groups and clusters ($M_{200c} \sim 10^{13} - 10^{15} \, M_\odot$), hydrodynamic models predict that approximately half of the total stellar mass is bound in satellite galaxies, while the remainder resides in the central galaxy and the diffuse intracluster light. The 3D stellar mass within the inner 30 kpc scales tightly with the host halo mass as:

$$M_{\mathrm{stars}} \propto (M_{500c})^{0.49}$$

with a narrow scatter of $\sim 0.12$ dex [Pillepich et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475..648P/abstract). Furthermore, cluster simulations suggest that $20-40\%$ of the total cluster light originates from a diffuse intra-cluster stellar population separate from the central galaxy [Romeo et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph..4348R/abstract). On broader scales, projects like the Horizon-AGN simulation capture the aggregate growth of galaxies across cosmic time, matching the observed cosmic star formation history from $z=6$ to the present [Kaviraj et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467.4739K/abstract). In terms of cluster-scale structure, quantitative power ratio analysis of cluster morphologies suggests that the abundance of substructure in simulated clusters favors low-density universes over standard CDM [Tsai et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.282...77T/abstract). 

While mergers are a key part of hierarchical growth, high-resolution simulations suggest that major mergers may have a relatively modest effect on driving star formation at redshifts $z > 1$ compared to earlier [[idealized-galaxy-models|Idealized galaxy models]]. Detailed comparisons of 2D light distributions and scaling relations for dwarf galaxies in simulations like [[newhorizon|NEWHORIZON]] reveal that simulated dwarfs can be systematically larger and bluer than local counterparts, highlighting areas for feedback model improvement [Watkins et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.537.3499W/abstract).

Galaxy clustering predictions derived from these models successfully match the non-linear two-point correlation functions observed in surveys like SDSS and DEEP2, including the small-scale upturn characteristic of luminosity-selected samples [Kravtsov et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..7463K/abstract). The clustering bias of different observational tracers exhibits significant scale dependence, which extends into the baryon acoustic oscillation regime and can cause correctable shifts in the acoustic peaks of around $5\%$ [Springel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475..676S/abstract).

### Variance Suppression and Emulation
To mitigate the severe computational costs of simulating both large-scale structure and galaxy formation, modern studies frequently employ the variance suppression techniques introduced previously, such as "fixed-and-paired" initial conditions [Angulo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462L...1A/abstract), [Maion et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...10..036M/abstract), [Hernández-Aguayo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2556H/abstract). Furthermore, rapid surrogate models and machine learning techniques are actively being developed to populate fast [[matter-only-simulations|Matter-only simulations]] with galaxies, bypassing the need for full hydrodynamics while preserving high statistical fidelity.

## Multi-Phase Gas: CGM, IGM, and Reionization

Modern [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] have expanded their focus beyond the dense interstellar medium to capture the complex, multi-phase gas of the circumgalactic medium (CGM) and intergalactic medium (IGM). The CGM acts as both the primary reservoir of accreting gas that fuels galaxy growth and the repository for metal-enriched outflows driven by galactic feedback.

The redistribution of baryons by these feedback processes is profound. Beyond individual halos, intermediate-mass galaxy groups serve as critical laboratories for studying environmental processes, the intragroup medium, and the complex redistribution of baryons driven by cooling and feedback [Oppenheimer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Univ....7..209O/abstract). Simulations indicate that intracluster magnetic fields grow exponentially with redshift, with mergers inducing steep transient increases in field strength [Bartelmann et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph..9541B/abstract). Furthermore, stellar and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback can eject gas far beyond the virial radius of the host dark matter halo. As a result of this feedback, the "closure radius"—the scale within which all baryons associated with a halo are finally found—is pushed to typically $1.5$ to $2.5 R_{\rm 200c}$ [Ayromlou et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5391A/abstract). On cosmological scales, approximately $40\%$ of baryons may be displaced more than $1 \text{ Mpc}$ from their initial Lagrangian regions by $z=0$, largely due to entrainment by AGN jets [Borrow et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.6102B/abstract). However, this large-scale baryon spread remains highly sensitive to the chosen [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]]; for instance, the fiducial SIMBA model displaces $\sim 40\%$ of baryons beyond $1 \text{ Mpc}$, whereas other models like IllustrisTNG and ASTRID displace only $\sim 10\%$ [Gebhardt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4896G/abstract). 

Despite their successes in matching global galaxy properties, many standard cosmological simulations struggle to reproduce the observed multiphase structure of the CGM. Synthetic quasar absorption line studies often reveal that the simulated CGM is too hot and lacks sufficient multiphase substructure, leading to an underprediction of high-ionization species like O VI [Hummels et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.430.1548H/abstract). Furthermore, standard mass-based refinement schemes tend to under-resolve the diffuse halo gas, causing an underestimation of neutral hydrogen column densities. Contrary to traditional textbook assumptions that high resolution necessitates prohibitively small volumes, recent [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] demonstrate that applying a uniform spatial refinement of $\sim 1 \text{ kpc}$ throughout the virial radius dramatically improves the covering fraction of Lyman-limit systems without a dominating increase in computational cost [van de Voort et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482L..85V/abstract). 

At high redshifts, modeling the IGM requires tracking the Epoch of Reionization. The reionization of hydrogen and helium is a highly inhomogeneous process driven by the percolation of ionized bubbles around early sources, necessitating 3D radiative transfer coupled with hydrodynamics [Razoumov et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...572..695R/abstract). The ionizing UV background provides a critical source of thermal feedback, heating the IGM and suppressing gas accretion and star formation in low-mass halos ($M_{\rm halo} < 10^{10} M_\odot$) [Ayromlou et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5391A/abstract). During the reionization epoch, simulations robustly predict that galaxy star formation histories are smoothly rising and follow a shape that is invariant with halo mass [Finlator et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410.1703F/abstract). The timing of local reionization introduces significant scatter in the gas fractions of dwarf galaxies, with those that have already been reionized exhibiting higher star formation rates than those that have not yet been reionized [Bird et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.3703B/abstract). To accurately capture these non-equilibrium radiation-hydrodynamic effects alongside cosmic dust and metal enrichment, contemporary large-scale frameworks like [[thesan|THESAN]] and the [[astrid-simulations|ASTRID simulations]] have been explicitly designed to model the intertwined evolution of the IGM and early galaxies during the first billion years of cosmic history [Garaldi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.3765G/abstract), [Bird et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.3703B/abstract).

## Specialized Simulation Strategies: Zoom-Ins and Large Volumes

Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations face a fundamental computational limitation: the trade-off between the simulated volume required for statistical completeness and the spatial and mass resolution necessary to resolve internal galactic physics. To address this, theorists typically employ two distinct numerical strategies: targeted zoom-in simulations and large-volume boxes.

### Zoom-In Simulations
Zoom-in simulations circumvent the volume-resolution trade-off by identifying a region of interest—such as a Milky Way-mass halo or a dwarf galaxy—within a low-resolution cosmological dark matter box. This specific region is then resimulated at exceptionally high resolution while preserving the large-scale cosmological tidal field. This approach allows codes to reach parsec-scale resolutions, enabling the explicit modeling of the multiphase interstellar medium, individual star formation, and kinetic stellar feedback without being bottlenecked by the computational cost of the surrounding universe [Wetzel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...44W/abstract). Suites such as the [[auriga-simulations|AURIGA simulations]] and [[newhorizon|NEWHORIZON]] utilize this technique to study morphological transformations, galactic disk kinematics, and the structural properties of dwarf galaxies in high detail [Dubois et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...651A.109D/abstract), [Grand et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.1814G/abstract). [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] of Milky Way-mass galaxies predict that stellar density profiles along the minor axis are governed by a power-law component that strongly correlates with the underlying dark matter profile [Lucey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982...87L/abstract). 

To further push the boundaries of resolving galactic structures, novel techniques like the "Superstars" method increase the effective stellar mass resolution by splitting star particles in specific regions. This significantly reduces artificial numerical heating in the stellar disc without fundamentally altering global galaxy properties [Pakmor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722104P/abstract). Zoom-ins are also particularly critical for understanding the circumgalactic medium (CGM), demonstrating that increased spatial resolution drastically alters the predicted radial profiles of neutral hydrogen and Lyman-limit systems [van de Voort et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482L..85V/abstract). This confirms that many predicted properties of the CGM remain strongly resolution-dependent.

### Large-Volume Boxes
Conversely, large-volume boxes prioritize the statistical power required for precision cosmology. These flagship simulations are designed to predict the matter power spectrum, the [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|halo mass function]], and large-scale clustering (such as baryon acoustic oscillations) to constrain cosmological parameters. Achieving $1\%$ accuracy in these bulk statistics demands rigorous numerical convergence and high-fidelity initial conditions [Reed et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431.1866R/abstract). Massive dark-matter-only runs, such as the [[uchuu-simulation|Uchuu simulation]], feature volumes up to $(2.0 \, h^{-1}\mathrm{Gpc})^3$ to trace structure from dwarf halos to the rarest massive galaxy clusters [Ishiyama et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.4210I/abstract). Similarly, the Horizon Run project utilizes massive N-body simulations spanning over $10 \, \mathrm{Gpc}$ to produce all-sky mock light-cones tailored for large-scale structure surveys like BOSS [Kim et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011JKAS...44..217K/abstract).

To mitigate the immense computational expense of these massive boxes, researchers increasingly employ "fixed-and-paired" initial conditions to suppress large-scale cosmic variance without requiring vastly larger simulated volumes [Angulo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462L...1A/abstract), [Maion et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...10..036M/abstract), [Hernández-Aguayo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2556H/abstract).

Historically, it was assumed that simultaneously resolving the internal structure of galaxies and maintaining a cosmologically representative volume was computationally infeasible. However, [[next-generation-cosmological-simulations|Next-generation cosmological simulations]] are successfully challenging this dichotomy. Building upon the subgrid models of the IllustrisTNG project, the [[millenniumtng|MillenniumTNG]] suite models a massive $(740 \, \mathrm{Mpc})^3$ volume with $80.6$ billion cells, achieving a baryonic mass resolution of $3.1 \times 10^7 \, \mathrm{M_\odot}$ [Pakmor et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2539P/abstract). This scale allows for the simultaneous study of precise clustering statistics and the detailed properties of the galaxy population using robust [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]], effectively bridging the gap between zoom-ins and traditional survey volumes.

## Probing Alternative Cosmologies and Dark Matter Physics

While the standard cold dark matter (CDM) paradigm excels at predicting large-scale structure, it has faced persistent challenges on sub-galactic scales, notably the "cusp-core" and "missing satellite" problems [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract). [[cosmological-and-hydrodynamical-simulations|Cosmological and Hydrodynamical Simulations]] play a critical role in determining whether these discrepancies represent a breakdown of the collisionless dark matter hypothesis or are simply artifacts of incomplete [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]]. Furthermore, the interplay between baryonic physics and dark matter is cosmology-dependent; findings from the CAMELS project demonstrate that baryonic feedback reduces virial masses and significantly redistributes dark matter, emphasizing the intrinsic dependence of this cosmological "back-reaction" on the underlying background parameters [Gebhardt et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260106258G/abstract). By varying the fundamental properties of the dark sector, researchers use simulations to generate observable predictions for alternative cosmologies.

### Self-Interacting and Warm Dark Matter
Self-interacting dark matter (SIDM) introduces elastic scattering between dark matter particles, which transfers kinetic energy to the dense centers of halos and transforms steep density cusps into constant-density cores. [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] demonstrate that SIDM with a cross-section over mass of $\sigma/m \simeq 0.1$ cm$^2$ g$^{-1}$ successfully reproduces the core sizes and central densities of dwarf galaxies, low surface brightness spirals, and galaxy clusters without spoiling the large-scale clustering successes of CDM. Complementing N-body techniques, simulations of self-interacting dark matter using the cosmological [[boltzmann-equation|Boltzmann equation]] also reproduce the observed invariance of halo central density with mass across several orders of magnitude [Firmani et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.10497F/abstract). Conversely, much larger cross-sections (e.g., $\sigma/m = 1$ cm$^2$ g$^{-1}$) produce central densities that are far too low to match observations [Rocha et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.430...81R/abstract). 

Warm dark matter (WDM) models, on the other hand, suppress structure formation below a characteristic free-streaming scale. Recent initiatives like the AIDA-TNG project—which couples the IllustrisTNG baryonic physics model to WDM and SIDM cosmologies—show that many global galaxy scaling relations remain remarkably robust across dark matter models, though extreme WDM scenarios yield deviations in galaxy sizes and the low-mass stellar mass function [Despali et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...697A.213D/abstract), [Despali et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250112439D/abstract). Using the DREAMS simulation suite, researchers have noted that the galaxy stellar mass function exhibits a measurable dependence on the WDM particle mass at $M_* \lesssim 10^8 M_\odot$, presenting a promising diagnostic [Silvestrini et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107543S/abstract). However, a major unresolved debate in the literature remains: it is highly ambiguous whether observed cores in dwarf galaxies are the signature of dark sector physics (WDM or SIDM) or the result of supernova-driven baryonic potential fluctuations [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract).

### Fuzzy Dark Matter
Fuzzy Dark Matter (FDM) replaces standard particle dark matter with an ultra-light scalar field, where quantum pressure resists gravitational collapse on small scales. Recent implementations in hydrodynamical codes (e.g., the FUZZY-GASOLINE code) model the quantum potential alongside comprehensive baryonic physics. These simulations reveal that FDM induces cores primarily in low-mass systems at high redshift [Nori et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241109733N/abstract). Crucially, however, the resulting stellar observables—such as star formation histories and velocity dispersion profiles—remain remarkably similar to CDM predictions. This highlights a severe degeneracy, making it exceedingly difficult to distinguish between FDM and CDM using stellar kinematics alone [Nori et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241109733N/abstract).

### Modified Gravity and Dynamical Dark Energy
Simulations are additionally deployed to test modified gravity and dynamical dark energy. In screened scalar-tensor theories, such as the symmetron model, simulations show that non-universal couplings between the scalar field, gas, and dark matter induce significant deviations in the baryon fraction and the gas-to-dark matter power spectrum bias [Hammami et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...584A..57H/abstract). Furthermore, models featuring a time-varying vacuum energy ($\Lambda(t)$) or clustering dark energy (with sound speeds $c_s \neq 1$) alter the growth rate of perturbations. These cosmologies predict an earlier epoch of cluster formation and modulate the [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|halo mass function]], prompting the development of new precision calibrations for cluster abundances in non-standard theoretical frameworks [Basilakos et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.395.2347B/abstract), [Castro et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250502762C/abstract).

## Synthetic Observables and Mock Survey Generation

To rigorously evaluate [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] against observational data, researchers employ forward-modeling techniques to generate synthetic observables. This approach translates intrinsic simulation properties—such as gas density, temperature, metallicity, and stellar age—into directly measurable quantities like fluxes, spectra, and surface brightness maps. 

At the foundational level, generating [[mock-galaxy-surveys-and-numerical-modeling|mock catalogs]] involves identifying galaxies within dark matter halos and assigning them luminosities and colors. This is typically achieved by convolving a simulated galaxy's star formation history and metallicity distribution with a stellar population synthesis model. Because telescopes observe objects along the past light-cone, mock surveys must also stitch together simulation snapshots across cosmic time to capture the correct evolutionary states and survey geometries. Algorithms such as the Halo Optimised Lightcone Constructor (HOLCon) have been developed to generate deep-field light-cones extending to $z=10$ for the [[uchuu-simulation|Uchuu simulation]], optimizing orientation to minimize repeating structures from finite simulation volumes [Ramakrishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251213801R/abstract).

Forward-modeling pipelines are frequently tailored to the specifications of specific observatories:
*   **Gaia:** For galactic archaeology, frameworks like *ananke* generate synthetic phase-space surveys from high-resolution zoom-in simulations. By applying realistic dust extinction and observational error models, these tools replicate the exact data structure and magnitude limits of Gaia Data Release 2 [Sanderson et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..246....6S/abstract).
*   **JWST and High-Redshift Observatories:** The unprecedented infrared sensitivity of JWST has driven the creation of synthetic spectra for the Epoch of Reionization. The [[thesan|THESAN]] project provides radiation-hydrodynamic synthetic observations of high-redshift galaxies [Garaldi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.3765G/abstract). Similarly, the MEGATRON suite couples non-equilibrium thermochemistry and radiation transport to generate a library of over $175,000$ mock spectra, predicting the spectral properties of Population III stars and early galaxies [Katz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251005201K/abstract). Furthermore, high-resolution adaptive mesh simulations predict that dust obscuration in Lyman break galaxies creates a characteristic 'galaxy desert' in the UV-FIR luminosity plane [Cen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...742L..33C/abstract).
*   **LSST and DESI:** Wide-field photometric and spectroscopic surveys require massive cosmological volumes. The [[millenniumtng|MillenniumTNG]] suite utilizes semi-analytic and phenomenological models to populate halos, producing full-sky light-cones for large-scale structure, weak lensing, and baryon acoustic oscillation analyses [Hernández-Aguayo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2556H/abstract). To overcome the computational cost of simulating these massive volumes, researchers increasingly use control variates to suppress cosmic variance in mock catalogs, achieving effective volumes up to 100 times larger than the native simulation [Doytcheva et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..184D/abstract). Tools like the Core Cosmology Library (CCL) further provide high-precision predictions for LSST observables [Chisari et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..242....2C/abstract).
*   **X-ray and SZ Observatories:** Modeling massive clusters requires synthesizing X-ray surface brightness and Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) maps. Mock eROSITA light-cones have been generated to study the intracluster medium and calibrate X-ray scaling relations [Zandanel et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480..987Z/abstract). Hydrodynamic simulations of galaxy clusters indicate that X-ray scaling relations (e.g., mass-temperature) remain stable between $z=0$ and $z=1$, though low-mass systems at higher redshifts are sensitive to the inclusion of AGN feedback [Truong et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.4089T/abstract). Meanwhile, to more accurately mimic observed X-ray spectra rather than simply taking standard emission-weighted averages, researchers compute a "spectroscopic-like temperature" ($T_{\mathrm{sl}}$) which naturally accounts for the complex thermal structure of simulated clusters [Mazzotta et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph.12536M/abstract). Through other channels, [[millenniumtng|MillenniumTNG]] deep light-cones yield Compton-$y$ maps consistent with Planck limits, revealing that simultaneous matching of simulated cluster masses and SZ signals requires assuming that observational SZ mass estimates are biased low by approximately $0.2\text{ dex}$ [Pakmor et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2539P/abstract).
*   **Cluster Kinematics:** Within massive clusters, intracluster planetary nebulae serve as kinematic tracers of the diffuse stellar component, allowing simulations to test the assembly history of nearby galaxy clusters observationally [Arnaboldi et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006EAS....20..167A/abstract).
*   **Radio and Multimessenger Astronomy:** The modeling of radio relics in merging galaxy clusters combines high-resolution AMR simulations with electron acceleration models to predict synchrotron emission from cosmological shocks [Skillman et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...735...96S/abstract). Simulations are also used to predict multimessenger signals, such as the rate of compact binary mergers detectable by LIGO, which depends sensitively on the star formation and metallicity history of host galaxies [O'Shaughnessy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.2831O/abstract).
*   **Dark Matter Annihilation:** To model indirect dark matter detection, tools like the NAJADS framework enable the direct computation of dark matter J-factor sky maps from simulation data, accurately accounting for complex halo substructure [Balaudo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...02..001B/abstract).

Emerging techniques increasingly leverage machine learning to streamline mock survey generation. Unsupervised deep learning anomaly detection is being deployed to quantitatively rate the image-level realism of simulated galaxies against real imaging surveys like SDSS, providing a robust validation metric that captures morphological nuances missed by traditional scaling relations [Jin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.3536J/abstract).

## Advanced Statistical Techniques and Precision Cosmology

The era of precision cosmology—targeting $\sim 1\%$ accuracy in clustering statistics—places stringent requirements on numerical structure formation models. Achieving this baseline accuracy in [[matter-only-simulations|Matter-only simulations]] demands high-fidelity initial conditions and strict control over force and mass resolution [Reed et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431.1866R/abstract). 

Even with highly accurate dynamics, the computational cost of simulating volumes large enough to overcome cosmic sample variance limits parameter space exploration. To address this bottleneck, "fixing-and-pairing" has emerged as a powerful variance suppression technique. In this approach, the initial Fourier mode amplitudes are explicitly fixed to the theoretical ensemble average power spectrum, and simulations are run in pairs with their initial mode phases inverted exactly out of phase relative to one another [Angulo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462L...1A/abstract). On non-linear scales, a single fixed-and-paired set can yield the statistical precision of 50 traditional independent realizations [Angulo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462L...1A/abstract). This technique has been adopted by next-generation projects like [[millenniumtng|MillenniumTNG]] to extract high-precision halo statistics from computationally constrained volumes [Hernández-Aguayo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2556H/abstract). While constructing analytic covariance matrices for fixed-amplitude simulations is non-trivial, effective mock generation algorithms such as EZMOCK can successfully recover them [Zhang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.3737Z/abstract).

A related variance reduction strategy leverages analytic control variates. By pairing expensive, small-volume [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] with matched, low-resolution gravity-only simulations, researchers can learn the galaxy-halo connection and extrapolate it to much larger volumes. This can enhance the effective volume of a hydrodynamical simulation by up to a factor of 100 in the non-linear regime and reduce measurement errors by factors of $5$ to $10$ [Hadzhiyska et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..38H/abstract), [Doytcheva et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..184D/abstract).

Another critical source of variance in precision statistics stems from the choice of (sub)halo identification algorithms; tools like HBT-HERONS versus traditional SUBFIND can yield measurable differences in fundamental derived properties such as the bound mass function and satellite radial distributions [Forouhar Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206932F/abstract).

To further accelerate cosmological parameter estimation, the community increasingly relies on simulation-trained emulators. These tools rapidly map cosmological parameters to observables—such as the non-linear matter power spectrum or [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|halo mass function]]—without the cost of full integrations [Lange et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.1870L/abstract), [Ramachandra et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107306R/abstract). However, the literature reflects ongoing tension regarding the robustness of these emulators to variations in [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]]. Disentangling the cosmological signal from the back-reaction of diverse baryonic feedback prescriptions (e.g., active galactic nuclei and supernova-driven winds) remains a difficult challenge at scales $k \sim 10 \, h\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$ [Gebhardt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4896G/abstract). To mitigate systematic uncertainties derived from these baryonic feedback models in large-scale structure tests, projects like [[bahamas-simulations|BAHAMAS simulations]] explicitly calibrate feedback efficiencies to accurately reproduce local observables, such as the present-day galaxy stellar mass function and cluster gas fractions [McCarthy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2936M/abstract). Advanced statistical frameworks, such as Cosmological Evidence Modelling (CEM), attempt to bypass the emulation of specific summary statistics entirely by computing the Bayesian evidence directly from simulations, marginalizing over complex galaxy-halo connection parameters during Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling [Lange et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.1870L/abstract).

Finally, modern machine learning architectures are transforming the creation of [[mock-galaxy-surveys-and-numerical-modeling|Mock Galaxy Surveys and Numerical Modeling]]. Score-based generative models and Transformer networks (e.g., CosmoGLINT) have been successfully trained to "inpaint" rich galaxy properties onto dark-matter-only simulations. These models reproduce the spatial clustering and intensity mapping statistics of full hydrodynamical runs at a fraction of the computational cost [Bourdin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240800839B/abstract), [Moriwaki et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250616843M/abstract).

## Open Questions and Exascale Frontiers

As [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]] transition toward exascale computing environments, they face a dual mandate: modeling vast cosmological volumes to suppress sample variance while achieving the fine resolution necessary to capture sub-galactic physics. Historically, computational constraints imposed a strict trade-off between volume and resolution. However, [[next-generation-cosmological-simulations|next-generation frameworks]] leveraging GPU acceleration, such as the CRK-HACC code, are actively being optimized for exascale platforms [Frontiere et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251121921F/abstract). This shift enables flagship suites like [[millenniumtng|MillenniumTNG]] to simulate large $(740\,\mathrm{Mpc})^3$ volumes while still maintaining a competitive baryonic mass resolution of $3.1 \times 10^7 \, \mathrm{M}_\odot$ [Pakmor et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2539P/abstract).

Despite these hardware advances, *resolution limits* and *numerical convergence* remain persistent challenges. In gravity-only simulations, extracting $1\%$ accuracy for the [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|halo mass function]] requires resolving halos with $\gtrsim 1000$ particles [Reed et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431.1866R/abstract). Furthermore, extracting reliable individual halo properties—such as spin or central density—exhibits systematic biases near the resolution limit, typically requiring at least $100$ to $400$ particles for robust subhalo identification and property measurement [Trenti et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711.1198T/abstract). For hydrodynamics, standard spatial refinement often under-resolves the multiphase circumgalactic medium (CGM), requiring targeted high-resolution techniques to accurately reproduce observed gas column densities [van de Voort et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482L..85V/abstract). Additionally, establishing formal numerical convergence across different hydrodynamical solvers—such as the differing shock-capturing and entropy-generation behaviors of Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH) versus Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR)—remains an ongoing pursuit benchmarked by collaborations like the [[agora-simulation|AGORA simulation]] project [Kim et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..210...14K/abstract).

A prominent open question involves the $\Lambda$CDM [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|small-scale controversies]]. The literature is currently strongly divided on whether these discrepancies stem from under-resolved baryonic physics or if they necessitate a departure from collisionless Cold Dark Matter. On one hand, energetic feedback from supernovae can drive rapid potential fluctuations that dynamically flatten central dark matter cusps into cores [Weinberg et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11212249W/abstract). On the other hand, alternative dark sector physics, such as Self-Interacting Dark Matter (SIDM) with cross-sections $\sigma/m \approx 0.1\,\mathrm{cm}^2\,\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ [Rocha et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.430...81R/abstract) or Fuzzy Dark Matter (FDM) exhibiting quantum pressure [Nori et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241109733N/abstract), also naturally produce constant-density cores without breaking large-scale structure constraints. Determining the exact relative contributions of baryonic feedback versus alternative dark matter physics is a central focus of specialized suites like AIDA-TNG [Despali et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250112439D/abstract), though the degeneracy between dark sector physics and baryon-induced core formation remains entirely unresolved.

To navigate the immense computational cost of full-physics runs in the exascale era, researchers are increasingly turning to the aforementioned machine learning techniques to map baryonic observables onto dark-matter-only fields. However, a critical open question is how to ensure these emulators remain robust to the systematic variations inherent in different [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]]. Future progress relies on calibrating these simulation-based emulators to map cosmological parameters to observables accurately, without absorbing the biases of the underlying subgrid choices [Lange et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.1870L/abstract), [Ramachandra et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107306R/abstract). To overcome current limitations in subgrid modeling entirely, upcoming endeavors like the COLIBRE project are prioritizing [[high-resolution-simulations|high-resolution]] modeling of the multiphase interstellar medium without utilizing an artificial pressure floor, tracking the non-equilibrium chemistry of hydrogen and helium to robustly capture the first principles of gas collapse [Schaye et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250821126S/abstract).
