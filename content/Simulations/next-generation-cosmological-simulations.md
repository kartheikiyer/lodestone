## Article

**Next-generation cosmological simulations** are state-of-the-art, high-performance computational models designed to track the co-evolution of dark matter and [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]] across cosmic history. Driven by the demands of precision cosmology, these models bridge an unprecedented dynamic range, simultaneously capturing the gigaparsec scales of [[non-linear-structure-formation|Non-linear Structure Formation]]—a regime traditionally dominated by gravity-only frameworks [Angulo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022LRCA....8....1A/abstract)—while also pushing to the sub-parsec scales of star formation and black hole accretion. To achieve this fidelity, [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] combine highly accurate $N$-body gravity solvers with advanced Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH) [Springel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ascl.soft04014S/abstract), moving-mesh [Kereš et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.2027K/abstract), mesh-free [Zhu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831...52Z/abstract), or Eulerian hydrodynamics. 

Because the fundamental physics of the interstellar medium operate well below the resolution limits of massive cosmic volumes, these codes rely on sophisticated [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] to phenomenologically represent unresolved processes like star formation and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback. While energetic feedback mechanisms successfully regulate star formation—reproducing the observed scaling relations of galaxy populations and resolving historical theoretical challenges like the [[overcooling-problem|Overcooling problem]]—discrepancies persist in how different [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]] implement these prescriptions. This leads to vastly different predictions for the spatial extent of galactic outflows and the survival of satellite substructures [Wright et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.3417W/abstract), [Garrison-Kimmel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471.1709G/abstract). Looking forward, the field is expanding to incorporate alternative physics—such as massive neutrinos and modified gravity—and increasingly leverages artificial intelligence to emulate complex hydrodynamics, enhance the resolution of cheaper [[matter-only-simulations|Matter-only simulations]], and generate robust synthetic skies for direct comparison with next-generation observational data.

## Historical Development and the Precision Cosmology Era

Before the advent of high-performance computing, the abundance and evolution of dark matter halos were primarily predicted using the analytic [[press-schechter-formalism|Press-Schechter formalism]] and its extended variants. To achieve greater accuracy, the field transitioned to numerical [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] treating dark matter as a collisionless fluid. This era was defined by flagship gravity-only N-body runs, most notably the Millennium Simulation, which established the standard for tracing large-scale structure and [[halo-merger-trees|Halo merger trees]] within the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm. Successive N-body projects rapidly expanded in both volume and [[mass-resolution|Mass resolution]], culminating in contemporary runs like the [[uchuu-simulation|Uchuu simulation]], which evolved 2.1 trillion particles in a $2.0 \ h^{-1} \, \rm Gpc$ volume [Ishiyama et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.4210I/abstract), and the Millennium-II simulation, which demonstrated exquisite convergence of dark matter statistics down to dwarf-galaxy scales [Boylan-Kolchin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1150B/abstract).

As observational cosmology entered an era of sub-percent precision, the limitations of gravity-only modeling became apparent. High-precision measurements of the halo mass function and matter power spectrum require highly accurate initial conditions; relying on the standard Zel'dovich approximation introduces unphysical transients that can specifically underpredict massive high-redshift halo abundances by up to $15-20\%$ at $z=1$ [McBride et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011AAS...21724907M/abstract). High-precision N-body models have also identified novel cosmological probes, such as turnaround density evolution, which can independently determine matter and vacuum density parameters in expanding volumes [Korkidis et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..87K/abstract). Consequently, modern high-precision simulations universally mandate Second-order Lagrangian Perturbation Theory (2LPT) to initialize particle displacements [Reed et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.431.1866R/abstract), [Garrison et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461.4125G/abstract).

More fundamentally, the field has undergone a paradigm shift from dark-matter-only to baryon-inclusive cosmology. Early numerical experiments identified foundational constraints to this approach; for example, two-body heating can cause the radiative cooling of gas to be unphysically suppressed if the dark matter particle masses are chosen to be too large relative to the baryonic elements [Steinmetz et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997MNRAS.288..545S/abstract). Overcoming these limits, baryonic physics is now recognized as a first-order systematic uncertainty that must be explicitly modeled to accurately interpret weak lensing and cluster surveys. Energetic feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei expels substantial amounts of gas from [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]]. This redistribution of baryons significantly suppresses the matter power spectrum on small scales ($k > 1-10 \ h/\text{Mpc}$) and systematically reduces halo virial masses, altering the halo mass function at the percent level [Castro et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.2316C/abstract), [Gebhardt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4896G/abstract). Calibration of the halo mass function in simulations like [[magneticum-simulations|Magneticum (simulations)]] explicitly demonstrates that these baryonic effects decrease the number density of massive galaxy clusters, introducing a systematic shift that must be accounted for in large-scale cluster surveys like eROSITA [Bocquet et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.2361B/abstract). Furthermore, this introduces a "back-reaction" effect where baryonic feedback not only redistributes gas but indirectly alters the dark matter profile itself, typically increasing central dark matter density via cooling while decreasing it at larger radii via expulsion [Gebhardt et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260106258G/abstract). The explicit definition of halo boundaries is also evolving in precision studies; for example, measuring the "splashback" radius in cluster outskirts provides a physically motivated halo boundary that correlates strongly with the growth rate and orientation of the central brightest cluster galaxy [Contigiani et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.2932C/abstract).

To capture these effects globally, the current standard is set by large-volume [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] such as IllustrisTNG [Nelson et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ComAC...6....2N/abstract), EAGLE [Crain et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.1937C/abstract), SIMBA, and Magneticum. At galactic scales, [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] like FIRE, [[nihao-simulations|NIHAO simulations]], and [[auriga-simulations|AURIGA simulations]] achieve parsec-scale resolution within targeted halos to self-consistently form realistic disk-dominated galaxies [Wang et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454...83W/abstract), [Grand et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467..179G/abstract). Pushing the boundaries further, the NEWHORIZON simulation reaches a $34\text{ pc}$ resolution over a large volume, successfully capturing the clumpy nature of star formation and the structural transition from turbulent, elliptical high-redshift galaxies to settled disks [Dubois et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...651A.109D/abstract). Similarly, the emerging COLIBRE suite introduces high-resolution modeling of a multiphase interstellar medium without an artificial pressure floor, incorporating non-equilibrium tracking of hydrogen and helium alongside dust grain evolution [Schaye et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250821126S/abstract).

## Numerical Methodologies: Moving-Mesh and Mesh-Free Hydrodynamics

In [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]], the evolution of the baryonic component is governed by the Euler equations coupled with Poisson's equation for gravity. Accurately solving these equations across the extreme density gradients of forming galaxies remains a primary challenge for [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]]. Historically, solvers were divided between Eulerian grid-based methods and Lagrangian Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH). Next-generation suites increasingly employ hybrid techniques to maximize dynamic range and minimize numerical artifacts.

**Adaptive Mesh Refinement**
Eulerian Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR) codes achieve high spatial resolution in collapsing regions by recursively subdividing grid cells. This approach captures shocks, fluid instabilities, and turbulent mixing in the circumgalactic medium with high fidelity. Because of its accuracy in diffuse environments, AMR is particularly effective in tracking the multiphase interstellar medium during the Epoch of Reionization [Rosdahl et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479..994R/abstract). Adaptive mesh refinement implementations have also expanded to include cosmic ray feedback, which has been shown to significantly alter cluster gas [[density-profiles|Density profiles]] by reducing the central density and enhancing gas density near the virial radius [Vazza et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421.3375V/abstract). However, traditional Eulerian meshes can suffer from Galilean non-invariance, which degrades accuracy in highly supersonic cosmological flows.

**Moving-Mesh Hydrodynamics**
To combine the continuous spatial adaptivity of SPH with the shock-capturing accuracy of Riemann solvers, codes like AREPO employ an unstructured, moving Voronoi tessellation [Kereš et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.2027K/abstract). By allowing the computational mesh to flow with the gas, AREPO drastically reduces Galilean invariance errors. In idealized comparisons, moving-mesh solvers form more extended, thinner, and smoother gaseous discs than traditional SPH, which often suffers from artificial viscosity that damps turbulent motions. AREPO also produces more efficient cooling flows, resulting in higher central gas densities and elevated star formation rates in massive haloes compared to standard implementations in [[gadget-simulation-code|GADGET (simulation code)]] [Kereš et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.2027K/abstract).

**Mesh-Free Methods**
Meshless Finite-Mass (MFM) and Meshless Finite-Volume (MFV) algorithms, implemented in [[gizmo-simulation-code|GIZMO (simulation code)]] and PKDGRAV3, offer another modern alternative [Zhu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831...52Z/abstract), [Alonso Asensio et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519..300A/abstract). These methods partition the fluid into discrete mass elements but solve the Riemann problem between interacting elements without constructing an explicit grid, rigorously conserving mass, momentum, and energy. When simulating the formation of Milky Way-sized galaxies, GIZMO preserves prominent spiral structures and turbulent motions. In contrast, standard SPH produces an artificially smoother rotation due to numerical viscosity, leading to a $\sim 20\%$ higher stellar mass and a $\sim 10\%$ lower gas fraction at $z=0$ caused by rapid, premature star formation in artificially dense cold gas clumps [Zhu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831...52Z/abstract). 

Beyond the gas solvers, modern suites also employ targeted techniques to artificially boost resolution for specific structural components. For example, the "Superstars" method computationally increases stellar mass resolution in a cheap way, substantially improving the sampling of stellar disks and halos in cosmological volumes without altering overarching global galaxy properties [Pakmor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722104P/abstract). Despite the fundamental differences in these hydrodynamical solvers, numerical discrepancies are typically sub-dominant to the uncertainties introduced by [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]]. Ultimately, the tuning of [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]] remains the primary driver of the global properties in simulated galaxy populations [Davé et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.3265D/abstract).

## Sub-grid Prescriptions and Baryonic Feedback

In [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]], an extreme dynamic range presents a fundamental computational challenge: while large-scale structures evolve over volumes spanning gigaparsecs, the physics governing star formation and supermassive black hole (SMBH) accretion operate on sub-parsec scales. To bridge this gap, modern hydrodynamical codes rely on [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] to represent unresolved baryonic processes phenomenologically.

**Star Formation and Stellar Feedback**
The conversion of cold gas into stars is typically modeled using a density threshold and a Schmidt-like scaling law. Below the characteristic "knee" of the galaxy stellar mass function ($M^\star$), star formation is primarily regulated by stellar winds and core-collapse supernovae [Crain et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.1937C/abstract). Feedback is injected into the surrounding interstellar medium either as thermal energy (often into a hot, diffuse phase where radiative cooling is inefficient) or as kinetic energy via two-phase outflows [Davé et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.3265D/abstract). High-resolution zoom-in simulations of major mergers further reveal a "bursty" mode of star formation characterized by significantly shorter gas consumption timescales—a crucial dynamical phase that standard-resolution cosmological volumes often fail to capture [Sparre et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.2418S/abstract). Recently, there has been a push to replace empirically tuned sub-grid recipes with pressure-regulated, feedback-modulated (PRFM) models derived from first-principles, high-resolution ISM simulations [Hassan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..151H/abstract).

**AGN Feedback and Large-Scale Calibration**
For massive galaxies (above $M^\star$), energetic feedback from active galactic nuclei (AGN) is strictly required to quench star formation and reproduce the observed abundance of massive ellipticals [Martizzi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1500M/abstract), [Crain et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.1937C/abstract). AGN energy injection is traditionally divided into two phenomenological regimes: a high-accretion "quasar" mode that couples thermal energy to the surrounding gas, and a low-accretion "radio" mode that drives powerful kinetic jets. Recent synthetic subgrid models like OBSIDIAN build upon this by incorporating spin-dependent radiative efficiencies across advection-dominated, quasar-like, and slim disc accretion regimes to more accurately reproduce global galaxy scaling relations [Rennehan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.4793R/abstract). Despite these advances, comparisons of AGN luminosity functions across major simulation suites reveal that while models generally match low-redshift data, their agreement with observations severely deteriorates at high redshifts, highlighting an area needing theoretical refinement for upcoming X-ray missions [Habouzit et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.3015H/abstract).

In projects like the [[bahamas-simulations|BAHAMAS simulations]], these subgrid models for stellar and AGN feedback are explicitly calibrated to match the observed galaxy stellar mass function and cluster gas fractions, ensuring the overarching matter distribution remains robust for large-scale structure cosmology [McCarthy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2936M/abstract). However, recent comparisons utilizing the FLAMINGO simulations stacked against kinetic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (kSZ) effect measurements suggest that observational data may actually prefer stronger feedback than what is predicted by models calibrated solely to standard cluster gas fractions [McCarthy et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241019905M/abstract). 

**Impact on Baryon Distribution**
Despite widespread agreement on the necessity of feedback, the specific scale and potency of gas expulsion vary dramatically between contemporary simulation suites. For example, the SIMBA and EAGLE simulations demonstrate that *stellar feedback* can drive potent outflows that reach out to $2-3 R_{\rm 200c}$ at low halo masses [Wright et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.3417W/abstract). In the high-mass regime dominated by AGN-driven outflows, the spatial extent diverges dramatically: while EAGLE's AGN outflows seldom reach beyond $2-3 R_{\rm 200c}$, SIMBA predicts extremely long-range outflows that push gas several times $R_{\rm 200c}$ away, reshaping the baryon distribution on massive scales [Wright et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.3417W/abstract). In fact, SIMBA spreads approximately $40\%$ of its baryons $>1 \ h^{-1}\text{Mpc}$ away from their initial locations [Ayromlou et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5391A/abstract), [Gebhardt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4896G/abstract). Conversely, the IllustrisTNG and [[astrid-simulations|ASTRID simulations]] models predict that gas recycling occurs much closer to the host galaxy, with only $\sim 10\%$ of baryons reaching similar distances [Gebhardt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4896G/abstract). Reconciling these differing models with large-scale clustering observations remains a central focus in the development of next-generation simulations.

## The Circumgalactic and Intergalactic Media (CGM/IGM)

The circumgalactic medium (CGM) and intergalactic medium (IGM) represent the critical interface governing the baryon cycle, regulating both pristine gas accretion from the cosmic web and the expulsion of metal-enriched gas via galactic outflows. Accurately capturing the thermodynamics of these dilute environments is a historic weakness of [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]]; because most codes concentrate resolution elements in high-density regions, the diffuse CGM is typically poorly resolved, leading to the artificial mixing and premature evaporation of cold gas clumps.

To combat this, next-generation [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] increasingly employ targeted spatial refinement strategies. Techniques such as Enhanced Halo Resolution (EHR) and the GIBLE suite selectively force high resolution throughout the entire virial volume [Hummels et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...882..156H/abstract), [Ramesh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.3320R/abstract). These models demonstrate that the CGM is not a uniform fluid but rather a "mist" of small, long-lived cool clouds suspended in a hot medium near the virial temperature. While the abundance of massive gas clouds appears numerically converged at extreme resolutions ($\sim 75\text{ pc}$), the size distribution of the smallest scale structures in the cold mist continues to increase with resolution, highlighting an ongoing convergence challenge [Ramesh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.3320R/abstract).

The spatial extent of the CGM is heavily dependent on feedback prescriptions. Because simulations like SIMBA predict highly potent outflows deep into the IGM, their "closure radius" $R_c$—the scale required to enclose the total cosmic baryon fraction $f_{b, cosmic}$—is significantly larger than in models like IllustrisTNG. Bridging the gap between individual halos and massive clusters, galaxy groups serve as crucial laboratories for testing environment-driven galaxy evolution. The Intragroup Medium (IGrM) represents a surprisingly complex and rich middle ground, requiring precise modeling of both energetic feedback and cosmological [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]] to reproduce observed X-ray scaling relations [Oppenheimer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Univ....7..209O/abstract). For galaxy groups and clusters, this closure scale can be parameterized universally as:
$$ \frac{R_c}{R_{200c}} - 1 = \beta(z) \left( 1 - \frac{f_b(<R_{200c})}{f_{b, cosmic}} \right) $$
where $\beta(z)$ is a redshift-dependent scaling factor, placing $R_c$ generally in the range of $1.5-2.5 R_{200c}$ [Ayromlou et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.5391A/abstract). Idealized comparative studies confirm that while galactic feedback dictates the multiphase structure of the inner CGM ($r \lesssim 0.5 R_{200c}$), the outer CGM is predominantly shaped by cosmological accretion and satellite interactions [Fielding et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903...32F/abstract).

Beyond the CGM, high-resolution models are required to probe the diffuse IGM. Precision modeling of the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest requires resolving the Jeans scale of the IGM while simultaneously capturing large-volume reionization topologies. Simulation suites like PRIYA and Sherwood-Relics utilize hybrid radiation-hydrodynamics schemes to capture the spatial fluctuations seeded by inhomogeneous reionization [Bird et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...10..037B/abstract), [Puchwein et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.6162P/abstract). These simulations demonstrate that patchy hydrogen and helium reionization imprint lasting thermal signatures on the IGM, creating distinct structural features in the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest, such as flat-bottom or double-dip absorption profiles [Puchwein et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.6162P/abstract).

## Cosmic Reionization and High-Redshift Observations

Simulating the Epoch of Reionization and the Cosmic Dawn requires the coupling of [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] with multifrequency radiation transport. Next-generation [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] aim to provide rigorous theoretical benchmarks to interpret emerging data from the *James Webb Space Telescope* (JWST) and upcoming 21cm intensity mapping experiments such as the Square Kilometre Array (SKA).

A critical objective in high-redshift cosmology is identifying the primary sources of ionizing photons and the precise timing of reionization. High-resolution radiation-hydrodynamical frameworks, such as the SPHINX suite, have demonstrated that stellar feedback and the detailed treatment of stellar populations are essential. Specifically, the inclusion of binary star systems dramatically alters reionization timelines; binaries exhibit a shallower decline in ionizing luminosity with age, yielding average global escape fractions of $f_{\rm esc} \approx 7-10\%$. Consequently, SPHINX models incorporating binaries achieve a $99.9\%$ ionized volume by $z \approx 7$, whereas single-star models fail to complete reionization by $z = 6$ [Rosdahl et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479..994R/abstract). Likewise, the Aurora project utilizes spatially adaptive radiative transfer to self-consistently model high-redshift galaxy formation and the complex metal enrichment of the ISM during reionization [Pawlik et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466..960P/abstract). 

On even larger scales, the ASTRID simulation explicitly tracks inhomogeneous hydrogen and helium reionization, demonstrating how these patchy, fluctuating radiation fields actually enhance early star formation, with recently reionized low-mass haloes at $z \approx 6$ exhibiting star formation rates up to 1.5 times greater than those that have not yet been reionized [Bird et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.3703B/abstract). To directly interface with JWST observations, large-scale projects like [[thesan|THESAN]] combine radiation-hydrodynamics solvers with established galaxy formation physics to provide a comprehensive view of the entire epoch [Garaldi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.3765G/abstract). Concurrently, the MEGATRON suite extends these efforts by modeling the transition from Population III to Population II stars utilizing a non-equilibrium thermochemistry network, directly predicting the spectral diversity observed by JWST at $z > 8$ [Katz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251005201K/abstract). Similarly, the AEOS project achieves an extreme $1\text{ pc}$ resolution in cosmological volumes to model star-by-star early chemical enrichment, explicitly capturing the complex transition from metal-free Population III to Population II stars in the early universe [Brauer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016366B/abstract).

Despite these successes, the literature remains in tension regarding the high abundance of UV-bright, massive galaxies observed by JWST at $z \gtrsim 10$. It is currently ambiguous whether this excess merely reflects incomplete subgrid physics models for early star formation, or challenges the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm. Alternative theoretical models, such as the introduction of cosmic strings as additional early gravitational seeds, have been simulated and show improved agreement with high-redshift UV luminosity functions while seamlessly converging back to standard $\Lambda$CDM predictions by $z \sim 6$ [Koehler et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241200182K/abstract).

Simulations are equally pivotal for predicting the 21cm signal from neutral hydrogen. Patchy, inhomogeneous reionization seeds large-scale temperature fluctuations that actively shape dwarf galaxy assembly and the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest. Theoretical modeling of the 21cm power spectrum is crucial to isolate the signal from bright foregrounds [Shekhar Murmu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221009612S/abstract). Furthermore, machine learning techniques applied to simulated 21cm maps demonstrate that upcoming tomographic data will have the statistical power to differentiate between galaxy-dominated and AGN-dominated reionization scenarios, even in the presence of realistic instrumental noise [Hassan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..333...47H/abstract).

## Small-Scale Challenges: Halo Substructure and the Core-Cusp Problem

The standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm successfully describes large-scale structure, but its predictions at sub-galactic scales rely heavily on the complex interplay between dark matter dynamics and baryonic physics. Two of the most persistent small-scale challenges in [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] are the core-cusp problem and the precise demography of dark matter subhaloes.

**The Core-Cusp Problem and Baryonic Feedback**
Collisionless $N$-body simulations consistently predict that dark matter haloes possess steeply rising central density cusps, typically modeled by the [[navarro-frenk-white-nfw-profile|Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) Profile]] or the [[einasto-profile|Einasto profile]]. However, observations of dwarf galaxies frequently indicate flattened central density cores. To self-consistently capture the complex physics of these systems, high-resolution radiation-hydrodynamical frameworks like the EDGE suite have been developed, successfully reproducing the observed scaling relations of dwarf galaxies emerging from the early universe [Rey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.541.1195R/abstract). Within such models, repeated, supernova-driven gas outflows induce rapid fluctuations in the local gravitational potential—so-called "baryonic breathing modes"—which irreversibly transfer kinetic energy to collisionless dark matter particles, thereby flattening the inner cusp into a core [Wang et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454...83W/abstract), [Sales et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6..897S/abstract). 

Alternatively, non-standard dark matter physics, such as Self-Interacting Dark Matter (SIDM) or Fuzzy Dark Matter (FDM), also predict the formation of central cores [Jiang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.4630J/abstract), [Nori et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241109733N/abstract). Distinguishing between baryon-induced cores and those formed by alternative dark matter is a major goal of next-generation simulations. To this end, the recently introduced AIDA-TNG project explicitly explores the joint, often degenerate, effects of IllustrisTNG baryonic physics and alternative dark matter models (such as Warm and Self-Interacting Dark Matter) on halo structure and galaxy sizes [Despali et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250112439D/abstract). For instance, massive cluster simulations featuring SIDM predict a distinct "wobbling" of the central brightest cluster galaxy—a harmonic oscillation within the dark matter core that peaks around $z = 0.25$ and does not occur in collisionless $\Lambda$CDM models [Harvey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919842H/abstract). Other alternative models, such as dissipative atomic dark matter (ADM), predict the formation of rotating "dark disks" and central bulges consisting of dark clumps. These structures significantly alter early star formation and baryonic distributions in Milky Way analogues [Roy et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954L..40R/abstract).

The literature, however, remains cautious regarding the boundary between physical core formation and numerical artifacts. Two-body scattering between unequal-mass stellar and dark matter particles can drive spurious energy equipartition, leading to an artificial expansion of galaxy sizes and cores [Ludlow et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488L.123L/abstract). Conversely, it has been argued that universal cuspy [[density-profiles|Density profiles]] themselves might be partially sustained by unphysical Fokker-Planck streams in standard smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) codes rather than pure physical reality [Baushev et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JCAP...05..042B/abstract). 

**Substructure, Tidal Stripping, and Tracking Algorithms**
The survival of dark matter subhaloes within galactic hosts constitutes another significant challenge. While dark-matter-only simulations predict an overabundance of surviving substructure, embedding a realistic centralized baryonic disc drastically alters the subhalo census. The enhanced tidal field from the central galaxy destroys nearly all subhaloes within $\sim 15$ kpc of the host center, reducing the inner subhalo count by a factor of 2 to 5 [Garrison-Kimmel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471.1709G/abstract). In extreme environments, intense tidal stripping during close encounters can even produce galaxies that are almost entirely deficient in dark matter [Moreno et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6..496M/abstract). Furthermore, cluster-scale models like the RomulusC simulation demonstrate that ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs) can naturally form via self-consistent environmental effects, such as ram pressure stripping, which quenches star formation and prevents subsequent morphological collapse [Tremmel et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AAS...23341604T/abstract).

Assessing the true abundance of substructure requires robust [[halo-merger-trees|Halo merger trees]] and tracking tools. Subhalo finders operating in configuration space (such as [[subfind|SUBFIND]]) versus phase space can yield bound mass estimates that differ by up to 75% at the high-mass end, complicating demographic predictions [Forouhar Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206932F/abstract). To combat premature satellite loss, codes like HBT+ have been developed as significant improvements; by employing history-space tracking, HBT+ successfully recovers resilient, massive subhaloes that traditional configuration-space finders typically lose near the host centers [Han et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474..604H/abstract). Similarly, advanced particle-tracking algorithms (e.g., Bloodhound and HBT-HERONS) have demonstrated that by improving tracking continuity through close pericentric passages, modern algorithms can extend the measured lifespan of subhaloes by $3-4$ Gyr, recovering more than twice the number of surviving satellites in the inner regions of Milky Way-mass haloes [Kong et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250310766K/abstract).

## Large-Scale Structure, Neutrinos, and Modified Gravity

Next-generation galaxy surveys, such as Euclid and the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), will map the large-scale structure of the Universe over unprecedented volumes. To fully exploit these data and avoid theoretical systematic errors, [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] must move beyond the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm to incorporate extended physics, including massive neutrinos, modified gravity, and general relativistic effects. Even within the standard paradigm, precision modeling of galaxy clustering faces severe systematic uncertainties; for instance, the velocity bias of galaxies in redshift-space distortion modeling remains a predominant challenge for accurate cosmological analyses [Wu et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.434.2556W/abstract).

**Massive Neutrinos**
The non-zero mass of neutrinos suppresses the growth of cosmic structure on small scales. Suites such as the [[massivenus|MassiveNuS]] simulations explore parameter spaces with the neutrino mass sum $\sum m_\nu$ ranging from $0$ to $0.6 \text{ eV}$ to quantify this suppression in the non-linear regime [Liu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JCAP...03..049L/abstract). Traditionally, simulating massive neutrinos required large numbers of tracer particles, which introduced significant shot noise. Recent algorithms bypass this computational bottleneck by employing a weak-field dictionary and a linear relativistic Boltzmann code, effectively absorbing the neutrino evolution into a time-dependent coordinate transformation. This technique allows for the rapid computation of the non-linear matter power spectrum to per-mille precision for neutrino masses in the range $0.1 \text{ eV} \leq \sum m_\nu \leq 0.3 \text{ eV}$ [Partmann et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JCAP...09..018P/abstract).

**Modified Gravity**
To test deviations from General Relativity, simulators have developed highly optimized codes, such as MG-GLAM, to run full $N$-body simulations of derivative-coupling models, including those with Vainshtein and Kmouflage screening mechanisms [Hernández-Aguayo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...01..048H/abstract). Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations have also been extended to include $f(R)$ modified gravity. These models demonstrate that while realistic disk galaxies can still form in a partially screened regime, $f(R)$ gravity substantially suppresses the neutral hydrogen power spectrum, offering a testable signature for upcoming radio continuum and intensity mapping surveys [Arnold et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NatAs...3..945A/abstract). 

**General Relativistic Effects**
As survey volumes expand to the scale of the cosmological horizon, Newtonian approximations in standard $N$-body simulations become insufficient. Observing galaxies on the past light-cone introduces local general relativistic effects, including Doppler contributions and gravitational potential projections. For example, local light-cone projection effects for squeezed shapes in the galaxy bispectrum at $z \sim 1$ can differ from Newtonian predictions by $\gtrsim 10\%$ when the short modes are $k \lesssim (50 \text{ Mpc})^{-1}$ [Umeh et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JCAP...03..034U/abstract). 

If left unmodeled, these relativistic projection effects can be mistakenly attributed to primordial non-Gaussianity. Neglecting lensing magnifications and light-cone effects introduces a bias of $>1\sigma$ in the recovery of standard cosmological parameters and the non-Gaussianity parameter $f_{\rm NL}$ [Viljoen et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...12..004V/abstract). Consequently, the integration of multi-tracer methods with horizon-scale simulations is now required to isolate these subtle relativistic signatures from cosmic variance, enabling potential detections with signal-to-noise ratios of $\sim 14$ in upcoming large-volume surveys [Alonso et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...814..145A/abstract).

## Data Accessibility, Synthetic Skies, and Observer Synergies

As [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|Numerical Cosmological Simulations]] generate petabytes of raw data, the field has increasingly embraced an open-science model through massive public data releases. Suites such as IllustrisTNG [Nelson et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ComAC...6....2N/abstract) and the FIRE-2 suite [Wetzel et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...44W/abstract), [Wetzel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250806608W/abstract) provide hundreds of terabytes of data, including raw particle snapshots, subhalo catalogs, and web-based APIs that alleviate the need to download entire datasets locally. To maximize the statistical utility of these releases, projects like the UNIT project explicitly employ variance suppression techniques to provide high-precision predictions for non-linear statistics in galaxy surveys, effectively increasing the survey volume far beyond the simulated box size [Chuang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487...48C/abstract). Similarly, the Skies & Universes repository hosts major gravity-only runs like the [[bolshoi-simulation|Bolshoi simulation]], [[multidark-simulations|MultiDark simulations]], and the [[uchuu-simulation|Uchuu simulation]] [Klypin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv171101453K/abstract), [Ishiyama et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.4210I/abstract).

The tremendous data footprint of next-generation runs necessitates a shift toward *in situ* analysis pipelines. Storing full 3D snapshots at a high temporal cadence is often prohibitive; consequently, modern codes increasingly generate light-cones directly during the integration. For instance, the extreme-scale Last Journey simulation generates particle light-cones *in situ* starting at $z \sim 10$ to facilitate the construction of synthetic skies without storing full periodic volumes [Heitmann et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..252...19H/abstract). Algorithmic advances such as dynamic zoom simulations (DZS) further optimize this process by dynamically degrading the resolution outside the light-cone surface. This method reduces the computational cost by up to a factor of two while maintaining sub-percent ($< 0.1\%$) accuracy for light-cone observables such as the halo mass function [Garaldi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.2685G/abstract).

A primary goal of these pipelines is the creation of multi-wavelength synthetic maps and synthetic catalogs to establish direct synergies with observational surveys. Because observers measure flux and redshift rather than 3D mass distributions, simulations must be "observed" through forward-modeling. The AGORA lightcone framework by Omori et al. (distinct from the galaxy comparison project of the same name) has been built on the MULTIDARK-PLANCK2 N-body box, for example, to create multi-component extragalactic skies that explicitly map cosmic microwave background (CMB) lensing, the thermal and kinetic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effects, the cosmic infrared background, and weak galaxy lensing directly onto the light-cone [Omori et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.5030O/abstract). To circumvent the computational cost of full hydrodynamics when generating these observer synergies, novel "baryonification" algorithms now allow dark-matter-only simulations to self-consistently predict gas pressure and temperature, facilitating direct comparisons with observables like the thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect [Aricò et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A.188A/abstract). Additionally, new frameworks like Hamlet-PM represent the state-of-the-art for producing constrained simulations of the Local Universe, employing forward-modeling of peculiar velocities to accurately reproduce our specific cosmic neighborhood [Valade et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260203699V/abstract).

## AI, Machine Learning, and Super-Resolution Techniques

Because exploring the vast parameter space of cosmology and [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] with full hydrodynamics is computationally expensive, the field has increasingly turned to artificial intelligence and machine learning. These techniques are primarily deployed to accelerate the generation of mock survey catalogs, enhance the resolution of computationally cheap simulations, and map complex baryonic physics onto underlying dark matter fields.

A prominent application is the development of super-resolution techniques, which employ architectures like Convolutional Neural Networks to upscale low-resolution dark matter density fields. By training on small-volume, high-resolution runs, these models learn to inject small-scale modes into the deeply non-linear regime. AI-assisted super-resolution models successfully generate high-resolution phase-space matter distributions by upsampling tracer particles by a factor of $512$ [Ni et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.1021N/abstract). This specific approach matches true high-resolution matter power spectra at the per cent level down to scales of $k \sim 10 \, h \, \mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$ and reliably reproduces halo substructures that cannot be resolved in the low-resolution input [Ni et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.1021N/abstract). Beyond upscaling, conditional generative models (such as score-based diffusion models) can directly synthesize 3D cosmic density fields without explicit N-body integration, accurately reproducing both matter power spectra and bispectra [Riveros et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250217087R/abstract). Additionally, Gaussian processes are frequently utilized as emulators to reconstruct the growth rate $f$ and the amplitude of matter fluctuations $\sigma_8$ from simulated survey data [Perenon et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PDU....3400898P/abstract).

To bypass the exorbitant cost of [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]], deep learning is also used to "paint" baryonic observables directly onto cheap [[matter-only-simulations|Matter-only simulations]]. Projects like CAMELS have generated thousands of hydrodynamic and N-body simulations specifically to serve as massive training sets for machine learning algorithms, allowing for rapid exploration of the joint cosmological and astrophysical parameter space [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...54V/abstract). In a similar statistical vein, the application of control variates has emerged as a powerful technique to obtain high-precision galaxy clustering predictions by selectively pairing expensive hydrodynamical simulations with their computationally cheap, low-resolution gravity-only counterparts, drastically reducing statistical noise without running massive full-physics volumes [Doytcheva et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..184D/abstract). 

Deep learning frameworks based on U-Net architectures have been successfully applied to map dark matter density fields to thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (Compton-$y$) and bolometric X-ray surface brightness maps for massive galaxy clusters. These networks can recover observable-mass scaling relations with average errors of $\sim 0.5\%$ [Caro et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241004229C/abstract). At the field level, models like EMBER-2 leverage data-driven approaches to learn the mapping between dark matter and neutral hydrogen (HI) density, reproducing the HI-dark matter cross-correlation to within $10\%$ accuracy down to scales of $k = 100 \, h \, \mathrm{cMpc}^{-1}$ [Bernardini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250705339B/abstract). Convolutional Neural Networks are also being deployed in suites like the DREAMS project to help disentangle the often degenerate effects of alternative dark matter physics and subgrid baryonic feedback on [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]].

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite the remarkable success of modern [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]], fundamental challenges remain in faithfully modeling galaxy formation across cosmic time. Future progress relies on bridging orders of magnitude in spatial scales, resolving algorithmic tensions, and adapting legacy software for the next generation of supercomputers.

**Numerical Convergence and Multi-Scale Physics**
While global statistics are broadly robust across suites, the internal structures of galaxies and the circumgalactic medium remain highly sensitive to resolution limits. The temperature and ionization states of halo gas in standard flagship suites are likely not fully converged, as ultra-high resolution techniques indicate a highly fragmented, small-scale phase structure in the CGM. Furthermore, numerical artifacts—such as spurious two-body heating from unequal particle masses or unphysical Fokker-Planck streams—continue to obscure our understanding of dark matter density profiles, necessitating cautious interpretation when analyzing central cores and satellite substructure.

**Self-Consistent Black Hole Dynamics**
Accurately capturing the orbital dynamics of supermassive black holes (SMBHs) is severely hampered by spatial resolution and gravitational softening. Low-mass black holes ($M_{\rm BH} \leq 5 M_{\rm DM}$) are particularly vulnerable to stochastic heating, preventing them from sinking to galactic centers realistically without new subgrid corrections calibrated directly to high-resolution, unsoftened $N$-body simulations [Genina et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240508870G/abstract). The inclusion of nuclear star clusters in recent resimulations has been shown to dramatically accelerate the sinking of seed black holes via tidal forces, significantly increasing the predicted gravitational-wave merger rate at $z > 4$ [Mukherjee et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...981..203M/abstract). Moreover, the phenomenological nature of AGN feedback leads to vastly divergent predictions for large-scale matter clustering. Reconciling these models requires leveraging novel observational benchmarks, such as the kinetic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (kSZ) effect, which can successfully rule out excessively potent or weak feedback prescriptions that deviate from survey constraints [Bigwood et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251015822B/abstract), [Bigwood et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250116983B/abstract).

**Exascale Computing and Algorithmic Innovations**
To address the demand for multi-scale physics within gigaparsec-scale volumes, the field must aggressively pursue exascale computing. This transition requires a fundamental restructuring of [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]] to leverage massive concurrency and GPU acceleration, as demonstrated by emerging frameworks like CRK-HACC [Frontiere et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251121921F/abstract). Integrating these architectures with algorithmic innovations—such as dynamic zoom simulations and AI-assisted super-resolution—will be essential to fully exploit the statistical power of next-generation cosmological surveys at a computationally feasible cost.
