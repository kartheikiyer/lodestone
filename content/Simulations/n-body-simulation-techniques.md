**N-body simulation techniques** are a class of numerical algorithms used to model the gravitational dynamical evolution of discrete particles representing mass distributions in space. In astrophysics and cosmology, these methods are the primary theoretical tool for studying the non-linear regime of structure formation, from the orbital dynamics of dense star clusters to the hierarchical assembly of the cosmic web. The fundamental challenge of any N-body model is efficiently solving the Newtonian equations of motion for a large ensemble of mutually interacting particles. For dense, "collisional" environments where close stellar encounters are physically dominant, researchers rely on exact [[direct-summation|Direct summation]] to resolve precise orbital trajectories and two-body relaxation [Portegies Zwart et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&A...337..363P/abstract). However, because this approach scales computationally as $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$, modeling massive, "collisionless" systems like macroscopic dark matter halos requires mathematically approximating the long-range gravitational forces. This necessity has driven the development of highly efficient spatial-domain algorithms, most notably the [[tree-code-n-body-simulations|Tree code (N-body simulations)]] and grid-based [[particle-mesh-simulations|Particle-mesh simulations]]. 

Driven by continuous leaps in supercomputing hardware, modern [[numerical-simulation-codes|Numerical Simulation Codes]] have grown to encompass trillions of particles, firmly establishing the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm's predictions for large-scale structure and cosmic web topology [Lemson et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..8019L/abstract). To bridge the vast dynamic range between the expanding universe and the dense central cusps of galaxies, modern architectures frequently employ hybrid solvers, such as [[p3m-gravity|P3M gravity]], alongside adaptive [[softening-parameter|force softening]]. A critical finding across the literature is that maintaining physical, collisionless evolution requires strict attention to [[mass-resolution|Mass resolution]]; pushing spatial resolution below the mean interparticle separation can introduce severe numerical discreteness artifacts and unphysical kinetic heating [Splinter et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...497...38S/abstract). Beyond pure dark matter dynamics, translating raw particle distributions into observable galaxies requires extensive post-processing—such as extracting halo merger trees—and coupling the gravitational backbone to complex baryonic physics via direct hydrodynamics or semi-analytic models.

Today, the field is rapidly diversifying to meet the sub-percent precision demands of next-generation galaxy surveys. To generate the massive ensembles of mock catalogs required for accurate statistical covariance matrices, researchers have developed ultra-fast approximate solvers, such as the [[cola-simulation|COLA simulation]] framework, which trade exact small-scale internal halo dynamics for immense gains in computational speed. Concurrently, traditional techniques are being aggressively adapted to test non-standard cosmologies by incorporating modified gravity, massive relic neutrinos, and dynamic dark energy. Despite decades of algorithmic refinement, significant open questions remain. Current research continues to grapple with the intrinsic limitations of discrete macro-particles, the theoretical challenge of formulating fully relativistic N-body gauges, and the persistent difficulty of robustly capturing sub-grid baryonic feedback within collisionless mathematical frameworks.

## Foundational Numerical Algorithms

The fundamental challenge in any $N$-body simulation is efficiently and accurately solving the Newtonian equations of motion for a large ensemble of particles. Because gravity is a long-range force, calculating the exact pairwise interactions between all particles requires evaluating the full sum, a method known as [[direct-summation|Direct summation]]. While this approach scales computationally as $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ and is prohibitively expensive for large cosmological volumes, it is indispensable for dense, collisional environments such as star clusters and galactic nuclei. Modern direct implementations frequently employ a fourth-order [[direct-hermite-scheme|Direct Hermite scheme]] with hierarchical individual block time-steps to achieve the extreme precision ($|\Delta E/E| \gtrsim 10^{-14}$) required to resolve close stellar encounters and binary orbits [Berczik et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ascl.soft06027B/abstract), [Rantala et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.492.4131R/abstract).

To circumvent the $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ bottleneck in collisionless systems, the [[tree-code-n-body-simulations|Tree code (N-body simulations)]] approximates the forces from distant particle groups using multipole expansions, treating distant clusters as single massive pseudo-particles. This reduces the computational complexity to $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$. Modern variants utilizing $\mathcal{O}(N)$ algorithms, such as the fast multipole method employed in `gyrfalcON`, can accelerate these calculations by a factor of 10 compared to optimally coded traditional tree codes [Dehnen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ascl.soft02031D/abstract). Oct-tree implementations are also highly amenable to GPU acceleration and hierarchical time-stepping, which can further optimize the time-to-solution [Miki et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017NewA...52...65M/abstract). 

A distinct, grid-based alternative is found in [[particle-mesh-simulations|Particle-mesh simulations]] (PM). By assigning particle masses to a spatial grid, the Poisson equation $\nabla^2 \Phi = 4\pi G \rho$ can be solved rapidly using Fast Fourier Transforms (FFTs) before interpolating the resulting forces back to the particles. Pure PM methods can be 50 to 200 times faster than standard tree codes [Sellwood et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1406.6606S/abstract), but their force resolution is strictly limited by the grid cell size. To bridge this gap, hybrid algorithms such as [[p3m-gravity|P3M gravity]] (Particle-Particle Particle-Mesh) and TreePM calculate long-range forces on a mesh and short-range forces via direct summation or tree methods. Advanced implementations employ an adaptive [[softening-parameter|Softening parameter]] to maintain collisionless evolution in high-density regions [Bagla et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.2211B/abstract). For multi-scale problems, specialized hybrid codes can even couple a [[direct-hermite-scheme|Direct Hermite scheme]] for a dense star cluster with a background tree code for the parent galaxy [Fujii et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007PASJ...59.1095F/abstract).

Despite these algorithmic advances, intrinsic discreteness limitations remain a subject of active debate in the literature. High force resolution alone is insufficient for convergence if the [[mass-resolution|Mass resolution]] is inadequate. Systematic comparisons between PM, [[p3m-gravity|P3M gravity]], and tree codes have shown that they rarely agree on spatial scales below the mean interparticle separation, raising questions about the reliability of simulated dark matter clustering on sub-galactic scales [Splinter et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...497...38S/abstract), [Splinter et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997astro.ph.11105S/abstract). Furthermore, finite particle numbers can induce artificial two-body relaxation, driving spurious kinematic heating in disk galaxies and highlighting that even sophisticated N-body techniques are not entirely free from collisional artifacts [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...769L..24S/abstract). Conversely, high-resolution suites like the Cosmogrid simulation have verified the convergence of dark matter halo mass functions down to ultra-faint dwarf scales ($\sim 10^7 M_\odot$), demonstrating agreement with standard fitting functions [Ishiyama et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...767..146I/abstract). Modern iterations of standard solvers, such as GADGET-4, integrate group-finding, merger tree construction, and power spectrum estimation directly into the massively parallel execution pipeline [Springel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ascl.soft04014S/abstract).

## Historical Evolution and Hardware Specialization

The historical trajectory of N-body techniques has been fundamentally shaped by the interplay between algorithmic innovation and hardware capabilities. Early methods utilizing [[direct-summation|Direct summation]] were severely constrained by their $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ computational complexity, limiting dynamical investigations to small systems. Despite these early limitations, foundational dissipationless collapse simulations naturally produced density distributions matching the $r^{1/4}$ power law, providing a historical basis for the structure of elliptical galaxies [van Albada et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982MNRAS.201..939V/abstract). To overcome the computational bottleneck in the 1990s, researchers developed the GRAPE (Gravity Pipe) architecture, a series of specialized hardware pipelines designed explicitly to accelerate Newtonian pairwise force calculations. By hardwiring the gravitational force equations, these machines achieved unprecedented speeds; the GRAPE-4 system, for example, reached a sustained performance of 332 Gflops, enabling galactic simulations with nearly $8 \times 10^5$ particles at a highly efficient price-to-performance ratio [Fukushige et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996astro.ph.12090F/abstract). GRAPE hardware proved vital for resolving collisional dynamics where the [[softening-parameter|Softening parameter]] must be small or zero, revealing a significant discrepancy with Fokker-Planck models of globular cluster dissolution due to the breakdown of dynamical equilibrium assumptions [Portegies Zwart et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998A&A...337..363P/abstract), and successfully capturing the secular resonance exchanges in barred galaxies [Athanassoula et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003IAUS..208..177A/abstract).

In the cosmological regime, where collisionless dark matter dominates and exact small-scale collisionality is undesirable, the emphasis shifted toward maximizing simulation volume and [[mass-resolution|Mass resolution]]. This transition necessitated a move away from application-specific hardware toward massively parallel, general-purpose supercomputers executing hybrid algorithms like [[p3m-gravity|P3M gravity]] and the [[tree-code-n-body-simulations|Tree code (N-body simulations)]]. A watershed milestone in this era was the Millennium Simulation, which tracked $10^{10}$ particles in a $500\,h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ box with a spatial resolution of $5\,h^{-1}\mathrm{kpc}$ [Lemson et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph..8019L/abstract). The Millennium Simulation firmly established the robustness of the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm's predictions for large-scale structure and provided the foundational merger trees for modern semi-analytic models of galaxy formation. It was subsequently complemented by high-resolution resimulations, such as the [[aquarius-simulations|Aquarius simulations]] and Millennium-II, which extended dynamic range down to dwarf-galaxy scales [Boylan-Kolchin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1150B/abstract).

As high-performance computing advanced into the petascale era, extreme parallelization allowed for unprecedented cosmological volumes. The Horizon Run 3 expanded structured simulations to 374 billion particles across a $10.8\,h^{-1}\mathrm{Gpc}$ box [Kim et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011JKAS...44..217K/abstract), while "The Last Journey" simulation evolved 1.24 trillion particles on the BG/Q architecture [Heitmann et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..252...19H/abstract). The Horizon Run 5 exemplifies the move toward hybrid parallel architectures (MPI-OpenMP), resolving 1 kpc scales within a gigaparsec volume to study the coupled evolution of large-scale structures and galaxies [Lee et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908...11L/abstract).

Today, modern [[numerical-simulation-codes|Numerical Simulation Codes]] increasingly rely on GPU acceleration to manage these massive datasets. GPUs are uniquely suited for the highly parallelizable force computations inherent to both tree and [[particle-mesh-simulations|Particle-mesh simulations]]. The Farpoint simulation exemplifies this current hardware specialization, utilizing the GPU-accelerated Summit supercomputer to evolve nearly two trillion particles and achieving a mass resolution of $m_p \sim 4.6 \times 10^7\,h^{-1} M_\odot$ over a gigaparsec volume [Frontiere et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..259...15F/abstract). GPU implementation has also revitalized high-accuracy integrators for smaller, denser systems; codes such as GOTHIC combine oct-tree methods with hierarchical time stepping on GPUs for significant speedups [Miki et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017NewA...52...65M/abstract), while tools like phi-GPU deploy the 4th-order [[direct-hermite-scheme|Direct Hermite scheme]] directly on graphics hardware for star cluster evolution [Berczik et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ascl.soft06027B/abstract). Basis function expansion libraries like EXP provide a non-parametric bridge between theoretical dynamics and N-body realizations, allowing for the automated discovery of dynamical signals in complex multi-component systems [Petersen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JOSS...10.7302P/abstract).

Conversely, the sheer computational expense of running full N-body codes on advanced hardware has spurred the simultaneous development of fast, approximate spatial-domain methods. Frameworks like the [[cola-simulation|COLA simulation]] trade exact small-scale force resolution for massive speed gains, utilizing Lagrangian perturbation theory for large scales and restricting N-body solvers to small-scale non-linearities, thereby allowing researchers to rapidly generate the massive ensembles of mock catalogs required for modern survey covariance matrices [Tassev et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013JCAP...06..036T/abstract).

## Initial Conditions and Pre-Simulation Setup

Setting up an N-body simulation requires translating a continuous primordial density field into a discrete set of massive particles. If particles are placed randomly, Poisson noise introduces spurious power that can artificially dominate small-scale non-linear dynamics via nearest-neighbor interactions [Baertschiger et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002EL.....57..322B/abstract). Consequently, careful attention must be paid to generating initial conditions that accurately respect linear perturbation theory while strictly minimizing discreteness noise.

The standard procedure begins with an unperturbed particle distribution, often called a pre-initial condition. A regular Cartesian grid is simplest but introduces preferred spatial axes that can survive into the non-linear regime. A widely adopted alternative is a "Glass distribution", formed by evolving a random distribution with reversed gravity until the net forces on all particles vanish. While glass distributions are statistically isotropic, they are computationally expensive to generate. An alternative to both is the Quaquaversal tiling of space, a mathematically rigorous geometric construction that is statistically isotropic, trivial to construct, and yields a rapidly vanishing large-scale power spectrum, $P(k) \sim k^4$ [Hansen et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...656..631H/abstract).

Once the uniform pre-initial condition is established, density and velocity fluctuations are imprinted by applying a displacement field. The foundational approach relies on the Zel'dovich approximation (first-order Lagrangian perturbation theory, or 1LPT), which maps initial comoving coordinates $\mathbf{q}$ to Eulerian coordinates $\mathbf{x}$ via a displacement field $\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{q})$. However, 1LPT systematically underestimates the collapse of dense structures. Modern simulations therefore typically employ Second-Order Lagrangian Perturbation Theory (2LPT), which accounts for tidal non-local terms and non-linear mode couplings at early epochs. For even higher accuracy, Augmented LPT (ALPT) combines 2LPT on large scales with a spherical collapse approximation on small scales. This hybrid approach improves the correlation with full N-body results by roughly 25% and 75% at scales of $k = 1$ and $2 \ h \text{Mpc}^{-1}$, respectively, compared to standard 2LPT [Kitaura et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435L..78K/abstract). These Lagrangian perturbation techniques also serve as the dynamical backbone for fast approximate solvers like the [[cola-simulation|COLA simulation]] method [Tassev et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013JCAP...06..036T/abstract).

Despite the use of accurate higher-order perturbation schemes, representing a continuous fluid with discrete "macroparticles" fundamentally suppresses the growth of structure near the particle grid's Nyquist frequency, $k_{\rm Nyquist}$. Because the assumed continuum growing modes are not the proper growing modes of a discrete particle system, uncorrected simulations fail to reproduce linear theory on small scales. Explicitly rescaling the mode amplitudes in the initial conditions to account for this suppression, when combined with configuration-space 2LPT, allows codes to achieve 1% accuracy in the late-time power spectrum down to $k_{\rm Nyquist}$, a dramatic improvement over uncorrected 1LPT initial conditions [Garrison et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461.4125G/abstract). 

Finally, the precise knowledge of the initial displacement field can be exploited during post-simulation analysis. Because the initial Zel'dovich displacements correlate strongly with the final evolved density field, they can be utilized as control variates. Pairing the analytic Zel'dovich predictions with the non-linear N-body realization can reduce sample variance in power spectrum estimations by a factor of 100, allowing relatively small-volume simulations to yield highly accurate ensemble average properties [Kokron et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...09..059K/abstract).

Beyond generating statistically generic universes, researchers also construct constrained simulations—such as those in the ELUCID project—which reconstruct initial conditions specifically to reproduce the local cosmic web and galaxy distribution observed in surveys like SDSS [Wang et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..164W/abstract). Reconstructing these exact primordial density fields from present-day observations can be achieved iteratively using N-body dynamics or perturbation theory; frameworks like the No Action Method (NoAM) reconstruct the past orbits of tracers to determine precise initial conditions [Keselman et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467.1915K/abstract). Beyond cosmological volumes, stable, self-consistent initial conditions for multi-component galaxies (bulge, disk, halo) can be generated iteratively using tools like GalIC, which find stationary solutions to the collisionless Boltzmann equation by adjusting initial velocities [Yurin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ascl.soft08008Y/abstract). Furthermore, to analyze these initial conditions and subsequent evolutions, stochastic-geometrical techniques, specifically Voronoi and Delaunay tessellations, provide optimal, unbiased estimators for volume-averaged velocity and density statistics from discrete N-body snapshots [van de Weygaert et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998pcls.work..207V/abstract).

## Hybrid Methods and Adaptive Resolution

To simulate structure formation across a vast range of spatial scales, from the cosmic web down to the central cusps of dark matter halos, N-body codes face a fundamental trade-off between computational efficiency and spatial fidelity. Grid-based [[particle-mesh-simulations|Particle-mesh simulations]] (PM) are fast and naturally enforce periodic boundary conditions, but their spatial resolution is strictly limited by the grid spacing. Conversely, algorithms such as the [[tree-code-n-body-simulations|Tree code (N-body simulations)]] provide excellent spatial resolution in dense regions but carry a heavier computational burden. To bridge the gap between large-scale accuracy and small-scale resolution, modern codes employ hybrid strategies that partition the gravitational potential.

As introduced previously, hybrid approaches like [[p3m-gravity|P3M gravity]] (Particle-Particle-Particle-Mesh) split the force calculation into a long-range mesh calculation and a short-range direct summation. While highly accurate, P3M suffers from severe performance degradation in highly clustered environments; as structures collapse, the number of particles within the short-range calculation radius grows, causing the computational cost to scale locally as $\mathcal{O}(N_{\rm local}^2)$. To circumvent this bottleneck, the TreePM algorithm replaces the short-range direct sum with a tree traversal, preserving $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$ scaling and maintaining high performance even in deep, non-linear overdensities [Bagla et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.2211B/abstract).

However, achieving high force resolution is insufficient if it is not matched by adequate [[mass-resolution|Mass resolution]]. There is an active debate in the literature regarding the physical validity of N-body simulations at scales below the mean interparticle separation. High-resolution studies have demonstrated that pushing force resolution too far beyond the mass resolution introduces severe discreteness noise and artificial two-body scattering, which violates the collisionless Vlasov-Poisson equations. Consequently, codes can yield divergent results for structural properties at sub-interparticle scales, suggesting that scales below the mean particle separation are often unreliable for precise cosmological predictions [Splinter et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...497...38S/abstract), [Splinter et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997astro.ph.11105S/abstract).

To suppress this artificial collisionality, codes regularize the short-range gravitational force using a [[softening-parameter|Softening parameter]], $\epsilon$, typically modifying the potential to the form $$\Phi(r) = -\frac{Gm}{\sqrt{r^2 + \epsilon^2}}$$
In highly inhomogeneous cosmological volumes, utilizing a globally fixed force softening is a compromise: it may be too large to resolve dense halo cores, yet too small to prevent two-body relaxation in underdense voids. This tension has driven the development of adaptive resolution techniques. Algorithms such as the Adaptive TreePM (ATreePM) method dynamically reduce the softening length in high-density regions to resolve compact structures, while explicitly ensuring that $\epsilon$ remains safely above the local mean interparticle separation [Bagla et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.2211B/abstract). This adaptive approach successfully mitigates departures from collisionless evolution while maximizing spatial resolution only where the particle sampling supports it.

Hybrid methods are also deployed to bridge the collisionless dynamics of large-scale galactic environments with the highly collisional dynamics of dense sub-systems, such as star clusters. The BRIDGE algorithm, for example, combines a highly accurate [[direct-hermite-scheme|direct Hermite integration scheme]] for the internal dynamics of dense clusters with a standard tree code for the broader galaxy. These disparate techniques are coupled using a mixed variable symplectic (MVS) integrator, which preserves long-term Hamiltonian stability and seamlessly integrates self-consistent forces across vastly different dynamical timescales [Fujii et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007PASJ...59.1095F/abstract).

## Fast Approximate Methods for Mock Catalogues

To interpret observations from large-scale galaxy surveys, cosmologists require tens of thousands of mock catalogues to accurately estimate covariance matrices and assess systematic errors [Izard et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.2327I/abstract), [Angulo et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442.3256A/abstract). Because running full, high-resolution integrations for this volume is computationally prohibitive, recent advancements have focused on fast approximate methods that trade exact small-scale dynamics for immense gains in speed.

Many of these algorithms are rooted in Lagrangian Perturbation Theory (LPT). While standard Zel'dovich approximations and second-order LPT (2LPT) accurately predict large-scale bulk flows, they break down on non-linear scales due to shell-crossing. Augmented LPT (ALPT) mitigates this by splitting the displacement field: it uses 2LPT for long-range modes and a spherical collapse approximation for short-range interactions. This hybrid approach improves the correlation with full N-body simulations by roughly 25% and 75% at scales of $k = 1$ and $2 \ h\text{Mpc}^{-1}$, respectively [Kitaura et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435L..78K/abstract). Recent Eulerian extensions of this framework (eALPT) that incorporate viscosity and vorticity can achieve up to 95% cross-correlation with exact solvers at $k \simeq 1 \ h\text{Mpc}^{-1}$ [Kitaura et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...683A.215K/abstract).

A major paradigm shift in approximate solvers is the [[cola-simulation|COLA simulation]] (COmoving Lagrangian Acceleration) framework. COLA integrates large-scale dynamics analytically using LPT and restricts the computationally expensive discrete particle interactions to small-scale non-linearities [Tassev et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013JCAP...06..036T/abstract). By decoupling these regimes, COLA requires as few as 10 to 40 global time steps. This achieves speed-ups of up to three orders of magnitude over standard N-body codes, while successfully recovering the matter power spectrum to within 1% for $k \lesssim 1 \ h\text{Mpc}^{-1}$ and the halo mass function to within 5% [Izard et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.2327I/abstract), [Tassev et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013JCAP...06..036T/abstract). 

Other predictive approaches bypass traditional particle stepping entirely to populate mock universes. PTHALOS combines perturbation theory with virialized halo models to directly populate non-linear density and velocity fields, efficiently reproducing two- and three-point clustering statistics [Scoccimarro et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.329..629S/abstract). Similarly, PINOCCHIO (PINpointing Orbit-Crossing Collapsed HIerarchical Objects) uses ellipsoidal collapse and LPT to generate dark matter halo merger trees directly from the linear density field. It predicts merger histories with a typical accuracy of 20% without requiring a conventional force integration [Taffoni et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.333..623T/abstract).

Despite their extreme computational efficiency, approximate methods are subject to inherent tensions. While sufficient for large-scale structure clustering and baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) reconstruction, these methods structurally struggle to capture the internal dynamics and precise density profiles of dark matter halos. Consequently, they often require calibration against full N-body runs to guarantee sub-megaparsec accuracy. The literature notes explicit ambiguity in this calibration process: cosmological parameter constraints derived from different approximate methods can vary by up to 20% depending on whether the mock halo samples are calibrated to match the mass, number density, or clustering amplitude of the reference N-body catalogues [Lippich et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.1786L/abstract). 

To further circumvent finite volume limitations and improve the statistical power of these fast mock catalogues, researchers increasingly employ variance suppression techniques alongside approximate solvers. For instance, pairing N-body realizations with Zel'dovich control variates can reduce the sample variance in the predicted power spectrum by a factor of 100, allowing small-box simulations to yield highly accurate ensemble statistics [Kokron et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...09..059K/abstract). The UNIT project employs FASTPM to suppress cosmic variance in N-body realizations, achieving substantial improvements in the statistical power of two-point clustering predictions for large survey volumes [Chuang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487...48C/abstract). Furthermore, recent refinements to fast solvers include empirical corrections to the identified halo properties, ensuring that virial mass statistics in FastPM-like simulations match full N-body results to within 1% accuracy [Wu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.531.4944W/abstract).

## Post-Processing: Halo Finding and Merger Trees

To translate the raw particle distributions produced by cosmological simulations into astrophysical observables, the data must undergo extensive post-processing. The primary objectives are identifying gravitationally bound structures and linking them across cosmic time to track their assembly histories.

**Halo Finding and Substructure**
The foundational algorithm for identifying dark matter halos is the Friends-of-friends (FoF) method, which links all particle pairs separated by less than a specified spatial threshold, known as the linking length [Nurmi et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436..380N/abstract). While computationally efficient, standard FoF is susceptible to artificially merging distinct halos that are connected only by tenuous particle bridges [Jiang et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440.2115J/abstract).

To extract bound substructures (subhalos) that may host satellite galaxies, phase-space and spherical overdensity-based halo finders—such as SUBFIND or AdaptaHOP—are typically employed [Tweed et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...506..647T/abstract), [Jiang et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440.2115J/abstract). A halo finder identifies local density maxima within the FoF groups and performs iterative gravitational unbinding checks to isolate robust subhalos. Simulations with sufficiently high [[mass-resolution|Mass resolution]], such as the Millennium-II or the [[aquarius-simulations|Aquarius simulations]], demonstrate that these algorithms successfully recover a subhalo mass function that departs from a simple power law at the high-mass end [Boylan-Kolchin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1150B/abstract), [Angulo et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.399..983A/abstract). Alternative approaches, such as the Dhalo algorithm, enforce strictly hierarchical subhalo grouping to maintain individual halo identities even when their diffuse outer boundaries overlap [Jiang et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440.2115J/abstract). Regardless of the exact finder, relaxed virialized halos universally exhibit the characteristic Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) density profile:
$$ \rho(r) \propto r^{-1}(r+r_s)^{-2} $$
where $r_s$ is the scale radius [Primack et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.10248P/abstract). Beyond halo finding, the topology of the simulated cosmic web can be robustly quantified using Minkowski functionals; tools like MEDUSA compute these by triangulating isodensity surfaces derived from Delaunay tessellations [Lippich et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508.3771L/abstract). Effective N-body models allow for the post-processing assignment of mass fractions to different galactic components (e.g., dark matter vs. stars) from a single-component particle realization, optimizing the study of tidal stripping [Nipoti et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.4221N/abstract). Additionally, extracting intracluster light (ICL) remains a challenge, as different detection thresholds (surface brightness vs. binding energy) can alter measured ICL fractions by up to a factor of two [Rudick et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...732...48R/abstract).

**Merger Trees**
Tracing the hierarchical assembly of these halos over successive time steps yields merger trees. A halo finder is run on each discrete snapshot, and particle memberships are compared across adjacent snapshots to determine progenitor-descendant relationships. Robust tracking often relies on following the "cores"—the most tightly bound central particles—of merging halos rather than the bulk particle distribution, thereby preventing misidentifications when outer particles are tidally stripped [Bose et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512..837B/abstract), [Rangel et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200808519R/abstract).

Constructing merger trees from N-body outputs is notoriously complex due to the dynamical, transient nature of subhalos. Trees frequently suffer from non-monotonic mass histories or deblending artifacts, where subhalos artificially "disappear" into the dense background of a larger host and reappear in subsequent snapshots. Advanced post-processing pipelines must clean these catalogs by identifying and repairing fragmented evolutionary tracks to produce a continuous history [Bose et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512..837B/abstract), [Tweed et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...506..647T/abstract).

High-fidelity merger trees form the essential backbone for semi-analytic models (SAMs) of galaxy formation. By applying analytic baryonic prescriptions—such as gas cooling, star formation, and feedback—onto the merger trees, researchers can rapidly populate large dark matter volumes with galaxies [Croton et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..222...22C/abstract), [Jiang et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440.2115J/abstract). For exceptionally large covariance studies where generating full N-body trees is computationally prohibitive, fast approximate techniques like the [[cola-simulation|COLA simulation]] method [Tassev et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013JCAP...06..036T/abstract) or Monte Carlo algorithms tuned to high-resolution simulations (e.g., PINOCCHIO) can successfully generate synthetic merger trees and mock catalogs directly [Taffoni et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.333..623T/abstract), [Parkinson et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.383..557P/abstract).

## Incorporating Baryonic Physics and Feedback

While collisionless N-body solvers accurately capture the gravitational assembly of the dark matter cosmic web, realistic galaxy formation models must incorporate the complex, dissipative nature of baryonic physics. Bridging the gap between the dark matter backbone and observable galaxy distributions is typically achieved through either computationally efficient semi-analytic models (SAMs) or direct hydrodynamical simulations.

Semi-analytic models (SAMs) populate dark matter halos by extracting merger trees from purely collisionless N-body runs (such as the [[bolshoi-simulation|Bolshoi simulation]]) and applying parameterized physical recipes to govern gas cooling, star formation, and feedback [Croton et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..222...22C/abstract). Because SAMs do not solve the hydrodynamic equations directly, they can rapidly generate mock galaxy catalogues over cosmological volumes. However, predictions from these models are highly sensitive to their specific sub-grid implementations. For example, comparisons between the Durham and Munich SAMs demonstrate that while large-scale galaxy bias predictions agree to within $10\%$, their predictions for small-scale clustering can differ by an order of magnitude due to varying treatments of star formation and satellite galaxy mergers [Contreras et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.432.2717C/abstract). Furthermore, SAMs applied to standard N-body trees must account for the fact that collisionless simulations inherently neglect baryonic pressure. At high redshifts ($z \ge 5$), hydrostatic pressure during reionization can suppress the growth of dwarf galaxy halos by up to a factor of 2; neglecting this effect leads N-body simulations to overestimate both the halo mass and the baryon fraction in low-mass systems [Qin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.1318Q/abstract), [Qin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467.1678Q/abstract). 

To capture the internal dynamics of galaxies, many [[numerical-simulation-codes|Numerical Simulation Codes]] directly couple N-body gravity with Eulerian mesh or smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH). A major challenge in these simulations is the proper modeling of radiative feedback and thermal energy injection from supernovae. Standard sub-grid thermal feedback models often overestimate heating because they neglect the ionization potential energy reservoir of the gas. When propagating Sedov-Taylor shocks through a partially ionized medium, up to $50\%$ of the injected supernova feedback energy can be consumed by ionizing the gas rather than raising its temperature, severely altering the efficiency of the feedback [Vandenbroucke et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...771...36V/abstract). Some pipelines, such as GAMESH, address these complexities by post-processing N-body outputs to apply self-consistent radiative and chemical feedback simultaneously [Graziani et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.449.3137G/abstract).

Tracking the metallicity of the interstellar medium and subsequent stellar generations requires the integration of chemical evolution models. Libraries like CELib are often coupled to N-body/hydrodynamical codes to calculate the yields of various elements from Type Ia and II supernovae, asymptotic giant branch stars, and neutron star mergers, typically employing the Simple Stellar Population (SSP) approximation [Saitoh et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AJ....153...85S/abstract). To directly compare these physical models with telescope data, researchers employ stellar population synthesis to generate synthetic Color-Magnitude Diagrams (CMDs) from N-body particles based on their individual age and metallicity histories [Pasetto et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...545A..14P/abstract). Furthermore, specialized utilities such as GalevNB have been developed to convert fundamental stellar properties (mass, temperature, luminosity) directly into observational magnitudes and spectra, allowing for a rigorous, one-to-one comparison between simulated galaxies and observational surveys [Pang et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016RAA....16...37P/abstract).

Where full hydrodynamical simulations remain too computationally expensive, hybrid approximations offer a middle ground. Particle tagging is one such technique, wherein dark matter particles in an N-body simulation are selected based on their rank-ordered binding energy to represent stellar populations. This method has proven to be a remarkably faithful and robust approximation of self-consistent hydrodynamical simulations, successfully reproducing the spherically averaged density profiles of stellar halos without the extreme computational overhead of tracking the gas physics directly [Cooper et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.1691C/abstract). Ultimately, achieving convergence in any of these models—whether via SAMs, tagging, or hydrodynamics—remains heavily dependent on the [[mass-resolution|Mass resolution]] of the underlying N-body integration.

## Numerical Limitations and Convergence Challenges

Dark matter halos and stellar distributions are generally modeled as collisionless fluids governed by the Vlasov-Poisson equations, which represent the lowest order of the [[bbgky-hierarchy|BBGKY hierarchy]]. However, standard N-body algorithms approximate this continuous phase space using a finite number of discrete macro-particles. This fundamental approximation introduces non-physical numerical artifacts—most notably discreteness error and two-body relaxation—which place strict limits on the dynamic range and physical validity of the results. To ensure physical fidelity, careful attention must be paid to [[numerical-stability-and-methods|Numerical Stability and Methods]].

**Discreteness Error and Mass Resolution**
A common misconception in N-body modeling is that spatial resolution can be arbitrarily improved simply by reducing the [[softening-parameter|Softening parameter]]. In reality, [[mass-resolution|Mass resolution]]—dictated by the macro-particle mass and the mean interparticle separation—is just as critical for maintaining collisionless dynamics. A rigorous convergence study by Splinter et al. comparing [[particle-mesh-simulations|Particle-mesh simulations]], [[p3m-gravity|P3M gravity]], and [[tree-code-n-body-simulations|Tree code (N-body simulations)]] methods revealed that phase-insensitive clustering statistics, such as the power spectrum $P(k)$ and correlation function $\xi(R)$, frequently diverge on spatial scales smaller than the mean comoving interparticle separation [Splinter et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...497...38S/abstract). Consequently, results on sub-galactic scales must be treated with caution if they fall below this separation threshold, even if empirical studies show that global metrics like the Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile can be successfully fit to halos containing as few as $\sim 10^3$ particles [Primack et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph.10248P/abstract).

Furthermore, representing a continuous density field with discrete particles suppresses the linear growth of structure near the Nyquist wavenumber $k_{\rm Nyquist}$. Correcting for this requires advanced initial condition generation techniques, such as applying second-order Lagrangian perturbation theory (2LPT) alongside mode amplitude rescaling [Garrison et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461.4125G/abstract). To prevent unphysical scattering in highly overdense regions, some codes implement adaptive softening lengths that dynamically ensure the force resolution remains strictly above the local interparticle spacing [Bagla et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.2211B/abstract).

**Two-Body Relaxation and Artificial Heating**
The discrete nature of N-body systems inevitably drives two-body relaxation, an effect characterized by artificial energy and momentum exchanges during close particle encounters. While physically accurate for collisional systems like dense star clusters, two-body relaxation is a purely spurious numerical artifact in collisionless simulations. 

In galactic dynamics, this effect is highly disruptive to cold structures. Sellwood demonstrated that N-body disk simulations are subject to higher rates of collisional relaxation than widely assumed, causing artificial vertical heating that thickens the disk and flattens the velocity ellipsoid over time [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...769L..24S/abstract). Relaxation noise also complicates the study of secular evolution. During bar-halo interactions, discrete particle noise can randomly perturb trajectories, knocking them out of orbital resonances. This artificially suppresses the angular momentum transfer and resonant torques between the bar and the dark matter halo [Weinberg et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375..425W/abstract). To mitigate these effects, simulators sometimes employ multimass schemes, which populate dynamically "interesting" regions of phase space with low-mass particles to explicitly minimize shot noise and the local relaxation rate [Zhang et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.387.1719Z/abstract). To bridge particle-based and continuum methods, novel Monte Carlo approaches follow orbits in smooth basis-set expanded potentials while scaled two-body relaxation is applied as a local diffusion process [Vasiliev et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.3150V/abstract).

**Dynamical Instabilities**
Beyond two-body scattering, discrete N-body systems are also susceptible to specific global numerical and dynamical instabilities. For instance, spherical self-gravitating N-body models can experience the dipole ($l=1$) instability, which provides a natural, dynamically driven explanation for the development of lopsidedness and off-centered nuclei in otherwise symmetric galaxies [Dattathri et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...993..150D/abstract).

**Finite Volume Limitations**
At the opposite end of the spatial spectrum, finite simulation box sizes impose limitations on cosmological structure formation. Truncating large-scale density fluctuations removes long-wavelength tidal forces and delays the hierarchical merging process. This delay artificially enhances the survival rate of small structures, leading to an overestimation of the number density of low-mass dark matter halos near the scale of non-linearity [Bagla et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.370..993B/abstract). Ultimately, to verify that a result is physically meaningful rather than numerically induced, a comprehensive convergence study must demonstrate stability across simultaneous variations in both mass resolution and volume.

## Simulating Non-Standard Cosmologies

While traditional N-body techniques solve the Newtonian equations of motion within a $\Lambda$CDM framework, modern large-scale structure surveys require high-precision theoretical predictions for extended parameter spaces. This has driven the adaptation of [[numerical-simulation-codes|Numerical Simulation Codes]] to include the effects of massive neutrinos, dynamic dark energy, and modified gravity (MG).

**Massive Neutrinos**
The non-zero mass of neutrinos suppresses the growth of cosmic structure on small scales due to free-streaming. A computationally efficient approach to modeling this suppression is the analytic linear-response approximation. This method couples the linear evolution of the neutrino density field to the non-linear dark matter evolution, and has been used in large simulation suites (such as MassiveNuS) to constrain neutrino mass sums in the range $M_{\nu} \in [0, 0.6]$ eV [Liu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JCAP...03..049L/abstract). Hybrid grid-particle methods have also been deployed to explicitly resolve the clustering of massive neutrinos on megaparsec scales [Brandbyge et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010JCAP...01..021B/abstract).

Representing neutrinos as discrete particles in standard N-body algorithms is challenging because their high thermal velocities introduce severe numerical shot noise. To circumvent this, cosmological Vlasov-Poisson simulations integrate the six-dimensional Vlasov equation directly on a phase-space grid. This provides a purely collisionless, noise-free representation of neutrino free-streaming and clustering alongside cold dark matter. Such hybrid N-body/Vlasov simulations reveal coherent neutrino streaming motions and show that the effective local neutrino "temperature" around massive galaxy clusters can vary by several percent relative to the cosmic mean, depending on the absolute neutrino mass [Yoshikawa et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904..159Y/abstract).

**Modified Gravity and Relativistic Effects**
Theories beyond General Relativity, such as $f(R)$ gravity, DGP gravity, and general Horndeski gravity, typically introduce an extra scalar degree of freedom. This mediates a fifth force that enhances large-scale structure formation but must be suppressed in high-density environments via non-linear screening mechanisms (e.g., Vainshtein or Kmouflage screening) to remain compatible with Solar System tests.

To capture these dynamics, the standard Newtonian Poisson equation must be replaced or augmented. For example, DGP and related higher-dimensional models can be simulated by treating the longitudinal mode of massive gravitons as a density-dependent modification to the gravitational potential [Khoury et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvD..80f4023K/abstract). For a broader class of models, the MG-GLAM code extends standard [[particle-mesh-simulations|Particle-mesh simulations]] by employing optimized multigrid relaxation techniques to solve the highly non-linear partial differential equations characteristic of both conformal and derivative coupling MG models [Hernández-Aguayo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...01..048H/abstract), [Ruan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JCAP...05..018R/abstract).

Furthermore, fully relativistic effects can be consistently included in Newtonian N-body frameworks by utilizing the N-body gauge. In Horndeski gravity, the dynamical nature of the scalar field introduces purely relativistic features at large scales. By using the quasi-static approximation to separate small-scale MG modifications from large-scale relativistic contributions, standard N-body simulations can be modified to predict novel large-scale features. Notably, this framework predicts the emergence of gravity acoustic oscillations (GAOs) in the matter power spectrum at scales $k \sim 10^{-3} - 10^{-2} \mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$ [Brando et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...09..024B/abstract), offering a unique observational signature for next-generation surveys.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

As cosmological surveys target sub-percent precision, the demands placed on N-body simulations have increasingly shifted from simply scaling up particle counts to resolving complex, multi-scale physical processes. Current frontiers in the field are defined by the need to capture intricate baryonic physics, incorporate general relativity, and overcome fundamental numerical limitations, all while minimizing computational expense.

**Baryonic Feedback and Sub-grid Physics**
Accurately bridging the gap between collisionless dark matter dynamics and baryonic processes remains a formidable challenge. While pure dark matter runs successfully reproduce the cosmic web, small-scale accuracy requires robust sub-grid physics. Recent studies demonstrate that up to $50\%$ of supernova feedback energy can be consumed by ionizing gas rather than heating it—an effect traditionally omitted from sub-grid models that fundamentally alters the thermal energy-temperature relation [Vandenbroucke et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...771...36V/abstract). To avoid the computational expense of full hydrodynamics, researchers are developing gradient-based post-processing techniques that mimic baryonic feedback on the dark matter distribution [Dai et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018JCAP...11..009D/abstract), as well as continuously refining the coupling between N-body merger trees and Semi-Analytic Models (SAMs) [Croton et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..222...22C/abstract).

**Relativistic Effects and Modified Gravity**
As observational surveys achieve unprecedented volumes, the Newtonian approximations inherent in standard [[numerical-simulation-codes|Numerical Simulation Codes]] become a limiting factor. A major future direction is the incorporation of relativistic effects to model horizon-scale physics accurately. Recent theoretical frameworks utilizing the N-body gauge have successfully introduced relativistic corrections into Newtonian simulations. This methodology allows for the evaluation of generic modified gravity theories, such as Horndeski models, revealing purely relativistic signatures like gravity acoustic oscillations (GAOs) in the matter power spectrum at large scales ($k \sim 10^{-3} - 10^{-2} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$) [Brando et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...09..024B/abstract).

**Phase-Space Structure and Discreteness**
The fundamental discreteness of N-body particles presents persistent open questions regarding the validity of the simulated phase-space structure on small scales. There is an active debate over whether N-body algorithms reliably converge on spatial scales below the mean interparticle separation, or if they are dominated by artificial two-body relaxation and collisional heating [Splinter et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ApJ...497...38S/abstract), [Sellwood et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...769L..24S/abstract). Achieving sufficient [[mass-resolution|Mass resolution]] is as critical as force resolution to prevent these numerical artifacts. To circumvent particle noise entirely, novel approaches are integrating the six-dimensional Vlasov-Poisson equations directly. This collisionless fluid approach is proving particularly effective for resolving the subtle phase-space clustering of massive relic neutrinos without the discrete sampling noise inherent to traditional [[particle-mesh-simulations|Particle-mesh simulations]] [Yoshikawa et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...904..159Y/abstract).

**Approximate Solvers and Cosmic Emulators**
Finally, the necessity of computing thousands of high-fidelity mock catalogs to construct precise covariance matrices has driven the development of cosmic emulators and approximate solvers. Fast approaches like the [[cola-simulation|COLA simulation]] method can accelerate structure formation calculations by orders of magnitude while maintaining percent-level accuracy for large-scale clustering ($k \lesssim 1 h \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$) [Tassev et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013JCAP...06..036T/abstract), [Izard et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.2327I/abstract). To handle vast cosmological parameter spaces, the community is increasingly relying on cosmic emulators and large-scale simulation suites. A major milestone in this effort is the CAMELS project, which provides a massive public data release of thousands of N-body and hydrodynamic runs explicitly designed for machine learning applications in cosmology [Villaescusa-Navarro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...54V/abstract). By combining Latin hypercube sampling of high-fidelity N-body or hydrodynamic runs with Gaussian process interpolation, these emulators can predict complex observables—such as the Lyman-$\alpha$ 1D flux power spectrum—with $<1\%$ interpolation error [Bird et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023JCAP...10..037B/abstract). For specialized dynamical problems, fast approximate algorithms continue to replace full integrations; for instance, fast particle spray algorithms offer a computationally efficient alternative to full N-body runs for modeling the thin stellar streams emerging from disrupting globular clusters [Chen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJS..276...32C/abstract). At the extreme computational frontier, machine learning surrogate models are being deployed to dynamically bypass ultra-short timestep bottlenecks (e.g., supernova explosions) during integration, enabling the first generation of 300-billion-particle, star-by-star galactic simulations [Hirashima et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251023330H/abstract).
