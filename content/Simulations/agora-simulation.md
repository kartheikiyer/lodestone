The **Assembling Galaxies of Resolved Anatomy (AGORA)** project is a multi-code collaborative initiative designed to provide a standardized, high-resolution framework for comparing [[galaxy-models|galaxy formation models]] across diverse numerical platforms [Kim et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..210...14K/abstract). In the broader field of [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]], computational constraints historically forced a severe compromise between simulation volume and spatial resolution. Consequently, processes occurring below the resolution limit—such as star formation, radiative cooling, and stellar feedback—must be approximated using [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]]. Because different [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]] employ distinct hydrodynamical architectures (such as smoothed particle hydrodynamics, adaptive mesh refinement, and moving-mesh schemes) alongside varying subgrid implementations, it has traditionally been difficult to determine whether discrepancies in simulated galaxy properties arise from the underlying algorithmic solvers or from differing astrophysical assumptions. AGORA addresses this bottleneck by standardizing the input physics across participating codes, thereby isolating numerical artifacts from physical modeling. Note that the name AGORA is also used for a distinct multicomponent simulation suite designed for cross-survey science (such as cosmic microwave background lensing and the thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect) based on the MultiDark-Planck 2 N-body simulation [Omori et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.5030O/abstract).

To achieve this standardization, the AGORA collaboration mandates a common "physics package" and rigorously constrained initial conditions for all its experiments. This includes utilizing uniform metal-dependent radiative cooling and ultraviolet background models, as well as an iterative, multi-step calibration process to synchronize [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]] across platforms [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract), [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract). A central finding of the project is that when these parameters are strictly controlled in high-resolution [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]], disparate codes achieve remarkable convergence on macroscopic galaxy anatomy. Across different numerical schemes, the simulated galaxies consistently form rotationally supported disks at low redshift, follow similar stellar mass assembly histories, and naturally resolve the "missing satellite problem" by sufficiently suppressing star formation in low-mass dark matter subhalos. Ultimately, AGORA demonstrates that modern simulations are significantly more sensitive to variations in input astrophysics than to the intrinsic algorithmic differences of their hydrodynamics solvers. 

Despite this macroscopic consensus, the article below details several persistent tensions and open questions that define the frontier of [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]]. While the stellar properties of simulated galaxies converge, the properties of the circumgalactic medium (CGM) show significant code-to-code variation, marking the CGM as a highly sensitive discriminator for numerical diffusion and feedback coupling. Furthermore, minor discrepancies in the precise timing of halo assembly persist due to differing gravity solver time-stepping strategies. The following sections will explore the evolution of the AGORA project from isolated disk tests to cosmological zoom-ins, its ongoing expansion to simulate 'Cosmic Dawn' galaxies for James Webb Space Telescope comparisons, and future directions aimed at pushing [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] to sub-parsec scales to better model feedback mechanisms and the circumgalactic medium [Nagamine et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IAUS..373..283N/abstract).

## The AGORA Collaboration and Project Philosophy

The AGORA (Assembling Galaxies of Resolved Anatomy) project was established to address a fundamental bottleneck in [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]]: distinguishing whether discrepancies in simulated galaxy properties arise from differing astrophysical assumptions or from the underlying numerical hydrodynamics schemes [Kim et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..210...14K/abstract). Historically, the field often relied on results from a single sophisticated code as a theoretical benchmark. The AGORA project represents a philosophical shift toward a multi-code collaboration, mandating that physical findings must be demonstrable across diverse numerical platforms to be considered robust and predictive. 

To achieve this level of reproducibility, the collaboration utilizes a standardized framework that provides common initial conditions and a shared "physics package." This package standardizes processes such as metal-dependent radiative cooling, ultraviolet background heating, and stellar yields across all participating codes [Kim et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..210...14K/abstract), [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract). A cornerstone of the AGORA methodology is its rigorous, iterative calibration protocol, typically applied to [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] of a Milky Way-mass halo ($M_{\rm vir} \approx 10^{12} M_\odot$ at $z=0$) [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract). By strictly constraining these parameters, AGORA reduces the degrees of freedom that traditionally confound code comparison studies. This need for robust validation extends beyond the core AGORA framework; for instance, external simulation platforms, such as NEXUS, rigorously reproduce [[idealized-galaxy-models|isolated galaxy models]] reported in earlier studies to validate their setups for controlled galaxy evolution experiments [Tepper-García et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.535..187T/abstract). More broadly, Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations are fundamental for investigating how the complex interstellar medium in forming galaxies responds to energetic feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei [Valentini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206954V/abstract).

When this systematic calibration is applied across codes featuring different fundamental architectures—such as smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH), adaptive mesh refinement (AMR), and meshless finite-mass solvers (e.g., GADGET, ENZO, RAMSES, ART)—the macroscopic properties of the simulated galaxies converge remarkably well. The participating codes consistently produce rotationally supported disks at low redshifts, match expected mass-metallicity relations, and trace similar stellar mass assembly histories [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract), [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..125R/abstract). Ultimately, the AGORA experiments confirm that modern high-resolution simulations are significantly more sensitive to the choice of [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] than to intrinsic algorithmic differences in their hydrodynamics solvers [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract).

Despite these successes, explicit tensions remain in the literature, which AGORA openly documents. While global stellar properties converge, the codes continue to diverge in their predictions for the circumgalactic medium (CGM) [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract). Differences in numerical diffusion and the coupling of feedback energy to the gas lead to significant variations in the properties of the CGM, marking it as a primary discriminator for models and a critical frontier for future multi-code calibration.

## Participating Astrophysical Simulation Codes

The AGORA project encompasses a diverse array of [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]], deliberately selecting platforms that span the three primary numerical methodologies in computational astrophysics: Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH), Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR), and Moving-mesh codes (as well as meshless finite-mass schemes). By employing a broad spectrum of hydrodynamics solvers, the collaboration aims to isolate numerical artifacts from physical modeling differences in high-resolution [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] of galaxy formation.

Participating codes include the AMR frameworks Enzo, Ramses, and ART-I; the SPH codes Gadget (specifically GADGET-3), GASOLINE, ChaNGa, and GEAR; and the moving-mesh/meshless codes AREPO-T and GIZMO [Kim et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..210...14K/abstract), [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract). To ensure that any divergence in simulation outcomes is attributable to the underlying numerical architecture rather than parameter tuning, the participating groups utilize common initial conditions and a standardized astrophysical infrastructure. This includes the implementation of the Grackle library for metal-dependent radiative cooling and ultraviolet background modeling, as well as a rigorously enforced calibration process designed to synchronize [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] across all platforms [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract), [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract). 

When subgrid physics parameters—such as star formation density thresholds, supernova feedback energy, and metal yields—are strictly constrained, these diverse [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] demonstrate remarkable convergence. Tests at spatial resolutions of $\lesssim 100$ proper parsecs reveal that macroscopic galaxy properties are largely independent of the chosen numerical scheme. Both particle-based and mesh-based codes successfully produce rotationally supported galactic disks, consistent mass-metallicity relations, and similar stellar-to-halo mass ratios down to redshift $z=2$ [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract), [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..125R/abstract). For instance, specific solver comparisons between Gadget (SPH) and GIZMO (meshless finite-mass) demonstrate that while both produce extended rotationally supported disks, Gadget produces smoother rotation, whereas GIZMO resolves more prominent spiral structures and turbulent motions [Zhu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831...52Z/abstract). 

Despite this macroscopic consensus, the literature acknowledges that subtle, architecture-dependent discrepancies remain. Tensions are particularly evident in the properties of the circumgalactic medium (CGM) and low-density gas regimes, where the inherent differences in numerical viscosity and diffusion between SPH and Eulerian mesh codes alter gas mixing and the high-density tail of the gas distribution [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract), [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract). 

## Standardization of Subgrid Physics and Cooling

A fundamental objective of the AGORA project is to decouple the artifacts of numerical hydrodynamics schemes from the effects of astrophysical assumptions [Kim et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..210...14K/abstract). To achieve this, participating codes—spanning smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH), adaptive mesh refinement (AMR), and moving-mesh architectures—must adopt a strictly constrained physics package to govern processes operating below the simulation resolution limit.

Central to this standardization is the treatment of gas thermodynamics. The AGORA collaboration utilizes the Grackle library to provide a uniform implementation of metal-dependent radiative cooling, alongside a standardized, redshift-dependent ultraviolet (UV) background [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract), [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200104354R/abstract). Alongside the UV background and radiative cooling, other [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]]—including the Jeans pressure floor, star formation density thresholds, supernova feedback energy injection, and metal yields—are systematically synchronized across all participating simulation codes [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract).

To ensure consistency, the project employs a rigorous, four-step calibration protocol designed to minimize the number of freely tunable parameters. This sequence progresses iteratively through non-star-forming gas cooling tests, feedback-free star formation, and finally fully calibrated stellar feedback runs. In the final stage, codes target specific stellar mass assembly histories, aiming for convergence with [[semi-analytic-modeling|semi-empirical models]] down to $z \sim 2$ at spatial resolutions of $\lesssim 100$ physical parsecs [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract), [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..125R/abstract).

By enforcing these standardized [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]], AGORA has demonstrated that global galaxy properties are remarkably robust against the choice of hydrodynamical architecture, and are instead predominantly driven by the input subgrid physics [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract). Across diverse codes, the simulations converge on macroscopic features such as the formation of rotationally supported disks at low redshifts, stellar-to-halo mass relations, and mass-metallicity gradients [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..125R/abstract). 

However, the literature is explicit that standardizing subgrid physics does not entirely eliminate code-to-code variations. Significant discrepancies remain in predictions for the properties of the circumgalactic medium (CGM), which remains a key discriminator between different hydrodynamical solvers [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract).

## Evolution of the Project: From Isolated Disks to Cosmological Zoom-ins

The AGORA (Assembling Galaxies Of Resolved Anatomy) project was initiated to systematically disentangle the effects of numerical hydrodynamics schemes from astrophysical assumptions in [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] [Kim et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..210...14K/abstract). The historical development of the project follows a phased approach, beginning with foundational dark matter-only tests of a halo reaching a virial mass of $M_{\rm vir} \approx 1.7 \times 10^{11} {\rm M}_\odot$ at $z=0$, before advancing to complex baryonic physics [Kim et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..210...14K/abstract). 

The first major baryonic phase centered on an [[idealized-galaxy-models|isolated disk test]] of a Milky Way-mass galaxy. Nine participating [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]]—spanning smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH), adaptive mesh refinement (AMR), and moving-mesh architectures—adopted strictly constrained [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]]. These included a common Jeans pressure floor, uniform star formation thresholds, and identical supernova feedback energy parameters, alongside standardized radiative cooling and UV background heating provided by the Grackle library [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract). With spatial resolution sufficient to resolve the disk scale height, the codes demonstrated robust convergence (within a few tens of percent) on macroscopic properties like rotation curves, velocity dispersions, and star formation rates [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract). This isolated disk test established a critical consensus: modern high-resolution simulations are generally more sensitive to the choice of input physics than to intrinsic differences between numerical schemes [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract). However, subtle systematic differences remained, particularly in the high-density tail of the gas distribution (driven by numerical diffusivity) and in the mass function of newly formed stellar clumps, which varied by up to a factor of three [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract).

Building on the isolated disk framework, AGORA progressed to a cosmological zoom-in simulation of a Milky Way-mass halo ($M_{\rm vir} \approx 10^{12} {\rm M}_\odot$ at $z=0$) [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract). To manage the increased complexity of cosmological gas inflows and cosmic expansion, the collaboration developed the aforementioned multi-step calibration process targeting convergence at $z=4$ [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract). These [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]], which achieved spatial resolutions of $\lesssim 100$ physical pc at high redshift, successfully reproduced rotationally supported disks across varying code architectures down to $z=2$ [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..125R/abstract). To further connect varying resolution scales, researchers have developed novel merger-tree-based matching techniques to calibrate galaxy properties between low- and high-resolution runs, highlighting that subhalos in high-resolution simulations typically exhibit approximately $0.5$ dex higher stellar and gas masses [Jung et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...965..156J/abstract). Using AGORA initial conditions, high-resolution zoom simulations have demonstrated that compact, dispersion-dominated galaxies settle into rotationally supported, dynamically cool disks over a period of roughly 7-8 Gyr [Ceverino et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467.2664C/abstract). In this context, AGORA serves as a benchmark for the formation of 'quiet' disk galaxies at low redshift, contrasting with more violent, dispersion-dominated dwarf formation seen at high-redshift epochs (Cosmic Dawn) in related simulation projects like VELA and FirstLight [Ceverino et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019IAUS..344..468C/abstract).

Despite these successes, the literature explicitly notes ongoing tensions in the cosmological runs. While macroscopic galaxy-scale properties converge well, the properties of the circumgalactic medium (CGM) show noticeable code-to-code variations [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract).

## Stellar Feedback and Star Formation Modeling

A major objective of the AGORA project is to disentangle the effects of numerical hydrodynamics schemes from the astrophysical assumptions embedded in [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]]. To achieve this, AGORA enforces a strict standardization of the physical processes that regulate the interstellar medium (ISM) and govern the star formation rate (SFR) across [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|participating grid-based, smoothed-particle, and moving-mesh codes]] [Kim et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..210...14K/abstract).

Central to this effort is the rigorous calibration protocol designed to minimize unconstrained degrees of freedom [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract). By adopting a common "physics package"—which includes a standardized UV background, metal-dependent radiative cooling via the Grackle library, and common stellar initial mass functions—AGORA isolates the specific impact of stellar feedback and star formation efficiencies on galaxy anatomy [Kim et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..210...14K/abstract).

In these [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]], the ISM is typically stabilized against artificial fragmentation using a calibrated Jeans pressure floor, with star formation implemented via stochastic prescriptions tied to local gas density thresholds. AGORA experiments, such as the [[idealized-galaxy-models|isolated disk test]], demonstrate that global galactic properties—including rotation curves, gas surface densities, and the empirical Kennicutt-Schmidt relation—are highly reproducible across different code architectures provided the subgrid parameters are strictly controlled. Crucially, the aggregate properties of the simulated galaxies prove to be significantly more sensitive to the choice of [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]] than to the intrinsic differences between Eulerian or Lagrangian numerical schemes [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract).

The modeling of supernova energy injection remains a critical discriminator in these simulations. Purely thermal feedback energy is often radiated away too quickly in dense gas or expended on local ionization rather than mechanical heating [Vandenbroucke et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...771...36V/abstract). Consequently, codes participating in the AGORA framework increasingly rely on hybrid approaches. For instance, mechanical momentum injection is necessary to effectively disrupt dense star-forming clouds, while stochastic thermal heating generates the hot, metal-enriched outflows required to populate a multiphase circumgalactic medium (CGM) [Granizo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251218945G/abstract). 

Despite this convergence in macroscopic properties, such as the mass-metallicity relation and the formation of rotationally supported disks at $z \leq 2$ [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..125R/abstract), tensions persist at both the largest and smallest scales, including discrepancies in the extended CGM. Furthermore, on cloud scales (typically $<100\ \mathrm{pc}$), current feedback models often struggle to reproduce the observed spatial decorrelation between molecular gas and recent star formation (the so-called "uncertainty principle" of star formation) [Fujimoto et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.1717F/abstract). Resolving this rapid, cloud-scale decorrelation remains an open challenge, indicating that while macroscopic calibration in [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] yields consistent global galaxy properties, the precise nature of pre-supernova feedback in the local ISM requires further physical refinement.

## Code Convergence and Sensitivity Analysis

A primary objective of the AGORA framework is to isolate the effects of underlying numerical hydrodynamics schemes from the astrophysical assumptions embedded in [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]]. Historically, discrepancies between [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] were difficult to diagnose due to simultaneous variations in code architecture (e.g., smoothed particle hydrodynamics, adaptive mesh refinement, and moving-mesh methods) and subgrid physics. 

To address this, the AGORA collaboration employs a rigorous multi-step calibration protocol—progressing from non-star-forming cooling to fully calibrated stellar feedback—ensuring identical initial conditions and physics packages across diverse codes such as GADGET, ENZO, RAMSES, and ART [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract), [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..125R/abstract). With this strict calibration, participating codes achieve strong numerical convergence on macroscopic galaxy properties. Early benchmark studies established that while halo mass functions converge at low particle counts ($N \sim 30$), internal properties such as spin and density require at least 100 to 400 particles per halo to avoid significant numerical bias [Trenti et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711.1198T/abstract). Building on these benchmarks, the codes today consistently predict the formation of rotationally supported disks at low redshifts and reproduce fundamental scaling relations, such as the mass-metallicity relation [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..125R/abstract). 

A central finding of the AGORA project is that parameter sensitivity significantly outweighs algorithmic differences. Specifically, the macroscopic evolution of galaxies is much more sensitive to variations in [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]] than to the intrinsic differences between numerical schemes [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract). Furthermore, recent sensitivity tests have quantified intrinsic model variability due to randomness in frameworks like the [[auriga-simulations|Auriga galaxy formation model]]; these studies find that while global properties like stellar mass remain within 10% across realizations, instantaneous star formation rates and satellite orbits after their first pericenter are much more sensitive to stochastic subgrid noise [Pakmor et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543.1761P/abstract). When star formation and feedback are tightly constrained, distinct code architectures yield highly consistent global results. This cross-code reproducibility demonstrates that modern [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] are primarily limited by the accuracy of their physical models rather than hydrodynamical solver errors.

Despite this global agreement, probing structures near the simulation resolution limits reveals persistent tensions. The literature remains conflicting regarding the predictions of the circumgalactic medium (CGM). Even when global galaxy properties match, different numerical schemes and feedback implementations can produce varying CGM properties [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract). Additionally, variations in the precise timing of halo assembly and low-redshift mergers have been observed across codes in [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]]. These chronological and structural discrepancies are often traced back to differences in gravity solver time-stepping strategies and variations in early stellar feedback, which can still drive unphysical variations in halo assembly history despite rigorous subgrid calibration [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..125R/abstract). To facilitate community-wide use and independent analysis of these effects, the collaboration released the raw output data for the CosmoRun suite in 2024 (covering redshifts $z=8$ to $z=0$), including halo centers and merger trees .

## Galaxy Anatomy: Satellites and the Circumgalactic Medium

Recent efforts within the AGORA project have leveraged high-resolution [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] to investigate the detailed anatomy of galaxies and their immediate environments. By pushing numerical resolutions to $\lesssim 100$ proper pc, these studies stringently test how different hydrodynamic solvers and [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] govern the formation of satellite galaxies and the properties of the diffuse circumgalactic medium (CGM) [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract).

A major triumph of the AGORA framework is its cross-code demonstration regarding the abundance of satellite galaxies. In a cosmological zoom-in simulation of a target halo designed to reach a mass of $10^{12}\ M_\odot$ at $z=0$, eight participating codes—including ART-I, ENZO, RAMSES, CHANGA, GADGET-3, GEAR, AREPO-T, and GIZMO—were analyzed at $z \sim 2$ to compare satellite populations [Jung et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..123J/abstract). Across all platforms, the number of luminous satellite galaxies containing stellar particles was found to be significantly lower than the total number of dark matter subhalos. This consensus firmly demonstrates that the inclusion of standardized baryonic physics, such as stellar feedback and ultraviolet background radiation, naturally resolves the long-standing missing satellites problem [Kim et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJS..210...14K/abstract), [Jung et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..123J/abstract). 

Furthermore, the aggregate properties of both the central and satellite galaxies show strong convergence across the different numerical schemes. For instance, the simulated galaxies reliably reproduce fundamental scaling relations, including the stellar-to-halo mass relation and the mass-metallicity relation at intermediate to high redshifts [Jung et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..123J/abstract), [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..125R/abstract). The codes agree within $0.5$ dex on the mass-metallicity relation, successfully capturing the expected gas metallicity gradients derived from low-redshift observations and [[semi-analytic-modeling|semi-analytic models]] [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..125R/abstract). To help consistently map these distributions, researchers increasingly rely on $R_1$ (the $1\ M_\odot/\text{pc}^2$ stellar surface density contour) as a physically motivated size definition that is robust across different code architectures and redshifts, effectively tracing a galaxy's total dynamical mass [Arjona-Gálvez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A.301A/abstract).

However, while the macroscopic properties of the stellar disks and satellite populations show excellent agreement, the circumgalactic medium (CGM) remains a site of active tension. The CGM serves as a critical discriminator for galaxy formation models because its diffuse gas is highly sensitive to how feedback energy couples to the interstellar medium and escapes into the halo. AGORA comparisons reveal that even when global galaxy properties match, different numerical architectures yield noticeable discrepancies in the properties of the circumgalactic medium (CGM) [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract). To address these discrepancies, studies utilizing the GIBLE project have applied super-Lagrangian refinement in moving-mesh codes like AREPO to achieve ultra-high resolution (up to 75 pc) in the CGM of AGORA-like halos. These tests indicate that while the number of small cold gas clouds increases with resolution, the properties of massive clouds are well converged [Ramesh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.3320R/abstract). These deviations highlight that the CGM is strongly influenced by intrinsic code differences, such as numerical viscosity and the specific time-stepping strategies employed by gravity solvers, which can subtly alter the timing of halo assembly and minor merger events [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...968..125R/abstract).

Ultimately, by exposing both the robust successes—such as the resolution of the missing satellites problem—and the remaining tensions in the CGM, the AGORA project underscores that high-resolution [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] are achieving a mature, predictive consensus on stellar anatomy, while highlighting circumgalactic gas as the next frontier for precision code calibration.

## High-Redshift Frontier and JWST Comparisons

Following the launch of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), the discovery of unexpectedly luminous high-redshift galaxies has challenged existing theoretical paradigms of galaxy assembly during the Cosmic Dawn. To provide robust theoretical predictions to be validated against these new observations, the AGORA project was expanded to simulate the formation and evolution of galaxies at $z \geq 10$ [Kim et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251104435K/abstract). Utilizing high-resolution [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]], the collaboration targeted environments centered on dark matter halos with masses in the range $M_{\mathrm{halo}} = 10^{10} - 10^{11} \mathrm{M}_{\odot}$ at $z=10$.

A primary objective of this high-redshift frontier research is to determine whether standard [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]]—which are typically calibrated to match low-redshift observables—are sufficient to explain early-universe populations without invoking novel astrophysical processes. The AGORA multi-code comparisons (featuring codes such as Enzo, RAMSES, Changa, GADGET, and GIZMO) demonstrate that for massive halos ($M_{\mathrm{halo}} \geq 5 \times 10^{10} \mathrm{M}_{\odot}$ at $z=10$), these standard calibrations successfully reproduce the observed stellar masses, metallicities, and the UV luminosity function in the redshift interval $10 \leq z \leq 12$ [Kim et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251104435K/abstract). 

Despite these successes at $z \sim 10-12$, a significant tension remains in the literature regarding galaxies at even earlier epochs. Results from AGORA indicate that current models systematically underpredict the abundance, UV luminosities, and stellar masses of the most extreme galaxies observed at higher redshifts beyond $z \sim 12$ [Kim et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251104435K/abstract). However, other contemporary high-resolution [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]], such as [[thesan|THESAN]], have demonstrated success in matching these reionization-era JWST observations at those epochs [Garaldi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.3765G/abstract). Furthermore, while integrated macroscopic properties like total stellar mass converge well across different numerical platforms, internal properties such as gas-phase metallicity show notable code-to-code variations. This discrepancy highlights that the properties of high-redshift galaxies are highly sensitive to the specific numerical implementations of stellar feedback and gas cooling in dense, early-universe environments [Kim et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251104435K/abstract).

The modeling of dust attenuation also serves as a critical systematic uncertainty when interpreting the UV luminosity function during the Cosmic Dawn. AGORA tests reveal that while varying the assumed dust-to-metal ratio within reasonable bounds only modestly alters the predicted UV luminosities, the complete absence of dust significantly enhances the brightness of simulated high-redshift galaxies [Kim et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251104435K/abstract). This sensitivity underscores the necessity for explicitly resolved, multi-phase interstellar medium models to properly forward-model JWST observables and break the degeneracy between extraordinarily high star formation efficiencies and low dust obscuration in the early universe.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While the AGORA project has successfully demonstrated that standardizing [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] can resolve major discrepancies between numerical codes—such as converging on galactic stellar mass histories and solving the "missing satellite problem" [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract), [Jung et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..123J/abstract)—significant open questions remain. Future directions for the collaboration are largely defined by the need to incorporate increasingly complex physics while mitigating residual numerical artifacts at higher resolutions.

A significant frontier for the field involves integrating complex physics such as Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD). For example, independent studies have utilized the standard AGORA initial conditions to run MHD simulations of [[idealized-galaxy-models|isolated disk galaxies]], demonstrating how saturated magnetic fields regulate star formation and limit supernova bubble sizes [Robinson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.534.1420R/abstract). Furthermore, the inclusion of MHD is necessary to accurately model the self-regulation of the multi-phase interstellar medium (ISM) and the structure of galactic outflows, which are sensitive to magnetic pressure and turbulence [Robinson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.534.1420R/abstract). Previous AGORA calibrations have focused heavily on stellar feedback and ultraviolet background radiation to regulate star formation [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract). However, active galactic nuclei (AGN) feedback is widely recognized as essential for quenching star formation in massive galaxies at late times, and different numerical implementations of black hole accretion and feedback can dramatically alter the surrounding gas [Habouzit et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.1940H/abstract). 

Even with standardized stellar feedback, active debates persist regarding the circumgalactic medium (CGM). While participating codes typically converge on macroscopic properties like rotation curves and the stellar-to-halo mass relation, they diverge significantly in their predictions for the CGM's properties [Roca-Fàbrega et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917...64R/abstract). The literature suggests that the CGM acts as a critical "discriminator" for testing models, as different [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|Subgrid physics prescriptions]] can yield identical stellar populations but vastly different gas flow rates [Crain et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ARA&A..61..473C/abstract). Understanding and correcting solver-driven differences is a prerequisite for achieving true cross-code convergence.

Pushing toward higher spatial and mass resolutions introduces new challenges on the scale of molecular clouds. Current [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] frequently fail to reproduce the observed spatial decorrelation of gas and stars on scales of $< 100$ pc, often referred to as the "uncertainty principle" of star formation [Fujimoto et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.1717F/abstract). Resolving this discrepancy requires explicit modeling of the non-equilibrium, multi-phase ISM without heuristic pressure floors, demanding resolutions where individual supernova feedback channels (thermal versus kinetic) can be self-consistently disentangled [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251002432Z/abstract).

Achieving these physical and resolution goals necessitates a transition to Exascale computing. As simulations scale to unprecedented particle counts and incorporate complex MHD networks, memory and I/O bottlenecks become prohibitive. To address this, the community is developing in situ analysis interfaces, such as `libyt`, which allow for parallel runtime data processing of AGORA simulations without costly intermediate data storage [Tsai et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251211142T/abstract).
