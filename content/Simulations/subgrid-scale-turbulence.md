In computational astrophysics, **subgrid-scale (SGS) turbulence** refers to the theoretical and numerical modeling of [[turbulence-in-astrophysics|Turbulence in Astrophysics]] occurring at spatial scales smaller than the resolution limit of a simulation. Because astrophysical environments—ranging from megaparsec-scale galaxy clusters to sub-parsec star-forming clouds—span an immense dynamic range, modern [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]] cannot explicitly resolve the entire turbulent cascade down to the physical dissipation scale. Without subgrid parameterizations, kinetic energy injected at macroscopic driving scales is artificially dissipated by numerical diffusion when it reaches the grid limit, leading to unphysical thermodynamics and structural evolution. 

To circumvent this, SGS frameworks utilize analytical and phenomenological techniques, such as Large Eddy Simulations (LES) and dynamic Smagorinsky models, to explicitly track unresolved energy and turbulent pressure. By capturing the interaction between macroscopic flows and microscopic dissipation, these [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] preserve the correct multiphase structure, non-thermal pressure support, and elemental mixing properties of the simulated fluid. The explicit inclusion of SGS turbulence is critical for accurately modeling phenomena across diverse regimes, including the regulation of star formation in the interstellar medium (ISM), the amplification of magnetic fields via the [[small-scale-dynamo|Small-scale dynamo]], and the transport of cosmic rays.

## Fundamental Principles of SGS Turbulence

Astrophysical fluid dynamics involves an energy cascade from the macroscopic driving scales—such as galactic disks and cluster mergers—down to the microscopic dissipation scale where viscosity converts kinetic energy into heat. [[large-scale-compressible-motions|Kiloparsec-scale atomic gas filaments]] and superbubbles play a fundamental role in driving this multiscale hierarchy of structure, injecting energy that propagates from galactic scales down to subparsec star clusters [Zhao et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..240Z/abstract). Because grid resolution effectively truncates this cascade prematurely, explicitly modeling the subgrid regime is necessary to prevent artificial numerical diffusion from altering the macroscopic thermodynamics of the simulated environment [Rennehan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.3810R/abstract).

The fundamental principle of SGS turbulence modeling is to analytically or phenomenologically capture fluid-dynamical interactions below the grid scale. In a turbulent medium, the flow of energy between the driving and dissipation scales generally assumes a power-law form. The velocity power spectrum is described by $P_v(k) \propto k^{-n}$, such that the velocity dispersion on a scale $l$ scales as:

$$ \sigma_v(l) \propto l^q $$

where $q = (n - 3) / 2$. In the subsonic limit ($M \ll 1$), classical Kolmogorov theory yields $n = 11/3$ and $q = 1/3$. Conversely, in highly [[compressible-turbulence|Compressible turbulence]], which dominates supersonic environments like the interstellar medium and governs [[molecular-cloud-dynamics|Molecular Cloud Dynamics]], the cascade steepens towards the Burgers scaling of $n = 4$ and $q = 1/2$. Massive $4096^3$ simulations have demonstrated that while the pure velocity scaling remains universal, the density-weighted velocity spectrum varies significantly between different [[driven-turbulence|driving modes]], scaling as $k^{-1.74}$ for purely solenoidal driving and $k^{-2.10}$ for purely compressive driving [Federrath et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.1245F/abstract). Modern [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] must account for these varying spectral indices to correctly estimate the flux of turbulent kinetic energy across the grid scale [Schmidt et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...528A.106S/abstract). Recent ultra-high-resolution $10080^3$ simulations have further resolved this picture, identifying two coexisting kinetic energy cascades: a non-locally interacting supersonic cascade scaling as $k^{-2}$ and a locally interacting, highly magnetized subsonic cascade scaling as $k^{-3/2}$ [Beattie et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9.1195B/abstract).

Failing to capture appropriate physics has severe consequences in hydrodynamical simulations. In galactic contexts, omitting explicit stellar feedback allows gas to artificially "overcool." This leads to runaway disk collapse to unphysically thin scale heights and an overprediction of the global star formation rate by factors of $10$ to $100$ [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract). While explicit feedback prevents this catastrophic failure, accurately modeling the underlying subgrid turbulence provides an important secondary regulatory refinement; [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|subgrid turbulence prescriptions]] generally impart a smaller, $\sim 10\%$ alteration to the overall star formation rate by supporting the gas with non-thermal pressure [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract). Similarly, in the intracluster medium ([[icm-turbulence|ICM turbulence]]), major mergers inject significant kinetic energy at large scales. Proper treatment of the turbulent energy cascade is required to accurately capture the conversion of this turbulent kinetic energy into the turbulent dissipation and heat diffusion necessary to regulate cooling flows in cluster cores [Iapichino et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AIPC.1241..928I/abstract), [Angelinelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.495..864A/abstract).

To mitigate overdiffusivity issues, techniques such as Large Eddy Simulations (LES) employ SGS models—like the dynamic Smagorinsky model—to compute a locally appropriate turbulent diffusivity and overcome the limitations inherent to fixed-scale models [Rennehan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.3810R/abstract). These frameworks track the unresolved turbulent kinetic energy as an independent variable, allowing it to exert a turbulent pressure on the resolved scales, mix metals and entropy, and ultimately dissipate into thermal energy at the physical dissipation scale [Schmidt et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...528A.106S/abstract), [Braun et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442.3407B/abstract). By bridging the gap between the grid scale and the dissipation scale, SGS models ensure that hydrodynamical simulations recover a physically motivated energy cascade and a realistic, multiphase astrophysical medium.

## Historical Development and the Turbulence-Regulated Paradigm

Historically, the interstellar medium (ISM) was often conceptualized as a discrete multi-phase medium in strict thermal pressure equilibrium. Under this classical framework, the support of molecular clouds against gravitational collapse was widely attributed to static magnetic fields. However, as observational and computational capabilities expanded, this static view was superseded by the turbulence-regulated paradigm. Modern models demonstrate that the ISM is ubiquitously turbulent across a vast range of spatial scales. Consequently, it is now understood that supersonic turbulence, rather than static magnetic support alone, is the primary agent in star formation regulation and the structural evolution of the ISM.

A major theoretical breakthrough in this transition was the generalization of the discrete multi-phase model into a scale-dependent phase continuum [Norman et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...467..280N/abstract). In this modern framework, the ISM is not strictly segregated into distinct, uniformly distributed thermal phases; instead, interstellar turbulence creates a continuous spectrum of densities and temperatures. Because the turbulent pressure vastly exceeds thermal pressure—often by factors of $P_{\rm turb} \sim 10-100 p_{\rm th}$ [Norman et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...467..280N/abstract)—these traditional "phases" are recognized as scale-dependent manifestations of the underlying turbulent cascade. Recognizing these non-thermal pressure components—comprising turbulence, magnetic fields, and cosmic rays—is crucial, as their combined support can reduce star formation rates by an order of magnitude and significantly prolong gas depletion timescales [Birnboim et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.3678B/abstract).

The shift to a turbulence-dominated picture required adapting fluid dynamics to the highly compressible conditions of the ISM. In the limit of negligible compression (Mach number $\mathcal{M} \ll 1$), the velocity power spectrum follows the classical Kolmogorov scaling $P_v(k) \propto k^{-11/3}$. In contrast, the cold, dense phases of the ISM exhibit highly supersonic motions where [[compressible-turbulence|Compressible turbulence]] dominates. This drives the velocity spectrum toward a steeper, Burgers-like scaling approaching $P_v(k) \propto k^{-4}$, which directly shapes the [[log-normal-distribution|Log-normal distribution]] of gas densities observed in [[molecular-cloud-dynamics|Molecular Cloud Dynamics]]. 

Crucially, the turbulence-regulated paradigm highlights the dual role of supersonic flows. On large scales, turbulence provides global support against gravity, preventing the runaway collapse of galactic disks into ultra-thin scale heights [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract). On scales smaller than the local Jeans length, however, supersonic turbulent compressions trigger hierarchical gravitational fragmentation, generating the dense filaments and cores necessary for active star formation [Vazquez-Semadeni et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1202.4498V/abstract). 

To maintain this dynamic equilibrium and avoid unphysical numerical "overcooling," modern galaxy models require explicitly modeled stellar feedback (such as supernova explosions and winds) to continuously inject kinetic energy and drive the turbulence [Mac Low et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..315..339M/abstract), [Padoan et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...822...11P/abstract). While tensions persist in the literature regarding the exact partition of driving energy between stellar feedback and large-scale mass accretion or gravitational instabilities, the consensus remains that a robust, turbulence-regulated framework is essential for establishing realistic, self-regulated star formation laws across cosmic time.

## Numerical Implementations: LES and SGS Models

Astrophysical fluid dynamics spans an enormous range of length scales, from the megaparsec diameters of galaxy clusters to the sub-parsec scales of viscous and resistive dissipation. Because it is computationally infeasible to resolve this entire cascade, [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]] rely on subgrid-scale (SGS) models. Without explicit [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]], grid-based and mesh-free codes rely entirely on numerical diffusion to dissipate kinetic energy, which fails to capture the correct thermodynamics, non-thermal pressure support, and metal mixing of the unresolved fluid.

### Large Eddy Simulations and the Smagorinsky Model
The Large Eddy Simulation (LES) formalism formally filters the fluid equations, calculating the large-scale motions exactly while approximating the effect of unresolved scales via an SGS stress tensor. Recent reviews of LES methodology in astrophysics emphasize the essential role of explicit SGS models for capturing both energy dissipation and dynamo action in non-relativistic and relativistic MHD [Schmidt-Brückner et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250906801S/abstract). A ubiquitous choice for closing these equations is the Smagorinsky model, which relates the subgrid stresses to the resolved rate-of-strain tensor using a prescribed turbulent length scale. However, standard fixed-scale Smagorinsky models are notoriously overdiffusive in the presence of strong, non-turbulent shear flows. To correct this, dynamic Smagorinsky models have been developed that compute the turbulent diffusivity locally and dynamically. These dynamic models successfully curb overdiffusivity, improving the preservation of density contrasts and preventing excessive angular momentum transport in galactic discs [Rennehan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.3810R/abstract).

### Filtering Techniques and Compressibility
Isolating the turbulent field requires robust filtering techniques. Multi-scale filtering techniques have proven to be far more robust than fixed-scale filters for disentangling turbulent eddies from bulk accretion flows or laminar motions in complex environments, such as the intracluster medium ([[icm-turbulence|ICM turbulence]]) [Vazza et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...544A.103V/abstract), [Valdarnini et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874...42V/abstract). Furthermore, because astrophysical environments frequently host [[compressible-turbulence|Compressible turbulence]], modern SGS implementations employ [[wavelet-helmholtz-decomposition|Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition algorithms]] to separate the resolved velocity field into solenoidal (divergence-free) and compressive (curl-free) components [Vallés-Pérez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021CoPhC.26307892V/abstract). For highly compressible regimes, mixed closures—combining an eddy-viscosity formulation with a term nonlinear in the velocity Jacobian—have been shown to accurately predict the SGS turbulence energy and dissipation rate independent of the forcing mechanism [Schmidt et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...528A.106S/abstract).

### Hydrodynamic and Magnetohydrodynamic Solvers
The choice of hydrodynamic solver critically affects the capture of subgrid turbulent motions. For example, Meshless Finite Mass (MFM) solvers have been shown to capture the velocity power spectrum of decaying subsonic turbulence with high accuracy, performing well down to low Mach numbers ($\mathcal{M} \sim 0.01$) and offering better convergence and mixing than traditional Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH), though both exhibit comparable turbulent energy decay [Groth et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526..616G/abstract). For magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) formulations, tracking turbulence accurately on moving meshes requires specialized treatments. Implementations of Constrained Transport (CT) schemes on moving meshes successfully prevent the unphysical magnetic field growth that plagues divergence-cleaning methods (like Powell cleaning), ensuring that small-scale magnetic energy properly saturates at equipartition rather than growing unbounded [Mocz et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.463..477M/abstract). Tracking this dissipation accurately requires resolving the intermittent nature of cosmic turbulence, which is typically characterized by extrema in velocity shears that identify coherent structures of intense dissipation [Lesaffre et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Atmos..15..211L/abstract). Furthermore, in MHD formulations, Alfvén wave collisions act as a natural mechanism for dynamically generating current sheets, which serve as localized sites of energy dissipation in plasma turbulence [Howes et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..28H/abstract). Advancing computational methodologies have introduced deep learning frameworks trained on 3D MHD simulations, which can now predict the full 3D magnetic structure and Mach numbers of interstellar clouds directly from spectroscopic H I observations [Hu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990...76H/abstract).

### Specialized Astrophysical Frameworks
Several specialized mathematical formalisms have been tailored to specific astrophysical regimes:
*   **FEARLESS** (Fluid mEchanics with Adaptively Refined Large Eddy SimulationS) couples SGS turbulence modeling directly with Adaptive Mesh Refinement. By refining the mesh based on regional vorticity rather than simple overdensity, FEARLESS effectively captures turbulence in [[subgrid-clumping|strongly clumped media]], properly modeling entropy enhancement and unresolved flow mixing in cluster cores and outskirts [Iapichino et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AIPC.1241..928I/abstract), [Iapichino et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.3641I/abstract).
*   **MIST** (Multi-phase Interstellar medium, Star formation, and Turbulence) is a framework designed for galactic discs that tracks unresolved turbulence explicitly within a two-phase (cold clumpy and diffuse warm) interstellar medium [Braun et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442.3407B/abstract). The MIST framework dynamically couples the subgrid turbulent kinetic energy to supernova feedback and star formation, successfully reproducing typical turbulent velocity dispersions of $\sim 10 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ in star-forming regions.
*   **Dust and Metal Diffusion**: Explicit subgrid models for the turbulent diffusion of metals and dust can significantly alter grain size distributions and improve the agreement between simulated galaxies and observed extinction curves [Romano et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.1441R/abstract).
*   **Numerical Dissipation Tracking**: Rather than relying purely on explicit feedback injections, some modern approaches track subgrid-scale turbulence sourcing directly via local numerical dissipation, which allows for a physically motivated parameterization of star formation efficiency even at moderate resolutions [Semenov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241023339S/abstract).

### Numerical Conservation
Finally, the [[numerical-stability-and-methods|numerical formulation]] of the SGS equations dictates their physical fidelity. Recent literature highlights that entropy-conserving schemes vastly outperform strictly energy-based formulations in highly supersonic flows. By integrating a conservative equation for modified entropy rather than solving a non-conservative energy density equation, entropy-conserving schemes prevent the spurious generation of subgrid non-thermal energy at shocks, which can otherwise artificially alter galactic morphologies and star formation rates by $20-30\%$ [Semenov et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..261...16S/abstract).

## Supersonic Turbulence in the ISM and Star Formation

Supersonic turbulence is ubiquitous in the interstellar medium (ISM) and acts as the primary regulator of star formation and [[molecular-cloud-dynamics|Molecular Cloud Dynamics]]. For highly [[compressible-turbulence|supersonic turbulence]] (Mach number $\mathcal{M} \gg 1$), the velocity power spectrum typically approaches the Burgers scaling $P_v(k) \propto k^{-4}$. This corresponds to a velocity dispersion scaling of $\sigma_v(l) \propto l^{1/2}$, which accurately describes the observed kinematics of molecular clouds. Observationally, higher-order statistical moments, such as the kurtosis in HI column density maps, serve as valuable empirical indicators of this multiscale turbulent structure, even though they may not directly trace localized star-forming regions at current observational resolutions [Maier et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....152..134M/abstract). Furthermore, the ISM often exhibits a regime of "strong" magnetic turbulence where fluctuations exceed the mean field strength, leading to the formation of sheet-like or filament-like magnetic coherent structures that coincide directly with peaks in star formation density [Ntormousi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A.149N/abstract).

In [[high-resolution-simulations|High-resolution simulations]] of galaxy evolution, finite spatial resolution necessitates explicit stellar feedback (supernovae, stellar winds, and radiation pressure) to continuously heat and stir the gas. For instance, radiation pressure-driven Rayleigh-Taylor instabilities can drive supersonic turbulence sufficient to explain the observed velocity dispersions in massive protocluster gas clouds [Krumholz et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...760..155K/abstract). Without explicit stellar feedback prescriptions, gas rapidly loses thermal energy, leading to numerical "overcooling," runaway disk collapse, and an extreme overprediction of the star formation rate (SFR) by factors of $10$ to $100$ [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract). Explicitly modeling the injection of this feedback energy into subgrid turbulent modes alongside macroscopic feedback acts as a fine-tuning regulator, further refining the SFR by roughly $10\%$ and helping to maintain a multi-phase ISM that naturally reproduces the empirical Schmidt-Kennicutt law [Braun et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442.3407B/abstract), [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract). Advanced implementations further show that magnetic-field-dependent star formation recipes, calibrated directly to subgrid magnetized turbulence, can effectively suppress initial starbursts and lead to a shallower Schmidt law index compared to non-magnetized models [Girma et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.6779G/abstract). The estimated coupling efficiency of supernova kinetic energy into driving large-scale turbulence is roughly $1.5\%$, though the presence of magnetic fields can increase this efficiency by a factor of two to three [Iffrig et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...604A..70I/abstract). 

However, the primary origin of this turbulence remains the subject of an active debate in the literature. While many models rely on stellar feedback as the dominant driver, competing studies demonstrate that large-scale gravitational instabilities and mass accretion alone can sustain observed turbulent velocity dispersions and produce a universal isothermal turbulent cascade, even in the absence of stellar feedback [Goldbaum et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...814..131G/abstract), [Ejdetjärn et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514..480E/abstract), [Fensch et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...672A.193F/abstract). Observational surveys studying the evolution of galactic turbulence across cosmic time suggest that it appears driven primarily by stellar feedback, with the caveat that measured velocity dispersions in cold gas tracers differ significantly from those in warm ionized gas tracers [Rizzo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.273R/abstract).

The structural evolution of the ISM is highly sensitive to the exact mode of [[driven-turbulence|turbulent driving]], which is parameterized by $b = \sigma_{\rho/\rho_0} / \mathcal{M}$, where $\sigma_{\rho/\rho_0}$ is the standard deviation of the density contrast. Purely solenoidal (divergence-free) driving yields $b \approx 1/3$, while purely compressive (curl-free) driving yields $b \approx 1$. Numerical models tracking individual molecular clouds find that $b$ is highly variable in space and time, fluctuating rapidly due to cloud collisions and tidal interactions, with an ensemble average typically reflecting a mixture of modes ($b \sim 0.5 - 0.7$) [Jin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469..383J/abstract), [Körtgen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497.1263K/abstract). Compressive modes are particularly effective at seeding local overdensities, and an excess of compressive turbulence induced by galactic mergers can drive galaxies off the main sequence and trigger extreme starbursts [Renaud et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442L..33R/abstract).

The interplay of supersonic turbulence and gravity ultimately dictates the Initial Mass Function (IMF) of stars. Supersonic turbulence generates a hierarchical density structure characterized by a [[log-normal-distribution|Log-normal distribution]]. The combination of this scale-free turbulent density field and local self-gravity maps directly to the protostellar core mass function, naturally reproducing the high-mass (Salpeter) tail of the IMF [Guszejnov et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.4137G/abstract). In extreme, highly turbulent starburst environments, theoretical models incorporating turbulent Jeans mass fragmentation predict that the IMF peak shifts to lower masses and the high-mass tail steepens, potentially reaching a power-law exponent of $\alpha \approx -2.7$ [Chabrier et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...796...75C/abstract). However, this turbulence-dominated picture is highly environment-dependent. In low-metallicity environments like the outer Galactic disk or dwarf galaxies, turbulence alone is often insufficient to counterbalance self-gravity, suggesting that magnetic fields may dominate cloud support in such regions and in the early Universe [Lin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025NatAs...9..406L/abstract).

## Turbulence in the Intracluster Medium (ICM)

The intracluster medium (ICM) is a highly dynamic, weakly collisional plasma where large-scale turbulence is continuously driven by cluster-cluster mergers and gas accretion from the cosmic web [Iapichino et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AIPC.1241..928I/abstract), [Vallés-Pérez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.504..510V/abstract). Cosmological simulations indicate that major mergers inject the bulk of this kinetic energy, generating [[icm-turbulence|ICM turbulence]]. Beyond the dense cores of massive clusters, observations extending to the cool circumgalactic medium also exhibit widespread turbulent signatures. For instance, empirical evidence from the Cosmic Ultraviolet Baryon Survey (CUBS) demonstrates that turbulent velocity dispersion scales with cloud size as $b_{\rm NT} \propto l^{0.3}$, consistent with a Kolmogorov cascade on scales less than $1 \text{ kpc}$ in typical galaxy halos [Chen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...955L..25C/abstract). 

While early models suggested turbulence could account for $20\%$ to $30\%$ of the thermal energy budget in merging systems [Vazza et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...529A..17V/abstract), recent [[high-resolution-simulations|high-resolution studies]] aligned with XRISM observations find much lower maximum turbulent pressure fractions of $\lesssim 5\%$ even during major mergers, decaying to $\sim 2\%$ as the cluster relaxes [Perrone et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260106250P/abstract). The kinetic energy of these unresolved random motions provides significant non-thermal pressure support against gravity, which introduces a hydrostatic mass bias when inferring cluster masses from X-ray or Sunyaev-Zel'dovich observations. Studies utilizing high-resolution simulations show that this non-thermal pressure support typically ranges from $\sim 5\%$ in relaxed cluster cores to $\sim 15\%$ in the cluster outskirts [Angelinelli et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.495..864A/abstract). Accurately capturing this energy budget in numerical simulations requires robust [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] to account for the dissipation of turbulent kinetic energy that occurs below the grid scale, a process that directly impacts the core entropy profiles [Maier et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...707...40M/abstract), [Iapichino et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AIPC.1241..928I/abstract).

In the highly stratified environment of the ICM, buoyancy forces strongly oppose radial gas motions, leading to anisotropic turbulence. This effect is most pronounced in cluster cores where stratification is strongest, corresponding to low Froude numbers ($Fr \lesssim 0.1$) [Valdarnini et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874...42V/abstract), [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4408W/abstract). Consequently, the turbulent dissipation rate $\epsilon$ and the turbulent diffusion coefficient $D$ are heavily suppressed by stratification, scaling as $\epsilon \propto Fr$ and $D \propto Fr^2$, respectively [Wang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.4408W/abstract). This implies that accretion-driven turbulence is mostly tangential in the inner regions, only transitioning to isotropic [[compressible-turbulence|Compressible turbulence]] in the cluster outskirts [Vazza et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...544A.103V/abstract), [Valdarnini et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874...42V/abstract).

A major outstanding debate in cluster astrophysics concerns the role of turbulent dissipation in preventing the "cooling catastrophe" in cool-core clusters. Early theoretical models proposed that turbulent heating and heat diffusion could self-regulate cluster cores, balancing radiative bremsstrahlung losses [Kunz et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410.2446K/abstract), [Ruszkowski et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414.1493R/abstract). However, this view is in tension with recent high-resolution X-ray observations (e.g., from the Hitomi satellite) and localized simulations, which reveal that turbulent velocities are generally too low to act as the primary heat source [Bambic et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478L..44B/abstract), [Valdarnini et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874...42V/abstract). It is now broadly suspected that turbulent heating is subdominant to AGN feedback in regulating core temperatures, providing $\lesssim 10\%$ of the necessary heating rate [Mohapatra et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.4881M/abstract). Furthermore, the complex interaction of AGN jet feedback cycles with the surrounding gas introduces significant baroclinicity near the supermassive black hole, which plays a crucial role in seeding the initial enstrophy of the turbulent flow during these events [Wittor et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521L..79W/abstract). Additionally, the precipitation of cold gas via thermal instability is highly sensitive to the driving mode of the turbulence, with significant condensation occurring in purely-vertical driving regimes when the ratio of cooling to free-fall timescales is $t_{\rm cool} / t_{\rm ff} \lesssim 5$ [Wibking et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241003886W/abstract).

Beyond direct kinetic dissipation, turbulence profoundly alters thermal conduction. Temperature gradients in the ICM trigger kinetic microinstabilities, such as the heat-flux buoyancy instability (HBI) in cluster cores and the magneto-thermal instability (MTI) in cluster outskirts. Turbulence plays a crucial regulatory role here: modest turbulent velocities ($\sim 100 \text{ km s}^{-1}$) are sufficient to tangle magnetic field lines and suppress the HBI, preventing a thermal runaway and allowing conductive heating to stabilize the core [Parrish et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...712L.194P/abstract). Conversely, in the cluster outskirts, the efficiency of the MTI depends sensitively on plasma microphysics. Extreme whistler-wave suppression of thermal conductivity can completely "kill" MTI-driven turbulence and its associated small-scale dynamo, unless the instability is revived and sustained by external, merger-driven turbulent flows [Perrone et al. ## The Small-Scale Turbulent Dynamo and Magnetic Fields

In both galactic and intracluster environments, magnetic fields are ubiquitously observed at microgauss ($\mu$G) levels. The primary theoretical mechanism for amplifying weak initial seed fields to these strengths is the [[small-scale-dynamo|Small-scale dynamo]] (also known as the fluctuation dynamo), which operates below the integral scale of the turbulent cascade. In this process, unresolved subgrid turbulent motions convert turbulent kinetic energy into magnetic energy through the continuous stretching, twisting, and folding of magnetic field lines [Lazarian et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MmSAI..82..636L/abstract), [Donnert et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018SSRv..214..122D/abstract).

The small-scale dynamo is characterized by an initial kinematic phase of exponential magnetic field growth. During this regime, the amplification is overwhelmingly dominated by the stretching of magnetic field lines by turbulent shear, particularly at high Alfvén Mach numbers ($M_A$) . This amplification proceeds until the magnetic energy density approaches equipartition with the turbulent kinetic energy, at which point the Lorentz force exerts a dynamical back-reaction on the flow, leading to nonlinear saturation [Maron et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...603..569M/abstract), [Shabala et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.405.1960S/abstract). High-resolution simulations consistently show that the statistical properties and final strength of the saturated magnetic field are largely independent of the strength and topological structure of the initial seed field; any memory of the initial state is entirely lost once the dynamo saturates [Seta et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.2076S/abstract). In partially ionized environments, ambipolar drift plays a critical role in regulating the saturation of small-scale dynamos, allowing large-scale field growth to proceed by localizing magnetic noise into intermittent ropes [Subramanian et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.294..718S/abstract). Furthermore, the dynamo can operate exceptionally efficiently in non-stationary conditions; in a collapsing background like a star-forming cloud, turbulent dynamo action leads to super-exponential magnetic field growth that significantly exceeds standard flux-freezing predictions [Irshad P et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250319131I/abstract).

The efficiency of the dynamo and the scale at which magnetic energy is concentrated depend significantly on the [[magnetic-prandtl-number|Magnetic Prandtl number]], $\mathrm{Pm} = \nu / \eta$ (the ratio of kinematic viscosity to magnetic diffusivity). In environments where $\mathrm{Pm} \gg 1$, such as the hot, dilute plasmas of the intracluster medium, magnetic field growth is exceptionally fast, and magnetic energy preferentially accumulates at the small resistive scale [Maron et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...603..569M/abstract), [Cho et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...759...91C/abstract). During the kinematic phase, the magnetic energy spectrum is theoretically described by the Kazantsev spectrum, scaling as $E_M(k) \propto k^{3/2}$ and peaking at the resistive scale, though signatures of steeper Batchelor or Saffman spectra can emerge depending on the scale separations and the saturation state [Rieder et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457.1722R/abstract), [Brandenburg et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.3312B/abstract). Notably, recent kinetic simulations have demonstrated that this turbulent dynamo action persists efficiently even in weakly collisional or collisionless plasmas where standard magnetohydrodynamics begins to break down [Rincon et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PNAS..113.3950R/abstract), [Chirakkara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.544..764C/abstract).

Accurately capturing the small-scale dynamo in global simulations presents severe challenges for [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]]. Because the dynamo relies heavily on capturing velocity gradients at the viscous scale, the observed genesis and growth rates of magnetic fields are heavily dependent on numerical resolution and the choice of refinement strategy [Martin-Alvarez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513.3326M/abstract). For instance, [[high-resolution-simulations|simulations utilizing quasi-uniform grid refinement]] tend to produce much faster magnetic energy amplification than traditional quasi-Lagrangian adaptive mesh refinement, with the magnetic energy growth factor scaling proportionally to $\Delta x_{\mathrm{max}}^{-1/2}$ [Martin-Alvarez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513.3326M/abstract). 

Furthermore, the literature reveals ongoing debate regarding the impact of non-steady driving forces. While classical dynamo theory typically assumes stationary turbulence, recent analytical and numerical models suggest that the inherent non-stationarity of astrophysical turbulence (e.g., driven by episodic supernova explosions) can significantly enhance the large-scale electromotive force (EMF) and the overall dynamo efficiency, sometimes dominating over standard diffusive mechanisms by several orders of magnitude [Mizerski et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..251...21M/abstract), [Mizerski et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...660A.110M/abstract).

## Cosmic Ray Transport and Non-Thermal Feedback

Cosmic rays (CRs) operate as a highly collisionless fluid whose transport and confinement are governed by pitch-angle scattering off magnetic field fluctuations. In astrophysical environments, this scattering is intrinsically linked to unresolved subgrid-scale [[alfv-nic-turbulence|Alfvénic turbulence]] and fast magnetosonic waves. Recent theoretical models emphasize that fast modes often dominate the scattering rate, rendering the CR diffusion tensor highly environment-dependent [Evoli et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...782...36E/abstract). Furthermore, traditional [[quasi-linear-theory|quasilinear theory]] has been extended to include non-linear diffusion effects, which demonstrate that a finite parallel mean free path is maintained even when the particle Larmor radius exceeds the maximum scale of the background turbulence [Shalchi et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvD..80b3012S/abstract).

A critical component of CR transport is the streaming instability, through which CRs drifting faster than the local Alfvén velocity excite resonant Alfvén waves, effectively self-confining the particle population [Aloisio et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013JCAP...07..001A/abstract). However, in realistic multiphase media, this self-generated turbulence is continuously damped by background [[compressible-turbulence|Compressible turbulence]]. The efficiency of this turbulent damping is strongly parametrized by the local Alfvén Mach number, $\mathcal{M}_A$ [Holguin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.1271H/abstract). Regarding macroscopic transport, recent models show that the large-scale transport of streaming CRs is well described by superdiffusion in ISM turbulence [Sampson et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.1503S/abstract). Furthermore, superdiffusive transport has also been inferred from remote observations of energetic electrons at supernova remnant shocks [Perri et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PPCF...60a4005P/abstract). The equilibrium spectrum of the turbulence is ultimately set by a balance between CR wave excitation and non-linear Landau damping, which regulates the transition between advective and diffusive transport and determines the vertical extent of the galactic CR halo [Chernyshov et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...937..107C/abstract).

The confinement of CRs results in a macroscopic non-thermal pressure gradient that serves as a vital feedback mechanism for driving galactic winds. The efficiency of this feedback is highly sensitive to the subgrid treatment of the streaming instability and its suppression. While idealized super-Alfvénic streaming models predict strongly enhanced galactic outflows, the inclusion of realistic turbulent damping yields more spatially extended gas and CR distributions. In highly turbulent interstellar environments, the increased damping of CR streaming reduces the instantaneous mass-loading factor of the galactic wind while simultaneously allowing for an increased star formation rate [Holguin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.1271H/abstract).

Beyond galactic disks, unresolved turbulence is the primary agent for stochastic particle reacceleration in the weakly collisional intracluster medium (ICM). During major cluster mergers, the injection and subsequent cascade of turbulent kinetic energy generates [[compressible-plasma-turbulence|Compressible plasma turbulence]]. The interaction of relativistic particles with these compressive fast modes results in second-order Fermi acceleration, which can reaccelerate a seed population of electrons to GeV energies. Recent hybrid-kinetic simulations demonstrate that supersonic collisionless turbulence can efficiently accelerate ions to power-law energy spectra ($q \approx 2.5$) even in the absence of large-scale shock structures [Gootkin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250918374G/abstract). This mechanism naturally explains the Mpc-scale diffuse synchrotron emission observed as giant radio halos in merging galaxy clusters [Brunetti et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011JApA...32..437B/abstract), [Brunetti et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412..817B/abstract). Subgrid implementations of this process, typically utilizing Fokker-Planck solvers coupled to macroscopic MHD variables, must carefully account for the non-linear back-reaction of the accelerated particles on the turbulent cascade itself to accurately predict the resulting non-thermal emission spectra [Brunetti et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.412..817B/abstract), [Donnert et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.3564D/abstract). Recent pedagogical reviews caution against relying on simple Fokker-Planck or ad-hoc two-moment formalisms for CR transport in these complex simulations, advocating instead for the adoption of more rigorous CR-MHD equations to fully capture the dynamical feedback loop [Hopkins et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250907104H/abstract). Beyond clusters, strongly magnetized turbulence in AGN coronae, such as in NGC 1068, may serve as a primary site for non-resonant cosmic ray acceleration, naturally explaining the normalization and spectral shape of observed high-energy neutrino fluxes from these extreme environments [Fiorillo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974...75F/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite significant advancements, the accurate parameterization of unresolved fluid motions in [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]] remains fraught with theoretical and computational challenges. Achieving strict numerical convergence in SGS models is a persistent hurdle. For example, the growth rates and saturation levels of the [[small-scale-dynamo|Small-scale dynamo]] are highly sensitive to the underlying spatial discretization, often exhibiting marked discrepancies between quasi-Lagrangian adaptive meshes and uniform high-resolution grids [Martin-Alvarez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513.3326M/abstract). Furthermore, widely used closures like the dynamic Smagorinsky model can be overly diffusive in regions of non-turbulent shear [Rennehan et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.3810R/abstract). There is also an ongoing debate regarding the optimal formulation of SGS equations; traditional energy-based schemes are prone to spurious energy generation at shocks, whereas entropy-conserving formulations offer precise adiabatic evolution and better stability but require methods to appropriately capture numerically dissipated kinetic energy [Semenov et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..261...16S/abstract). To overcome parameterization limits, scientific machine learning, including neural ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and symbolic regression, is emerging as a powerful tool to enhance the accuracy of subgrid models by directly discovering the underlying physical equations for cloud-environment interactions from high-fidelity data [Tan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.537.3383T/abstract).

In weakly collisional environments such as the intracluster medium (ICM), capturing plasma microphysics remains a frontier challenge. In high-$\beta$ plasmas, turbulent stresses naturally induce pressure anisotropies that trigger fast kinetic microinstabilities, such as the firehose and mirror instabilities. These instabilities pin the pressure anisotropy to marginal levels, fundamentally altering the effective macroscopic viscosity and conductivity [Kunz et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JPlPh..86f9003K/abstract). A major open question is whether buoyancy-driven turbulence, specifically the magneto-thermal instability (MTI), can survive in cluster outskirts if heat transport is strongly anisotropic. Recent models suggest that whistler-wave suppression of thermal conductivity can severely dampen or entirely "kill" the MTI dynamo, drastically reducing the generation of [[icm-turbulence|ICM turbulence]] [Perrone et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...682A.125P/abstract), [Perrone et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240206718P/abstract). Bridging these kinetic microscales with macroscopic [[compressible-plasma-turbulence|Compressible plasma turbulence]] is an active area of research. Beyond baryonic matter, emerging research investigates turbulence in self-gravitating superfluids as a novel model for dark matter haloes, where [[inverse-cascade|inverse energy cascades]] may actively contribute to large-scale structure formation [Shukla et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250220006S/abstract).

In galactic environments, the interaction between turbulence and multiphase gas introduces extreme complexity. While modern theories rely on a scale-dependent phase continuum shaped by supersonic turbulence [Norman et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...467..280N/abstract), capturing stellar feedback accurately requires properly resolving [[subgrid-clumping|Subgrid clumping]]. The momentum and energy deposited by supernova explosions depend heavily on the unresolved density distribution, meaning that spherically symmetric subgrid blast-wave models often fail to capture the true spatial extent of feedback [Ohlin et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.3887O/abstract). Consequently, the literature remains sharply divided on whether global disc turbulence is driven primarily by this localized stellar feedback or by large-scale gravitational instabilities and accretion [Su et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471..144S/abstract), [Ejdetjärn et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514..480E/abstract).

Finally, resolving these theoretical tensions requires robust empirical validation from high-resolution spectroscopy. Observations by the Astro-H/Hitomi satellite—which measured surprisingly low turbulent velocities in the Perseus cluster core—have fueled an active debate over whether turbulent heating and dissipation are truly sufficient to balance radiative cooling and prevent a "cooling catastrophe," or if alternative heating mechanisms like AGN-driven shocks and cosmic ray transport must dominate [Bambic et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478L..44B/abstract), [Valdarnini et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...874...42V/abstract). Future high-spectral-resolution observatories are essential to separate line-of-sight bulk motions from true small-scale turbulent broadening, providing the data necessary to calibrate [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] across diverse astrophysical regimes. Additionally, novel observational techniques are emerging; for example, measuring radio-wave scattering from Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) is now suggested as a promising probe of extragalactic ISM turbulence on spatial scales many orders of magnitude smaller than those accessible via traditional emission-line widths [Simard et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210711334S/abstract). Pulsar scintillation observations have also revealed an abundance of turbulent structures and shocks within the Local Bubble, providing a high-resolution probe of small-scale plasma dynamics on au-scales and below [Reardon et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241021390R/abstract).
