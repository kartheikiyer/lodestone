## Article

**GADGET** (GAlaxies with Dark matter and Gas intEracT) is a widely used, [[parallel-computing-in-astrophysics|massively parallel]] [[numerical-simulation-codes|numerical code]] designed for high-resolution [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] and isolated galactic dynamics. As a foundational tool in [[computational-astrophysics|Computational astrophysics]], GADGET tracks the collisionless dynamics of dark matter and stars alongside the gas dynamics of the interstellar and intergalactic mediums [Faltenbacher et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.362.1099F/abstract), [Springel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ascl.soft04014S/abstract). To compute gravitational forces over vast dynamic ranges, the code employs a hybrid "[[p3m-gravity|TreePM]]" algorithm, which seamlessly couples a Barnes-Hut [[tree-code-n-body-simulations|Tree code (N-body simulations)]] for short-range interactions with Fourier-based [[particle-mesh-simulations|Particle-mesh simulations]] for long-range periodic forces. For fluid dynamics, standard GADGET utilizes Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH), a Lagrangian, mesh-free technique that represents continuous gas as a set of discrete tracer particles. This approach provides natural spatial adaptivity, ensuring high computational resolution in dense, collapsing regions like galactic cores without wasting resources on empty voids.

Because cosmological simulations cannot resolve the microphysics of individual stars or the multiphase interstellar medium from first principles, GADGET heavily relies on parameterized [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]]. These modules simulate unresolved processes such as radiative cooling, star formation, and energetic feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei (AGN), which are critical for preventing artificial overcooling and reproducing observed galaxy mass functions. The framework also supports advanced extensions like magnetohydrodynamics (SPH-MHD) to track the self-consistent amplification of cosmic magnetic fields from primordial seeds to observable micro-Gauss strengths [Dolag et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1678D/abstract). While traditional astrophysics textbooks often state that simulations of cosmologically representative volumes fundamentally lack the spatial resolution to capture the internal structure of individual galaxies, modern massively parallel runs using GADGET and its derivatives have pushed these limits. However, the claim that single gigaparsec-scale simulations can simultaneously resolve sub-kiloparsec internal galactic morphologies remains inaccurate; instead, projects like MillenniumTNG and IllustrisTNG use a suite of different volumes and resolutions to capture both regimes [Genel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.445..175G/abstract), [Hernández-Aguayo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2556H/abstract).

Over more than two decades, GADGET has evolved continuously to address both physical inaccuracies and computational bottlenecks, serving as the architectural scaffolding for other major [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]] like the moving-mesh code AREPO and the meshless code [[gizmo-simulation-code|GIZMO (simulation code)]]. A major historical focus of the code's development has been mitigating the artificial surface tension inherent to traditional density-based SPH, which artificially suppresses fluid instabilities and phase mixing compared to Eulerian alternatives. Active research is currently focused on adapting GADGET's synchronous domain decomposition to highly concurrent GPU architectures for [[next-generation-cosmological-simulations|Next-generation cosmological simulations]], untangling the exact relative contributions of magnetic fields versus AGN feedback in regulating cluster entropy, and implementing sub-grid dynamical friction to resolve the "final parsec problem" of supermassive black hole binary mergers.

## Historical Development and Evolution

Developed primarily by Volker Springel, the GADGET software suite has undergone continuous evolution over more than two decades, securing its position as a foundational tool for [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]]. 

The public release of GADGET-2 in the mid-2000s represented a watershed moment for [[computational-astrophysics|Computational astrophysics]]. It introduced an entropy-conserving formulation of smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH), which improved upon earlier hydrodynamics schemes by explicitly tracking entropy as an independent thermodynamic variable [O'Shea et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJS..160....1O/abstract). In this formulation, the pressure $P$ of a gas particle is determined by its density $\rho$ and an entropic function $A$ via the equation of state:
$$P = A \rho^\gamma$$
where $\gamma$ is the adiabatic index. This algorithmic choice strictly conserved both energy and entropy in non-shocking flows, allowing GADGET-2 to become a standard tool for modeling galaxy formation and large-scale structure.

While GADGET-2 was widely distributed, its successor, GADGET-3, was maintained primarily as an internal developer release. During the GADGET-3 era, the literature became increasingly focused on the numerical limitations of standard SPH. Multiple comparative studies demonstrated that the traditional GADGET SPH scheme suffered from spurious artificial surface tension, which suppressed phase mixing and hindered the accurate resolution of fluid instabilities, such as Kelvin-Helmholtz and Rayleigh-Taylor instabilities [Sijacki et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.2999S/abstract). While Eulerian and moving-mesh codes initially demonstrated superior fluid mixing [Kereš et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.2027K/abstract), developers successfully patched GADGET-3 with advanced SPH refinements—such as Wendland kernels, pressure-entropy formulations, and time-dependent artificial conduction—that effectively resolved these historical inaccuracies [Beck et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2110B/abstract). The GADGET-3 codebase consequently fractured into numerous specialized branches, including versions optimized with [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] and magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) to successfully model the micro-Gauss amplification of cosmic magnetic fields in both galaxy clusters [Dolag et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1678D/abstract) and individual Milky Way-like halos [Beck et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.3575B/abstract). Other specialized derivatives include the GEAR code, a parallel chemo-dynamical implementation built on GADGET-2 specifically optimized for high-resolution simulations of dwarf spheroidal galaxies [Revaz et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...538A..82R/abstract).

The expanding ecosystem around GADGET also saw the development of utility codes to aid in simulation setups and analysis. For example, the GalIC code was introduced to construct collisionless steady-state initial conditions specifically formatted for isolated galactic dynamics and merging simulations in GADGET [Yurin et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ascl.soft08008Y/abstract). Similarly, open-source post-processing frameworks like SPHGR (Smoothed-Particle Hydrodynamics Galaxy Reduction) were created to analyze baryonic group-finding and track galaxy merger histories directly from GADGET outputs [Thompson et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ascl.soft02012T/abstract).

The ongoing evolution of the code culminated in the release of GADGET-4, which was engineered to address extreme scalability on modern [[parallel-computing-in-astrophysics|supercomputing architectures]] [Springel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ascl.soft04014S/abstract). Written to exploit hybrid shared- and distributed-memory environments, GADGET-4 features vastly improved domain decomposition and memory management.

Despite its tremendous success, the transition to modern exascale computing poses ongoing challenges. While GPU acceleration can offer speed-ups of one to two orders of magnitude, porting monolithic legacy codes like GADGET to heavily parallelized GPU environments requires significant structural rewrites. As a result, the code's lineage is now just as visible in the [[astrophysical-simulation-codes|Astrophysical Simulation Codes]] it inspired as in its own direct updates. The core N-body tree-walk and MPI infrastructure of GADGET served as the foundational scaffolding for several state-of-the-art successors, most notably the moving-mesh code AREPO, which saw its official public release in 2019 [Weinberger et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..248...32W/abstract), and the meshless finite-mass code [[gizmo-simulation-code|GIZMO (simulation code)]] [Zhu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831...52Z/abstract), [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.480..800H/abstract), ensuring that GADGET's architectural legacy remains central to modern computational astrophysics.

## Numerical Methodology: Gravity and Hydrodynamics

GADGET computes gravitational interactions and fluid dynamics using a [[parallel-computing-in-astrophysics|highly optimized, massively parallel architecture]]. The conceptual basis for GADGET is rooted in the foundational [[tree-code-n-body-simulations|TreeSPH]] method, which originally combined hierarchical tree gravity with smoothed particle hydrodynamics for cosmological simulations [Katz et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJS..105...19K/abstract). Over its development history, the code has evolved to employ a hybrid gravity solver and has seen significant algorithmic iterations in its fluid dynamics to address inherent numerical limitations.

### Tree-PM Gravity
For collisionless dynamics (such as dark matter and stars) and self-gravitating gas, GADGET evaluates gravitational forces using a hybrid [[p3m-gravity|Tree-PM]] algorithm [O'Shea et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJS..160....1O/abstract). Short-range forces are computed using a Barnes-Hut [[tree-code-n-body-simulations|Tree code (N-body simulations)]], which groups distant particles into multipole expansions to reduce the $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ direct-summation problem to a more tractable $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$ scaling. 

To efficiently capture long-range gravitational fields in periodic [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]], the tree is coupled to a Fourier-based [[particle-mesh-simulations|Particle-mesh simulations]] algorithm. The long-range potential is found by solving the Poisson equation on a Cartesian grid in Fourier space:
$$ \nabla^2 \Phi = 4\pi G \rho $$
The PM calculation dominates on large cosmological scales, seamlessly matching the short-range tree forces at an intermediate smoothing scale. This hybrid approach circumvents the severe performance bottlenecks that traditional tree codes suffer from in near-homogeneous periodic volumes. Accurate gravity solvers also require precise initial conditions; studies have demonstrated that using second-order Lagrangian perturbation theory (2LPT) to generate initial displacements improves power spectrum accuracy near the Nyquist frequency in such simulations [Garrison et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461.4125G/abstract).

In pure collisionless galactic dynamics, GADGET has been thoroughly benchmarked against alternative tree and grid codes. For instance, a comparison study of bar dynamics between GADGET, ART, and PKDGRAV emphasized that properly resolving the vertical disc height (disc thickness) is crucial, as it strongly impacts orbital integration accuracy and overall bar evolution [Klypin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1027K/abstract). GADGET has also been employed to study stellar migration in barred spiral discs, demonstrating that star particles can migrate across spiral arms without increasing their velocity dispersion [Grand et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426..167G/abstract).

### Evolution of the SPH Formulation
The baseline hydrodynamics solver in classic GADGET versions (e.g., GADGET-2) utilizes density-based Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH). Fluid properties are interpolated over a set of nearest neighbors using smoothing kernels (traditionally a cubic B-spline). A hallmark of the classic GADGET implementation was its explicitly entropy-conserving formulation. By integrating the thermodynamic state using an entropic function rather than thermal energy, the code ensures strict entropy conservation even in highly adaptive time-stepping regimes. 

Despite its robustness, standard GADGET SPH suffers from well-documented numerical inaccuracies in multi-phase fluids. The density-based SPH formulation creates a spurious artificial surface tension across sharp density gradients, which severely suppresses the growth of Kelvin-Helmholtz and Rayleigh-Taylor fluid instabilities [Sijacki et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.2999S/abstract). In cosmological contexts, this lack of phase mixing prevents the stripping of cold gas from infalling substructures and leads to artificially concentrated gas disks due to spurious angular momentum transport [Torrey et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.427.2224T/abstract), [Zhu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831...52Z/abstract). Furthermore, standard SPH produces unphysical, declining entropy cusps in the cores of non-radiative galaxy clusters, whereas mesh-based codes predict flat entropy cores [Sembolini et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457.4063S/abstract).

To address these shortcomings and compete with the accuracy of moving-mesh or meshless finite-mass codes (such as [[gizmo-simulation-code|GIZMO (simulation code)]]), modern GADGET branches (e.g., GADGET-3, GADGET-4) and derived schemes (like SPHENIX or Anarchy) have extensively overhauled the SPH formulation. These "Modern SPH" implementations replace the classic spline with higher-order Wendland kernels (pairing with ~100 or more neighbors) to eliminate clumping instabilities [Beck et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2110B/abstract). They also feature a time-dependent artificial viscosity equipped with shear-flow limiters to restrict dissipation strictly to shocks, alongside artificial thermal conduction to promote entropy mixing across phase boundaries [Valdarnini et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...546A..45V/abstract), [Borrow et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.2367B/abstract). To more accurately capture dissipation across discontinuities, modern iterations of the code have developed on-the-fly geometrical shock detection algorithms that calculate precise Mach numbers during runtime rather than relying on standard artificial viscosity limiters alone [Beck et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.458.2080B/abstract).

Specialized multiphase fluid extensions have also been implemented directly into the GADGET framework. For simulating ram pressure stripping, researchers developed a novel two-phase SPH approach that treats the hot intracluster medium (ICM) and the cold interstellar medium (ISM) as distinct sets of gas particles, mitigating standard SPH's artificial surface tension across stark density contrasts [Jáchym et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007A&A...472....5J/abstract). Further advancing the core solver, the OpenGadget3 branch introduced a Meshless Finite Mass (MFM) implementation, providing superior handling of subsonic turbulence compared to standard SPH formulations [Groth et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526..616G/abstract). OpenGadget3 has additionally been equipped with an anisotropic Braginskii viscosity solver specifically designed to model weakly collisional plasmas in the hot intracluster medium of galaxy clusters [Marin-Gilabert et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251025847M/abstract). To analyze the complex turbulent velocity fields generated in advanced particle-based simulations like OpenGadget3, tools such as vortex-p have been developed to perform Helmholtz-Hodge and Reynolds decompositions directly on the discrete particle data [Vallés-Pérez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024CoPhC.30409305V/abstract).

While these modern SPH and meshless methods yield more accurate phase mixing and produce more extended gaseous disks, the literature remains conflicted on the absolute importance of the hydrodynamics scheme. Multiple comparative studies explicitly note that discrepancies arising from the hydrodynamic solver are often sub-dominant to the uncertainties in parametrizing [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] for energetic stellar and black hole feedback [Schaller et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.2277S/abstract), [Davé et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.3265D/abstract).

## Sub-grid Physics and Galaxy Formation

In [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] using GADGET, computational resolution limits prevent the *ab initio* simulation of individual stars or the multi-phase structure of the interstellar medium (ISM). Consequently, phenomenological [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] must be implemented to capture the baryonic lifecycle—including radiative cooling, star formation, and feedback—that dictates galaxy evolution [Lia et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000MNRAS.314..145L/abstract). Without these energetic feedback mechanisms, simulations suffer from a severe "overcooling" problem, wherein excessive amounts of gas condense and form stars, failing to reproduce the observed stellar mass function and the stellar mass-to-halo mass relation [Vogelsberger et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3031V/abstract), [Genel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.445..175G/abstract).

**Radiative Cooling and Chemical Enrichment**
Gas thermodynamics in GADGET are governed by radiative cooling. While early implementations assumed a primordial gas composition in collisional ionization equilibrium, modern extensions incorporate metal-line cooling and track the chemical enrichment of the ISM across multiple species (typically including H, He, C, N, O, Ne, Mg, Si, and Fe). Metal injection from stellar winds and supernovae significantly enhances cooling rates. Furthermore, implementing non-equilibrium metal cooling—as opposed to equilibrium approximations—has been shown in grid and particle codes (such as GASOLINE2) to alter gas thermodynamics, producing warmer ISM phases, higher molecular-gas fractions, and smoother galactic disks [Capelo et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.475.3283C/abstract).

**Star Formation**
Star formation is treated statistically by identifying cold, dense gas particles that exceed a specific density threshold or become Jeans-unstable, which then probabilistically spawn collisionless star particles. The star formation rate is calibrated to reproduce the empirical Schmidt-Kennicutt law. A critical sub-grid parameter in [[numerical-simulation-codes|particle-based codes]] like GCD+ is the star formation efficiency, $C_*$, which dictates the fractional conversion rate of eligible gas into stars over a dynamical time [Rahimi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.422.2609R/abstract). To bridge the gap between these simulation predictions and actual observational astronomy, post-processing tools such as GalevNB have been developed to convert N-body simulation data directly into observable magnitudes and synthetic spectra [Pang et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016RAA....16...37P/abstract). Additionally, radiative transfer codes like TORUS have been utilized to process smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH) outputs to generate synthetic 21-cm HI observations, allowing for direct morphological and kinematic comparisons with observed spiral galaxies [Acreman et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.406.1460A/abstract).

**Stellar and Supernova Feedback**
Feedback from stellar populations is typically partitioned into contributions from asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stars, Type II supernovae (SNe II), and Type Ia supernovae (SNe Ia) [Lia et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000MNRAS.314..145L/abstract), [Kawata et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.340..908K/abstract). To handle chemical enrichment from these various sources under the simple stellar population (SSP) approximation, software plugins like the CELib library have been developed to interface directly with hydrodynamics codes [Saitoh et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ascl.soft12016S/abstract). Each SN event injects a characteristic energy, $E_{\rm SN}$, into the surrounding ISM. Because thermal energy injected directly into dense gas is often radiated away unphysically fast in standard smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH), simulations frequently employ [[subgrid-physics-prescriptions|kinetic feedback prescriptions]]—explicitly launching gas particles as winds—to drive outflows and suppress star formation. These specific kinetic feedback prescriptions and accompanying sub-grid physics models were notably designed for and employed by moving-mesh codes like AREPO [Vogelsberger et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.3031V/abstract). Studies using chemodynamical codes like GCD+ have demonstrated that delayed feedback from SNe Ia plays a particularly crucial role in regulating the chemical and structural evolution of massive elliptical galaxies [Kawata et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.340..908K/abstract). Furthermore, appropriate time-step limiters must be applied during sudden, energetic feedback events to accurately capture energy deposition and match the low-mass end of the galaxy stellar mass function [Schaller et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.2277S/abstract).

**AGN Feedback**
To halt star formation in massive haloes and match the bright end of the observed galaxy luminosity function, modern GADGET variants and derived codes include feedback from active galactic nuclei (AGN). This involves seeding supermassive black holes in the centers of converging gas flows, tracking their growth via accretion and merging, and coupling a fraction of the accreted rest-mass energy to the surrounding gas. Implementations often bifurcate into a "quasar mode" (thermal/radiative feedback) for high accretion rates and a "radio mode" (mechanical/kinetic feedback) for low accretion rates to suppress cooling flows. Despite these advances, the specific parametrization of AGN feedback remains a major source of ongoing uncertainty in regulating star formation within massive structures.

## Magnetohydrodynamics and Radiative Transfer

To accurately capture the full physical complexity of the interstellar and intracluster medium, standard smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH) in GADGET has been extended to include magnetic fields and radiation fields. 

**Smoothed Particle Magnetohydrodynamics (SPMHD)**
GADGET simulates the evolution of cosmic magnetic fields via the SPMHD formalism. In standard SPMHD implementations, the magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ is evolved by integrating the ideal *induction equation*:
$$ \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} = \nabla \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) $$
where $\mathbf{v}$ is the gas velocity. A fundamental numerical challenge in SPMHD is maintaining the solenoidal constraint, $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$. GADGET addresses this either through active divergence-cleaning algorithms (such as the Dedner scheme) or by reformulating the problem using Euler potentials. Researchers have compared direct induction equation solvers featuring divergence cleaning against Euler potential formulations in SPH codes; while Euler potentials guarantee a divergence-free field by construction, the direct method relies on active cleaning algorithms [Kotarba et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.397..733K/abstract).

SPMHD is heavily utilized in [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] to model the magnetization of large-scale structures. Simulations have demonstrated that weak primordial seed fields of $\sim 10^{-9}$ G are efficiently amplified by compression and shear flows during hierarchical halo assembly. In the cores of massive galaxy clusters, the field is amplified by approximately three orders of magnitude to yield $\mu$G field strengths, which successfully reproduce observational Faraday rotation measures [Dolag et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999A&A...348..351D/abstract). Furthermore, SPMHD simulations of galaxy mergers—such as the Antennae galaxies—also demonstrate significant magnetic field amplification up to $\sim 10 \mu$G strengths during the interaction [Kotarba et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...716.1438K/abstract). Beyond isolated mergers and clusters, GADGET's SPMHD capabilities have been applied to model group-wide shocks and predict resultant synchrotron emission in compact galaxy groups, such as Stephan's Quintet [Geng et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.3160G/abstract).

Alternatively, the origin of these fields can be explicitly tied to [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] of star formation via *magnetic seeding*. In this framework, supernova explosions self-consistently inject localized dipole fields into the surrounding gas at typical rates of $\approx 10^{-9}$ G Gyr$^{-1}$, which are subsequently driven to observable $\mu$G amplitudes by turbulent dynamos [Beck et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.3575B/abstract). Advanced variations of the SPMHD solver also incorporate non-ideal MHD terms; models of turbulent intracluster media have shown that a macroscopic magnetic resistivity of $\eta_m \sim 6 \times 10^{27}$ cm$^2$ s$^{-1}$ is required to reproduce the specific magnetic field profiles observed in the Coma cluster [Bonafede et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418.2234B/abstract).

**Radiative Transfer**
To study cosmic reionization and local interstellar heating, GADGET tracks the propagation of ionizing radiation. Because exact ray-tracing scales poorly with the number of distinct radiation sources, GADGET heavily relies on *moment-based RT* techniques. 

A prominent implementation uses the Optically Thin Variable Eddington Tensor (OTVET) approximation as a closure relation for the moments of the radiative transfer equation [Petkova et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.1383P/abstract). This formulation approximates local photon transport as an anisotropic diffusion process mapped directly onto the SPH particle distribution. To bypass the stringent Courant constraints associated with the speed of light, the radiation fields are evolved using an implicit time-integration scheme solved iteratively via conjugate gradient methods, ensuring both robustness and strict photon conservation [Petkova et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.1383P/abstract).

More recent developments in SPH-based radiation hydrodynamics have introduced the SPH-M1RT method, which modifies the M1 closure relation. This scheme simultaneously solves the radiation energy and flux equations, employing anisotropic artificial viscosity and high-order artificial diffusion to stabilize radiation fronts across both optically thick and thin regimes [Chan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.5784C/abstract). Crucially, the computational cost of these moment-based solvers is independent of the number of emitting sources, making them highly efficient tools for resolving radiation hydrodynamics in extreme [[computational-astrophysics|Computational astrophysics]] environments.

## Massive Cosmological Projects and Benchmarks

GADGET’s [[parallel-computing-in-astrophysics|highly scalable architecture]] has made it the foundational code for many of the most computationally demanding [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] and dark-matter-only projects. These massive efforts are designed to benchmark the standard $\Lambda\rm{CDM}$ paradigm and constrain the [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] that regulate galaxy formation. 

A landmark example of this scalability is the MillenniumTNG project, which provides high-precision predictions for large-scale structure and matter clustering. Its flagship dark-matter-only run tracks 1.1 trillion particles within a massive $(3000\, {\rm Mpc})^3$ volume to minimize cosmic variance, demonstrating excellent agreement with linear theory on large scales, while its hydrodynamical counterpart simulates a $(500 \, h^{-1}{\rm Mpc})^3$ volume [Hernández-Aguayo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2556H/abstract). The project has additionally been extended to model compact binary mergers and provide gravitational wave event catalogs using the associated Arepo-GW module [Marinacci et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251006311M/abstract).

In the realm of baryonic physics, the [[c-eagle-simulations|EAGLE simulation project]] heavily utilized a modified version of GADGET to evaluate the lifecycle of galaxies. Comparisons between standard GADGET SPH and the modernized "ANARCHY" SPH formulation in EAGLE revealed that while basic global galaxy masses and sizes are robust, the detailed phase mixing of gas is highly sensitive to the hydrodynamics scheme [Schaller et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.2277S/abstract). Standard SPH's artificial surface tension suppresses fluid mixing, which artificially throttles the efficiency of active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback in quenching star formation within massive galaxies [Schaller et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.2277S/abstract). Furthermore, EAGLE demonstrated that rigorous time-step limiters are strictly required to properly couple explosive supernova feedback to the surrounding gas; without them, simulations fail to reproduce the low-mass end of the stellar mass function [Schaller et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.2277S/abstract).

The Illustris project and its successor, IllustrisTNG, rely on the moving-mesh code AREPO, which was originally developed from the GADGET framework to address the fluid instability shortcomings of traditional density-based SPH. The original Illustris run modeled a $(106.5\, {\rm Mpc})^3$ volume, successfully reproducing the $z=0$ stellar mass function and cosmic star formation history [Genel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.445..175G/abstract). Pushing the boundaries of resolution, the TNG50 simulation resolved baryonic elements down to masses of $8.5 \times 10^4 M_\odot$ and spatial scales of $70-140\rm{\, pc}$ [Pillepich et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.3196P/abstract). The scope of the TNG project was later broadened with the introduction of the TNG-Cluster simulation, dedicated entirely to high-resolution magnetohydrodynamical re-simulations of hundreds of massive galaxy clusters [Nelson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A.157N/abstract). 

A critically important successor dataset in this domain is the augmented [[auriga-simulations|Auriga Project]]. Utilizing the AREPO code, the project simulated a large suite of Milky Way-mass and dwarf galaxy haloes, culminating in a public data release that provides an invaluable resource for studying galactic disk formation and detailed substructure [Grand et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.1814G/abstract). Finally, these flagship simulations serve as critical benchmarks for numerical convergence in large-scale structure modeling. ## Computational Performance and Scaling

GADGET is a cornerstone of [[computational-astrophysics|Computational astrophysics]], renowned for its ability to scale across massively parallel distributed-memory architectures. The code primarily achieves parallelization via the Message Passing Interface (MPI), which enables the execution of massive dark matter-only and [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]]. Successive iterations of the code, culminating in GADGET-4, have refined its domain decomposition strategies to dynamically balance the computational workload and memory consumption across thousands of CPU nodes [Springel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ascl.soft04014S/abstract). 

Historically, achieving high efficiency in large-scale $N$-body integrations required specialized hardware solutions. Early variants of GADGET were adapted to interface with GRAPE (GRAvity PipE) special-purpose hardware boards. By offloading the computationally expensive $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$ gravitational force calculations of the [[tree-code-n-body-simulations|Tree code (N-body simulations)]] to GRAPE coprocessors, researchers achieved significant speed-ups in high-resolution gas dynamical simulations [Dolag et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999astro.ph..6329D/abstract), [Steinmetz et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003IAUS..208..283S/abstract). This hybrid software-hardware approach was instrumental in pioneering early high-resolution studies of galaxy clusters and cosmic magnetic fields before the widespread availability of general-purpose multi-core CPUs.

As [[parallel-computing-in-astrophysics|Parallel computing in astrophysics]] has shifted toward many-core processors and GPU accelerators, traditional MPI-based synchronous codes face mounting scalability bottlenecks. While modern GPUs can offer theoretical performance speed-ups of $10\times$ to $100\times$ over traditional CPUs, porting legacy codes like GADGET to highly concurrent architectures via [[cuda|CUDA]] (or optimizing for specific hardware like the [[maxwell-gpu-architecture|Maxwell GPU architecture]]) requires substantial structural rewrites. The primary limitation stems from the synchronous nature of GADGET's domain decomposition and tree-walk algorithms, which can suffer from severe load-imbalance and cache inefficiencies when distributed across thousands of GPU threads. 

Consequently, the simulation community is currently experiencing a paradigm shift regarding parallelization strategies. While recent versions like GADGET-4 introduce improved shared-memory parallelization via OpenMP to better exploit modern multi-core nodes [Springel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ascl.soft04014S/abstract), extreme scaling on next-generation exascale facilities is increasingly driving the development of alternative codes. New frameworks, such as SWIFT, adopt task-based parallelism and asynchronous communication to overcome the inherent limitations of standard MPI/OpenMP domain decomposition, achieving excellent weak-scaling performance on modern architectures [Theuns et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150800115T/abstract). The official release of the SWIFT code establishes it as a modern, highly-parallel gravity and smoothed particle hydrodynamics solver that serves as a next-generation task-based parallel successor to standard MPI-based GADGET frameworks [Schaller et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.2378S/abstract). Nevertheless, GADGET remains a highly efficient and widely used standard for both collisionless and smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH) simulations on traditional CPU-based high-performance computing clusters.

## Code Comparisons and Reliability

The reliability of GADGET’s numerical schemes has been extensively tested against alternative [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]], particularly Eulerian grid-based solvers like [[ramses-simulation-code|Ramses (simulation code)]] and moving-mesh codes like AREPO. Standard GADGET-2 and GADGET-3 rely on density-based smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH), which has well-documented difficulties capturing fluid instabilities (e.g., Kelvin-Helmholtz and Rayleigh-Taylor) and gas mixing due to artificial surface tension at density boundaries [Sijacki et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.2999S/abstract). 

In cosmological [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]], standard SPH suffers from spurious angular momentum transfer, leading to overly compact, centrally concentrated gaseous disks [Kereš et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.2027K/abstract), [Torrey et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.427.2224T/abstract). By contrast, moving-mesh and meshless codes like [[gizmo-simulation-code|GIZMO (simulation code)]] limit artificial viscosity and better capture subsonic turbulence, producing more extended disk morphologies. When comparing these solvers, standard GADGET typically produces a larger fraction of cold dense gas at high redshift, fueling rapid early star formation and resulting in roughly 20% higher final stellar masses at $z=0$ compared to GIZMO [Zhu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831...52Z/abstract). Morphological comparisons of isolated isothermal galactic discs also reveal intrinsic methodological footprints; particle-based codes like GIZMO and SPHNG tend to exhibit more small-scale substructure and pronounced secondary spiral arms compared to adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) codes like [[ramses-simulation-code|Ramses (simulation code)]], which tend to smooth such features [Few et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.4382F/abstract).

The nIFTy comparison project specifically evaluated GADGET against grid-based and moving-mesh codes in the context of galaxy cluster formation. In non-radiative regimes, classic GADGET SPH produces a declining central entropy profile (an entropy "cusp"), whereas Eulerian codes generate extended, flat entropy cores driven by turbulent mixing [Sembolini et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457.4063S/abstract). However, the adoption of modern SPH formulations—such as pressure-entropy SPH or the inclusion of artificial conduction—within the GADGET framework successfully alleviates these discrepancies, yielding entropy profiles that are virtually indistinguishable from grid-based results [Beck et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455.2110B/abstract). Furthermore, the radiative phase of the nIFTy comparison highlighted that the inclusion of subgrid radiative cooling and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback can actually mitigate code-based entropy discrepancies typically observed in purely non-radiative runs, blurring the lines between SPH and grid schemes in fully physics-rich regimes [Sembolini et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.2973S/abstract). However, stark differences remain in extreme environments. Comparative studies evaluating shock statistics in large-scale structures have noted significant discrepancies in Mach number distributions and shock thermal energy fluxes between SPH codes like GADGET and Eulerian grid codes (such as Enzo and TVD), particularly within rarefied, low-density regions [Vazza et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.418..960V/abstract).

The AGORA project performed controlled comparisons of isolated Milky Way-mass disks across nine [[numerical-simulation-codes|gravito-hydrodynamics codes]]. This collaboration demonstrated that when [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] (such as radiative cooling, stellar feedback, and metal yields) are strictly standardized, GADGET's predictions for macroscopic properties—such as rotation curves, velocity dispersions, and gas surface densities—agree with mesh-based codes to within a few tens of percent [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract). This suggests that the systematic uncertainties introduced by the parameterization of subgrid physics often dominate over the intrinsic differences between hydrodynamics schemes [Davé et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.3265D/abstract), [Kim et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...833..202K/abstract).

However, in extremely high-resolution simulations that explicitly resolve individual supernova explosions without subgrid delay models, the underlying hydrodynamics methodology still strongly impacts feedback efficiency. Because Lagrangian codes like GADGET allow gas to collapse to much higher densities than Eulerian codes (which are stabilized by a minimal cell size), GADGET simulations can exhibit burstier star formation and drive significantly stronger galactic outflows than [[ramses-simulation-code|Ramses (simulation code)]] under identical physical setups [Hu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...950..132H/abstract).

For very large-scale dark matter studies, full N-body codes like GADGET are sometimes contrasted with approximate methods such as [[cola-simulation|COLA]] (COmoving Lagrangian Acceleration), which trade the exact small-scale accuracy of a full N-body solver for massive computational speed-ups in generating large halo catalogs [Tassev et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013JCAP...06..036T/abstract). Finally, numerical convergence in GADGET is highly sensitive to mass resolution, gravitational softening, and time-stepping criteria. Robust convergence of fundamental dark matter halo properties generally requires $100$ to $400$ particles per halo, with rare, high-sigma density peaks converging more readily than common ones [Trenti et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711.1198T/abstract). For detailed internal kinematics, such as properly resolving the inner circular velocity profile of a halo, strict collisional relaxation criteria necessitate on the order of $3000$ particles within the resolved region [Power et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003MNRAS.338...14P/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While GADGET has cemented its role as a foundational tool in [[computational-astrophysics|Computational astrophysics]], the transition to [[next-generation-cosmological-simulations|Next-generation cosmological simulations]] presents significant physical and computational hurdles. Emerging challenges in the field are broadly categorized into hardware optimization for exascale systems, resolving sub-parsec dynamics in cosmological volumes, and disentangling numerical artifacts from physical feedback effects.

**Exascale Computing and Hardware Adaptation**
As the field moves toward exascale computing, legacy codes like GADGET face severe scalability bottlenecks. Traditional MPI-based domain decomposition and synchronous tree-walks struggle to maintain load balancing on modern many-core and GPU-accelerated architectures. Studies have demonstrated that GPU-accelerated codes can outperform traditional CPU-based solvers by orders of magnitude [Schive et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.4815S/abstract). However, adapting GADGET to fully exploit these hardware advances—such as through native [[cuda|CUDA]] implementations—requires substantial algorithmic rewrites. Consequently, to achieve the scalability demanded by trillion-particle simulations, the community is increasingly exploring alternative asynchronous, task-based scheduling paradigms to overcome the communication bottlenecks inherent to standard N-body/SPH codes [Theuns et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150800115T/abstract).

**The Final Parsec Problem and Black Hole Mergers**
A major physical challenge in cosmological modeling is accurately tracking the orbital evolution and coalescence of supermassive black hole (SMBH) binaries, commonly referred to as the "final parsec problem." Standard GADGET simulations lack the spatial resolution to capture the sub-parsec stellar and gaseous dynamics that drive binary hardening. To address this, hybrid algorithmic extensions have recently been developed. For example, the KETJU module incorporates regularized regions around SMBHs into GADGET-3, introducing post-Newtonian terms to the equations of motion that push the resolved merger separation down to $\sim 0.005$ pc [Rantala et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...840...53R/abstract). Similarly, sub-grid tracking models like RAMCOAL (implemented within [[ramses-simulation-code|Ramses (simulation code)]]) demonstrate how local dynamical friction, loss-cone scattering, and gravitational wave emission can follow real-time MBHB evolution below standard softening limits [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.232L/abstract).

**Sub-grid Physics and Small-Scale Structure**
The refinement of [[subgrid-physics-models|Subgrid physics models]] remains a highly active and debated area of research. While integrating energetic supernova and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback is strictly necessary to prevent "overcooling" and reproduce the observed stellar mass function, the specific parameterizations of these sub-grid recipes introduce significant uncertainties. The literature is currently conflicting regarding the extent to which simulation outcomes are dictated by the underlying hydrodynamics solver versus the chosen sub-grid parameters. While earlier studies highlighted standard SPH's failure to capture fluid instabilities—often advocating for moving-mesh codes or meshless alternatives like [[gizmo-simulation-code|GIZMO (simulation code)]]—recent comparisons suggest that the choice of sub-grid feedback parameterization often dominates the systematic uncertainties in the galaxy stellar mass function over the hydrodynamics methodology [Davé et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.3265D/abstract).

These baryonic processes fundamentally alter small-scale structure and the galaxy-halo connection. Energetic feedback can trigger significant stellar mass loss in satellite galaxies, which complicates empirical mapping techniques. Applying Subhalo Abundance Matching (SHAM) to hydrodynamical simulations with strong feedback has been shown to overpopulate massive haloes and artificially overestimate small-scale clustering by up to a factor of $\sim 2.5$ [Simha et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.3458S/abstract). Environmental effects, such as pre-processing in galaxy groups, also contribute heavily to the scatter in the stellar mass-to-halo mass relation [Joshi et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483..235J/abstract). Finally, the exact relative contributions of cosmic magnetic fields versus energetic AGN feedback in establishing the flat entropy profiles of galaxy cluster cores remains an open question, requiring continued refinement of both MHD solvers and black hole feedback recipes in future simulation suites. Furthermore, the sheer volume of data generated by massive GADGET runs has enabled new approaches utilizing artificial intelligence. Simulation data is increasingly used to train generative machine-learning models, such as the FLORAH framework, which efficiently reconstruct halo assembly histories and explore manifestations of assembly bias without the overhead of full N-body integrations [Nguyen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.3144N/abstract).
