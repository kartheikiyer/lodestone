In computational cosmology, **halo finders** are algorithmic tools designed to identify, resolve, and characterize gravitationally bound structures—such as dark matter halos and their constituent subhalos—within large-scale $N$-body and hydrodynamical simulations. Because dark matter cannot be observed directly, these algorithms serve as the critical bridge between theoretical models of cosmic structure formation and observable astrophysical phenomena. Historically, halo finders operated purely in 3D configuration space, defining boundaries either through density thresholding criteria (Spherical Overdensity) or via spatial percolation techniques like the [[friends-of-friends-algorithm|Friends-of-friends algorithm]]. Foundational studies established that these spatial definitions successfully capture the bulk distribution of cosmic matter [Governato et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.307..949G/abstract); however, the choice of algorithm and its free parameters fundamentally shapes the resulting theoretical predictions, highlighting the inherent ambiguity in defining a dark matter halo [Knebe et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1618K/abstract). 

As computational power expanded, simulations transitioned from producing smooth, monolithic halos to revealing highly complex, hierarchically nested substructures. This resolution revolution necessitated an evolution in finding methodologies. Purely spatial finders frequently struggle to disentangle overlapping structures in dense environments, leading to the artificial disruption of satellite systems against the dense background of a host cluster. Consequently, modern algorithms increasingly rely on 6D phase-space kinematics to robustly separate distinct dynamical components. Furthermore, to combat the numerical loss of highly stripped [[sub-resolution-haloes|Sub-resolution haloes]] and to construct the stable merger trees required for [[galaxy-halo-modeling|Galaxy Halo Modeling]], state-of-the-art finders have shifted from single-snapshot analyses to explicit temporal history matching and [[halo-core-tracking|Halo core tracking]]. 

Despite these advanced methodologies, the systematic uncertainties introduced by halo-finding algorithms remain a central focus of modern astrophysics. Comprehensive community comparison projects demonstrate that while basic metrics like host halo position and bulk velocity are robustly recovered across different codes, derived internal properties—such as spin, triaxial shape, and central subhalo abundance—exhibit massive algorithm-dependent scatter [Knebe et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1618K/abstract). These discrepancies propagate into downstream empirical models, complicating efforts to constrain [[halo-occupation-and-abundance-matching|Halo Occupation and Abundance Matching]] and limiting the precise measurement of secondary environmental effects like assembly bias and small-scale [[halo-clustering|Halo clustering]] [Wechsler et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...652...71W/abstract), [Croton et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.374.1303C/abstract). Today, the field is driven by several open questions, including how to define physical boundaries beyond the standard virial radius (e.g., the splashback radius), how to consistently handle the thermal unbinding of diffuse gas in baryonic simulations, and how to leverage generative AI models to rapidly map these complex virtual structures onto next-generation observational surveys.

## Foundational Algorithms and Halo Definitions

The identification of gravitationally bound structures in cosmological N-body simulations relies heavily on algorithms that map discrete particle distributions into distinct halos. The two classical paradigms for defining halo boundaries are configuration-space percolation and density thresholding.

The [[friends-of-friends-algorithm|Friends-of-friends algorithm]] (FoF) is a foundational configuration-space method that defines halos as percolation clusters. Particles are linked into a common structure if their pairwise spatial separation is less than a specific percolation parameter, the linking length $b$, typically expressed in units of the mean interparticle spacing. Heuristic arguments based on the spherical collapse model suggest a standard value of $b \simeq 0.2$, which identifies structures with a mean overdensity of $\sim 180$ relative to the background universe. However, utilizing a single, fixed linking length introduces an element of arbitrariness into the resulting halo mass functions, which complicates comparisons against analytical models. Furthermore, standard FoF methods are notoriously prone to "bridging," where distinct halos are artificially merged by tenuous filaments of particles [Lukić et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...692..217L/abstract). To mitigate these shortcomings, modern variations have introduced adaptive linking lengths that depend on the individual gravitational potential and local environment of particles rather than relying on a fixed global parameter [Gupta et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhRvD.108j3509G/abstract).

The primary alternative to percolation is the Spherical Overdensity (SO) method. SO algorithms define a halo's extent by growing a sphere around a local density peak until the enclosed mean density drops below a predetermined overdensity threshold. This boundary is classically associated with the virial radius. While FoF mass functions exhibit a high degree of universality, converting between FoF and SO mass definitions is non-trivial. Accurate analytical mappings exist for only 80--85% of isolated halos in the mass range of $10^{12.5}$ to $10^{15.5} \ h^{-1} \mathrm{M}_{\odot}$, breaking down entirely for systems with complex substructure or those bridged by FoF algorithms [Lukić et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...692..217L/abstract). To circumvent the limitations of strictly spatial boundaries, researchers have also introduced the 'TREE halo' definition, which identifies structures based on merger tree branches as a physically motivated alternative to traditional FoF or SO methods; when applied to large samples, this approach reveals that halo properties such as spin exhibit notable deviations from the standard lognormal distribution [Bett et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.376..215B/abstract).

The literature reflects significant ambiguity regarding the optimal physical definition of a halo boundary. Standard SO definitions measure the overdensity threshold relative to the universe's evolving mean or critical density. This shifting reference frame induces a spurious "pseudo-evolution" in halo mass over time, artificially inflating mass definitions even when the physical density profile of the halo remains completely static [Diemer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...766...25D/abstract). Beyond total mass, density profiles can be observationally and theoretically characterized by [[halo-sparsity|Halo sparsity]], a proxy defined by the ratio of masses enclosed at two different overdensities, which provides a consistency check for recovering internal mass distributions [Corasaniti et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.4382C/abstract). High-resolution simulations suggest that the inner slopes of these dark matter halo density profiles are not universal, varying systematically with halo mass from approximately $-1.5$ to $-1.1$ [Jing et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...529L..69J/abstract). In terms of standard parameterizations, while the concentration-mass relation exhibits a flattening and upturn with increasing mass, the relation between halo concentration and the linear rms density fluctuation ($c-\sigma$) follows a nearly universal U-shaped behavior, exhibiting a minimum at a well-defined scale of $\sigma \sim 0.71$ [Prada et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.3018P/abstract). Despite these internal slope variations, the overall density profiles of dark matter haloes exhibit a remarkably universal structure that can be described by simple fitting formulae across an extraordinary dynamic range of 20 orders of magnitude in mass, extending from Earth-mass clumps all the way up to cluster-scale haloes [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Natur.585...39W/abstract).

The underlying particle physics of dark matter also systematically affects these structural definitions. For example, in non-standard models such as self-interacting dark matter (SIDM), haloes develop flatter inner density cores and adopt more spherical shapes compared to standard cold dark matter (CDM) predictions [Davé et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...547..574D/abstract). Similarly, warm dark matter (WDM) haloes appear significantly more spherical at galaxy mass scales, exhibiting average axis ratios of $\sim 0.77$ compared to $\sim 0.71$ for their CDM counterparts [Bullock et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002sgdh.conf..109B/abstract). 

Furthermore, the choice of boundary radically impacts substructure demographics. Transitioning from the traditional virial radius to the dynamically motivated splashback radius—which better captures the apocenters of infalling orbits—can double the identified subhalo abundance and reclassify many "flyby" objects as bound satellites [Diemer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909..112D/abstract). The internal demographic alignments of these structures are tightly coupled to the host; tidal fields of the host halo potential induce an intrinsic alignment between the minor axes of subhaloes and the major axes of their host haloes [Lee et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...629L...5L/abstract).

Ultimately, the disparity in spatial boundary definitions and the exact procedures used for unbinding particles (iteratively removing particles with positive total energy) introduce significant systematic uncertainties. Code comparison projects reveal that these foundational algorithmic choices lead to a scatter of up to 20% in the recovered halo mass and maximum circular velocity functions across different finders, emphasizing that halo properties are highly sensitive to the definitions hardcoded into the finder [Knebe et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1618K/abstract). Furthermore, the practical translation of these theoretical definitions into observable quantities remains complex; in galaxy groups, the comparative accuracy of mass estimation reveals that while empirical relations based on the stellar-to-halo mass relation (SHMR) are highly sensitive to baryonic physics, classical dynamical estimators based on the virial theorem offer an alternative approach to measuring halo mass [Van Kempen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250812556V/abstract).

## Historical Evolution: From Monolithic Halos to Substructure Resolution

Up until the late 1990s, cosmological $N$-body simulations were severely limited by numerical resolution. Early halo finders primarily utilized the 3D spatial [[friends-of-friends-algorithm|Friends-of-friends algorithm]] (FoF). Because of the relatively small number of particles available at the time, these early, low-resolution simulations produced halos that appeared as monolithic, smooth density fields devoid of internal detail. This lack of visible substructure was exacerbated by the "overmerging problem," a numerical and algorithmic artifact where dense satellite systems artificially dissolved into the background potential of their host halos long before physical tidal disruption should have occurred.

The theoretical paradigm shifted drastically at the turn of the millennium as algorithmic improvements and exponentially increasing computing power drove a revolution in numerical resolution. By the late 1990s and early 2000s, foundational simulations conclusively demonstrated that dark matter halos are not monolithic, but instead contain vast, hierarchically nested populations of substructure. Resolving this substructure placed profound new demands on identification algorithms, which now needed to disentangle bound subhaloes from the dense, dynamically hot background of the host halo's central regions. To evaluate the performance of these modern algorithms, researchers have since utilized landmark ultra-high-resolution zoom-in suites to systematically assess subhalo recovery and [[halo-clustering|Halo clustering]] [Pujol et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.3205P/abstract).

To capture these complex hierarchies, finders evolved beyond simple 3D spatial linking. Configuration-space algorithms, such as [[subfind|SUBFIND]], successfully identify subhaloes by searching for local density maxima and gravitationally unbinding the associated particles. However, the literature reveals a persistent tension: pure real-space finders struggle to resolve substructure near the centers of parent halos, often underestimating satellite counts and overestimating the host core mass. To mitigate this, modern algorithms increasingly rely on 6D phase-space information (e.g., ROCKSTAR, VELOCIraptor). By utilizing the full phase-space dispersion tensor, these finders robustly separate distinct kinematic structures that overlap in configuration space, proving essential for identifying subhaloes deep within cluster environments [Elahi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PASA...36...21E/abstract).

Despite these advancements, snapshot-based phase-space finders still face distinct challenges, particularly concerning the survival time of subhaloes during major mergers. These merging histories are highly dependent on the halo environment; for instance, cluster-progenitor haloes exhibit significantly higher merger rates at high redshifts than isolated field haloes [Gottlöber et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...546..223G/abstract). Algorithms lacking temporal information frequently misidentify host-subhalo relationships or lose track of subhaloes as they pass through dense cores, leading to "missing" or "switched" links in merger trees [Behroozi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.3020B/abstract). Consequently, the field is undergoing a paradigm shift toward history-based tracking methods (e.g., HBT+, SYMFIND, and [[halo-core-tracking|Halo core tracking]]). By explicitly tracking the temporal history of bound particles across simulation snapshots, these tools significantly improve subhalo survival times and recover substantially more substructure at fixed mass resolutions [Mansfield et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..178M/abstract). 

Nonetheless, ambiguities remain in the modern literature. While basic properties like halo position and bulk velocity converge to within a few percent across state-of-the-art algorithms, derived properties such as spin, shape, and central subhalo mass functions continue to exhibit significant scatter depending on the specific particle unbinding and temporal tracking methodologies employed [Knebe et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1618K/abstract), [Forouhar Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543.1339F/abstract).

## Methodologies: Configuration-Space vs. Phase-Space Finders

The identification of bound structures within cosmological simulations fundamentally relies on the choice of search space. Historically, halo-finding algorithms operated purely in configuration space (3D spatial coordinates). However, the necessity of resolving complex substructures in dense environments has driven the development of finders that operate in 6D phase-space.

**Configuration-Space Finders**
Traditional algorithms rely on spatial proximity. Substructure finders built on spatial densities, such as [[subfind|SUBFIND]] and HALOMAKER, search for local density maxima within parent groups and determine the gravitationally bound subsets of particles. While configuration-space finders excel at identifying isolated field haloes, they struggle significantly in high-density environments like galaxy groups and clusters. In these regimes, the spatial density contrast of a subhalo against the background of its host is often negligible. Consequently, 3D finders frequently fail to separate overlapping structures, artificially merging subhaloes with the parent halo. Purely spatial algorithms therefore systematically undercount subhaloes near the centers of parent haloes and over-assign mass to the central host [Joshi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..761J/abstract).

**6D Phase-Space Finders**
To overcome the blending of spatially overlapping structures, modern finders utilize 6D phase-space information, incorporating particle velocities alongside spatial coordinates. Algorithms such as ROCKSTAR and VELOCIraptor epitomize this approach. By operating in phase-space, these finders can easily disentangle a fast-moving satellite from the stationary background of its host cluster. 

VELOCIraptor, for example, utilizes the full phase-space dispersion tensor for particle assignment, allowing it to robustly identify subhaloes deep within host potentials that would otherwise be lost to spatial finders [Elahi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PASA...36...21E/abstract), [Cañas et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.2039C/abstract). This phase-space information is essential for accurately recovering subhalo radial distributions and capturing physical phenomena such as mass segregation within $0.5 R_{\rm vir}$ of parent halo centers [Joshi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..761J/abstract). Moreover, highly efficient Bayesian frameworks have been developed to infer host halo mass and concentration directly from the 6D phase-space coordinates of remarkably sparse tracer populations, requiring as few as 20 satellite galaxies to achieve accurate determinations [Li et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...886...69L/abstract).

**The Unbinding Procedure and Derived Properties**
Regardless of whether candidate structures are identified in 3D or 6D, all sophisticated finders must perform an unbinding procedure to iteratively remove particles that are not gravitationally bound to the local potential. The numerical implementation of this unbinding procedure is a primary source of algorithmic divergence. 

Systematic comparison projects have shown that basic properties—such as halo position, bulk velocity, and peak circular velocity—are remarkably robust across different finders, exhibiting scatter of only a few percent [Knebe et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1618K/abstract). However, derived properties, particularly spin and shape, show larger deviations due to subtle differences in particle collection and unbinding criteria [Knebe et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1618K/abstract). 

While 6D phase-space finders remain the gold standard for high-density environments, they can still suffer from artificial subhalo disruption if they only analyze single simulation snapshots. Traditional finders can converge to false solutions for subhalo mass functions at high resolution [Mansfield et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..178M/abstract). Consequently, the newest generation of algorithms supplements phase-space tracking with explicit temporal histories—such as HBT+, SYMFIND, or explicit [[halo-core-tracking|Halo core tracking]]—to preserve the identity of heavily stripped subhaloes and maintain robust merger trees [Han et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474..604H/abstract), [Mansfield et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..178M/abstract), [Forouhar Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206932F/abstract).

**Alternative Methodologies and Extensions**
Beyond geometric and kinematic finders, the literature features several alternative approaches. Probabilistic and Bayesian group-finding algorithms based on marked point processes offer a non-deterministic alternative to standard geometric finding techniques [Tempel et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...618A..81T/abstract). Other approaches, such as the HADRON-code, perform mass reconstruction by assigning halo masses based on local density and the cosmic web environment while strictly accounting for halo-exclusion effects [Zhao et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.451.4266Z/abstract). Specialized algorithmic frameworks also enable the direct analysis of non-standard cosmologies; finders have been tailored to create 'effective halo catalogs' in modified gravity ($f(R)$) models, allowing these theories to be analyzed similarly to standard cold dark matter models [He et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PhRvL.115g1306H/abstract).

At larger scales, minimal spanning tree algorithms have been deployed to identify the filamentary building blocks of the cosmic web, revealing that cluster-sized haloes form the primary backbone of the universe's large-scale structure [Colberg et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.375..337C/abstract). Additionally, advanced topological data analysis techniques, such as persistent homology, can be applied to subhalo catalogs to differentiate between Cold Dark Matter (CDM) and Warm Dark Matter (WDM) models by identifying distinct differences in the topology of subhalo clusters and loops at scales of $\sim 100$ kpc [Cisewski-Kehe et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106b3521C/abstract).

## Temporal Tracking and Merger Tree Construction

Merger trees encode the assembly histories of dark matter haloes over cosmic time, serving as the foundational framework for semi-analytic models of [[galaxy-halo-modeling|Galaxy Halo Modeling]] [Jiang et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440..193J/abstract), [Poulton et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...42P/abstract). Constructing these trees requires algorithms to link progenitor and descendant haloes across discrete simulation snapshots, a process that is highly sensitive to the methodologies used to define and track bound structures.

Traditional configuration-space and phase-space finders (such as [[subfind|SUBFIND]] and ROCKSTAR) evaluate haloes on a snapshot-by-snapshot basis. During complex dynamical events—such as major mergers or close flyby interactions, which can be as frequent as mergers for massive haloes at $z < 2$ [Sinha et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AAABS...4..120S/abstract)—snapshot algorithms frequently suffer from "switched" host-subhalo relationships or "missing" links [Behroozi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.3020B/abstract). When subhaloes pass near the dense center of a host, traditional finders may artificially lose them against the background density, leading to premature numerical disruption and fragmented merger trees.

To mitigate these issues, careful merger tree construction is necessary to enforce physical consistency in mass accretion histories and avoid problematic tracking artifacts [Behroozi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.3020B/abstract). Additionally, specialized algorithms like Dhalo operate by regrouping subhaloes to ensure strictly hierarchical histories, avoiding the artificial structural bridging that often plagues the standard [[friends-of-friends-algorithm|Friends-of-friends algorithm]] [Jiang et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440.2115J/abstract). While these post-processing tools stabilize merger trees, they are fundamentally limited by the input catalogs. 

A major paradigm shift in resolving substructure is the transition to history-based progenitor tracking directly within the halo-finding step. Algorithms like HBT+ (Hierarchical Bound-Tracing) identify haloes as they form and track their specific bound particles forward in time [Han et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474..604H/abstract). Because the finder knows where a subhalo was in the previous timestep, this approach naturally builds merger trees while simultaneously identifying subhaloes. This drastically reduces the incidence of missing links and improves the recovered survival time of massive subhaloes in dense environments, which otherwise appear artificially disrupted by snapshot finders [Han et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474..604H/abstract), [Forouhar Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206932F/abstract).

Modern particle-tracking tools, such as SYMFIND, push this further by tracking subhaloes to orders-of-magnitude lower masses than purely snapshot-based methods, demonstrating that many traditionally "disrupted" subhaloes physically survive much longer than previously estimated [Mansfield et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..178M/abstract). Related techniques, such as [[halo-core-tracking|Halo core tracking]], bypass standard unbinding entirely. Instead, they explicitly follow the [[core-halo-structure|central density cores]] of merged haloes across all simulation timesteps, creating a stable proxy to model the spatial distribution of satellite galaxies [Korytov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..24K/abstract), [Souza Vitório et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240700268S/abstract).

As merger trees and tracking algorithms grow more sophisticated, the complexity of the resulting temporal histories requires advanced analysis tools. Dendrograms have been introduced as a novel visualization method to map these extensive assembly histories, allowing researchers to accurately diagnose algorithmic artifacts while tracking subhalo orbits, merger events, and the continuous evolution of halo properties over cosmic time [Poulton et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...42P/abstract). Beyond standard dendrograms, novel geometric representations such as 'halo root systems' utilize continuous particle pathlines to visualize the infall of matter into collapsed structures, offering a rich, temporally continuous map of mass assembly [Neyrinck et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250321015N/abstract).

## Halo Finder Systematics and Comparison Projects

The proliferation of structure-identifying algorithms in cosmological simulations has necessitated rigorous community benchmarking, most notably through the Halo Finder Comparison Project. These comparative studies aim to quantify the statistical scatter and algorithmic biases introduced when extracting dark matter structures, as the choice of finder can profoundly impact downstream theoretical predictions and applications such as [[halo-occupation-and-abundance-matching|Halo Occupation and Abundance Matching]]. It is worth noting that community comparison projects also extend to void-finding algorithms, where different methods (such as watershed or tessellation-based algorithms) show robust structural agreement in locating the centers and boundaries of major cosmic voids [Colberg et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.387..933C/abstract). To standardize these large-scale analyses, modular platforms like the VIDE toolkit have been introduced to provide robust, watershed-based void identification and hierarchical characterization [Sutter et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&C.....9....1S/abstract).

Evaluations across dozens of algorithms—ranging from simple real-space [[friends-of-friends-algorithm|Friends-of-friends algorithm]] implementations to sophisticated phase-space and history-tracking codes—reveal that basic halo properties are generally robust. Across different finders, the statistical scatter in host halo position and bulk velocity remains below a few percent [Knebe et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1618K/abstract). However, mass reconstruction and the derivation of secondary parameters are significantly more algorithm-dependent. Variations in particle collection methods and the specific criteria used to remove gravitationally unbound particles lead to a 10-20% scatter in the recovered halo mass and maximum circular velocity ($V_{\rm max}$) functions [Knebe et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1618K/abstract). When isolating the gravitationally bound mass of high-mass haloes, these discrepancies can inflate up to 75% [Forouhar Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543.1339F/abstract). When evaluating the impact of these methodological systematics on observational cluster mass reconstruction projects, studies find that richness-based mass estimation methods often deliver lower scatter than dynamical tracers, especially for systems with few member galaxies [Old et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441.1513O/abstract). In observational contexts, methodological trade-offs also arise when identifying large-scale cluster populations; for instance, combining Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) flux maps with optical galaxy catalogs yields significant improvements in cluster detection and structural characterization over single-wavelength finding techniques [Cohn et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.393..393C/abstract).

Derived internal properties are even more susceptible to algorithmic definitions. Measurements of the spin parameter and structural shape bias exhibit substantial deviations across codes, primarily driven by how different finders resolve the ambiguous boundaries of substructures and their diffuse outer envelopes [Knebe et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1618K/abstract). In hydrodynamic simulations, this disparity is exacerbated by the treatment of baryonic physics; finders show major disagreements in the diffuse gas content of low-mass satellites due to varying thermal energy unbinding procedures [Knebe et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.2039K/abstract).

A major axis of tension in the literature is the treatment of subhaloes in dense environments, such as cluster centers. Purely spatial, configuration-space finders (e.g., [[subfind|SUBFIND]] or HALOMAKER) systematically overestimate the mass of central host galaxies while underestimating or completely missing satellite subhaloes due to the lack of kinematic contrast against the dense host background. Incorporating 6D phase-space information is essential for successfully recovering these central substructures [Joshi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..761J/abstract), [Cañas et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.2039C/abstract). However, even state-of-the-art snapshot-based phase-space finders can converge to false solutions for subhalo disruption masses and radial distributions. Modern history-based tracking algorithms dramatically improve subhalo survival times by explicitly tracking the temporal history of bound particles [Han et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474..604H/abstract), [Mansfield et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..178M/abstract).

The temporal consistency of halo finders is also a critical systematic for the construction of merger trees. Standard algorithms struggle to resolve major mergers, particularly at high redshifts ($z > 1$), frequently resulting in "switched" relationships between host haloes and subhaloes over successive timesteps [Behroozi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454.3020B/abstract). To mitigate this, algorithms that strictly enforce hierarchical assembly histories—such as "Dhalo" constructions—are often required to generate stable merger trees for semi-analytic models [Jiang et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440.2115J/abstract), [Gómez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.5500G/abstract). Ultimately, while global distribution statistics remain broadly stable, the exact mass assembly history, shape, and survivability of any individual subhalo remains highly sensitive to the chosen tracking paradigm.

## Finding Structures in Gaseous and Hydrodynamical Environments

The transition from dark matter-only N-body simulations to full hydrodynamical models introduces significant algorithmic complexities for halo finders. In these environments, identification codes must account for baryonic physics, including star formation, gas hydrodynamics, and stellar feedback. 

One of the primary algorithmic challenges is the unbinding procedure used to determine if a particle is gravitationally bound to a subhalo. In a hydrodynamical simulation, a gas particle's total energy must include its thermal component. The condition for a particle of mass $m$, velocity $v$, specific internal (thermal) energy $u$, and local gravitational potential $\Phi$ to remain bound is typically expressed as:
$$ E = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 + m \Phi + m u < 0 $$
Evaluations of the thermal energy $u$ vary significantly among different halo finding algorithms. This leads to a persistent disparity in the recovered diffuse gas content, particularly for low-mass satellite galaxies [Knebe et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.2039K/abstract). Overall, the performance of subhalo finders is systematically worse in hydrodynamical simulations than in dark matter-only simulations. Differences in mass assignment and miscentering can produce a $\sim 10\%$ scatter in the recovered $M_{\rm 200c}$ halo mass functions, while the bound mass functions can differ by up to $75\%$ at the high-mass end [Forouhar Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543.1339F/abstract). 

The choice of finder also heavily influences derived galactic properties. Simple configuration-space finders struggle in dense environments, frequently overestimating the stellar mass, star formation rate, and physical size of host central galaxies by factors of $>2$, while simultaneously underestimating the properties of satellites. Consequently, the use of phase-space or history-tracking algorithms is essential for correctly disentangling substructure in baryonic simulations [Cañas et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.2039C/abstract).

Beyond algorithmic scatter, the physical presence of baryons fundamentally alters the underlying dark matter structures. Baryonic cooling and feedback shift the overall halo mass function to lower masses. Accurately reproducing galaxy clustering from hydrodynamical runs using semi-analytic methods requires explicit corrections to the halo mass function as well as the inclusion of velocity and assembly bias parameters [Beltz-Mohrmann et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.5771B/abstract). Furthermore, processes such as AGN feedback alter the relationship between galaxy stellar mass and luminosity, often making it non-monotonic. This severely complicates standard [[halo-occupation-and-abundance-matching|Halo Occupation and Abundance Matching]] models, as it implies that luminosity-threshold galaxy samples occupy a different range of halo masses than stellar-mass-threshold samples [McCullagh et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170501988M/abstract). Furthermore, the coupling of dark matter and baryons extends into the deepest cores; cosmological simulations reveal a positive correlation between supermassive black hole mass and dark matter halo concentration at a fixed halo mass, reflecting the physical connection between central gas density, cooling, and black hole accretion rates [Nino et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251217964N/abstract). Furthermore, the inclusion of gas physics allows for the direct modeling of the intracluster medium. Analytical fits to the density profiles of hot galactic halo gas in cosmological simulations reveal a non-trivial dependence on the total underlying dark matter mass profile, highlighting the complex interplay between bound gas and the host potential well [Hansen et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...653L..17H/abstract).

To address the heterogeneity of subhalo finder outputs in hydrodynamical environments, community tools such as SOAP (Spherical Overdensity and Aperture Processor) have been developed. SOAP acts as a post-processing layer that takes a generic subhalo catalogue and consistently calculates an array of galaxy and halo properties—such as spherical overdensities and fixed physical apertures—providing a standardized interface for comparing hydrodynamic simulations against observational data [McGibbon et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250722669M/abstract).

## Advanced Core-Tracking and Orphan Modeling

As subhaloes orbit within the dense environments of host clusters, they are subjected to severe tidal stripping and dynamical friction. Traditional configuration-space halo finders (such as the [[friends-of-friends-algorithm|Friends-of-friends algorithm]] and [[subfind|SUBFIND]]) frequently fail to identify these highly stripped substructures once their density contrast drops below the background threshold. This premature numerical disruption leads to the artificial creation of "orphan galaxies"—satellite galaxies in semi-analytic models that have lost their resolved dark matter envelopes but would physically still survive.

To address the fragility of standard subhalo finders, modern algorithms utilize [[halo-core-tracking|Halo core tracking]] to follow the temporal evolution of [[sub-resolution-haloes|Sub-resolution haloes]]. Rather than relying solely on instantaneous density peaks, this technique identifies the most tightly bound [[core-halo-structure|central particles of a halo]] prior to its accretion and explicitly tracks this conservative particle set throughout the simulation [Korytov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..24K/abstract). Tracking the central core preserves the structural identity of the subhalo even after it undergoes substantial tidal stripping, ensuring a stable and reliable proxy for the hosted galaxy [Souza Vitório et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240700268S/abstract). An effective alternative to standard subhalo-galaxy correspondence, particularly in massive galaxy groups, is the most bound member particle (MBP) scheme. By utilizing the specific dynamical center of the initial subhalo, the MBP method provides a more accurate tracking proxy for hosted galaxies after severe stripping [Hong et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...823..103H/abstract).

Recent particle-tracking algorithms, such as SYMFIND, have demonstrated that dark matter substructure durability is significantly higher than traditionally estimated by phase-space finders like ROCKSTAR. By effectively tracking subhaloes to orders-of-magnitude lower masses, SYMFIND recovers $15{-}40\%$ more subhaloes within the virial radius, $R_{\rm vir}$, and $35{-}120\%$ more subhaloes in the deep inner regions within $R_{\rm vir}/4$ [Mansfield et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..178M/abstract). These advanced algorithms prove that mass loss can be robustly resolved at modest numerical resolutions (e.g., peak particle counts of $n_{\rm peak} \gtrsim 4 \times 10^3$), allowing simulations to trace subhaloes until the physical point of galaxy disruption without needing to invoke *post hoc* orphan modeling [Mansfield et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..178M/abstract).

Similarly, history-based tracking codes like HBT+ and its successor HBT-HERONS construct temporal merger trees concurrently with subhalo identification. This approach drastically reduces the occurrence of missing or switched links that often afflict snapshot-by-snapshot finders [Han et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474..604H/abstract), [Forouhar Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206932F/abstract). 

Despite these algorithmic advancements, the literature remains somewhat conflicting regarding the exact properties of subhaloes in highly dense environments. Comparisons of various subhalo finders reveal that bound mass functions can still differ by up to $75\%$ at the high-mass end, while the number of well-resolved subhaloes near the boundary $R_{\rm 200c}$ diverges by roughly $20\%$ depending on the specific mass assignment and unbinding procedures utilized by the code [Forouhar Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206932F/abstract). Consequently, predictions from different finders become increasingly divergent towards the central regions of host haloes.

Ultimately, advanced core-tracking mechanisms play a critical role in [[galaxy-halo-modeling|Galaxy Halo Modeling]]. By maintaining a physically motivated one-to-one correspondence between dark matter cores and satellite galaxies, these tracking methods bridge the gap between large-volume gravity-only simulations and smaller-scale hydrodynamical models, ensuring that the spatial distribution and abundance of galaxies within massive clusters are accurately reproduced [Korytov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..24K/abstract), [Souza Vitório et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240700268S/abstract).

## AI-Assisted Finders and Generative Models

As cosmological simulations grow in volume, the computational cost of resolving fine substructures and accurately tracing the galaxy-halo connection via hydrodynamic codes has driven the adoption of machine learning. These techniques, particularly deep learning, are increasingly utilized to bypass traditional algorithmic bottlenecks, infer underlying halo properties from sparse observable data, and generate mock catalogs with high physical fidelity. Alongside neural network models, fully Bayesian methodologies, such as the HADES algorithm, have been developed to rigorously connect inferred three-dimensional density fields directly to halo detection probabilities, correctly accounting for complex observational noise [Merson et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.1340M/abstract).

Graph Neural Networks (GNN) have proven highly effective for modeling the irregular, sparse phase-space distribution of galaxies within dark matter haloes. Traditional steady-state kinematic mass estimators often suffer from projection effects and simplistic dynamical assumptions. GNNs offer a robust, data-driven alternative by learning the complex, non-linear correlations between a halo's true mass and the phase-space coordinates and intrinsic properties (such as stellar masses and radii) of its constituent galaxies. By training on state-of-the-art hydrodynamic simulations, GNNs can infer the total mass of a host dark matter halo with an accuracy of $\sim 0.2$ dex [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935...30V/abstract). Furthermore, these models demonstrate strong generalizability, preserving their predictive accuracy even when evaluated on simulations run with entirely distinct subgrid physics models [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935...30V/abstract).

Deep learning is additionally being leveraged to create super-resolution simulations, bridging the gap between computationally inexpensive low-resolution (LR) runs and resource-intensive high-resolution (HR) N-body simulations. AI-assisted super-resolution models can generate up to 512 times more tracer particles from an LR input field, extending structure identification deep into the non-linear regime [Ni et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.1021N/abstract). By synthesizing the full phase-space matter distribution—including both displacements and velocities—these algorithms recover complex small-scale structures and [[sub-resolution-haloes|Sub-resolution haloes]] that are unresolved in the initial LR conditions. The resulting generated fields match the power spectra, bispectra, and pairwise velocity distributions of true HR simulations at the percent level down to scales of $k \sim 10 \, h \, {\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ [Ni et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.1021N/abstract).

The push for rapid mock catalog generation has also led to the development of generative AI frameworks that extend beyond traditional [[halo-occupation-and-abundance-matching|Halo Occupation and Abundance Matching]] methods. A prominent example is NeHOD, which utilizes a variational diffusion model combined with a Transformer architecture to emulate satellite galaxy and subhalo populations [Nguyen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240902980N/abstract). Unlike voxel-based generative models, NeHOD treats subhaloes as continuous point clouds, which allows it to resolve spatial scales down to the fundamental resolution limit of the training data. By painting central and satellite properties (including positions, velocities, masses, and concentrations) directly onto dark matter structures, diffusion models like NeHOD reproduce the complex subhalo statistics of detailed hydrodynamic simulations—such as concentration-mass relations and small-scale [[halo-clustering|Halo clustering]]—at a computational cost comparable to standard analytical HODs [Nguyen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240902980N/abstract). To further accelerate structure mapping without the expense of full N-body runs, computationally lightweight frameworks like COmoving Lagrangian Acceleration (COLA) have also proven highly successful for generating fast and accurate mock catalogues, particularly for low-mass galaxies [Koda et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.2118K/abstract). Another powerful alternative for rapid structure generation is the ExSHalos method, which utilizes excursion set theory combined with Lagrangian perturbation theory to provide a fast, parameter-free construction of halo catalogues that reproduce accurate clustering statistics [Voivodic et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190606630V/abstract).

## Applications: The Galaxy-Halo Connection and Assembly Bias

The outputs of halo-finding algorithms serve as the foundational infrastructure for mapping the distribution of galaxies to the underlying dark matter field. By supplying robust catalogs of host halos and subhalos, finders enable the calibration of empirical frameworks such as [[halo-occupation-and-abundance-matching|Halo Occupation and Abundance Matching]] (which encompasses both Halo Occupation Distribution, or HOD, and Subhalo Abundance Matching, or SHAM). Standard formulations of these models assume that the number and properties of galaxies depend exclusively on the host halo mass, $M_{\rm vir}$. However, tests against hydrodynamic simulations and high-precision survey data reveal that mass-only models are insufficient to fully capture small-scale [[halo-clustering|Halo clustering]] and galactic conformity [Beltz-Mohrmann et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.5771B/abstract), [Alam et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497..581A/abstract). Empirical calibrations must also account for large-scale environmental factors such as cosmic variance, which can drastically impact the observed galaxy stellar mass functions. For instance, observations situated in local underdense regions at low redshift can lead to significant underestimations of the low-mass end of the galaxy distribution [Chen et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...872..180C/abstract).

Extensive analyses of $N$-body catalogs demonstrate that at fixed mass, halo clustering depends heavily on secondary parameters—a phenomenon termed halo assembly bias. For low-mass halos ($M < M_\ast$), those that form early or have high concentrations cluster significantly more strongly than their late-forming counterparts; interestingly, this trend reverses for rare, massive halos where $M > M_\ast$ [Wechsler et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...652...71W/abstract). Similarly, at a fixed halo mass, the number of satellite galaxies depends strongly on halo age, with older haloes typically hosting fewer satellites due to them having more time to merge onto the central galaxy [Zhu et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...639L...5Z/abstract). Furthermore, the slope of the specific mass accretion history (sMAH) acts as a highly stable proxy for these secondary clustering dependencies, tracking closely with the star formation and quenching histories of the galaxies they host [Montero-Dorta et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508..940M/abstract). To accurately reproduce data from surveys like SDSS and eBOSS, researchers must employ "decorated" HOD models. These extensions incorporate both assembly bias and velocity bias—the latter accounting for the fact that satellite galaxies do not perfectly trace the velocity field of the host dark matter [Beltz-Mohrmann et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...948..100B/abstract). To capture the diverse properties of hosted galaxies, researchers have developed two-parameter matching schemes that utilize both halo mass and concentration (or the inner profile slope); this dual approach better captures complex scaling relations such as the Fundamental Plane of early-type galaxies [Kulier et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.4013K/abstract).

Beyond internal assembly history, [[halo-clustering|Halo clustering]] is profoundly dependent on the broader cosmic web. Halos residing in cosmic voids can exhibit a clustering bias enhanced by up to a factor of 10 relative to the overall population, whereas bias is significantly suppressed for halos in massive clusters [Yang et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...848...60Y/abstract). Recent topological analyses also reveal a "topological bias," demonstrating a mass-dependent linear scaling relation between halo masses and the multiscale connectivity of their environment (e.g., elongated filaments versus sheet-like walls) that is distinct from standard two-point correlation bias [Bermejo et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.4325B/abstract). Modern multitracer inference frameworks, such as ABACUSHOD, increasingly rely on these environmental metrics rather than internal halo concentration to parameterize secondary biases efficiently [Yuan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.3301Y/abstract). In fact, secondary galaxy bias contains a distinct beyond-mass component, wherein haloes at a fixed mass tend to host more galaxies when they are more strongly associated with cosmic web nodes or filaments [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.4616W/abstract). At the largest cosmological scales, the distribution of halos is also sensitive to the initial conditions of the universe; specifically, primordial non-Gaussianity introduces a characteristic scale-dependence in halo bias that can be analytically modeled to constrain inflationary physics with forthcoming large-scale surveys [Matarrese et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...677L..77M/abstract).

Refining empirical models requires addressing their foundational statistical assumptions. For example, assuming non-Poissonian (either sub-Poisson or super-Poisson) satellite distributions in HOD formulations significantly impacts both the one-halo term and count-in-cells statistics [Vos-Ginés et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.3458V/abstract). Additionally, capturing the full impact of large-scale structure requires mapping the "response functions" of Halo Occupation Distributions. These responses describe how galaxy occupation fundamentally reacts to long-wavelength matter perturbations and represent an essential new ingredient for calculating accurate linear galaxy biases [Voivodic et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...05..069V/abstract). The high-precision calibration of both linear and higher-order assembly bias has also been achieved using the 'Separate Universe' technique, which measures the response of halo abundance to infinite-wavelength background perturbations [Paranjape et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.2984P/abstract).

Despite robust theoretical predictions derived from phase-space finders and the [[friends-of-friends-algorithm|Friends-of-friends algorithm]], the observational detection of assembly bias remains highly elusive. For example, analyses decoupling projection effects from weak lensing and central galaxy properties in SDSS redMaPPer clusters found no significant difference in clustering amplitudes at a fixed cluster richness, suggesting that observed central galaxy properties may not strongly correlate with halo assembly histories [Sunayama et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220503277S/abstract). Furthermore, while substituting $M_{\rm vir}$ with the maximum circular velocity $V_{\rm max}$ is physically motivated by the depth of the potential well, doing so as the primary HOD variable does not necessarily improve empirical fits to the projected two-point correlation function $w_p(r_p)$ [Mezini et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RNAAS...6...80M/abstract). The literature therefore reflects an ongoing tension within [[galaxy-halo-modeling|Galaxy Halo Modeling]] between the clear secondary biases predicted by dark matter halo catalogs and the complex, baryon-impacted reality of the observed galaxy distribution.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

A persistent conceptual and algorithmic challenge in structure identification is defining the physical boundary of a halo. Traditional algorithms, such as the [[friends-of-friends-algorithm|Friends-of-friends algorithm]] or spherical overdensity finders, rely on essentially arbitrary density thresholds (e.g., $R_{\rm vir}$ or $R_{200}$). However, the literature increasingly advocates for physically motivated boundaries like the splashback radius, which naturally separates bound satellite galaxies from transient flyby interactions [Diemer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909..112D/abstract). Using the splashback radius instead of a virial boundary can double the recovered abundance of subhaloes, reclassifying the majority of "backsplash" or flyby haloes as true satellites [Diemer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909..112D/abstract). Flyby interactions themselves are a major driver of halo evolution, occurring as frequently as major mergers for massive haloes ($M > 10^{11} h^{-1} M_\odot$) at $z < 2$ [Sinha et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013AAABS...4..120S/abstract). At even larger scales, identifying the turnaround radius—detectable as a divergence from spherical collapse models in stacked density profiles—offers a novel probe of the transition between relaxed cluster infall and the Hubble flow [Korkidis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240411183K/abstract).

While basic halo properties like mass and bulk velocity are robustly recovered across modern finders, significant scatter remains in the identification of substructure in dense environments [Knebe et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.435.1618K/abstract), [Forouhar Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206932F/abstract). Standard snapshot-based finders frequently struggle with artificial disruption or "missing links" in merger trees, yielding false convergence in subhalo mass functions and radial distributions near the host center [Mansfield et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..178M/abstract). Newer history-based particle-tracking tools, including [[halo-core-tracking|Halo core tracking]] and algorithms like SYMFIND, resolve this fragility by recovering significantly more subhaloes at the same resolution limits than widely used phase-space finders like ROCKSTAR [Mansfield et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970..178M/abstract). Reconciling these central discrepancies is critical for accurate [[galaxy-halo-modeling|Galaxy Halo Modeling]]. Furthermore, incorporating baryonic physics introduces new algorithmic ambiguities; finders show considerable disparity in how they handle the thermal energy and diffuse gas content of low-mass satellites during the particle unbinding phase [Knebe et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.2039K/abstract), [Forouhar Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206932F/abstract). Another open question is how best to leverage observational proxies to constrain these internal histories. The 'halo mass-gap'—defined strictly as the logarithmic difference in mass between the host dark matter halo and its most massive subhalo, which is connected observationally via the 'stellar mass-gap' (the difference in magnitude or stellar mass between central and satellite galaxies)—has been proposed as a potent probe of halo age and concentration, though recent mergers in so-called fossil systems can create significant scatter in the age-gap relationship, complicating its direct application [Deason et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777..154D/abstract).

Looking forward, the era of precision cosmology driven by next-generation surveys (e.g., DESI, Euclid, Rubin) demands massive, highly accurate mock galaxy catalogs. This necessitates continuous improvements in lightcone construction to bridge the gap between simulation outputs and observer-space data. Modern frameworks focus on optimizing lightcone orientation to minimize the repetition of cosmic structures when survey volumes exceed the native simulation box, utilizing linear interpolation to track evolving halo properties across discrete snapshots [Ramakrishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251213801R/abstract). 

To alleviate the computational bottlenecks of running full hydrodynamical simulations for these large volumes, future directions heavily feature machine learning. Frameworks such as NeHOD utilize diffusion models and point clouds to emulate satellite and subhalo populations—including their 6D phase-space properties and concentrations—matching the accuracy of hydrodynamical runs at the low computational cost of standard [[halo-occupation-and-abundance-matching|Halo Occupation and Abundance Matching]] [Nguyen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240902980N/abstract). Similarly, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have demonstrated the ability to infer host halo masses from satellite kinematics and positions to within $\sim 0.2$ dex accuracy [Villanueva-Domingo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...935...30V/abstract). Integrating these advanced inference techniques with robust, history-based subhalo tracking will be essential for mapping the subtle signals of assembly bias and environmental dependence in upcoming survey data.
