The **Latte simulation suite** is a flagship collection of high-resolution cosmological [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] designed to model the formation and evolution of Milky Way-mass galaxies within a standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology. Developed as part of the Feedback In Realistic Environments (FIRE) project, the suite utilizes the FIRE-2 physics framework to directly resolve the multiphase interstellar medium (ISM) and the formation of individual star clusters down to parsec scales [Wetzel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..23W/abstract). Unlike large-volume [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] that rely on heavily parameterized macroscopic subgrid models to regulate [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], Latte implements explicit stellar feedback channels—including core-collapse and Type Ia supernovae, continuous stellar winds, and radiation pressure. By bridging the gap between large-scale cosmological environments and the sub-galactic physics of star formation, the suite provides a rigorous theoretical testbed for understanding the chemo-dynamical history of the Milky Way and the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]] [Wetzel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...44W/abstract).

A major achievement of the Latte suite is its ability to naturally resolve long-standing small-scale tensions in the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm, such as the "missing satellites" and "too big to fail" problems. Early dark-matter-only models vastly overpredicted the number and central densities of [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|dwarf galaxies]] orbiting Milky Way-mass hosts. Latte demonstrates that explicit baryonic physics drives powerful, bursty outflows that kinematically heat the dark matter, transforming central density cusps into shallower cores [Wetzel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..23W/abstract). Furthermore, the suite highlights the critical role of "baryonic back-reaction": the presence of a massive central stellar disk drastically enhances the tidal destruction of surrounding substructure, depleting the dark matter subhalo population by a factor of several compared to dark-matter-only models. This robustly reconciles the predicted satellite populations with observations of [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]].

Beyond addressing dark matter kinematics, the Latte simulations are extensively utilized for near-field cosmology and Galactic archaeology. The suite tracks multiple explicit elemental abundances, allowing researchers to study the chemical and structural evolution of the Galactic disk and stellar halo. By utilizing chemical abundance ratio distributions (CARDs) and tracking [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]], these models can successfully disentangle stars formed *in situ* from those deposited by disrupted dwarf galaxies. To facilitate direct comparisons with observational datasets like *Gaia*, the suite is paired with forward-modeling frameworks (such as *ananke*) to generate synthetic multidimensional phase-space surveys. This capability allows the astronomical community to test observational inference methods against the exact evolutionary "ground truth" of the simulated galaxies.

While the Latte suite has established a new standard for high-resolution galaxy modeling, several ongoing challenges and active areas of research remain. Open questions exist regarding the transient dynamical impacts of recent, massive satellite accretions—such as the infall of a Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC) analog—which can significantly perturb the host's dark matter halo and satellite orbits. Additionally, researchers are actively working to incorporate previously omitted physical processes, including cosmic ray transport and active galactic nuclei (AGN) feedback, to determine their influence on circumgalactic gas and large-scale outflows. Finally, variant runs of the suite are increasingly being used to test microphysical dark matter models, exploring how intricate baryonic effects might masquerade as, or be distinguished from, the signatures of alternative dark matter candidates.

## Introduction to the Latte Suite and FIRE-2 Framework

The Latte simulation suite is a prominent set of high-resolution [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] designed to model the formation and evolution of Milky Way-mass galaxies within a $\Lambda$CDM cosmology. As a flagship component of the broader Feedback In Realistic Environments (FIRE) project, Latte aims to self-consistently resolve the multiphase ISM and the explicit effects of stellar feedback without relying on the heavily tuned macroscopic subgrid prescriptions typically required in large-volume [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] [Wetzel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..23W/abstract). The suite comprises numerous zoom-in runs targeting host halos with masses $M_{\rm 200c} \sim 10^{12} M_\odot$, along with detailed tracking of their extensive satellite populations across cosmic time [Wetzel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...44W/abstract).

The simulations are integrated using the `GIZMO` code, employing a mesh-free finite-mass method for accurate hydrodynamics alongside a Tree-PM gravity solver [Wetzel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..23W/abstract). Central to the Latte suite is the implementation of the FIRE-2 physics framework. This model explicitly tracks gas cooling and heating down to low temperatures and incorporates direct, multi-channel models for stellar evolution and feedback. Feedback mechanisms include momentum from stellar winds, radiation pressure, photoionization, photoelectric heating, and energy injection from both core-collapse and Type Ia supernovae [Wetzel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...44W/abstract). By resolving structures down to parsec scales—with gas softening lengths reaching $1\,$pc and median smoothing lengths of $25-60\,$pc at $z=0$—and utilizing initial baryon particle masses of $7070\, M_\odot$, the FIRE-2 framework allows star formation to naturally self-regulate in dense, self-gravitating molecular clouds [Wetzel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..23W/abstract).

By bridging the gap between cosmological scales and the parsec-scale ISM, Latte provides critical insights into near-field cosmology and [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]]. Specifically, the suite demonstrates that the inclusion of explicit baryonic physics naturally reconciles the cold dark matter model with small-scale observations in the Local Group. A major paradigm shift demonstrated by the Latte suite is the importance of baryonic "back-reaction" on the dark matter distribution itself, which creates highly structured phase-space features around the Solar Circle and fundamentally alters the local tidal field [Garrison-Kimmel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471.1709G/abstract), [Lawrence et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2606L/abstract). 

## Numerical Methods and Parsec-Scale Physics

Historically, standard astrophysical texts have noted that computational constraints restrict simulations to either large representative volumes or high internal resolution. Operating as a series of [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]], Latte pushes the boundary of [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] by circumventing this dichotomy. By dedicating extreme [[high-resolution-simulations|resolution]] exclusively to a Milky Way-mass halo and its immediate environment, Latte achieves a baryonic particle mass of $7070\ M_{\odot}$ [Wetzel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..23W/abstract), [Wetzel et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...44W/abstract). 

Crucially, this parsec-scale resolution enables the suite to self-consistently model individual star cluster formation and disruption. Within these models, the local star formation efficiency per free-fall time, $\epsilon_{\rm ff}$, and the momentum boost from supernova feedback, $f_{\rm boost}$, serve as fundamental parameters [Li et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...861..107L/abstract). The literature presents a nuanced distinction regarding their respective impacts. Global star formation is governed by a "gas cycling" process where feedback regulates the transition of gas between star-forming and non-star-forming states. Consequently, the global galactic star formation rate and gas depletion times are highly sensitive to the strength of stellar feedback ($f_{\rm boost}$) but remain largely independent of the local efficiency $\epsilon_{\rm ff}$ [Li et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...861..107L/abstract), [Semenov et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...861....4S/abstract). 

However, the local efficiency parameter is paramount for determining small-scale cluster properties [Li et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...861..107L/abstract), [Brown et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514..280B/abstract). Specifically, higher values of $\epsilon_{\rm ff}$ yield higher maximum cluster masses, shorter stellar age spreads within individual clusters, and higher integrated star formation efficiencies [Brown et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514..280B/abstract). When these small-scale properties are properly calibrated, the disrupted and surviving cluster populations successfully reproduce the Galactic globular cluster mass function and age-metallicity relation when extrapolated to $z=0$ [Brown et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514..280B/abstract). Resolving these parsec-scale details also allows the suite to track clustered stellar feedback in the form of superbubbles, driving multiphase outflows with mass-loading factors that closely match observational constraints across cosmic time [Porter et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.535.3451P/abstract).

To contextualize these high-resolution zoom-in results, the Latte suite is often complemented by simulations like FIREbox, which models a representative cosmological volume ($L = 22.1$ Mpc) using the exact same GIZMO-based FIRE-2 physics framework. This parallel approach validates that the detailed, parsec-scale insights derived from Latte's Milky Way analogs remain consistent with the properties of broader galaxy populations tracked across wider cosmological environments [Feldmann et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.3831F/abstract).

## Structural Evolution: Disk and Halo Formation

The Latte simulation suite provides a high-resolution framework for investigating [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] in Milky Way-mass halos, naturally yielding rotationally supported disks and extended stellar halos without relying on rigid structural priors [Wetzel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..23W/abstract). Recent studies utilizing enhanced-physics simulations suggest that such Milky Way-mass progenitors could form rotationally supported stellar disks as early as $z=6-7$ [Semenov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240918173S/abstract). To contextualize these detailed findings, the structural diversity of galaxies in large-volume simulations—such as the Milky Way and Andromeda analogues from the TNG50 simulation—serve as an important comparison sample for Latte's individual zoom-in galaxies [Pillepich et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230316217P/abstract). Analyses of the resulting rotation curves in the Latte suite have identified systematic deviations of 5-20% when standard observational inference methods, such as the Jeans equation, are applied. These systematic offsets in mock inferences potentially explain the recently observed decline in the Milky Way's outer rotation curve [Ou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250305877O/abstract).

A major focal point of the Latte suite is distinguishing between *in situ* star formation and the [[galaxy-accretion|accreted components]] of the stellar halo. While classical models often assume that stellar halos are built entirely from disrupted satellite galaxies, Latte simulations demonstrate that a substantial fraction of the local metal-rich halo ($[\mathrm{Fe/H}] > -1$) actually formed *in situ* [Bonaca et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...845..101B/abstract). These stars originally formed within the dense Galactic disk but were subsequently driven into kinematically hot, halo-like orbits via radial migration and dynamical heating. Because of their origin, these *in situ* halo stars preferentially co-rotate with the Galactic disk, a kinematic signature that strongly contrasts with the highly isotropic velocity dispersion characteristic of the metal-poor, accreted halo [Bonaca et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...845..101B/abstract).

For the surviving and disrupted satellites, the accreted stellar halo serves as a pristine fossil record of the host's accretion history. The simulations reveal that the phase-space and chemical signatures of stellar debris—specifically the abundance ratios of $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ versus $[\mathrm{Fe}/\mathrm{H}]$—are strictly governed by the progenitor's stellar mass and its time of accretion [Horta et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...943..158H/abstract). Debris in the inner halo ($R_{\rm GC} \lesssim 30$ kpc) is typically fully phase-mixed and originates from a diverse spectrum of satellite masses. In contrast, the outer halo ($R_{\rm GC} > 50$ kpc) retains distinct kinematic substructures deposited by low-mass satellites that were accreted at early epochs [Horta et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...943..158H/abstract). 

Finally, the structural evolution of the halo remains highly sensitive to recent, massive accretion events. The Latte suite has been instrumental in modeling the dynamic response of the Milky Way to the infall of massive satellites analogous to the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC). The simulations show that an LMC-mass satellite dynamically displaces the inner regions of the halo relative to the outer halo, inducing an apparent co-rotating motion among outer-halo tracers [Garavito-Camargo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..100G/abstract). This dynamical response clusters the orbital poles of existing satellites, suggesting that some observed kinematically coherent planes of satellites may be transient features driven by massive late-time infall rather than stable, primordial structures [Garavito-Camargo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..100G/abstract).

## Dwarf Galaxies and Baryonic Impacts on Dark Matter

The standard $\Lambda$ Cold Dark Matter ($\Lambda$CDM) cosmological model has historically faced significant challenges on [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|sub-galactic scales]], most notably the "missing satellites" problem (the overprediction of subhalos compared to observed dwarf galaxies) and the "too big to fail" problem (the overprediction of central kinematic densities in the largest subhalos). Early dark-matter-only (DMO) [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] failed to account for these observables in the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]]. The Latte suite directly addresses these tensions, demonstrating that high-resolution, explicit baryonic physics naturally reconciles $\Lambda$CDM predictions with observational data without necessitating alternative dark matter models [Wetzel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..23W/abstract).

**Baryonic Back-Reaction on Dark Matter Profiles**
By utilizing the FIRE-2 physics model, strong, bursty stellar feedback drives gas outflows that cause rapid fluctuations in the central gravitational potential. Studies modeling isolated [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|dwarf galaxies]] with the FIRE framework have established that for dwarfs with stellar masses $M_\star \gtrsim 2 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{M_\odot}$, this "baryonic back-reaction" dynamically transfers energy to the dark matter, flattening steep central density cusps into shallower cores [Fitts et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471.3547F/abstract). When modeled in a cosmological context within the Latte suite, similar internal feedback processes in the satellite populations yield reduced central dynamical masses and stellar velocity dispersions that align quantitatively with the observed kinematics of Local Group satellites down to masses of $10^5\ \mathrm{M_\odot}$, alleviating the too big to fail problem [Wetzel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..23W/abstract). Lower-mass ultra-faint dwarfs, however, fall below the critical feedback threshold required for core formation and generally retain their cuspy profiles in standard $\Lambda$CDM models, making them an area of active debate when testing alternative dark matter candidates [Fitts et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471.3547F/abstract). Independent of internal kinematics, [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|high-resolution models]] like Latte and EAGLE also generally predict very small spatial offsets between the centers of stellar distributions and their host dark matter halos, a theoretical consensus that places collisionless CDM in potential tension with some observational inferences in galaxy clusters [Schaller et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453L..58S/abstract).

Furthermore, baryonic back-reaction significantly affects the dark matter kinematics of the primary Milky Way-mass halo. Latte simulations reveal that the assembly of the central galaxy and the continuous tidal disruption of subhalos generate non-equilibrium kinematic structures—or "gusts"—in the dark matter wind near the Solar Circle [Lawrence et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2606L/abstract). This yields a local dark matter velocity distribution that deviates substantially from the standard Maxwell-Boltzmann approximation, introducing critical astrophysical uncertainties into the interpretation of signals for direct dark matter detection experiments on Earth [Lawrence et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.524.2606L/abstract).

**Subhalo Depletion and Tidal Destruction**
In addition to internal feedback, the Latte simulations highlight the profound impact of the host galaxy's central potential on the survival of subhalos. The central stellar disk acts as a primary engine for the tidal destruction of substructure passing through the inner halo. Comparisons between Latte runs and their corresponding DMO counterparts reveal that DMO models predict approximately twice as many subhalos within a halocentric radius of $100\ \mathrm{kpc}$. This discrepancy amplifies significantly closer to the host: within $25\ \mathrm{kpc}$, subhalo depletion in the baryonic runs is higher by a factor of $\gtrsim 5$ [Garrison-Kimmel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471.1709G/abstract). In fact, the inner $15\ \mathrm{kpc}$ of the simulated Latte Milky Way-mass halos are completely devoid of subhalos down to a mass resolution limit of $3 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{M_\odot}$ [Garrison-Kimmel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471.1709G/abstract). This efficient tidal destruction preferentially eliminates satellites on highly eccentric or radial orbits, solving the missing satellites problem through purely standard baryonic processes. Similar profound subhalo depletion near a central disk is also supported by independent models, such as the Phat ELVIS simulation suite, which pairs DMO zoom-in runs with embedded analytical galaxy potentials [Kelley et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.4409K/abstract). Further corroborating these results, the TNG50 simulation finds satellite abundances that are highly consistent with Latte, while also emphasizing the significant host-to-host scatter in the number of satellites even at a fixed halo mass [Engler et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.4211E/abstract).

## Chemo-dynamics, Galactic Archaeology, and Synthetic Surveys

The Latte simulation suite tracks the evolution of 11 explicit elemental abundances in both gas and star particles [Wetzel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...44W/abstract). This detailed subgrid chemical tracking, combined with high-resolution kinematics, makes Latte an ideal laboratory for Galactic archaeology within the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]].

To reconstruct the assembly history of Milky Way-mass halos, researchers frequently employ chemical abundance ratio distributions (CARDs). By modeling the CARDs of a host galaxy's stellar halo as a linear combination of template distributions from disrupted dwarf galaxies, it is possible to infer the properties of the progenitor systems. Using the Latte suite, this chemical tagging technique has been shown to recover the mass spectrum of accreted dwarf galaxies to a precision of $\sim 10\%$ [Cunningham et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934..172C/abstract). While the diverse star formation and chemical enrichment histories of dwarf galaxies can sometimes introduce modeling ambiguities, these failure modes are robustly identifiable via high statistical residuals in the fits [Cunningham et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934..172C/abstract). 

**The *ananke* Framework**
The advent of multidimensional kinematic and astrometric datasets, most notably from the *Gaia* mission, has inaugurated a new era of precision Galactic archaeology. To translate theoretical predictions from these [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] into the observational plane, the Latte suite utilizes the *ananke* framework. This software pipeline generates synthetic surveys that closely mimic the data structure, magnitude limits, and selection functions of *Gaia* Data Release 2 (DR2) and subsequent releases [Sanderson et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..246....6S/abstract).

The *ananke* framework operates by designating multiple "solar viewpoints" within the disk of each simulated galaxy. It calculates line-of-sight dust extinction natively tracked by the simulation's 3D gas metallicity distribution, and applies an empirical error model that replicates specific astrometric and photometric observational uncertainties [Sanderson et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJS..246....6S/abstract). Crucially, the resulting mock catalogs retain a pointer linking every synthetic star back to its generating star particle in the simulation. This functionality provides a theoretical "ground truth," allowing researchers to test the accuracy of observational inference methods directly against the exact multidimensional phase-space distribution. Kinematic analyses using the Latte suite have further revealed that the stellar halo exhibits significant field-to-field variations in velocity anisotropy ($\beta$), suggesting that the halo remains significantly un-phase-mixed even at relatively close distances of $23\ \mathrm{kpc}$ [Cunningham et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...879..120C/abstract).

The utility of the *ananke* framework has also expanded to support multi-instrument mock observations, including synthetic spectroscopic catalogs for the Apache Point Galactic Evolution Experiment (APOGEE). Because these multidimensional phase-space and chemical abundance datasets are massive—exceeding $40$ terabytes—they are distributed via dedicated SQL databases on platforms like SciServer for efficient server-side analysis [Beaton et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022RNAAS...6..125B/abstract).

## Evolution of Galaxy Formation Modeling

The historical development of [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] initially prioritized [[numerical-cosmological-simulations|collisionless dark matter dynamics]] due to severe computational limitations. High-resolution dark matter-only (DMO) zoom-in simulations, notably the Via Lactea INCITE simulation [Kuhlen et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008JPhCS.125a2008K/abstract) and the [[aquarius-simulations|Aquarius simulations]] [Navarro et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.402...21N/abstract), established the standard structural predictions for Milky Way-mass halos. These models successfully resolved thousands of self-bound subhalos and demonstrated that dark matter density profiles are better described by an [[einasto-profile|Einasto profile]] than a strictly universal power law. However, by neglecting baryonic physics, they severely overpredicted the number of observable dwarf galaxies and their central dark matter densities.

To address the limitations of DMO models, the field transitioned toward [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]]. Early and concurrent large-volume suites, such as the EAGLE and Illustris projects, incorporated gas cooling, star formation, and feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei. Because these processes operate on scales far below the resolution limit of large cosmological boxes, they required extensive feedback calibration. Subgrid efficiencies were explicitly tuned to reproduce macroscopic observables, such as the $z=0$ galaxy stellar mass function and central black hole scaling relations [Schaye et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446..521S/abstract). While successful at reproducing global population statistics, the reliance on calibrated subgrid models restricted their predictive power for the internal, parsec-scale structure of the ISM. As highlighted in retrospective reviews, transitioning from large-volume, low-resolution simulations to high-resolution zooms like Latte has been essential for revealing emergent baryonic phenomena [Faucher-Giguère et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018NatAs...2..368F/abstract).

The Latte simulation suite represents a paradigm shift toward resolving these internal structures directly. Rather than tuning feedback parameters to match global scaling relations, Latte implements explicit models for stellar winds, radiation pressure, photoionization, and core-collapse supernovae within a resolved, multi-phase ISM [Wetzel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..23W/abstract). Alongside Latte, other major efforts have advanced high-resolution zoom-in techniques. The [[auriga-simulations|Auriga Project]], for example, is a prominent magneto-hydrodynamical zoom-in suite often compared to Latte for its ability to produce realistic Milky Way-mass disks across cosmic time [Grand et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.467..179G/abstract). Building on this framework, the HESTIA project utilizes constrained initial conditions to model the specific cosmography of the Local Group [Libeskind et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.2968L/abstract). In contrast to Latte's specific focus, the [[nihao-simulations|NIHAO simulations]] suite provides a larger sample of zoom-in simulations spanning a wider halo mass range, and has recently evolved to incorporate explicit black hole formation, accretion, and feedback [Blank et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.5476B/abstract).

Despite these successes, the exact partitioning between radiative and supernova feedback in setting the mass-metallicity relation in the [[early-galaxy-formation|ultra-faint dwarf regime]] remains an active area of investigation. Rather than framing this as an unresolved tension, recent models from the EDGE simulations, evolved at high resolution ($\approx 3\ \mathrm{pc}$), demonstrate that this sensitivity is a valuable, testable prediction for probing the baryon cycle with next-generation spectrographs [Rey et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250303813R/abstract).

## Public Data Releases and Community Access

The Latte simulation suite operates with a strong commitment to open science, providing the astronomical community with comprehensive access to high-resolution [[zoom-simulations|Zoom simulations]] of Milky Way-mass halos. The primary data repositories are hosted and maintained by the Flatiron Institute, offering both raw simulation outputs and value-added data products designed to facilitate independent analysis.

The initial public data release (DR1) encompassed 20 core simulations, featuring 14 Milky Way-mass galaxies, 5 Large and Small Magellanic Cloud (LMC/SMC) mass analogs, and 4 lower-mass dwarf systems [Wetzel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...44W/abstract). This release provided 39 snapshot catalogs spanning the redshift range $z=10$ to $z=0$. The snapshot catalogs contain complete phase-space, mass, and property information for all dark matter, gas, and star particles. For the baryonic components, the data include formation times (stellar ages) and 11 individual elemental abundances, allowing for detailed chemical evolution tracking. To support studies of galactic archaeology and near-field cosmology, DR1 also provided extensive subhalo catalogs, particle tracking pointers across snapshots, and multipole basis expansions of the host halo mass distributions. Crucially, star particles in the $z=0$ snapshots are equipped with formation coordinates and an "ex situ" flag, allowing researchers to readily distinguish between stars formed *in situ* and those accreted from satellite mergers [Wetzel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..265...44W/abstract).

A subsequent second data release (DR2) significantly expanded both the temporal resolution and the physical variations available for the Latte suite [Wetzel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250806608W/abstract). DR2 increased the output frequency to 601 snapshots per core simulation, achieving a time spacing of $< 25 \text{ Myr}$ from $z \approx 99$ down to $z=0$. In addition to the fiducial FIRE-2 physics runs, DR2 released resimulations designed to assess modeling uncertainties. These variant runs include dark-matter-only counterparts, models with a modified ultraviolet background (delaying reionization to $z=7.8$), and advanced physics modules incorporating magnetohydrodynamics (MHD), anisotropic conduction, gas viscosity, and cosmic-ray injection and transport [Wetzel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250806608W/abstract). Comprehensive [[halo-merger-trees|Halo merger trees]] are also provided to trace the assembly histories of the host halos and their satellite populations.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite the success of the Latte simulation suite in reproducing realistic Milky Way-mass galaxies and resolving the "missing satellites" and "too big to fail" problems via explicit baryonic feedback [Wetzel et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...827L..23W/abstract), several critical questions and theoretical challenges remain at the frontier of near-field cosmology.

A major ongoing area of research involves the dynamical impact of [[galaxy-accretion|massive satellite accretion]]. The recent pericentric passage of the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC) is now understood to drastically reshape the phase-space distribution of the Milky Way's dark matter and satellite populations. Fully disentangling these transient, LMC-induced dynamical perturbations from the secular evolution of the Milky Way remains a primary objective [Garavito-Camargo et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..100G/abstract).

The framework of Latte is also increasingly being used to test the microphysics of dark matter, though the interplay between exotic dark matter and baryonic physics introduces new degeneracies. For instance, simulations incorporating self-interacting dark matter (SIDM) with a cross-section of $\sigma/m = 1 \text{ cm}^2 \text{ g}^{-1}$ predict that the dark matter halo's minor axis remains aligned with the stellar disk out to $200-400 \text{ kpc}$ [Baptista et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...958...44B/abstract). In contrast, standard CDM halos diverge in orientation beyond $\sim 30 \text{ kpc}$. However, interactions with massive satellites can independently align the halo's major axis, leaving it ambiguous whether observed alignments are a signature of SIDM or simply the byproduct of a recent major merger. In addition to SIDM, researchers utilizing the same FIRE-2 physics framework have explored "Atomic Dark Matter" (aDM) variants in isolated dwarf galaxies. These independent simulations demonstrate that a strongly dissipative aDM subcomponent can drastically alter internal kinematics, enhancing the central dark matter densities of classical dwarf galaxies by over an order of magnitude [Roy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982..175R/abstract). Similarly, explorations of Fuzzy Dark Matter (FDM) in zoom-in simulations highlight that quantum-induced cores in low-mass halos are difficult to distinguish from baryon-driven cores using stellar observables alone, further complicating the search for exotic dark matter signatures [Nori et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241109733N/abstract).

Looking forward, extending the predictive power of Latte requires the inclusion of previously omitted physics and an expansion in statistical scale. While the FIRE-2 framework excels at modeling stellar feedback, characterizing the exact role of active galactic nuclei (AGN) feedback remains an open challenge. Although AGN feedback is known from larger [[cosmological-simulations|Cosmological simulations]] to be the primary driver of quenching in [[high-mass-galaxies|High-mass galaxies]], its exact small-scale implementation (e.g., thermal versus kinetic modes [Grayson et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251019924G/abstract)) and its influence on the matter power spectrum in Milky Way-mass environments are highly uncertain and remain a point of tension in the literature [Bigwood et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251015822B/abstract), [Bigwood et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250116983B/abstract). Additionally, future directions involve fully exploring the non-thermal components, such as magnetic fields and cosmic rays, recently introduced in the DR2 data set [Wetzel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250806608W/abstract). Determining whether cosmic rays systematically alter galactic outflow mass-loading or the multiphase structure of the circumgalactic medium constitutes a crucial next step for [[next-generation-cosmological-simulations|high-resolution galaxy models]]. Finally, a significant future direction is overcoming the statistical limitations of the suite's sample size. To accurately disentangle halo-to-halo cosmic variance from underlying astrophysical uncertainties, researchers have recently launched the DREAMS Project. Operating as an independent, large-scale simulation suite that incorporates black hole feedback, DREAMS contextualizes Milky Way physics using a new statistical sample of 1,024 simulated galaxies [Rose et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251200148R/abstract).
