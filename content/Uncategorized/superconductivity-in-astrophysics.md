# Superconductivity and Superfluidity in Astrophysics

**Superconductivity and superfluidity in astrophysics** refer to the emergence of highly degenerate, superfluid-like states within the ultra-dense environments of compact objects, most notably neutron stars. Unlike terrestrial phase changes observed at cryogenic temperatures, the extreme conditions within these cosmic bodies prompt a macroscopic phase transition to a superfluid state during their cooling phases [Longair et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011hea..book.....L/abstract). This macroscopic quantum phase transition fundamentally alters the thermodynamic, rotational, and magnetic properties of the star, establishing compact objects as premier cosmic laboratories for studying degenerate matter at and beyond nuclear saturation density [Fryer et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956...19F/abstract).

The eventual transition to a superfluid state dictates the macroscopic evolution and observable phenomena of pulsars and magnetars. In the liquid core, this transition is inextricably linked to the internal magnetic field evolution. For young compact objects, particularly newly born magnetars, the decay of extreme $10^{16}$ Gauss magnetic fields serves as a massive internal heat source. This thermal heating strongly couples with the early cooling processes, significantly delaying the transition to a superfluid state [Dall'Osso et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1869D/abstract).

At the even more extreme densities theorized to exist in the deepest cores of massive neutron stars or during core-collapse supernovae, hadronic matter may undergo a further [[qcd-in-astrophysics|quantum chromodynamics (QCD) phase transition]] [Denton et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.111j3006D/abstract). In this ultra-dense regime, models of QCD in astrophysics predict the emergence of deconfined [[quark-matter|Quark matter]]. However, the exact equation of state (EOS) for deconfined matter and the critical thresholds required to trigger these high-density phase transitions remain major open questions in modern theoretical astrophysics. Ongoing and future multi-messenger diagnostics—integrating X-ray thermal emission data, gravitational wave signatures of internal dynamics, and time-resolved supernova neutrino profiles—aim to empirically constrain these exotic quantum states and resolve the phase diagram of supra-nuclear matter.

## Introduction to Cosmic Superfluidity

Compact astrophysical objects, particularly core-collapse remnants, serve as crucial cosmic laboratories for probing matter at extreme pressures and nuclear densities [Fryer et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956...19F/abstract). Understanding these transformations requires acknowledging the physical basis of cosmic bodies, where thermal physics and gas laws are fundamentally essential to the interpretation of stellar structure and evolution [Shore et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002tma..book.....S/abstract). The precursors to these compact objects evolve within the broader context of the interstellar medium, where intricate feedback loops and chemical phases provide the foundational environment for stellar evolution and subsequent gravitational collapse [Klessen et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016SAAS...43...85K/abstract). In these early phases, key magnetohydrodynamics and plasma physics processes are essential for determining the overall rate, efficiency, and initial mass function of star formation [Zweibel et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S.334Z/abstract). To comprehensively model these environments, it is necessary to incorporate the fundamental physics of radiative processes, ionization, and heating in the interstellar medium [Draine et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011piim.book.....D/abstract). Furthermore, observations of tiny-scale atomic and ionized microstructures on the Astronomical Unit (AU) scale serve as valuable probes of magnetized, turbulent dissipation within this interstellar gas [Stanimirović et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ARA&A..56..489S/abstract). 

From these initial conditions arises the presupernova evolution of massive stars—typically between 13 and 120 solar masses—which eventually undergo catastrophic core collapse [Limongi et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017hsn..book..513L/abstract). These massive stars play a central role in the reionization of the universe and drive the chemical evolution of the interstellar medium [Kennicutt et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IAUS..227....3K/abstract). Such supernova explosions are fundamentally transformative, enriching the Galaxy with vital elements like oxygen, iron, and calcium, and strongly influencing the birth of subsequent stellar generations [Burrows et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000Natur.403..727B/abstract). Locally, solar wind and heliospheric particles act as immediate witnesses to the composition of matter from the galactic reservoir at the time of our solar system's formation [Bochsler et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IAUS..257...17B/abstract).

In ultra-dense remnant environments, standard plasma transitions into degenerate states that are well-described by Fermi liquid theory. There is strong theoretical consensus that newly born neutron stars and magnetars eventually undergo a macroscopic phase transition to a superfluid state. For young magnetars, however, the decay of massive internal magnetic fields provides intense thermal heating that significantly delays the onset of this superfluid phase. During this prolonged, pre-superfluid epoch, the magnetic field influences the macroscopic equilibrium shape of the star, dictating how magnetic flux is distributed and driving early-stage gravitational wave emission from the rapidly rotating, ultra-magnetized neutron star long before it transitions to a superfluid state [Dall'Osso et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1869D/abstract).

At the even higher densities found in the deepest cores of neutron stars, or during the highly dynamic environment of a core-collapse supernova, matter may undergo a further [[qcd-in-astrophysics|quantum chromodynamics (QCD) phase transition]] [Denton et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.111j3006D/abstract). Theoretical models of QCD in astrophysics suggest this transition could lead to the formation of [[quark-matter|Quark matter]]. However, the literature remains explicitly ambiguous in this domain; significant uncertainties persist regarding the exact EOS for deconfined matter and the precise astrophysical thresholds required to trigger such high-density phase transitions.

## Historical Development of the Theory

The historical overview of dense matter models and superfluidity in theoretical astrophysics spans a half-century of development that saw the overarching paradigm shift from static to deeply evolving cosmological models [Ostriker et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ARA&A..54....1O/abstract). The history of astrophysical theory is enriched by the profound contributions of Donald Lynden-Bell, particularly through the concepts of violent relaxation and the thermodynamics of galaxies, which famously introduced the idea of negative heat capacities [Evans et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200709474E/abstract). The mathematical groundwork for understanding these extreme environments was established using relativistic fluid dynamics, which serves as a foundational mathematical tool for modeling the internal stability and gravitational collapse of neutron stars [Anile et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989LNM..1385.....A/abstract). A general foundational understanding of the end states of stellar collapse, as well as the intricate interactions between radiation and matter in stellar astrophysics, is critical to modeling these objects [Choudhuri et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010asph.book.....C/abstract). 

Concurrently, the historical context and general relativistic derivation of black hole properties, such as mass and spin, provided critical advancements in describing collapsed states of matter [Fabian et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191104305F/abstract). Concurrently, the advancement of numerical methods, particularly robust magneto-hydrodynamic simulations, became essential for investigating the active environments and radiation imprints of astrophysical black holes [Fendt et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Univ....5...99F/abstract). These milestones evolved alongside parallel developments in relativistic astrophysics, including the postulation of massive black holes in the early Universe [Volonteri et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012RPPh...75l4901V/abstract), as well as historical upper limits placed on the density of magnetic monopoles derived from the survival of galactic and stellar magnetic fields [Byrne et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975Natur.253...27B/abstract). Over the last three decades, studying dynamical processes in the vicinity of supermassive black holes within galactic nuclei has also emerged as a key focus area of relativistic astrophysics [Merritt et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013degn.book.....M/abstract). Over the decades, the theoretical framework has transitioned from idealized analytical treatments into complex, multi-physics simulations that bridge nuclear physics, relativistic fluid dynamics, and high-performance computing. Such complex simulations are buttressed by advances in laboratory astrophysics—spanning atomic, molecular, plasma, and nuclear physics—which are fundamentally required to fully realize the potential of new cosmological observations [WGLA et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0902.4666W/abstract). Today, compact objects are widely recognized as premier cosmic laboratories for probing matter at extreme nuclear densities.

A major milestone in the historical development of these models was the realization that the macroscopic observable properties of neutron stars—such as their cooling rates, spin evolution, and magnetic field decay—are strongly coupled to their internal dynamical states. The incorporation of accurate thermal and magnetic models into modern astrophysical simulations has revealed crucial implications for multi-messenger astronomy. Specifically, it is now understood that the intense thermal coupling and magnetic field decay in young magnetars strongly govern their evolution *before* they cool sufficiently to become superfluids, and it is during this early, hot pre-superfluid stage that rapidly rotating, ultra-magnetized neutron stars emit their early-stage gravitational waves [Dall'Osso et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1869D/abstract).

In recent years, the frontier of dense matter models has expanded to consider the extreme cores of neutron stars, where the density may substantially exceed the nuclear saturation threshold. This has led to the exploration of [[quark-matter|Quark matter]] and the broader role of [[qcd-in-astrophysics|QCD in astrophysics]]. Theoretical models now hypothesize that at sufficiently high densities and low temperatures, hadronic matter may undergo a phase transition to deconfined matter. Identifying the exact EOS of the deepest neutron star interior remains an active and unresolved challenge in modern theoretical astrophysics.

## Superfluidity in Neutron Star Cores

The interior of a neutron star provides a unique cosmic laboratory for probing degenerate matter at and above nuclear saturation density. As a newly born neutron star or magnetar cools from its violent formation, its internal temperature eventually drops below a critical threshold, prompting a macroscopic phase transition. The liquid core undergoes a transition to a superfluid state, a phase that fundamentally alters the star's long-term internal properties and macroscopic behavior.

The emergence of superfluidity has profound implications for the star's magnetic field evolution. In the highly dense environment of a fully degenerate neutron star core, the magnetic field is intimately tied to the plasma dynamics. This coupled thermal and magnetic evolution directly impacts long-term pulsar dynamics. Before the core temperature drops enough to initiate the superfluid phase, the structural effects of the intense internal magnetic field on the star's equilibrium shape strictly govern the electromagnetic torque exerted on the newly born oblique rotator [Dall'Osso et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1869D/abstract). Furthermore, newborn neutron stars rotating more rapidly than $\sim 100$~Hz may be subject to the r-mode instability, potentially spinning down over the course of a year while emitting continuous gravitational waves [Owen et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AIPC..523...55O/abstract). 

Despite the consensus that compact objects eventually transition into these quantum fluid states, significant uncertainties remain regarding the exact composition and EOS of the deepest inner core. It is currently ambiguous whether the central core consists purely of nucleonic matter or undergoes a further phase transition to deconfined [[quark-matter|Quark matter]]. Until nuclear physics inputs are further constrained via [[qcd-in-astrophysics|QCD in astrophysics]], models of cooling rates, magnetic field decay, and particle acceleration in pulsar-like environments must account for a wide range of possible inner core states.

## QCD Phase Transitions and Deconfined Quark Matter

The interior of compact objects and the core-collapse environments of supernovae serve as unique cosmic laboratories for probing matter at and beyond nuclear densities [Fryer et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956...19F/abstract). Under such extreme conditions, hadrons may undergo a [[qcd-in-astrophysics|QCD phase transition]], dissolving into deconfined [[quark-matter|Quark matter]]. The exact equation of state for deconfined matter at these densities—and by extension, the potential existence and precise internal structure of theorized hybrid stars—remains a subject of significant uncertainty. The application of QCD in astrophysics aims to model these extreme states, but definitive observational confirmation of internal Quark matter is still an active and unresolved area of research. 

Exploring extreme environments extends beyond individual stellar collapses to galactic scales, where there is broad consensus that active galactic nuclei share a central engine consisting of a supermassive black hole fueling the system via accretion [Krolik et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999agnf.book.....K/abstract). Broader contextual models, such as hydrodynamic cosmological simulations, are crucial for capturing the connection between massive black hole growth and galaxy formation across cosmic dawn [Di Matteo et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230411541D/abstract).

To constrain compact interior theories, astrophysicists look to multi-messenger diagnostics of core-collapse supernovae [Fryer et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956...19F/abstract). Because supernovae are fundamentally neutrino-driven phenomena, their emission carries an unprecedented wealth of information about the nuclear and particle physics of the collapsing core. Recent research demonstrates that the time delay of the neutrino signal and the Mikheyev-Smirnov-Wolfenstein (MSW) effect within the dense stellar environment can be used to probe the masses of individual neutrino eigenstates [Denton et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.111j3006D/abstract). Furthermore, if a QCD phase transition occurs within the supernova, it provides an independent, additional source of information alongside these neutrino mass signatures [Denton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241113634D/abstract), [Denton et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.111j3006D/abstract). These features are most pronounced during sharp changes in the flux, such as the initial neutronization burst. Understanding these fundamental particle properties provides critical context for the ongoing search for exotic phases in the deepest cores of extreme compact objects.

## Influence on Magnetic Field Stability and Rotational Dynamics

Neutron stars act as premier cosmic laboratories for studying matter at nuclear densities. Following their birth as rapidly rotating, ultra-magnetized objects, these compact stars undergo a period of early cooling before the core ultimately transitions into a superfluid state. This eventual phase transition profoundly alters the internal dynamical state of the object, permanently coupling its thermal, magnetic, and rotational evolution. As the debris of stellar evolution, magnetars represent some of the most extreme forms of strongly magnetized matter in the Universe [Gómez de Castro et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007uasb.conf.....G/abstract).

A pulsar's observable rotational stability is externally governed by the electromagnetic torque acting on the star, which continuously extracts angular momentum and causes the spin rate to decline. While the exact EOS for high-density [[quark-matter|deconfined matter]] remains an area of significant uncertainty, the precise rotational dynamics provide crucial indirect constraints on exotic interior phases, broadly informing the application of [[qcd-in-astrophysics|QCD in astrophysics]].

Furthermore, understanding the timescale of magnetic field decay is paramount. Modeling the resistive properties of this degenerate plasma is critical; for example, resistive general-relativistic magnetohydrodynamics (GRMHD) is essential for simulating magnetic reconnection and current sheet formation within neutron-star magnetospheres [Ripperda et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJS..244...10R/abstract). In these extreme regimes, magnetic reconnection phases can arise from plasmoid instabilities in current sheets, leading to multiple X-line reconnection events analogous to processes seen in both laboratory and broader astrophysical plasmas [Ji et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhPl...18k1207J/abstract). The structural properties of these fields can also be informed by the multiscale relationship between magnetic field strength and gas density (the B-n relation) derived from Bayesian analysis of MHD simulations [Whitworth et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240718293W/abstract). This is complemented by an understanding of the broad phenomenology of magnetic fields across cosmic scales, stretching from filaments and voids to vast galaxy clusters [Kronberg et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016cmf..book.....K/abstract). These local phenomena are inextricably linked to wider cosmological properties, including the possibility that cosmological magnetic fields with non-vanishing magnetic helicity were generated in the early universe and survived until the epoch of galaxy formation [Semikoz et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IJMPD..14.1839S/abstract). Additionally, exploring how fully convective M dwarfs generate magnetic fields above presumed saturation limits in dipole states offers a comparative perspective for understanding the powerful dynamos of neutron stars [Shulyak et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017NatAs...1E.184S/abstract). 

In the extreme environments of newly born magnetars, the decay of internal magnetic fields is strongly coupled to the thermal history of the neutron star at very early stages. The dissipation associated with the movement and decay of these internal fields provides a substantial internal heat source that drastically delays the onset of the superfluid transition, driving the early-stage rotational stability and continuous gravitational wave emission of the star while it remains in this hot, pre-superfluid regime [Dall'Osso et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1869D/abstract). Ultimately, the interpretation of electromagnetic torque and field stability continues to be an active front in resolving the internal chemical stratification of neutron stars.

Beyond internal stellar mechanics, a realistic population of gamma-ray emitting supernova remnants provides the essential energy budget necessary to explain the observed flux of hadronic cosmic rays below the knee [Becker Tjus et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016APh....81....1B/abstract). This context includes mapping the specific properties of charged galactic cosmic rays across various energy ranges, such as the prominent 'knee' at $4 \times 10^{15}$ eV and the subsequent transition to extragalactic cosmic rays [Castellina et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1110.2981C/abstract). Understanding cosmic-ray transport is also a prerequisite for dark matter indirect searches, as it defines the astrophysical background necessary to identify potential annihilation or decay signatures [Gaggero et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180200636G/abstract).

## Thermal Evolution and Cooling Rates

The thermal evolution of young compact objects is initially driven by rapid, volumetric neutrino emission. However, as the internal temperature inevitably drops, the ultra-dense matter within the stellar core undergoes a phase transition to a superfluid state. This transition fundamentally alters the star's cooling rates and overall thermal history once established.

The precise impact of thermal evolution and late-stage phase transitions on cooling curves is heavily dependent on the internal dynamical state and the EOS of the compact object. In the extreme environment of newly born magnetars, there is strong consensus that the thermal trajectory of these rapidly rotating, ultra-magnetized neutron stars is inextricably linked to the decay of their magnetic fields long before the core becomes a superfluid. For magnetars harboring internal toroidal fields on the order of $\sim 10^{16}$ G, the decay of the magnetic field acts as an intense internal heating source that strongly couples with the early-stage cooling. This coupling significantly slows the thermal evolution and explicitly delays the transition to superfluidity; the internal magnetic field can remain at $\sim 10^{16}$ G for at least $10^3$ years, during which the core temperature is sustained at levels higher than several times $10^8$ K, preventing the superfluid phase from immediately taking hold [Dall'Osso et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1869D/abstract).

Despite these established models, significant theoretical uncertainties remain regarding the exact EOS of degenerate matter at nuclear densities. Predicting the exact cooling rates is complicated by ambiguities in the core's composition. The potential presence of [[quark-matter|Quark matter]] in the deepest regions of the core would introduce exotic cooling channels governed by [[qcd-in-astrophysics|QCD in astrophysics]]. 

While neutron stars offer a primary laboratory for nuclear-density phase transitions, the study of cooling in degenerate matter extends to other compact objects. In white dwarfs, cooling rates and fundamental particle properties are constrained through asteroseismology. This technique enables precise measurements of stellar mass and core chemical stratification, allowing researchers to probe neutrino emission and degenerate cooling in a lower-density regime [Althaus et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&ARv..18..471A/abstract). Moreover, analyzing the internal structure and EOS of magnetized white dwarfs provides critical complementary insights, as they serve as parallel rotating magnetosphere laboratories to neutron stars [Terada et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.4053T/abstract). Moving from stellar to cosmological implications, tight constraints on stellar remnants like white dwarfs and neutron stars as dark matter candidates have been evaluated based on chemical overproduction limits [Freese et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph..7444F/abstract). Additionally, the exact determination of thermal and chemical properties in stars is inherently complex; for instance, inelastic hydrogen atom collisions introduce persistent uncertainties in non-LTE modeling and chemical abundance determinations for metal-poor stars [Barklem et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JPhCS.397a2049B/abstract).

## Multi-messenger Diagnostics of Dense Matter

The internal dynamical state of compact objects serves as a cosmic laboratory for matter at nuclear densities. Determining the precise EOS and the conditions under which neutron star matter transitions into a superfluid phase relies increasingly on multi-messenger astronomy. By combining data from gravitational waves, supernova neutrinos, and X-ray emissions, astrophysicists can place stringent empirical constraints on the microscopic properties of dense matter. These targeted observations are complemented by our understanding of larger-scale reservoirs, such as the intergalactic medium, which tracks baryons over cosmic time and provides vital context for large-scale structure and galaxy evolution studies [Tejos et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250412539T/abstract).

**Gravitational Waves and Rotational Dynamics**
Newly born magnetars and rapidly rotating neutron stars offer a primary target for gravitational wave (GW) searches. Theoretical models indicate that the early-stage GW emission of newly born magnetars is driven by dynamics that occur *prior* to the transition to a superfluid state [Dall'Osso et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1869D/abstract). Because an internal toroidal magnetic field on the order of $10^{16}$~G strongly couples to the star's early cooling rate, it acts as a massive heat source that drastically slows thermal dissipation. As a result, the core temperature can remain above several times $10^8$~K for at least $10^3$ years before the full onset of superfluidity. The GW signals generated by such ultra-magnetized, oblique rotators during this hot, pre-superfluid regime are predicted to be detectable by current-generation interferometers out to the Virgo cluster, with an estimated event rate of $\sim 1 \text{ yr}^{-1}$ [Dall'Osso et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1869D/abstract). Furthermore, rapidly rotating newborn neutron stars (spinning above $\sim 100$~Hz) may be subject to gravitational radiation reaction instabilities driven by fluid r-modes, which extract angular momentum over the first year of the star's life and produce a potentially detectable GW background [Owen et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AIPC..523...55O/abstract).

**Supernova Neutrinos and Nuclear Physics Tracers**
Core-collapse supernovae emit a prodigious neutrino flux that encodes the thermodynamic history of the explosion and the EOS of the collapsing core. The pursuit of mapping these high-energy neutrinos necessitated the historical development of kilometer-scale neutrino telescopes [Halzen et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996astro.ph..5014H/abstract). Today, facilities like the ANTARES neutrino telescope actively search for astrophysical neutrinos and provide critical constraints on neutrino emission aligned with gravitational wave events [Spurio et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017NPPP..291..175S/abstract), while experiments like Baikal-GVD have recently confirmed high-energy astrophysical neutrino fluxes associated with blazars [Troitsky et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhyU...67..349T/abstract). 

Observations of supernova neutrinos provide unique multi-messenger diagnostics. Historical events like SN1987A established early upper bounds on the absolute mass of neutrinos [Bilenky et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003PhR...379...69B/abstract). Looking forward, the detection of a galactic core-collapse supernova via coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering (CE$\nu$NS) could determine the nuclear neutron distribution radius, $R_n$, to an unprecedented precision of $\sim 0.1\%$, while the nuclear neutron skin thickness, $R_{\text{skin}}$, could be constrained to an absolute precision of $\sim 0.006$~fm for lead [Huang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106l3034H/abstract). Additionally, the time delay and Mikheyev-Smirnov-Wolfenstein (MSW) effects imprinted on the supernova neutrino burst carry signatures of individual neutrino masses [Denton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241113634D/abstract), [Denton et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.111j3006D/abstract). The potential transition to [[quark-matter|Quark matter]], governed by the rules of [[qcd-in-astrophysics|QCD in astrophysics]], provides an alternative and additional source of information regarding the stellar interior [Denton et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241113634D/abstract), [Denton et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.111j3006D/abstract). Within our own solar system, the Sun serves as a unique laboratory for dark matter detection; the absence of weakly interacting massive particle (WIMP) signatures in the solar core via neutrino and helioseismic data strongly disfavors certain WIMP mass ranges [Turck-Chièze et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012RAA....12.1107T/abstract).

**X-ray Emissions, Cosmic Rays, and Plasma Constraints**
Electromagnetic observations, particularly in the X-ray band, trace the long-term cooling curves of neutron stars. Observations of supernova remnants surrounding Anomalous X-ray Pulsars (AXPs) and Soft Gamma-Ray Repeaters (SGRs) constrain the electromagnetic energy input from the central neutron star to be $\leq 10^{51}$~erg [Dall'Osso et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1869D/abstract). These X-ray luminosity measurements are direct indicators of the internal thermodynamic state. Multi-wavelength efforts also clarify broader stellar lifecycles: for example, the detection of radioactive $^{26}\text{Al}$ provides definitive evidence of ongoing nucleosynthesis by massive stars in the Galaxy [Prantzos et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996PhR...267....1P/abstract). Refined assessments detail the impact of revised $^{27}\text{Al}(p, \alpha)^{24}\text{Mg}$ reaction destruction rates on interpreting Galactic $^{26}\text{Al}$ abundances and core-collapse supernova rates [La Cognata et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhLB..82636917L/abstract). Furthermore, the detailed detection of CNO isotopes, such as $^{13}\text{C}$ and $^{14}\text{N}$, serve as important complementary tracers of nucleosynthesis in low-mass stars and robust indicators of broader galactic evolution [Audouze et al. (1977)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977ASSL...67....3A/abstract). Similarly, observations of cosmic radio-isotopes provide a clear window into the sources and chemical evolution of gas in galaxies via gamma-ray spectroscopy [Diehl et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ASSL..453.....D/abstract).

Additionally, understanding cosmic-ray acceleration in supernova remnants—challenged by precise measurements of proton and helium spectra from the PAMELA experiment [Adriani et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...332...69A/abstract)—and tracing cosmic-ray electrons through their unique energy-loss processes via TeV observations [Picozza et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013APh....43..163P/abstract) helps complete the multi-messenger picture of compact object environments. Ground-based observatories such as the MAGIC telescopes play a complementary role by providing high-sensitivity observations of supernova remnants, pulsars, and cosmic ray signatures in the TeV energy range [Doro et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017NCimC..40..115D/abstract). Scaling up to galactic sizes, the role of cosmic ray feedback and plasma physics acts as a crucial driver of galactic winds, significantly preventing excessive star formation in clusters [Ruszkowski et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&ARv..31....4R/abstract). Likewise, observing cold fronts in galaxy clusters provides excellent probes for constraining macroscopic plasma transport properties, including thermal conduction and viscosity [Zuhone et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016JPlPh..82c5301Z/abstract). On larger galactic scales, precise 3D velocity measurements for stars and stellar streams have proven capable of revealing the mass, shape, and lumpiness of the dark matter halo in the Milky Way [Shaya et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S.274S/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The theoretical framework governing superfluidity in compact objects relies heavily on the EOS of matter at extreme densities. However, the precise nature of the high-density EOS remains a major tension in the literature, driven by significant nuclear physics uncertainties—such as poorly constrained reaction cross-sections and thermodynamic properties at supra-nuclear densities [Côté et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191100035C/abstract), [Fernandez et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019clrp.2020...41F/abstract). Further complicating empirical validations is the fact that abundance measurements of neutron-capture elements in metal-poor stars are highly sensitive to errors in effective temperature and other stellar model parameters [Lusk et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PASP..123..253L/abstract). A primary unresolved issue is the exact topology of the phase diagram for [[qcd-in-astrophysics|QCD in astrophysics]], particularly concerning the conditions required for a phase transition to deconfined [[quark-matter|Quark matter]] in the cores of neutron stars. 

Additionally, theoretical models must reconcile stellar evolution with broader cosmological puzzles, such as the precise nature of dark matter. While standard $\Lambda$CDM theory demonstrates excellent agreement with large-scale observations, significant unresolved issues remain regarding the physics of galaxy centers and dark matter substructure [Primack et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005NewAR..49...25P/abstract). For instance, bounds derived from high-redshift galaxy luminosity functions and Planck data suggest that dark matter particles might reside in the 2-3 keV mass range (warm dark matter) as an alternative to standard GeV-scale WIMPs [de Vega et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1007.2411D/abstract), [Lapi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015JCAP...09..003L/abstract). In addressing these broader cosmological mysteries, there is growing evidence that Warm Dark Matter (WDM) particles in the keV mass scale naturally reproduce the cored halo profiles observed in galaxies [de Vega et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1307.1847D/abstract). Furthermore, dark matter interactions are evaluated as potential thermally stable heating sources—specifically through dark matter-baryon scattering—in efforts to resolve the overcooling problem in galaxy clusters [Shoji et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.528.4082S/abstract).

Despite these uncertainties, there is a strong theoretical consensus regarding the thermal trajectory of newly born magnetars. The interplay between early-stage cooling and profound magnetic dissipation acts to keep the core hot, significantly delaying the transition to a superfluid state [Dall'Osso et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1869D/abstract). Furthermore, it is during this prolonged, pre-superfluid phase that these ultra-magnetized, rapidly rotating newborn neutron stars are predicted to emit continuous gravitational waves [Dall'Osso et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1869D/abstract). Late-stage transitions to superfluidity and the onset of r-mode instabilities provide additional complex mechanisms that could generate distinct signals detectable by advanced interferometers over the lifespan of the star [Owen et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AIPC..523...55O/abstract).

The era of multi-messenger astrophysics has already begun to narrow the theoretical parameter space for dense matter. The historic detection of the binary neutron star merger GW170817 confirmed such mergers as dominant sites for r-process nucleosynthesis [Frebel et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ARNPS..68..237F/abstract), [Liccardo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018EPJA...54..221L/abstract). Future observations of nearby core-collapse supernovae will offer additional direct probes of the stellar interior. Time-delay measurements and Mikheyev-Smirnov-Wolfenstein (MSW) oscillation effects in supernova neutrino signals are expected to probe individual neutrino masses [Denton et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.111j3006D/abstract). Separately, coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering (CE$\nu$NS) is poised to provide high-precision constraints on the nuclear neutron skin of elements like lead [Huang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhRvD.106l3034H/abstract), while signatures of a [[qcd-in-astrophysics|QCD phase transition]] provide additional, alternative insights into the collapsing core. 

Definitively resolving the remaining ambiguities in the dense matter phase diagram will require a synergistic observational and computational approach. High-resolution numerical solvers are advancing rapidly; next-generation smoothed particle hydrodynamics codes like SWIFT will play a major role in accurately modeling planetary physics, cosmology, and galaxy formation [Schaller et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.2378S/abstract). Next-generation space telescopes, such as the proposed ATHENA mission for high-fidelity X-ray measurements [Fernandez et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019clrp.2020...41F/abstract) and the e-ASTROGAM mission designed to study accreting neutron stars and magnetars in the MeV-GeV energy range [Tatischeff et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016SPIE.9905E..2NT/abstract), will be critical. When combined with upcoming data from upgraded gravitational wave observatories, advanced hydrodynamic simulations, and experimental rare-isotope beam facilities, these next-generation multi-messenger tools hold the potential to definitively map the emergence and physical impact of macroscopic quantum phases in astrophysical environments.
