## Article

**High-Redshift Galaxy Observations** constitute a major subfield of [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] dedicated to [[ultra-high-z-galaxy-search|identifying and characterizing the first generations of galaxies]], primarily focusing on the Epoch of Reionization ($z \gtrsim 6$) and the subsequent peak of cosmic star formation. The fundamental goals of this field are to trace the early [[stellar-mass-function|assembly of stellar mass]], understand the physical state of the primordial interstellar medium (ISM), and constrain the ionizing sources responsible for the phase change of the intergalactic medium. Historically, the search for the earliest cosmic structures relied on extreme, heavily biased beacons like powerful radio galaxies or intervening quasar absorption lines—such as damped Lyman-alpha systems, which provided the first kinematic and chemical evidence of high-redshift gaseous progenitors [Smith et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...310..583S/abstract), [Wolfe et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..230..619W/abstract). Today, the field is dominated by highly sensitive, multi-wavelength [[deep-imaging-surveys|Deep imaging surveys]] and [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]] that utilize the [[lyman-break-selection-and-galaxies|dropout technique]] alongside sophisticated [[spectrophotometric-redshifts|photometric redshift]] modeling to systematically isolate distant targets from foreground populations.

The observational landscape has been recently revolutionized by the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]], whose wide-field [[nircam|NIRCam]] imaging and multiplexed [[nirspec|NIRSpec]] spectroscopy have pushed the frontier of galaxy detection well beyond $z = 10$. Foundational deep-field programs, such as the [[jades|JADES]] survey, have successfully identified thousands of reionization-era candidates and definitively confirmed their redshifts through rest-frame optical emission lines [Eisenstein et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230602465E/abstract). These [[resolved-spectroscopic-observations|Resolved spectroscopic observations]] have unveiled a highly dynamic early universe. Instead of solely irregular, dispersion-dominated systems, observers have discovered kinematically mature, rapidly rotating disks as early as $z \sim 5$ [Nelson et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231006887N/abstract), alongside extraordinarily high ISM electron densities and rapid chemical enrichment. Concurrently, [[submillimeter-astronomy|Submillimeter Astronomy]] utilizing facilities like ALMA traces the dust continuum and molecular gas reservoirs of these early systems. By leveraging local galactic baselines to anchor scaling relations [Kennicutt et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003PASP..115..928K/abstract), astronomers attempt to map early star formation efficiencies, though extreme high-redshift environments continuously challenge local textbook assumptions.

Despite these rapid observational advancements, the influx of high-redshift data has introduced significant tensions into the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological paradigm. Most notably, ultra-deep surveys have revealed a pronounced overabundance of UV-bright galaxies at $z > 10$, igniting debate over whether this excess requires exotic cosmological modifications or simply reflects highly efficient, bursty star formation within early dark matter halos. Furthermore, observers face ongoing challenges in interpreting ambiguous source populations, such as the enigmatic [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]], which sit at a complex intersection of genuine ultra-high-redshift galaxies, heavily obscured intermediate-redshift starbursts, and local brown dwarf interlopers. This article explores the evolution of candidate selection techniques, the spectroscopic characterization of early galaxy kinematics, the critical role of strong gravitational lensing in probing the ultra-faint regime, and the open questions defining the next decade of astrophysics—from the search for Population III stars to resolving the unreliability of dust as a molecular gas tracer in the early universe.

## Historical Context: From Radio Galaxies to the Hubble Deep Fields

Before the advent of sensitive, wide-field multi-wavelength detector arrays, the search for high-redshift galaxies relied heavily on targeting extreme, highly luminous astronomical beacons. Powerful high-redshift radio galaxies and quasars at $z > 4$ were among the first objects utilized to trace the early universe, serving as highly biased markers of primordial large-scale structure and nascent proto-clusters [Djorgovski et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..5159D/abstract), [Carilli et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..240..101C/abstract). Targeted optical and infrared imaging around ultra-steep-spectrum radio sources, such as the giant $0.5$ Mpc radio galaxy 4C 23.56 at $z=2.479$, revealed complex host morphologies with enormous biconical emission-line nebulae and dense fields of candidate high-redshift companions [Knopp et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJS..109..367K/abstract). Further reinforcing the multi-wavelength nature of these early targets, deep IRAS observations of 3C radio galaxies provided pioneering evidence for the far-infrared properties of high-redshift active galaxies [Yates et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989MNRAS.241...29Y/abstract). Furthermore, the detection of polarized ultraviolet emission from high-redshift radio galaxies, such as 3C 265, suggested that a large fraction of their early UV light was scattered or non-thermal in origin [Jannuzi et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...366L..69J/abstract). 

Concurrently, astronomers probed the early interstellar medium independently of galaxy emission by studying absorption lines superimposed on background quasar spectra. For instance, Ca II absorption studies in QSO spectra showed severe underabundances in the gas phase, suggesting significant condensation of metals onto dust grains in the early universe [Robertson et al. (1988)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988ApJ...325..635R/abstract). Damped Lyman-alpha systems—characterized by neutral hydrogen column densities of $N_{\rm H\, I} > 2 \times 10^{20}$ cm$^{-2}$—were identified as the high-redshift progenitors of modern galaxy disks [Smith et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...310..583S/abstract), [Wolfe et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..230..619W/abstract). Early [[high-resolution-spectroscopy|high-resolution spectroscopic surveys]] established that these self-damped gaseous disks covered approximately $20\%$ of the sky at $z \sim 2-3$ [Smith et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...310..583S/abstract). Kinematic and chemical abundance analyses of these systems provided crucial early constraints on metal enrichment, directly connecting damped Lyman-alpha systems to the formation of the thick stellar disks seen in local galaxies [Wolfe et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..230..619W/abstract).

The landscape of [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] shifted dramatically in the 1990s with the initiation of dedicated [[deep-field-campaigns|Deep field campaigns]], most notably the [[hubble-space-telescope-deep-surveys|Hubble Space Telescope Deep Surveys]] and the [[medium-deep-survey|Medium Deep Survey]]. These deep imaging efforts enabled the routine application of the [[lyman-break-selection-and-galaxies|Lyman-break technique]], which isolates high-redshift sources by targeting the pronounced rest-frame ultraviolet drop-out caused by intergalactic neutral hydrogen absorption. Morphological classifications in the Hubble Deep Field demonstrated that the observed excess of faint, blue galaxies was largely driven by an evolving population of intrinsically faint irregular galaxies, rather than significant evolution in the spiral or elliptical populations. To trace galaxy evolution at longer wavelengths, early infrared observatories pioneered deep mapping; notably, the Infrared Space Observatory (ISO) mid-infrared survey of the Hubble Deep Field South at 6.7 and 15 $\mu$m represented a critical step in characterizing dusty components [Oliver et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.332..536O/abstract).

While the Lyman-break technique revolutionized the census of the early universe, researchers recognized that selecting galaxies strictly by their rest-frame ultraviolet light inherently missed dust-enshrouded starbursts and older, quiescent systems. To construct a more representative picture of galaxy assembly and overcome the cosmic variance inherent in small deep fields, observations transitioned toward expansive [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]]. Foundational programs like the [[noao-deep-wide-field-survey|NOAO Deep Wide-Field Survey]] scaled these photometric selection methods over 18 square degrees, pushing broadband detection limits to $B_W R I \geq 26$ AB magnitudes [Jannuzi et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999AAS...195.1207J/abstract). This combination of targeted radio searches, absorption-line spectroscopy, and pioneering deep-field photometry established the historical and statistical foundation for all modern high-redshift galaxy evolution studies. To fully exploit these fields, large-scale optical campaigns like the VLT-VIRMOS survey were subsequently initiated as major spectroscopic efforts designed to sample over 150,000 galaxies [Le Fevre et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998wfsc.conf..327L/abstract).

## Selection Techniques and Photometric Redshifts

Locating high-redshift galaxies traditionally begins with multi-band photometric surveys, as obtaining resolved [[spectroscopic-follow-up|Spectroscopic follow-up]] for millions of faint sources remains observationally prohibitive. To efficiently identify candidates and estimate their distances, observers rely on color-selection techniques and full spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting to derive [[spectrophotometric-redshifts|photometric redshifts]].

**UV-Rest Frame Selection and the Dropout Technique**
The classical [[lyman-break-selection-and-galaxies|Lyman-Break Selection and Galaxies]] (or dropout technique) leverages the sharp absorption of rest-frame ultraviolet flux below 912 Å by neutral hydrogen in the intergalactic medium and the galaxy's own interstellar medium. While highly successful at identifying high-redshift systems, UV-selected samples are strongly biased toward actively star-forming galaxies and inherently miss dust-enshrouded or passively evolving populations. 

In the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] era, the dropout technique has been pushed deeply into the [[near-infrared-astronomy|near-infrared]] (e.g., F200W-dropouts) to select ultra-high-redshift candidates at $z > 15$ [Gandolfi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202637G/abstract). However, this color-selection space suffers from significant degeneracies. High-redshift deep field searches are measurably contaminated by cool, local brown dwarfs (specifically L and T dwarfs) at a rate of approximately 0.1%, which masquerade as ultra-high-redshift candidates in color-color space [Tu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250116648T/abstract). Furthermore, faint F200W-dropouts can also be lower-redshift ($z \sim 2-6$) dusty dwarf galaxies or $z \sim 5$ strong line-emitters mimicking the near-infrared colors of a $z \sim 13$ galaxy [Gandolfi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202637G/abstract). 

**Rest-Frame Optical and Near-IR Selection**
To build a mass-selected rather than UV-selected census, surveys moved to rest-frame optical wavelengths, corresponding to the near-infrared at $z \gtrsim 2$. The deployment of large-format NIR detectors facilitated the discovery of Extremely Red Objects (EROs), defined by stringent optical-to-NIR colors such as $R - K > 5$. Spectroscopy reveals that EROs lie primarily at $0.7 \lesssim z \lesssim 1.5$ and comprise a roughly equal mix of galaxies with old stellar populations (featuring a pronounced 4000 Å break redshifted past the $R$-band) and heavily dust-reddened starbursts or AGN.

Extending this methodology to higher redshifts, the Distant Red Galaxy (DRG) criterion utilizes the color cut $J_s - K_s > 2.3$ to isolate galaxies at $z \gtrsim 2$. Like EROs, the DRG selection isolates galaxies with prominent Balmer or 4000 Å breaks alongside dusty star-forming systems. The brightest DRGs ($K_s < 20$) represent some of the most massive galaxies at these epochs, with stellar masses $M_* \gtrsim 10^{11} M_\odot$. In the $2 \lesssim z \lesssim 3$ interval, DRGs dominate over Lyman-break galaxies in both overall number density and total stellar mass density.

Broadening the search beyond foundational optical and near-infrared criteria, several key surveys have utilized wider wavelength regimes and cluster physics to map out distant systems. The FLAMINGOS Extragalactic Survey (FLAMEX) marked an early milestone as the first deep, wide-area near-infrared survey to utilize both $J$ and $K_s$ filters to investigate the massive galaxy population at $1 < z < 2$ [Elston et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...639..816E/abstract). Moving into the mid-infrared, the [[mips-spitzer-instrument|Spitzer Space Telescope]] vastly expanded high-redshift selection capabilities; the Spitzer Deep, Wide-field Survey (SDWFS) operated as a 10-square-degree multi-epoch legacy project that efficiently selected infrared-variable objects and distant galaxies [Ashby et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...701..428A/abstract). Spitzer's $24\ \mu\text{m}$ imaging further uncovered a robust population of infrared-luminous galaxies at $z > 1$ with diverse morphologies ranging from normal disks to violently interacting systems [Papovich et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004astro.ph..8454P/abstract). Additionally, high-redshift environments can be located indirectly by targeting the hot intracluster medium; observations of the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect provide a powerful method for identifying [[massive-cluster-survey|galaxy clusters]] at $z \sim 1$ and calculating their total gas and dynamical masses [Muchovej et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...663..708M/abstract). Indeed, the optical identification of Planck SZ sources has successfully discovered clusters at $z=0.7\text{--}0.9$, with some featuring strong gravitational lensing arcs [Burenin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018AstL...44..297B/abstract). However, discrepancies between X-ray and optical mass estimates in such clusters at $z \sim 1$ can indicate complex line-of-sight structures that complicate mass derivations [Cain et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...679..293C/abstract).

To aggregate the expanding infrared datasets, the Spitzer Data Fusion emerged as a critical multi-wavelength database for merging IRAC-selected sources with mid-infrared, far-infrared, and radio observations [Vaccari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015fers.confE..27V/abstract). In this wider context, the Spitzer South Pole Telescope Deep Field (SSDF) stood as one of the largest Spitzer IRAC surveys outside the Milky Way, covering 94 square degrees to study galaxy clusters and the cosmic baryon budget [Ashby et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJS..209...22A/abstract). In parallel, the Spitzer Public Legacy survey of the UKIDSS Ultra Deep Survey provided a vital contiguous resource for measuring stellar masses and evolution out to $z=6$ [Dunlop et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007sptz.prop40021D/abstract). To explicitly uncover distant overdensities, researchers utilized near-infrared follow-ups like the NEWFIRM survey of the SDWFS region, aimed at finding the first galaxy clusters using deep $J, H,$ and $K_s$ imaging [Gonzalez et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AAS...21641513G/abstract).

**Advancements in Photometric Redshifts**
Wide-field multi-wavelength campaigns—such as the [[noao-deep-wide-field-survey|NOAO Deep Wide-Field Survey]]—have long anchored the derivation of photometric redshifts by tracing broad SEDs across optical and infrared bands [Jannuzi et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999AAS...195.1207J/abstract). Recently, the inclusion of [[newfirm-medium-band-survey|medium-band filters]] has substantially improved photometric redshift precision. Incorporating [[nircam|NIRCam]] medium-band imaging has been shown to improve the scatter and reduce the catastrophic outlier rate of photometric redshift estimates by factors of 2 to 3 compared to using broadband filters alone [Suess et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240413132S/abstract), [Suess et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976..101S/abstract).

## The JWST Revolution: NIRCam and Deep Imaging Surveys

The advent of the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] has fundamentally transformed [[deep-imaging-surveys|Deep imaging surveys]], extending the frontier of galaxy detection well beyond $z = 10$. Central to this revolution is the [[nircam|NIRCam]] instrument, whose wide wavelength coverage from 0.9 to 5 $\mu$m enables the capture of the rest-frame UV and optical continuum of galaxies during the Epoch of Reionization [Bunker et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..352..342B/abstract). Unprecedented observational campaigns, such as the [[jades|JADES]] program targeting the [[goods-survey|GOODS survey]] fields, have utilized deep multi-band imaging to successfully isolate thousands of high-redshift targets via [[lyman-break-selection-and-galaxies|Lyman-Break Selection and Galaxies]] [Eisenstein et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230602465E/abstract), [Rieke et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..352..337R/abstract).

These observations have revealed a significant overabundance of UV-bright galaxies at $z > 10$ compared to previous expectations. Precise measurements of UV-continuum slopes enabled by [[nircam|NIRCam]] are critical in testing galaxy formation models, allowing astronomers to constrain stellar population ages, dust attenuation, and the potential contribution of Population III stars [Bunker et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..352..342B/abstract).

The search for the earliest galaxies has pushed selections to even longer wavelengths, notably with the identification of F200W-dropouts. These photometrically selected sources represent [[ultra-high-z-galaxy-search|candidate ultra-high-redshift galaxies]] at $z > 15$. However, as discussed in the context of selection techniques, the literature remains strongly divided on their true nature, given the potential for contamination by heavily obscured dusty dwarfs, strong line emitters, and ultracool brown dwarfs [Tu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250116648T/abstract), [Gandolfi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202637G/abstract).

To mitigate these degeneracies and push intrinsic detection limits further, modern surveys increasingly combine [[nircam|NIRCam]] imaging with strong gravitational lensing and [[newfirm-medium-band-survey|medium-band imaging]]. Programs like UNCOVER and MegaScience, which target the Abell 2744 cluster, have demonstrated that the inclusion of medium-band imaging dramatically reduces both the scatter and the catastrophic outlier rates of photometric redshifts [Bezanson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974...92B/abstract), [Suess et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976..101S/abstract). Concurrently, the [[glimpse-survey|GLIMPSE survey]] leverages the lensing power of the galaxy cluster Abell S1063 to probe intrinsic absolute UV magnitudes down to $M_{\rm UV} = -12$ at $z \sim 6$. Such capabilities provide crucial observational constraints on the extreme faint end of the UV luminosity function, directly identifying the low-mass systems primarily responsible for cosmic reionization [Atek et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251107542A/abstract).

## Spectroscopic Characterization of the First Galaxies

The advent of the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] and its [[nirspec|NIRSpec]] instrument has revolutionized the [[spectroscopy-surveys|spectroscopic characterization]] of galaxies in the Epoch of Reionization. Prior to this era, selections of early galaxies were largely restricted to photometric dropouts or rest-UV features, though dedicated campaigns like the VANDELS ESO public spectroscopic survey utilized ultra-long exposures (up to 80 hours) to study star-forming galaxies at $2.4 < z < 5.5$ [Pentericci et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...616A.174P/abstract). Furthermore, observations of Gamma-Ray Burst (GRB) host galaxies at $z \sim 5$ offered a unique window into high-redshift chemical evolution, suggesting a slower metallicity evolution compared to [[stellar-mass-function|stellar mass density]] and implying significant metal loss to the intergalactic medium [Chary et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...671..272C/abstract). To interpret these extreme environments, hydrodynamical simulations of low-mass systems at high redshift have been employed to reproduce the H$_2$ abundance and star formation efficiencies observed in these GRB hosts [Robertson et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IAUS..254...35R/abstract). Beyond the optical and near-infrared, the potential for [[high-resolution-spectroscopy|high-resolution X-ray imaging spectroscopy]] (such as that proposed for the [[astro-h|Astro-H]] mission) presents an alternative avenue to constrain the metal and energy flow out of high-redshift starburst galaxies [Paerels et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.1174P/abstract).

Early advancements in [[integral-field-spectroscopy|integral field spectroscopy]], such as VLT/SPIFFI observations of submillimeter galaxies like SMM 14011+0252, had already begun characterizing these systems as dusty precursors to massive local bulges [Eisenhauer et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Msngr.113...17E/abstract). Now, moving beyond these foundational techniques, massive spectroscopic campaigns like the [[jades|JADES]] survey have provided multiplexed, high-signal-to-noise spectra for thousands of targets, securely confirming redshifts out to $z \sim 13$ [D'Eugenio et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240406531D/abstract).

By spanning the $0.6$-$5.3 \ \mu\text{m}$ range with resolving powers up to $R \sim 2700$, NIRSpec captures critical rest-frame optical nebular emission lines (e.g., H$\alpha$, H$\beta$, [OIII], and [OII]) that were previously inaccessible for $z > 5$ sources [Bunker et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..352..342B/abstract). Spatially resolved maps of these nebular emission lines reveal that the interstellar medium (ISM) of early galaxies is highly dynamic, often featuring clumpy, extended ionized gas that is physically offset from the stellar continuum [Suess et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240413132S/abstract). 

A major breakthrough enabled by NIRSpec is the measurement of electron densities in the early ISM using the resolved [OII] doublet ($\lambda\lambda 3726, 3729$). In the gravitationally lensed galaxy MACS0647-JD at $z=10.167$, researchers measured a doublet line flux ratio of $[\text{OII}]3729/3726 = 0.9 \pm 0.3$. This corresponds to an electron density of $\log(n_e / \text{cm}^{-3}) = 2.9 \pm 0.5$, which is 10 to 100 times higher than typical local values [Abdurro'uf et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240416201A/abstract). This contradicts historical assumptions that local star-forming templates are adequate proxies for the $z > 10$ universe. Instead, observations suggest a redshift evolution scaling of $n_e \propto (1+z)^{1.2}$, driven by the increasingly compact and metal-poor nature of early galaxies [Abdurro'uf et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240416201A/abstract).

Deep spectroscopy has also facilitated the first direct metallicity measurements in the universe's first 500 million years. By utilizing [[nirspec|NIRSpec]] spectroscopy, astronomers detected the faint auroral line [OIII] $\lambda4363$ alongside strong nebular lines in MACS0647-JD. When this data was combined with mid-infrared observations from the [[miri-instrument|MIRI (instrument)]]—which successfully detected H$\alpha$ and [O III] $\lambda5008$—astronomers were able to perform an electron temperature-based calculation of the oxygen abundance:
$$ 12 + \log(\text{O/H}) = 7.79 \pm 0.09 $$
This metallicity ($\sim 0.13 Z_\odot$) is surprisingly high given the galaxy's extreme specific star formation rate ($\log(\text{sSFR/yr}^{-1}) = -7.4 \pm 0.3$), indicating highly efficient early chemical enrichment [Hsiao et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...973...81H/abstract).

## Kinematics and the Assembly of Early Stellar Mass

Traditionally, the timeline of early galaxy assembly and disk formation was thought to be protracted, with large, rapidly rotating disk galaxies primarily maturing around $z \sim 2$. Prior to this epoch, textbook models and high-resolution simulations predicted that the first billion years of cosmic time were dominated by clumpy, irregular systems characterized by a bursty star formation history and dispersion-dominated kinematics. Recent theoretical work has formalized this view into a turbulent framework for early star formation, where gas is highly supersonic and extends to halo scales without a clear phase boundary between the interstellar and circumgalactic media, differing fundamentally from the steady equilibrium disks seen at lower redshifts [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250804768S/abstract). In these models, standard local templates do not apply, and star formation is driven by high-density fluctuations in a profoundly chaotic environment [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250804768S/abstract). Early observational support for the gaseous precursors to these structures was found in damped Ly$\alpha$ absorption systems, which provide kinematic evidence of early rotating structures at $z > 3$ [Wolfe et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..230..619W/abstract).

However, the advent of the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] has initiated a paradigm shift by enabling [[resolved-spectroscopic-observations|Resolved spectroscopic observations]] of the rest-frame optical emission in galaxies at $z > 5$. While some high-redshift observations confirm that line emission (e.g., [O III]) in the Epoch of Reionization can be clumpy, extended, and spatially offset from the stellar continuum [Suess et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976..101S/abstract), others have revealed surprisingly mature, kinematically settled disks much earlier than anticipated.

A striking example of early disk maturation is "Twister-z5," a massive galaxy at $z = 5.3$ identified through combined NIRCam grism spectroscopy and [[jades|JADES]] medium-band imaging. Observations of Twister-z5 reveal significant velocity gradients across the system, indicating a massive, rapidly rotating disk with a velocity of $V_{rot} = 240 \pm 50$ km/s [Nelson et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231006887N/abstract). Despite the young age of the universe at this epoch, the galaxy possesses a substantial rest-frame optical effective radius of $r_e = 2.25$ kpc. The inferred dynamical mass is impressively high, measured at $\log(M_{dyn}/M_\odot) = 11.0 \pm 0.2$, which is approximately a factor of four greater than the stellar mass enclosed within the effective radius.

Furthermore, detailed [[spatially-resolved-galaxy-analysis|Spatially Resolved Galaxy Analysis]] of Twister-z5 indicates that both the radial H$\alpha$ equivalent width and the specific star formation rate are suppressed by a factor of $\sim 1.5$ in the central regions compared to the disk outskirts [Nelson et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231006887N/abstract). This morphology—a star-forming disk surrounding a quiescent central bulge—strongly implies an inside-out growth mechanism typically associated with much later cosmic epochs. 

The literature currently reflects an active tension regarding how to reconcile these findings. While simulations emphasize an early universe dominated by turbulent, bursty gas dynamics, the empirical discovery of dynamically mature, rotating disks after only 1 Gyr of cosmic time mandates a reevaluation of how quickly baryonic mass can assemble and settle into equilibrium in the early universe.

## Submillimeter Observations and the Dust Content of Early Systems

In the domain of [[submillimeter-astronomy|Submillimeter Astronomy]], observations of the [[dust-continuum-galactic-plane-surveys|dust continuum]] and molecular gas are essential for characterizing the cold interstellar medium (ISM) and tracking the efficiency of early cosmic star formation. The historical discovery of the extragalactic [[far-infrared-astronomy|far-infrared]] and sub-millimeter background provided crucial early evidence of these processes, revealing luminous star-forming phases in the early universe that were entirely obscured in optical bands [Franceschini et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph..9121F/abstract). Today, facilities such as the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) have enabled the routine detection of rest-frame far-infrared dust continuum and atomic fine-structure lines at high redshifts [Hatziminaoglou et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv211207452H/abstract), [Faisst et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..341...12F/abstract). Submillimeter surveys preferentially select heavily dust-enshrouded systems with exceptionally high star formation rates (SFRs), revealing a population of luminous [[ultrabright-lensed-submillimeter-galaxy|submillimeter galaxies]] whose properties differ markedly from those of typical present-day galaxies. The primary source of this massive early dust production is theoretically debated, though supernova remnants are generally modeled as the dominant contributors to dust in the primordial ISM [Marchenko et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ASPC..353..299M/abstract). This extreme dust production is also seen in active galactic nuclei; sensitive [[deep-radio-surveys|radio continuum detections]] of high-redshift dusty QSOs reveal spectral energy distributions often consistent with massive starbursts forming on the order of $1000\ M_\odot/\text{yr}$ [Yun et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...528..171Y/abstract). Submillimeter techniques are similarly invaluable in dense environments; for example, submillimeter imaging of $z \sim 0.8$ [[massive-cluster-survey|galaxy clusters]] like RCS J022434-0002.5 reveals potential luminous cluster members that are entirely missed in standard optical surveys [Webb et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...631..187W/abstract). To interpret these distant observations accurately, establishing low-redshift anchors is essential. The [[kingfish-survey|KINGFISH survey]] serves as a key far-infrared baseline, tracing the detailed ISM heating and cooling processes in nearby galaxies to contextualize the extreme properties observed at high redshifts [Kennicutt et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PASP..123.1347K/abstract). 

To trace the dense molecular cloud cores that act as the direct sites of massive star formation, astronomers rely on observations of high-dipole moment molecules such as HCN. Detections of $\mathrm{HCN}(1-0)$ in early-universe molecular emission-line galaxies and submillimeter galaxies demonstrate that the SFR per solar mass of dense molecular gas systematically exceeds the local scaling relations [Gao et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...660L..93G/abstract). Furthermore, the observed $\mathrm{HCN/CO}$ line luminosity ratios are elevated, comparable to those of local Ultraluminous Infrared Galaxies (ULIRGs) rather than normal local spiral disks. This indicates that high-redshift starbursts contain a substantially higher fraction of dense gas relative to their total molecular gas mass [Gao et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...660L..93G/abstract).

A critical and ongoing debate in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] concerns the reliability of the long-wavelength dust continuum as a proxy for total cold molecular gas. In standard practice, the dust continuum flux is used to derive the dust mass $M_{\mathrm{dust}}$, which is then converted to a total molecular gas mass $M_{\mathrm{mol}}$ via an assumed gas-to-dust mass ratio $\delta_{\mathrm{GDR}}$:

$$ M_{\mathrm{mol}} = \delta_{\mathrm{GDR}} M_{\mathrm{dust}} $$

Typically, a value of $\delta_{\mathrm{GDR}} \approx 100$ is adopted for massive, metal-rich systems. However, this textbook assumption is now in significant tension with recent targeted observations of high-redshift quiescent galaxies. ALMA $870\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ continuum observations of massive quiescent galaxies at $z \sim 1$ show surprisingly weak dust emission relative to their $\mathrm{CO}(2-1)$ luminosities [Spilker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250716914S/abstract). These measurements imply unusually high gas-to-dust ratios, estimated to range from $300$ to at least $1200$, which fall $0.4\text{--}0.8$ dex below the established CO-to-continuum correlation [Spilker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250716914S/abstract). 

The conflicting literature suggests that dust may be preferentially destroyed or less efficiently formed in quiescent systems. Consequently, the dust continuum is likely a highly unreliable tracer of the molecular gas reservoir in these galaxies [Spilker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250716914S/abstract). This systematic uncertainty implies that the cold gas content of early, evolved systems may have been significantly underestimated, complicating the interpretation of gas depletion timescales. Furthermore, assumptions regarding the spectral energy distributions (SEDs) of submillimeter sources continue to introduce strong selection effects, which must be carefully modeled to avoid biased interpretations of the high-redshift galaxy population [Blain et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001dmsi.conf..195B/abstract).

## The Role of Gravitational Lensing in Probing the Faint End

To constrain the abundance and physical properties of the faintest galaxies during the Epoch of Reionization, observers routinely exploit massive foreground galaxy clusters as natural telescopes. Through strong gravitational lensing, the flux and apparent spatial extent of background galaxies are highly magnified, bringing intrinsically faint populations at $z > 6$ into the observable regime that would otherwise remain well below the detection thresholds of current instrumentation.

Recent [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] [[deep-field-campaigns|Deep field campaigns]] heavily utilize this strategy. Early strategies recognized the utility of tunable Fabry-Perot imaging and multi-fiber spectroscopy for efficiently targeting the optimal lines-of-sight to find lensed star-forming galaxies [Hewett et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ASPC..195...94H/abstract). For example, the UNCOVER and MegaScience treasury programs targeted the massive cluster Abell 2744 (at $z = 0.308$). By combining the cluster's magnification with ultra-deep [[nircam|NIRCam]] imaging down to $29-30$ AB magnitudes, these surveys provide unparalleled spatially-resolved maps of rest-optical line emission and continuum structures in reionization-era galaxies [Bezanson et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974...92B/abstract), [Suess et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240413132S/abstract), [Suess et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...976..101S/abstract). Pushing the faint-end frontier even further, the [[glimpse-survey|GLIMPSE survey]] targets the cluster Abell S1063. Achieving intrinsic source-plane depths beyond $33$ AB magnitudes, GLIMPSE has identified hundreds of galaxy candidates spanning $6 < z < 16$ with intrinsic ultraviolet magnitudes as faint as $M_{\mathrm{UV}} = -12$ [Atek et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251107542A/abstract). Accessing this ultra-faint regime is essential for accurately anchoring the faint-end slope of the UV luminosity function.

Crucially, gravitational lensing does not merely boost the detection signal-to-noise ratio; the spatial stretching of the source enables resolved spectroscopy on sub-galactic scales. Observations of the highly magnified $z=10.167$ galaxy system MACS0647-JD provide a premier example. Using exclusive [[high-resolution-spectroscopy|high-resolution spectroscopy]] from [[nirspec|NIRSpec]], observers resolved the rest-frame UV [O II] $\lambda\lambda 3726,3729$ doublet, extracting an estimated electron density of $\log(n_e / \mathrm{cm}^{-3}) = 2.9 \pm 0.5$—significantly higher than typical values in the local universe [Abdurro'uf et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240416201A/abstract). The magnification also facilitated the [[nirspec|NIRSpec]] detection of the faint auroral [O III] $\lambda 4363$ emission line, which, when combined with [[miri-instrument|MIRI (instrument)]] detections of H$\alpha$ and [O III] $\lambda5008$, yielded the first direct gas-phase metallicity measurement at $z > 10$, constrained to $12 + \log(\mathrm{O/H}) = 7.79 \pm 0.09$ [Hsiao et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...973...81H/abstract). High-resolution spectroscopy further resolved the system into distinct dynamical components with a velocity offset of $\sim 90$ km/s, implying a dynamical mass on the order of $10^8 M_\odot$ [Worku et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251211985W/abstract). Additionally, these cluster environments offer secondary observational benefits; the search for Type Ia supernovae (SN Ia) in massive high-redshift clusters provides vital constraints on the stellar delay time and the metal enrichment history of the intracluster medium [Sharon et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AIPC..924..460S/abstract).

However, the use of cluster lenses introduces severe complexities when determining intrinsic population statistics. Because gravitational lensing conserves surface brightness, the amplification of a background source's flux is accompanied by a proportional decrease in the effective source-plane survey volume. This phenomenon, known as magnification bias, can artificially inflate the number counts of bright sources while simultaneously depleting the sampled volume for faint sources. Consequently, accurately reconstructing the intrinsic luminosity function from a lensed sample requires high-fidelity mass models of the foreground cluster to compute precise magnification maps and effective volumes [Atek et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251107542A/abstract). Uncorrected or poorly constrained magnification factors can drastically skew inferred demographic properties and global star formation rates in high-redshift populations. Consequently, expanding the catalog of well-characterized foreground lenses is vital. Ongoing efforts include the confirmation of new high-redshift ($z > 0.9$) galaxy clusters selected from the SRG/[[erosita|eROSITA]] all-sky survey ([[erass|eRASS1]]) catalog, leveraging $J$-band follow-up observations to confirm the presence of high-redshift clusters through a radially weighted colour over-density, without assuming a specific red sequence a priori, securing new targets for future gravitational lensing campaigns [Zimmermann et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250911305Z/abstract).

## Current Tensions and Challenges to ΛCDM

The advent of the [[james-webb-space-telescope-jwst|James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)]] has enabled unprecedented near-infrared observations that have revealed a significant abundance of UV-bright galaxies at $z > 10$. This excess challenges the standard predictions of $\Lambda$CDM cosmology, which historically anticipated a steeper drop-off in the high-redshift UV luminosity function based on dark matter halo assembly rates. 

Attempts to resolve this galaxy abundance tension fall into two primary categories: cosmological modifications and astrophysical evolution. Some theoretical models propose altering the cosmological background—such as introducing an Anti-de Sitter vacuum in the dark energy sector—to enhance early structure formation. However, models tuned to reproduce the $z > 10$ galaxy excess create strong tensions with the Cosmic Microwave Background and other low-redshift constraints [Chakraborty et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250902431C/abstract). Consequently, the literature increasingly favors astrophysical solutions. Recent hydrodynamical frameworks suggest that star formation in early galaxies was not confined to steady-state equilibrium disks, but instead occurred in a supersonically turbulent, bursty medium lacking clear boundaries between the interstellar and circumgalactic environments [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250804768S/abstract). These highly turbulent conditions can yield transient periods of exceptionally high star formation efficiency, temporarily boosting UV luminosities to match observed values without requiring physics beyond $\Lambda$CDM [Katz et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..44K/abstract), [Chakraborty et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250902431C/abstract).

In addition to the sheer abundance of early galaxies, kinematic observations have challenged traditional timelines for structural maturation. Standard hierarchical assembly models predict that massive, rapidly rotating disks typically mature around $z \sim 2$. However, the discovery of "Twister-z5," a massive rotating disk galaxy at $z = 5.3$, indicates that mature kinematics can emerge within the first billion years of cosmic time. Kinematic mapping reveals a rotation velocity of $V_{rot} = 240 \pm 50$ km/s and a high dynamical mass of $\log(M_{dyn}/M_\odot) = 11.0 \pm 0.2$, far exceeding the expected structural maturity at this epoch [Nelson et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231006887N/abstract). Reconciling these rapid assembly timescales with foundational $\Lambda$CDM models remains a central theoretical pursuit in [[extragalactic-astronomy|extragalactic astrophysics]].

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite the transformative capabilities of recent multi-wavelength observatories, the rapid influx of data from the Epoch of Reionization has introduced several profound astrophysical questions that will guide observations over the next decade.

A critical frontier is determining the true nature of faint [[near-infrared-astronomy|near-infrared]] dropouts and ambiguous populations like the [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]]. While some F200W-dropout sources may represent genuine ultra-high-redshift galaxies, ultra-deep searches are heavily complicated by low-redshift interlopers. Notably, cool T and L-type brown dwarfs can masquerade as high-redshift candidates in color-color space, presenting a contamination rate of approximately $0.1\%$ in extragalactic deep fields [Tu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250116648T/abstract), [Gandolfi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202637G/abstract). Moving forward, high-resolution spectroscopy will be vital to definitively classify these objects and to search for the elusive signatures of Population III stars, such as He II emission lines, in ultra-deep [[jades|JADES]] observations [Bunker et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..352..342B/abstract). Beyond standard photometric methods, astronomers are exploring the search for high-redshift water masers as a potential method for obtaining independent geometrical distances to objects in the Hubble flow, offering novel cosmological constraints [Herrnstein et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..156..275H/abstract).

In the submillimeter regime, the physical conditions of the early interstellar medium (ISM) present substantial outstanding questions. A significant tension has emerged regarding the assumption that the long-wavelength dust continuum is a reliable tracer of molecular gas. Recent Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array observations indicate that quiescent high-redshift galaxies exhibit unusually weak dust emission, implying gas-to-dust mass ratios ranging from $300$ to over $1200$ [Spilker et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250716914S/abstract). If the dust continuum is a highly unreliable proxy in these systems, estimates of early cold gas reservoirs will require fundamental recalibration.

To address the sheer volume and complexity of upcoming [[spectroscopy-surveys|spectroscopic data]], future observations will demand both wider surveys and new automated tools. The importance of next-generation multi-wavelength facilities cannot be overstated in uncovering the first massive virialized halos, the key epochs where star formation and AGN activity peak [Mantz et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019BAAS...51c.279M/abstract). For example, the proposed Chronos near-infrared spectroscopic survey aims to extract stellar kinematics and chemical abundances from a vast, mass-limited sample, greatly expanding our statistical understanding of galaxy assembly [Ferreras et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ExA....51..729F/abstract). Unsupervised machine learning architectures, specifically Variational Autoencoders, are now being deployed to systematically identify novel, unclassified galaxy populations directly from public JWST spectra without prior classification biases [Saxena et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251105439S/abstract).

Finally, the future of high-redshift astrophysics relies on bridging ultra-deep, narrow-field pointings with wide-field surveys and local baselines. Establishing a robust "ground truth" using local sources is essential for interpreting unresolved distant systems. For interpreting the warm interstellar medium at high redshifts, programs like the [[phangs-survey|PHANGS-HST-H$\alpha$ survey]] serve as foundational high-resolution nearby-galaxy baselines for characterizing warm ionized gas physics [Chandar et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025AJ....169..150C/abstract). Likewise, the MMT/Hectospec and NAOC 2.16m spectroscopic surveys of star-forming regions act as important local anchors for understanding dust extinction and elemental abundance variations [Kong et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ChA&A..38..427K/abstract). Early release observations from the Euclid mission have already demonstrated exquisite surface brightness limits of $30.5$ mag/arcsec$^2$, mapping the deep anatomy and resolved stellar populations of nearby galaxies to provide definitive low-redshift anchors [Hunt et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240513499H/abstract), [Euclid Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A.259E/abstract). As massive multi-wavelength [[future-astronomical-facilities-and-surveys|Future Astronomical Facilities and Surveys]] come online, integrating their panoramic wide-field observations with targeted JWST spectroscopy and forward-modeling cosmological simulations (such as Sphinx$^{20}$ [Katz et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..44K/abstract)) will be crucial for assembling a coherent, multi-scale picture of galaxy formation.
