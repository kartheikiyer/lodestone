## Article

The domain of extreme astrophysics encompasses environments dominated by deep gravitational potential wells, ultra-strong magnetic fields, and relativistic plasma dynamics [Longair et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011hea..book.....L/abstract). At the center of this field are [[massive-compact-objects|Massive compact objects]]—particularly neutron stars and supermassive black holes (SMBHs)—which act as the [[central-engines|Central engines]] for the most luminous phenomena in the Universe. Governed by the Kerr spacetime metric in [[black-hole-physics|Black Hole Physics]], these objects convert gravitational binding energy into immense radiative and kinetic output via accretion processes that frequently approach or exceed the [[eddington-limit|Eddington limit]] [Eardley et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975ARA&A..13..381E/abstract). This fundamental energy conversion powers [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]], where phenomenologically diverse sources are united under a common structural paradigm consisting of an accretion disk, broad and narrow line regions, and highly collimated [[relativistic-jets|Relativistic Jets]] [Krolik et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999agnf.book.....K/abstract).

Beyond the immediate vicinity of the event horizon, these extreme environments are characterized by violent transient phenomena and collisionless shock waves. Relativistic shocks, propagating through jets or supernova remnants, are the primary sites for [[particle-acceleration-mechanisms|Particle acceleration mechanisms]] such as diffusive shock acceleration. The microphysics of these shocks dictates the efficiency of particle scattering and governs the production of non-thermal radiation, including [[snr-synchrotron-emission|SNR synchrotron emission]]. For example, relativistic jets efficiently accelerate particles through shock-drift mechanisms [Kirk et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999JPhG...25R.163K/abstract), while galactic and extragalactic environments successfully accelerate cosmic rays to PeV energies and beyond [Morlino et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.504.6096M/abstract), [Simeon et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250310795S/abstract). Furthermore, the immense energy injected by these central compact objects does not remain localized; rather, mechanisms like [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion and Feedback]] directly regulate the co-evolution of galaxies, quenching star formation in massive halos and dynamically altering central dark matter distributions over cosmic time.

In the exceptionally dense stellar environments surrounding central black holes, multi-body chaotic dynamics lead to episodic, strong-gravity interactions. Among the most critical of these are Extreme Mass Ratio Inspirals (EMRIs)—where [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]] or neutron stars gradually spiral into an SMBH—and tidal disruption events (TDEs). These strong-field interactions extensively map the spacetime multipole moments of the central massive body, establishing them as vital low-frequency gravitational wave sources for future space-based observatories like LISA [Merritt et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013degn.book.....M/abstract). Additionally, the asymmetric gravitational wave emission following the coalescence of [[compact-objects-and-binary-mergers|Compact Objects and Binary Mergers]] can eject [[recoiling-supermassive-black-holes|Recoiling supermassive black holes]] from their host galaxies, producing kinematically offset quasars and powerful off-nuclear flares.

This article explores the physical mechanisms driving these extreme objects and the modern multi-wavelength observations that continually challenge classical paradigms. For example, recent findings covered herein demonstrate that massive, bulgeless spiral galaxies can natively launch megaparsec-scale jets, overturning the longstanding textbook assumption that such powerful outflows strictly require bulge-dominated elliptical hosts. Finally, the article previews several unresolved tensions at the frontier of high-energy astrophysics. These open questions include the geometrical degeneracy between Kerr black holes and exotic matter (such as boson stars) in current millimeter-wavelength interferometry, the enduring ambiguity between hadronic and leptonic emission models in young supernova remnants, and the growing realization that standard steady-state dynamical models likely overestimate the long-term astrophysical rates of EMRIs and tidal disruptions.

## Historical Development of High-Energy Astrophysics

The historical development of high-energy astrophysics has been largely defined by the effort to understand the extraordinarily luminous, compact energy sources at the centers of galaxies. The realization that quasars emit immense amounts of energy from very small spatial volumes led to the foundational Salpeter-Zeldovich paradigm in the 1960s. Proposed independently by Salpeter (1964), Zel'dovich & Novikov (1964), and Lynden-Bell (1969), this paradigm posited that the [[central-engines|Central engines]] of these objects must be supermassive black holes (SMBHs) converting gravitational potential energy into luminous radiation via accretion. Over subsequent decades, direct observational evidence—most notably the precise tracking of stars orbiting close to the Galactic center—has conclusively established supermassive black holes as the most luminous persistent radiation sources in the Universe [Fabian et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191104305F/abstract).

Early theoretical work rapidly established the criteria for gravitational collapse, the dynamics of continuous accretion flows, and the observable signatures of stellar-mass black holes in binary X-ray sources, cementing Cygnus X-1 as a prime black hole candidate [Eardley et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975ARA&A..13..381E/abstract). By the late 1990s, the field had synthesized decades of multi-wavelength observations into a cohesive Unified Model of [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]]. In this framework, phenomenologically diverse classes of active galaxies—such as quasars, Seyfert galaxies, and blazars—are understood to share a common anatomical structure: an accreting SMBH surrounded by an accretion disk, broad and narrow emission line regions, and, in some cases, [[relativistic-jets|Relativistic Jets]] [Krolik et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999agnf.book.....K/abstract). The vast observational differences among these objects are primarily attributed to varying viewing angles and mass accretion rates.

As computational capabilities advanced, the focus of the field expanded from isolated central engines to the integration of black holes into cosmological structure formation, formalizing the study of the SMBH growth history. A major theoretical pursuit has been explaining the tight scaling relations observed between SMBH masses and host galaxy properties (e.g., the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ relation). While early standard models assumed that direct AGN feedback was strictly required to couple black hole growth to galaxy evolution, recent numerical investigations highlight a theoretical tension. [[various-black-hole-accretion-models|Various black hole accretion models]], utilizing viscous disc or gravitational torque-driven mechanics, demonstrate that the co-existence of SMBHs and stellar mass can emerge naturally from a shared central gas supply, independent of direct feedback coupling [Soliman et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525...12S/abstract).

The historical trajectory of the field is also marked by the overturning of long-held paradigms. For decades, standard theory dictated that powerful, kiloparsec-to-megaparsec scale jets were almost exclusively launched from bulge-dominated elliptical galaxies. This consensus was directly contradicted by the discovery of systems like J2345-0449, a massive, bulgeless spiral galaxy driving $\sim 1.6 \text{ Mpc}$ relativistic jets [Bagchi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...788..174B/abstract). Such discoveries indicate that jet launching is fundamentally tied to the spin and advection-dominated accretion modes of the black hole, rather than the morphology of the host galaxy.

Today, the culmination of this historical progress is tested directly through precision [[black-hole-physics|Black Hole Physics]]. While millimeter-wavelength imaging by the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) and X-ray spectroscopy remain highly consistent with the Kerr metric predicted by General Relativity, a subtle tension persists in the literature: current imaging resolutions are not yet sufficient to definitively rule out exotic, non-Kerr compact objects (such as rotating boson stars or wormholes), which can theoretically produce identically blurred shadow images [Vincent et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646A..37V/abstract).

## The Physics of Kerr Black Holes and Compact Objects

Astrophysical black holes are canonically described in [[black-hole-physics|Black Hole Physics]] by the Kerr metric, a vacuum spacetime entirely defined by its mass and angular momentum. Observational efforts to map this strong-field regime rely heavily on millimeter-wavelength interferometry and X-ray spectroscopy. The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) has provided critical insights into the immediate environment of these objects. For instance, analytical models of the Galactic center black hole, Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*), indicate its shadow spans approximately $50\,\mu\text{as}$, which can be successfully resolved by the EHT's angular resolution of $\sim 20\,\mu\text{as}$ in the $0.87\text{--}1.3\,\text{mm}$ band [Vincent et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...574A..48V/abstract). Complementary X-ray binary observations with NuSTAR reveal spacetime deformation parameters that remain strictly consistent with the Kerr hypothesis [Tripathi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240108545T/abstract). 

Despite these successes, a significant theoretical and observational ambiguity remains regarding the absolute confirmation of the Kerr metric in targets like the supermassive black hole M87*. Geometric modeling indicates that non-Kerr exotic compact objects—such as rotating boson stars or Lamy spinning wormholes—can produce highly lensed features and blurred images that are virtually indistinguishable from a Kerr black hole at current EHT resolutions [Vincent et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646A..37V/abstract). While the Kerr paradigm remains heavily favored, strong-field tests of general relativity cannot yet unambiguously exclude these exotic alternatives.

The physics of the innermost accretion flow is tightly coupled to black hole spin. Minute-scale X-ray variability directly probes this near-horizon region, where infalling gas releases gravitational energy and governs the launching of a [[spin-driven-jet|Spin-driven jet]] [Kara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ARA&A..63..379K/abstract). In extreme galactic nuclei, compact object dynamics are additionally driven by multi-body interactions and gravitational wave emission. For instance, the central parsec of the Milky Way is expected to host a dense cusp of approximately 20,000 stellar-mass black holes and neutron stars surrounding Sgr A*, while higher estimates near 40,000 remnants are largely ruled out by X-ray constraints unless the accretion is extremely radiatively inefficient [Deegan et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.377..897D/abstract). Following a galaxy merger, binary black hole coalescence can result in anisotropic gravitational wave emission, imparting a kick velocity of up to several thousand $\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ [Komossa et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AdAst2012E..14K/abstract). These [[recoiling-supermassive-black-holes|Recoiling supermassive black holes]], such as the candidate system 3C 186, can carry a retinue of bound stars and present as kinematically offset quasars, potentially producing powerful off-nuclear X-ray flares via stellar tidal disruptions [Komossa et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...683L..21K/abstract), [Boschini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A.245B/abstract). 

## Accretion Dynamics and Super-Eddington Environments

The dynamics of mass transfer onto compact objects span a vast parameter space, primarily governed by the [[dimensionless-accretion-rate|Dimensionless accretion rate]] $\dot{m} = \dot{M}/\dot{M}_{\rm Edd}$ and the resulting [[eddington-ratio|Eddington ratio]]. Depending on this accretion rate, the geometry, radiative efficiency, and outflow characteristics of the central engine vary drastically.

At the [[low-accretion-rate-objects|Low accretion rate objects]] ($\dot{m} \ll 1$), the accretion flow becomes radiatively inefficient, transitioning into an advection-dominated accretion flow (ADAF). In this regime, the characteristic inflow timescale is much shorter than the cooling timescale, causing the gas to trap its thermal energy. These flows are geometrically thick, optically thin, and highly magnetized, making them ideal environments for launching powerful outflows. For example, observations of the massive spiral galaxy J2345-0449 demonstrate that an ADAF operating at a low Eddington ratio can drive megaparsec-scale [[relativistic-jets|Relativistic Jets]] [Bagchi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...788..174B/abstract). Furthermore, ADAFs and compact jet models natively predict the existence of the "millimetre fundamental plane"—a tight empirical scaling relation linking $1\,\text{mm}$ radio luminosity, intrinsic $2\text{--}10\,\text{keV}$ X-ray luminosity, and black hole mass. This plane holds universally from [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]] in the hard X-ray state up to supermassive scales [Elford et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260104007E/abstract). 

Analytic modeling of these low-density environments often relies on fully general relativistic frameworks, such as the Komissarov magnetized torus. By assuming a constant specific angular momentum and a polytropic equation of state, these models can successfully reproduce the millimeter-wavelength synchrotron morphology of Sgr A* (whose shadow spans $\sim 50\,\mu\text{as}$). However, they currently struggle to simultaneously fit centimeter-wavelength spectra, indicating that the plasma physics governing these flows is multi-component and highly complex [Vincent et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...574A..48V/abstract).

Conversely, environments characterized by $\dot{m} \gtrsim 1$ trigger sustained super-Eddington accretion. [[luminous-variable-and-hard-spectrum-x-ray-sources|Luminous variable and hard-spectrum X-ray sources]] (ULXs) serve as the premier observational laboratories for these extreme mass transfer events [Amato et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220265A/abstract). In the super-Eddington regime, intense radiation pressure dramatically inflates the accretion disk geometry, driving massive outflows and potentially leading to significant [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion and Feedback]] on the host galaxy's interstellar medium. 

Despite their brightness, the exact physical nature of ULXs remains a subject of active tension in the literature. While the discovery of coherent X-ray pulsations confirms that a subset of these objects is powered by highly magnetized, accreting neutron stars, the most luminous ULXs are frequently proposed as candidates for intermediate-mass black holes [Amato et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220265A/abstract). Disentangling the true mass of the compact object from the geometric beaming effects produced by a super-Eddington funnel remains a persistent observational challenge. Consequently, fundamental questions regarding the precise accretion disk geometry, the nature of the donor stars, and the maximum duration of the super-Eddington phase remain unresolved [Amato et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220265A/abstract).

Finally, extreme accretion dynamics can be realized transiently in tidal disruption events (TDEs). The tidal destruction of a star provides a sudden influx of mass, forming a new, highly variable accretion disk capable of feeding the [[central-engines|Central engines]] of these systems. High-cadence X-ray monitoring of these transients probes the formation of the inner accretion flow down to the event horizon, offering a real-time view of mass transfer dynamics and disk formation [Kara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ARA&A..63..379K/abstract).

## Relativistic Shocks and Particle Acceleration

Relativistic shocks are paramount among [[particle-acceleration-mechanisms|Particle acceleration mechanisms]] in extreme astrophysical environments, governing the production of high-energy cosmic rays and non-thermal emission. The microphysics of particle acceleration at [[forward-shocks|Forward shocks]] fundamentally differs from non-relativistic diffusive shock acceleration (first-order Fermi acceleration). In non-relativistic regimes, the accelerated particle spectrum is uniquely determined by the macroscopic compression ratio of the shock. However, in relativistic shocks, the energy spectrum and acceleration efficiency depend critically on the level and nature of particle scattering by magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) waves [Kirk et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999JPhG...25R.163K/abstract). In the absence of strong upstream scattering, particles may undergo shock-drift acceleration, simply being compressed as they cross the shock front. This enhances the energy density of the particles without necessarily altering the upstream spectral index [Kirk et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999JPhG...25R.163K/abstract).

On Galactic scales, supernova remnants (SNRs) are the standard candidates for cosmic ray acceleration. Beyond isolated remnants, superbubbles can act as collective particle accelerators; within these expansive regions, the continuous competition between particle reacceleration and escape naturally produces harder spectra at lower energies [Ferrand et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...510A.101F/abstract). However, observational confirmation of SNRs as the primary sources of high-energy cosmic rays remains hindered by a major ambiguity: it is remarkably difficult to disentangle hadronic emission (gamma rays from neutral pion decay) from leptonic emission (inverse Compton scattering and [[snr-synchrotron-emission|SNR synchrotron emission]]) in the spectra of young remnants [Acero et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013APh....43..276A/abstract). Furthermore, systematic searches for spatially extended gamma-ray emission in Fermi-LAT SNRs continue to challenge simple existing emission models [Cohen et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhDT.......190C/abstract). Future missions and observatories will be critical in this domain; for instance, instruments like ASTRO-H/Hitomi were designed to deeply probe shock dynamics and nucleosynthesis in young SNRs and galaxy clusters [Aharonian et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.1175A/abstract).

There is growing consensus that isolated SNRs struggle to accelerate particles to PeV ($10^{15}$ eV) energies. Consequently, alternative "PeVatron" candidates have been proposed. For example, the termination shocks of star cluster winds provide guaranteed upstream particle confinement due to their geometry, allowing diffusive shock acceleration to efficiently reach the PeV regime. Under reasonable turbulent dissipation conditions, these environments yield accelerated particle spectra with a power-law momentum distribution:
$$ f(p) \propto p^{-s} $$
where the spectral slope $s \approx 4.0 - 4.3$, perfectly aligning with the requirements of standard Galactic cosmic ray transport models [Morlino et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.504.6096M/abstract). 

On extragalactic scales, [[relativistic-jets|Relativistic Jets]] driven by supermassive black holes represent even more extreme acceleration sites. Within these jets, multi-TeV leptons can be produced when compact objects, such as globular clusters or bulge stars, physically collide with the jet plasma, inducing localized multiple shock fronts [Bednarek et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...807..168B/abstract). As an alternative to standard shock acceleration frameworks, the [[cannonball-model|Cannonball model]] posits that gamma-ray bursts and cosmic rays are instead driven by highly relativistic plasmoids ejected during stellar collapse or accretion events [de Rújula et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IJMPA..20.6562D/abstract). 

To explain the origins of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays (UHECRs), recent models propose a hierarchical shock framework spanning multiple astrophysical scales. In this paradigm, cosmic rays are progressively accelerated by SNR shocks, galactic wind termination shocks, and finally by massive accretion shocks surrounding cosmic filaments and galaxy clusters [Simeon et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250310795S/abstract). These large-scale accretion shocks harbor microgauss magnetic fields and possess a volume-averaged power density exceeding the required UHECR luminosity density by three orders of magnitude, capable of explaining the cosmic ray spectrum up to $\sim 200$ EeV [Simeon et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250310795S/abstract). 

Finally, extreme environments in the early Universe or involving beyond-Standard-Model physics offer exotic acceleration pathways. Blast waves from the first cosmic explosions at $z \sim 20$ likely drove Weibel-mediated non-relativistic shocks that accelerated the Universe's first protons to GeV energies [Ohira et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PhRvD.100f1301O/abstract). Additionally, theoretical "Exotic PeVatrons"—such as spinning black hole vortex-string systems or dark matter compact objects (e.g., axion stars)—could generate ultra-high-energy gamma rays through intense magnetic fields induced by millicharged dark matter interactions [Addazi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251000254A/abstract).

## Extreme Mass Ratio Inspirals and Gravitational Wave Sources

An Extreme Mass Ratio Inspiral (EMRI) occurs when a compact object, such as a neutron star or one of the many [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]] residing in a galactic nucleus, spirals into a central supermassive black hole. These systems are characterized by extreme mass ratios:
$$ q = \frac{m}{M} \in [10^{-9}, 10^{-4}] $$
where $m$ is the mass of the compact object and $M$ is the mass of the supermassive black hole [Mazzolari et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.516.1959M/abstract). Because they map the spacetime multipole moments of the central massive body over hundreds of thousands of orbits, EMRIs are primary observational targets for the upcoming millihertz gravitational wave observatory LISA.

The standard formation channel for EMRIs relies on two-body relaxation processes within dense nuclear star clusters, which gradually diffuse compact objects into the [[relativistic-loss-cone|Relativistic loss cone]]. However, calculating the exact astrophysical rates of these events remains an area of active theoretical tension. Time-dependent, two-dimensional Fokker-Planck simulations reveal that mass segregation and the evolution of the distribution function cause EMRI rates to reach an early peak before undergoing a nearly exponential decay [Broggi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3270B/abstract). Furthermore, mass segregation heavily regulates the delicate balance between gradual EMRIs and direct plunges, favoring a higher EMRI-to-plunge ratio in galaxies where stellar-mass black holes are less abundant [Rom et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240619443R/abstract).

Alternative, dynamically chaotic formation channels can also dramatically alter expected detection rates. For instance, massive black hole binaries formed in the aftermath of galaxy mergers can trigger sharp bursts of EMRI formation. In such scenarios, the secular and chaotic dynamics around the binary can elevate peak EMRI rates by factors of 10 to 100 compared to standard two-body relaxation, potentially yielding $\mathcal{O}(10)$ LISA detections per year [Mazzolari et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.516.1959M/abstract). Overall population forecasts predict that LISA may resolve $\approx 2 \times 10^3$ EMRIs with a signal-to-noise ratio greater than 20 over a 4-year mission, while a cosmological background of unresolved EMRIs is expected to produce a significant confusion noise in the $1-10\text{ mHz}$ frequency band [Rom et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240619443R/abstract).

While EMRIs are traditionally considered to be "dark" standard sirens completely devoid of electromagnetic counterparts, recent models suggest potential multi-messenger signatures. One proposed scenario involves the tidal disruption of a massive star's helium envelope by a supermassive black hole. This interaction produces a luminous precursor flare, while the surviving compact core continues on a bound trajectory, evolving into an EMRI that enters the LISA band $10-20$ years later [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...886L..22W/abstract). Although the volumetric rate for this specific channel is quite low, estimated at:
$$ \mathcal{R} \sim 2 \times 10^{-4} \, \mathrm{Gpc}^{-3} \, \mathrm{yr}^{-1} $$
the detection of such an electromagnetic precursor would pinpoint the host galaxy, perfectly calibrating the EMRI for use as [[standard-sirens|Standard sirens]] in cosmology [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...886L..22W/abstract). Additionally, exotic extreme transients in active galactic nuclei, such as X-ray Quasi-Periodic Eruptions (QPEs), have recently been theorized to be the electromagnetic manifestations of EMRIs periodically crashing through or perturbing physical accretion disks (as described by [[various-black-hole-accretion-models|Various black hole accretion models]]) surrounding the [[central-engines|Central engines]] [Kara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ARA&A..63..379K/abstract), [Kara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250322791K/abstract).

## High-Energy Transients and Tidal Events

High-energy transients provide unique observational windows into the immediate vicinity of event horizons. Minute-to-hour scale X-ray variability and episodic outbursts trace accretion dynamics in the strongest regimes of gravity, revealing the mass, spin, and geometric properties of the central compact object [Kara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250322791K/abstract), [Kara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ARA&A..63..379K/abstract).

When a star is scattered onto an orbit that penetrates the tidal radius of a supermassive black hole, it undergoes a tidal disruption event (TDE). These events often manifest as giant-amplitude X-ray flares from otherwise optically inactive galactic nuclei [Komossa et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..249..450K/abstract). Recent observations in [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] have also uncovered quasi-periodic eruptions (QPEs), which are theorized to be extreme mass ratio inspirals (EMRIs) interacting with an existing accretion disk [Kara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ARA&A..63..379K/abstract), [Kara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250322791K/abstract). Beyond standard disruptions, the partial tidal stripping of a massive star's helium envelope can produce a luminous electromagnetic precursor to an EMRI. While their estimated volumetric event rate is quite low, such interactions would be highly valuable for calibrating [[standard-sirens|Standard sirens]] [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...886L..22W/abstract).

As with EMRIs, the predicted occurrence rates of TDEs are currently a subject of active debate. Time-dependent Fokker-Planck simulations indicate that mass segregation causes TDE event rates to decline continuously over time, warning against the use of steady-state assumptions that likely overestimate long-term frequencies [Broggi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3270B/abstract). Exotic transient phenomena may also arise from [[recoiling-supermassive-black-holes|Recoiling supermassive black holes]] that have been ejected from galactic centers following a binary merger. These wandering black holes can retain a bound cluster of stars, and their subsequent tidal disruptions produce powerful, spatially offset intergalactic X-ray flares [Komossa et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...683L..21K/abstract).

In the stellar-mass regime, extreme episodic events are characterized by gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) driven by the collapse of massive stars or the coalescence of [[compact-objects-and-binary-mergers|Compact Objects and Binary Mergers]]. The broader framework of stellar collapse unifies the physics of standard supernovae, [[hypernovae|Hypernovae]], and GRBs, establishing them all as diverse products of massive stellar death [Fryer et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..302.....F/abstract). During massive stellar collapse, protoneutron star oscillations can serve as a primary source of gravitational waves, potentially detectable by ground-based interferometers if the supernova occurs within our Galaxy [Abdikamalov et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv201004356A/abstract). Emission from these transients is commonly interpreted within the framework of the [[relativistic-fireball-model|Relativistic fireball model]], which requires the presence of ultra-relativistic shock waves. Particle acceleration at these shocks is complex: in the absence of scattering, particles gain energy purely through shock-drift compression, but when magnetic turbulence is present, multiple shock encounters mediated by magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) wave scattering dictate the energetic particle spectrum [Kirk et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999JPhG...25R.163K/abstract). 

The multi-messenger detection of binary neutron star mergers has solidified the link between short-duration GRBs and gravitational wave events [Wiggins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...865...27W/abstract). Determining the precise nature of the circum-merger environment is essential, as the strength and temporal evolution of the electromagnetic afterglow are highly sensitive to the local gas density and the hierarchical structure of the surrounding cosmic web [Wiggins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...865...27W/abstract). Furthermore, deep optical follow-up observations of candidate neutron star-black hole mergers, such as GW190814, provide crucial constraints on kilonova parameters—including the total ejected mass and lanthanide abundance—through dedicated simulations [Morgan et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...901...83M/abstract).

## Feedback and Co-evolution in Extreme Environments

The regulation of star formation and the structural evolution of galaxies across cosmic time depend critically on energy injection from [[massive-compact-objects|Massive compact objects]] and stellar processes. Cosmological simulations robustly demonstrate that gravity alone over-predicts the stellar mass of galaxies. While stellar feedback is sufficient to regulate star formation in dwarf galaxies [Wang et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454...83W/abstract), for massive halos ($M_{\rm halo} \gtrsim 10^{12} M_\odot$), massive energy injection from central supermassive black holes—termed AGN feedback—is essential to quench star formation and reproduce the "red and dead" elliptical population [Blank et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.5476B/abstract). To understand this co-evolution observationally, surveys such as the Spitzer Extragalactic Representative Volume Survey (SERVS) have mapped the environments of distant active nuclei, revealing significant galaxy overdensities around quasars at $z \sim 2.0$ and $z \sim 3.3$, highlighting these regions as crucial nodes of massive structure formation [Falder et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...735..123F/abstract). Additionally, environmental clustering plays a role; for example, observations reveal that some BL Lac objects reside in rich galaxy clusters, supporting their evolutionary link to FR 1 radio galaxies [Wurtz et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993AJ....106..869W/abstract). The cooling mechanisms in these active systems are also complex; in powerful 3CR radio galaxies, evidence points to intense hot dust emission ($\simeq 340$ K) as the most dissipative source of cooling [Rocca-Volmerange et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...433...73R/abstract).

The NIHAO project (Numerical Investigation of a Hundred Astrophysical Objects) highlights that AGN feedback has profound collateral consequences beyond simply truncating star formation. It is computationally required to reduce the central dark matter density of massive galaxies, bringing the concentration-halo mass ($c-M$) relation into alignment with observations [Waterval et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.5307W/abstract). Furthermore, galaxy formation and feedback processes in these simulations result in rounder dark matter halos, particularly for Milky Way-mass systems [Chua et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515.2681C/abstract). AGN feedback regulates the kinematic dichotomy between slow and fast rotators; without it, simulations drastically over-predict the abundance of fast-rotating galaxies [Waterval et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.5307W/abstract). NIHAO also offers profound insights into galactic structure, distinguishing between stellar kinematic discs and photometric exponential profiles [Obreja et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459..467O/abstract), while demonstrating the in situ formation and angular momentum preservation of bulge stars in spiral galaxies [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.5477W/abstract). Furthermore, simulations of high-redshift environments reveal that these galaxies frequently exhibit clumpy light distributions driven by localized star formation rather than actual clumpy stellar mass discs, with dust deeply suppressing their intrinsic brightness [Buck et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468.3628B/abstract). However, tensions remain in the precise timing of these transitions: simulated green valley crossing times triggered by AGN feedback are approximately $400$ Myr, which is noticeably shorter than the $\sim 1$ Gyr timescale inferred from observations [Blank et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.5296B/abstract).

The tight empirical scaling relations between black hole mass and host galaxy stellar mass are traditionally attributed to active feedback mechanisms closely coupling the two scales. However, the literature remains divided on whether this represents active Galaxy-black hole co-evolution or passive co-existence. Recent investigations utilizing [[various-black-hole-accretion-models|Various black hole accretion models]]—such as viscous disc or gravitational torque-driven accretion, which possess a weaker dependence on black hole mass than standard Bondi-Hoyle models—demonstrate that realistic scaling relations can emerge naturally from a shared central gas supply, even without direct feedback coupling [Soliman et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525...12S/abstract). Thus, whether feedback is the primary driver of cosmic co-evolution remains an open question in [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]].

The efficacy of feedback is heavily modulated by the thermodynamics of cosmic gas accretion. At high redshifts ($z \approx 2-4$), massive galaxies are primarily fed by cold filaments (cold-mode accretion). These narrow streams bypass shock-heated outflowing gas, sustaining extreme star formation rates ($\gtrsim 100\, M_\odot\, \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$) while barely interacting with the outward-propagating feedback [Waterval et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.537.2726W/abstract). An entropy criterion based on the equation of state of the intergalactic medium is often required to accurately distinguish this from hot-mode accretion, where gas is shock-heated to the halo virial temperature [Tollet et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515.3453T/abstract).

On the largest scales, energy injected into the Intracluster medium (ICM) by [[central-engines|Central engines]] offsets cooling flows and regulates cluster environments. Observations of X-ray cavities indicate that jet activity from central cD galaxies is highly intermittent, operating on timescales of $<10^5$ years. This episodic behavior is likely driven by radiation pressure instabilities in the accretion disk [Siemiginowska et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..427..326S/abstract). As these jets propagate, they dissipate energy into the hot, dilute thermal plasma of the ICM, establishing galaxy clusters as premier cosmic laboratories for studying magnetic field amplification, turbulence, and the acceleration of non-thermal cosmic rays [Kunz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022hxga.book...56K/abstract).

## Star Formation in Extreme Low-Density and High-Pressure Regimes

Star formation behaves markedly differently at the extremes of galactic environments, spanning from the exceptionally diffuse gas reservoirs of ultra-diffuse galaxies to the hyper-turbulent, high-pressure cores of merging starbursts.

**The Extreme Low-Density Regime: Ultra-Diffuse Galaxies**
Ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs) represent the extreme low-density tail of star-forming environments. Characterized by stellar masses typical of dwarf galaxies ($10^7 - 10^{8.5} M_\odot$) but extended effective radii comparable to the Milky Way ($>1$ kpc), UDGs exhibit highly inefficient star formation [Di Cintio et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466L...1D/abstract). There is an active debate in the literature regarding whether UDGs constitute a distinct, unique galaxy class or simply the diffuse tail of the standard dwarf population. Recent cross-simulation comparisons (e.g., the NIHAO and Romulus25 suites) and H I observational surveys support the latter: when controlling for stellar mass, the gas richness ($M_{\rm HI}/M_*$) and star formation rates of UDGs are continuous with classical field dwarfs, rendering them observationally and physically indistinguishable as a separate population [Motiwala et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250214971M/abstract), [Motiwala et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...989...86M/abstract).

In the low-density dwarf galaxy regime, environmental interactions play a defining role. Contrasting gas enrichment between Local Group dwarf galaxies and field systems reveals that past interactions with other dwarfs can drive metal-rich gas into their halos, uniquely shaping their chemical evolution [Arora et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.6134A/abstract). Despite their extensive H I reservoirs, UDGs are generally $\rm H_2$-poor and exhibit anomalously low star formation rate surface densities down to $\sim 500$ pc scales [Kado-Fong et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...939..101K/abstract), [Kado-Fong et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...11K/abstract). However, when modeled within a pressure-regulated, feedback-modulated (PRFM) framework, this low efficiency is a natural consequence of their low midplane pressures. The scaling relations between midplane pressure and star formation in UDGs agree with those of normal dwarfs and more massive galaxies, indicating that the star formation mechanism itself is not exotic despite the extreme low-density environment [Kado-Fong et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...939..101K/abstract). The diffuse structure of these galaxies can be naturally reproduced in simulations through repeated episodes of bursty star formation, where the resulting stellar feedback drives gas outflows that induce subsequent dark matter and stellar expansion [Di Cintio et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.466L...1D/abstract).

**The Extreme High-Pressure Regime: Starbursts and Mergers**
Conversely, the extreme high-pressure environments triggered by major galaxy mergers drive the most intense clustered star formation. High-resolution observations from early Hubble Space Telescope imaging indicated that the formation of young massive clusters (YMCs) or "super star clusters" is a dominant star formation mode in these starburst events [de Grijs et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph..8232D/abstract), [de Grijs et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ASSL..329..157D/abstract). Later modern surveys, such as the Hubble imaging Probe of Extreme Environments and Clusters (HiPEEC), have further quantified this extreme cluster formation, showing that these environments are incredibly efficient at converting dense gas into stellar mass, yielding compact clusters with ages of $1-500$ Myr and masses exceeding $10^7 M_\odot$ [Adamo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.3267A/abstract). 

The mass distribution of these extreme clusters is typically described by an exponentially truncated power law. In advanced merger stages, the power-law slope is generally $\beta \sim -1.5$ to $-2.0$, with a truncation mass $M_c$ between $10^6$ and a few times $10^7 M_\odot$—among the highest values observed in the local Universe [Adamo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.3267A/abstract). The resulting super star clusters are physically analogous to the young, metal-rich progenitors of typical Milky Way globular clusters [de Grijs et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph..8232D/abstract).

In other intense local environments, self-consistent multi-frequency modeling of the starburst nucleus in NGC 253 highlights its significance as a robust high-energy gamma-ray source [Domingo-Santamaría et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005A&A...444..403D/abstract). Furthermore, shock-driven activity frequently sculpts these regions; for instance, compact emission-line objects and shells have been observed at shell boundaries in the IC 1613 star-forming complex [Lozinskaya et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ARep...46...16L/abstract), while bow shock models of ultracompact H II regions demonstrate the impact of massive stellar winds moving rapidly through molecular clouds [Mac Low et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...369..395M/abstract).

The extreme turbulence and gas densities in these starburst regions can also alter the stellar Initial Mass Function (IMF). By applying the concept of the turbulent Jeans mass as the characteristic fragmentation scale, theoretical models predict that the peak of the IMF shifts toward lower masses in highly turbulent environments, producing a "bottom-heavy" distribution [Chabrier et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...796...75C/abstract). Concurrently, the time-dependence of the fragmentation process in dense, high-Mach-number gas can steepen the high-mass tail of the IMF beyond the standard Salpeter index, approaching a limit of $\alpha \simeq -2.7$ [Chabrier et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...796...75C/abstract). This steepening fundamentally impacts the expected rates of supernovae and the subsequent formation of [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]] in these extreme environments.

## Open Questions and Future Observational Frontiers

As astrophysical observations probe increasingly extreme energy scales and spacetime curvatures, several foundational questions regarding the nature of compact objects, the limits of general relativity, and non-standard particle physics remain unresolved. Future multi-messenger facilities will be critical in addressing these observational frontiers.

**Testing Spacetime and Accretion Dynamics**
The advent of very long baseline interferometry in the millimeter regime has provided a direct window into [[black-hole-physics|Black Hole Physics]]. While high-resolution imaging by the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) and X-ray spectral modeling from NuSTAR currently yield parameters that are fully consistent with the Kerr metric of General Relativity [Tripathi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240108545T/abstract), a significant ambiguity persists in the literature. Theoretical modeling demonstrates that at the EHT's current spatial resolution ($\sim 20\,\mu\text{as}$ at $\lambda = 1.3\text{ mm}$), non-Kerr compact objects—such as rotating boson stars or Lamy spinning wormholes—can produce blurred images that closely mimic a Kerr black hole's shadow [Vincent et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646A..37V/abstract). Consequently, definitive tests proving the Kerr hypothesis require next-generation arrays with enhanced baseline coverage. Additionally, studies of strong gravitational lens galaxies out to $z \sim 0.8$ from the SuGOHI survey show that while lens fields exhibit local overdensities, their line-of-sight structure counts remain consistent with control samples once the lens plane is carefully removed [Wong et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...867..107W/abstract). Future missions like PRIMA will utilize far-infrared spectral timing to probe the rapid variability of accretion flows and jets in X-ray binaries, offering novel multi-wavelength constraints on inner-disk physics [Tetarenko et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250310739T/abstract).

**Compact Object Dynamics and Recoils**
In dense galactic nuclei, asymmetric gravitational wave emission during binary black hole coalescence can impart kick velocities up to several $1000\text{ km s}^{-1}$, creating [[recoiling-supermassive-black-holes|Recoiling supermassive black holes]] [Komossa et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AdAst2012E..14K/abstract). These recoils may manifest as kinematically and spatially offset active galactic nuclei. An example is the candidate 3C 186, though reproducing its exact projected properties requires substantial fine-tuning of the spin and kick vectors [Boschini et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A.245B/abstract). Furthermore, stars remaining bound to a recoiling black hole can be tidally disrupted, generating powerful off-nuclear X-ray flares [Komossa et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...683L..21K/abstract).

Understanding the broader dynamics of dense nuclei is also vital for predicting Extreme Mass Ratio Inspirals (EMRIs) for the upcoming LISA mission. Additionally, refined time-dependent dynamical models suggest that previous steady-state estimates may severely overestimate the long-term rates of EMRIs and TDEs [Broggi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.514.3270B/abstract). Notably, if an EMRI involves the tidal stripping of a massive star's helium envelope prior to inspiral, the resulting electromagnetic precursor could transform the system into a valuable multi-messenger [[standard-sirens|Standard sirens]] [Wang et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...886L..22W/abstract).

**High-Redshift Environments and Cosmic Variance**
As observatories like JWST push the frontier of cosmic structure formation to ultra-high redshifts ($z > 8$), the discovery of unexpectedly luminous galaxies presents a new theoretical tension. However, interpreting the significance of these rare extreme objects requires careful consideration of cosmic variance, which can heavily bias demographic inferences in deep, narrow-field surveys [Jespersen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...982...23J/abstract). Furthermore, interpreting these observations demands advanced synthetic models. The release of panchromatic resolved photometry and integrated spectra from the NIHAO project highlights a critical challenge: the assumed energy balance between dust attenuation and re-emission can be significantly violated in complex, realistic galaxy morphologies [Faucher et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...957....7F/abstract).

**Particle Acceleration and Primordial Magnetic Fields**
Identifying the exact [[particle-acceleration-mechanisms|Particle acceleration mechanisms]] responsible for Galactic PeV cosmic rays remains a major observational hurdle. A fundamental open question is whether hadronic or leptonic processes dominate the gamma-ray emission in young supernova remnants [Acero et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013APh....43..276A/abstract). The upcoming Cherenkov Telescope Array (CTA) is expected to resolve this ambiguity through high-sensitivity observations in the very-high-energy regime. At high energies, Fermi's decade-scale findings—including deep pulsar studies, Crab Nebula variability, and large-scale structures like the Fermi Bubbles—have mapped the gamma-ray sky with unprecedented detail [Thompson et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013NuPhS.243...58T/abstract). Theoretical models also propose that baryonic dark clusters and molecular clouds residing in galactic halos may act as significant sources of gamma-ray flux via cosmic-ray interactions [de Paolis et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996IJMPD...5..151D/abstract).

At cosmological scales, observations must account for the origins of ubiquitous large-scale magnetization. The intergalactic medium in cosmic voids may host weak primordial magnetic fields ($B \sim 10^{-16}\text{ G}$) coherent on megaparsec scales, which likely served as seed fields for subsequent dynamo amplification in collapsed structures [Subramanian et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016RPPh...79g6901S/abstract). 

**Exotic Matter Limits**
Finally, extreme environments serve as laboratories for exotic physics. Theoretical models suggest that non-standard objects, such as ultra-spinning black hole vortex-strings or axion stars, could act as "exotic PeVatrons," generating ultra-high-energy gamma rays through interactions with millicharged dark matter [Addazi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251000254A/abstract). Furthermore, interstellar gas heating in dwarf galaxies, such as Leo T, provides stringent astrophysical constraints against the presence of extended dark matter compact objects (EDCOs) heavier than a solar mass [Kim et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250818344K/abstract). Empirical searches for macroscopic dark matter—such as nuclearites—have yielded no detections, placing stringent upper limits on their isotropic flux ($< 5.4 \times 10^{-20}\text{ cm}^{-2}\text{ s}^{-1}\text{ sr}^{-1}$ for masses between $100\text{ g}$ and $100\text{ kg}$) [Piotrowski et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvL.125i1101P/abstract). Reconciling these null results with the required presence of dark matter highlights the necessity of next-generation instruments to probe the intersection of astrophysics and fundamental particle physics. Next-generation instruments must also account for exotic cosmological contributions, such as the gamma-ray background generated by neutralino annihilation in the dark matter microhalos of the first cosmological objects [Oda et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...633L..65O/abstract). Finally, theoretical physics pushes the boundaries of these environments even further, exploring speculative scenarios such as universes without the weak force, where the presence of stable neutrons would fundamentally alter classical nucleosynthesis and star formation processes [Grohs et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvD..97d3003G/abstract).
