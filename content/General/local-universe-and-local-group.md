The **Local Universe and Local Group** encompass our immediate extragalactic environment, offering an unprecedented, highly resolved laboratory for testing models of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] and [[cosmogony|Cosmogony]]. The Local Group is a gravitationally bound, binary galaxy system dominated by the Milky Way and the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] (M31), accompanied by dozens of lower-mass dwarf companions [van den Bergh et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000glg..book.....V/abstract). Extending outward, the Local Universe—often referred to as the Local Volume—includes surrounding cosmographical features such as the flattened Local Sheet, the bounding "Council of Giants," and the broader filamentary structures extending toward the [[laniakea-supercluster|Laniakea Supercluster]]. Because their proximity allows for the observation of individual stars and precise [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]] kinematics, these local structures form the foundational testing ground for [[near-field-cosmology|Near-field cosmology]]. By treating individual stars as fossil records, researchers can reconstruct the detailed star formation and chemical enrichment histories of nearby galaxies. The interplay of nucleosynthesis, star formation, and gas flows acts as the fundamental ingredients of these galactic chemical evolution models [Matteucci et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Ap&SS.284..539M/abstract). This provides a direct, [[sub-megaparsec-scales|Sub-megaparsec scales]] probe of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]], helping to clarify the comparable importance of [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]] and continuous [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]] in the assembly of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] at high redshift [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract), [Boylan-Kolchin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462L..51B/abstract).

A central pillar of research in this domain focuses on mapping the [[mass-distribution-and-functions|Mass Distribution and Functions]] and tracing the chemo-dynamical assembly of galaxies. Foundational dynamical models, stemming from early timing arguments of the Milky Way-M31 orbit [Lynden-Bell et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982ac...proc...85L/abstract), have evolved into complex orbital reconstructions that incorporate massive interlopers and high-precision astrometry to measure the total mass of the Local Group. Simultaneously, galactic archaeology utilizes the spatial and chemical distributions of resolved stellar populations to decode the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]]. Observations of stellar streams, extended stellar halos, and morphologically segregated [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] reveal how environmental factors dictate the survival of low-mass systems. Through interactions such as tidal stripping, ram pressure from the cosmic web, and photoionization during the [[early-universe|Early universe]], the local environment actively reshapes these galaxies as they fall into massive host halos [Grebel et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ESASP.445...87G/abstract), [Grebel et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001dge..conf...45G/abstract).

Despite the overwhelming success of the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm on large scales, the Local Universe presents several persistent "small-scale challenges" that remain active areas of theoretical and observational pursuit. Discrepancies such as the "missing satellites" and "too big to fail" problems, alongside the highly correlated planar distributions of satellite galaxies, question whether strictly collisionless cold dark matter governs [[subhalo-physics-and-galactic-substructure|Subhalo Physics and Galactic Substructure]] [Benson et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.333..177B/abstract). While many researchers argue that advanced baryonic physics—captured in high-resolution [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]]—naturally resolves these tensions through supernova feedback and cosmic reionization, others propose that these anomalies require self-interacting dark matter (SIDM) or fundamental modifications to Newtonian gravity. Furthermore, mapping the [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]] reveals that the Local Group is participating in a massive bulk flow ($> 300$ km s$^{-1}$) toward distant overdensities. Because this flow persists well beyond 200 Mpc, the exact dynamical scale of cosmic homogeneity remains a profound open question in modern [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]].

## Cosmography and the Architecture of the Local Sheet

The Local Group is embedded within the Local Sheet, a flattened cosmological structure that is both geometrically and dynamically distinct from the larger Local Supercluster [McCall et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440..405M/abstract). The Local Sheet is inclined by approximately $8^\circ$ relative to the Local Supercluster and exhibits remarkable spatial planarity; the dispersion of its giant member galaxies about the mid-plane is a mere $230$ kpc [McCall et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440..405M/abstract). Cosmological reconstructions suggest that the Local Sheet originated from a primordial density perturbation of very low amplitude ($\sim 10\%$), yet it incorporated normal matter into galaxies with an unusually high efficiency of $\sim 40\%$ [McCall et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440..405M/abstract).

The boundary and dynamical realm of influence of the Local Group are delineated by the "Council of Giants," a ring of massive galaxies with a radius of $3.75$ Mpc [McCall et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440..405M/abstract). The outer limit of the Local Group's gravitational dominance is situated where its attractive forces balance those of the Council. Notably, the only two giant elliptical galaxies in the Local Sheet occupy opposite sides of this Council, raising the possibility that they have shepherded the evolutionary history of the Local Group [McCall et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440..405M/abstract). Furthermore, the Local Group appears to be moving away from a ridge in the potential surface of the Council on a path roughly parallel to the axis connecting these two ellipticals [McCall et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440..405M/abstract).

Within the broader [[local-cosmic-domain|Local cosmic domain]] ($D < 11$ Mpc), the environment is demonstrably underdense compared to the global cosmic average. Measurements indicate a mean total matter density of $\Omega_m = 0.17$ and a mean stellar density of $\Omega_* = 0.0044$ (in units of the critical density) [Karachentsev et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018AN....339..615K/abstract). This local volume is characterized by a strong, prolate tidal field with an axial ratio of $0.5 \pm 0.13$ [Lee et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.389.1001L/abstract). The minor principal axis of this tidal field points toward equatorial coordinates $\alpha_p = 15^\mathrm{h}00^\mathrm{m}$, $\delta_p = 20^\circ$. The global tides exerted on the Local Group by this surrounding mass distribution may be partially responsible for the relatively low abundance of [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] observed within it [Lee et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.389.1001L/abstract). Furthermore, the planar distribution of dwarf galaxies may be reinforced by tidal compression from external massive galaxies, which can flatten a system of mass-points beyond simple tidal stripping [Pasetto et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...499..385P/abstract).

Mapping the complete architecture of the Local Universe is a major ongoing effort in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], complicated by the Milky Way's disk, which creates a Zone of Avoidance that obscures approximately $25\%$ of the extragalactic sky [Kraan-Korteweg et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000LNP...556..301K/abstract). Despite this, multi-wavelength surveys and analyses of the [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]] have traced the local kinematic environment out to the [[laniakea-supercluster|Laniakea Supercluster]] and the Great Attractor. Recent studies of peculiar velocities reveal a bulk flow of $315 \pm 40$ km s$^{-1}$ at a distance of $150 \ h^{-1}$ Mpc [Courtois et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.187C/abstract). Crucially, these measurements indicate that the dynamical scale of homogeneity has not yet been reached even within distances of $200-300 \ h^{-1}$ Mpc, demonstrating that large-scale structural asymmetries continue to exert a kinematically significant influence on the Local Group over vast distances [Courtois et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.187C/abstract), [Courtois et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250201308C/abstract).

## Historical Development of Local Group Dynamics

[[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] effectively began in the 1920s when Edwin Hubble identified Cepheid variables in the Andromeda Nebula, eventually leading to his description of the Local Group as a collection of galaxies gravitationally isolated in the general cosmic field [van den Bergh et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000PASP..112..529V/abstract). For decades, this assumption of absolute isolation dominated dynamical studies. The foundational paradigm of Local Group dynamics was established by the Kahn-Woltjer argument, which provided early, compelling dynamical evidence for the existence of massive [[dark-matter-halo-properties-and-statistics|Dark Matter Halo Properties and Statistics]].

The Kahn-Woltjer timing argument assumes that the Milky Way (MW) and Andromeda (M31) formed close together in the early Universe and initially expanded apart with the Hubble flow. Because they are currently observed to be approaching each other, their mutual gravitational attraction must have been strong enough to halt their expansion and cause them to turn around. Assuming a simple two-body Keplerian orbit, the system's kinematics are used to match the current separation and approach velocity to the age of the Universe. Early historical modeling of Local Group member paths was utilized to infer the dynamic mass ratios between the Milky Way and M31 [Mishra et al. (1985)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1985MNRAS.212..163M/abstract). This M31-MW timing argument historically yielded a total Local Group mass 2 to 3 times larger than the mass inferred from internal rotation curves alone [Zheng et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991A&A...247...20Z/abstract). Donald Lynden-Bell formalized this concept further, exploring the "Genesis of the Local Group" by deducing the total mass and limits on the transverse velocity of Andromeda strictly under Newtonian mechanics [Lynden-Bell et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982ac...proc...85L/abstract). 

However, the assumption that the Milky Way and M31 constitute an unperturbed, isolated two-body system was subsequently challenged by the presence of massive nearby galaxies. Research in the 1990s highlighted the dynamical importance of massive interlopers, particularly Maffei 1, Maffei 2, and the [[ic-342-group|IC 342 group]]. Zheng et al. demonstrated that if Maffei 1 is located at a distance of approximately $2.1$ Mpc, it would have experienced a close dynamical encounter with the Local Group roughly $6.5$ Gyr ago [Zheng et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991A&A...247...20Z/abstract). Such an encounter would impart a peculiar velocity of $\sim 66$ km s$^{-1}$ to the Local Group, significantly perturbing the two-body timing kinematics. Some models from this era even suggested that Maffei 1/2 and IC 342 might have been violently ejected from the Local Group during a "little bang" approximately $8$ Gyr ago [Valtonen et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995AIPC..336..450V/abstract). 

The literature regarding the exact dynamical history of the Local Group remains somewhat conflicting. While the historical hypothesis that interloper encounters entirely remove the need for massive dark halos [Zheng et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991A&A...247...20Z/abstract) has fallen out of favor, the perturbing effects of neighboring structures are now recognized as essential for precise mass mapping. Modern orbital reconstructions, utilizing advanced proper motion data, incorporate the gravitational influence of external attractors to refine the Local Group's total mass to approximately $4.33^{+0.37}_{-0.32} \times 10^{12} M_\odot$ [Banik et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.2237B/abstract). These contemporary models continue to build upon the historical legacy of the Kahn-Woltjer argument, balancing the fundamental two-body MW-M31 dynamics against the complex environmental tidal fields mapped by [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|Local Volume and Nearby Galaxy Studies]]. The group's dynamical boundary is further highlighted by remote members, such as the Cetus [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]], which is situated well within the group's dynamical boundary but remains isolated from the main spirals [Whiting et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999AJ....118.2767W/abstract). A comprehensive census of the outer fringes of the Local Group has historically emphasized objects like IC 10, the Aquarius dwarf, and the Sagittarius Dwarf Irregular Galaxy (SagDIG) as likely physical members [van den Bergh et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994AJ....107.1328V/abstract). Modern studies also emphasize the ongoing destruction of the Local Group and the formation of tidal streams as an essential framework for understanding hierarchical assembly [Aparicio et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..310...19A/abstract). Contemporary orbital models heavily rely on initiatives like the HSTPROMO collaboration, which achieved breakthrough precision in measuring the proper motions of stars, stellar streams, and satellite galaxies [van der Marel et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ASPC..480...43V/abstract). Looking ahead to its ultimate dynamical fate, theoretical scenarios suggest that the Local Group will eventually merge into a single, massive elliptical galaxy containing approximately 700 [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]] [Forbes et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000A&A...358..471F/abstract).

## Near-Field Cosmology and Resolved Stellar Populations

[[near-field-cosmology|Near-field cosmology]] leverages the proximity of the Local Group to study resolved [[stellar-component|Stellar component]] populations, treating individual stars as a "fossil record" of past star formation, chemical enrichment, and hierarchical assembly [Boylan-Kolchin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462L..51B/abstract). By observing these local structures in detail, astronomers can indirectly probe [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] and the physical conditions of the early Universe on sub-megaparsec scales.

Reconstructing unambiguous [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] requires deep, high-resolution multi-band photometry to generate Color-Magnitude Diagrams (CMDs) that reach the oldest main-sequence turnoffs and solar-luminosity main-sequence stars [Tolstoy et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006IAUS..232..293T/abstract), [Brown et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1209.4141B/abstract). Observational breakthroughs with the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) and the Very Large Telescope (VLT) have been fundamental in resolving these older stellar populations within Local Group dwarfs [Tolstoy et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001dge..conf...61T/abstract), while VLT deep imaging has further resolved the complex star formation histories of metal-poor dwarf galaxies beyond the Milky Way [Tolstoy et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000fepc.conf..287T/abstract). To successfully trace these histories, astronomers also rely on narrow-band surveys capable of detecting specific populations, such as symbiotic stars, which help discriminate between complex stellar types in nearby galaxies [Magrini et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASPC..303..539M/abstract). Variable stars also serve as critical chrono-chemo-kinematical tracers; for example, long-period variables (LPVs) have been utilized to reconstruct the SFH of the Andromeda IX dwarf spheroidal, revealing a maximum star formation rate of $\approx 0.00082 \pm 0.00031 M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$ [Abdollahi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230207599A/abstract). Asymptotic Giant Branch (AGB) stars play a similar role; determining their abundances provides insights into chemical evolution and shows how star formation histories across different Local Group environments can calibrate distant observations [Tolstoy et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..445..463T/abstract). Furthermore, the mass return from these dusty evolved stars can act as a crucial gas reservoir to sustain or even rejuvenate star formation in dwarf galaxies [Mahani et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023IAUS..373..264M/abstract). While some nearby galaxies, such as IC 10, exhibit recent starburst events, these bursts are often stochastic and do not fundamentally alter their long-term evolutionary histories when compared to typical dwarf irregulars [Cole et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010PASA...27..234C/abstract).

Historically, the Milky Way has been utilized as the standard template for $L^*$ galaxy formation. However, comprehensive spectroscopic surveys and deep CMDs have revealed significant tensions between the assembly histories of the Milky Way and the [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] (M31). For instance, the M31 stellar halo contains a prominent intermediate-age stellar population, whereas the Milky Way's halo is almost entirely old. This growing body of evidence indicates that M31's formation history differs substantially from that of our Galaxy, challenging the use of the Milky Way as a universal template for similar-mass galaxies [Grebel et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005nfcd.conf....1G/abstract), [Escala et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhDT........14E/abstract).

Local Group dwarf galaxies provide further critical tests for cosmological models, particularly regarding the impact of cosmic reionization. While early theoretical models predicted a complete cessation of star formation in the lowest-mass halos due to photoionization feedback, near-field observations reveal a persistent tension with this simple picture. Resolved stellar populations in several dwarf spheroidals (dSphs) exhibit extended star formation episodes lasting for gigayears after the epoch of reionization, complicating models that predict immediate quenching [Grebel et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005nfcd.conf....1G/abstract). In striking cases, near-field observations have revealed that some dwarf irregulars, such as Leo A, may be predominantly young systems, with the vast majority of their star formation occurring only within the last 2 Gyr [Tolstoy et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..7154T/abstract). 

The spatial distribution of these fossil populations within host halos offers crucial diagnostics of the galaxy assembly process. Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations, such as the [[apostle-simulation-suite|Apostle simulation suite]], predict strong radial gradients in Milky Way-sized galaxies. These models indicate that the fraction of the oldest ($t_{\rm form} < 0.8$ Gyr) and most metal-poor ($[\text{Fe/H}] < -2.5$) stars increases outwards. Somewhat counter-intuitively, over half of these highly pristine stars are expected to reside outside the solar circle, having been deposited there by the disruption of early accreted subhalos [Starkenburg et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2212S/abstract). Finding and characterizing these sparse, ancient populations remains a primary driver for next-generation massively multiplexed spectroscopic surveys in the local cosmic domain.

## Dwarf Galaxy Populations and Small-Scale Challenges

The Local Group serves as a primary laboratory for studying [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]], whose properties and evolutionary tracks are heavily influenced by their environment. By acting as nearly pristine "single cell" star-forming entities, dwarf galaxies provide the most straightforward laboratories for testing universal star formation laws [Tolstoy et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Ap&SS.284..579T/abstract). Seminal reviews have provided a comprehensive census and categorization of the photometric, kinematic, and chemical properties of the Local Group dwarf population [Mateo et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ARA&A..36..435M/abstract). A stark morphological segregation is observed among these systems: gas-rich dwarf irregulars (dIrr) preferentially inhabit the low-density outskirts of the Local Group, whereas gas-poor dwarf spheroidals (dSph) are clustered near the massive central galaxies, the Milky Way and M31 [Grebel et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001dge..conf...45G/abstract). This dichotomy is largely driven by environmental quenching mechanisms. Processes such as tidal forces, ram-pressure stripping, and "cosmic web stripping" efficiently remove cold gas from low-mass halos as they traverse high-density filaments and enter the virial radii of massive hosts [Pasetto et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003A&A...405..931P/abstract), [Benítez-Llambay et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763L..41B/abstract).

Despite the success of the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm on large scales, the properties of these dwarf satellites present several persistent "small-scale challenges" regarding [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]]. The classic "missing satellites problem" arises because collisionless dark matter N-body simulations predict an order of magnitude more subhalos than the observed number of dwarf companions [Benson et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.333..177B/abstract). The lowest luminosity galaxies in the Local Group are observationally the most dark-matter dominated systems known [Wyse et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008IAUS..244...44W/abstract). Consequently, charting the star-by-star histories of the lowest-mass galaxies has become a paramount tool to probe the fundamental nature of dark matter and the effects of cosmic reionization [Weisz et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019BAAS...51c...1W/abstract). Early efforts to investigate this discrepancy included surveying high-velocity clouds (HVCs) and compact HVCs as potential neutral hydrogen-rich counterparts to the missing dark matter subhalos [Putman et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ASPC..254..245P/abstract). A related tension is the "too big to fail" (TBTF) problem, wherein the most massive, dense subhalos predicted by simulations are kinematically incompatible with the central velocity dispersions of the brightest observed [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] [Sawala et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.2748S/abstract). 

Within the standard $\Lambda$CDM framework, these discrepancies are primarily addressed via baryonic physics. The cosmic reionization epoch is thought to have suppressed the formation of small galaxies through photoionization, while the inclusion of baryonic physics alongside dark matter successfully resolves these small-scale discrepancies in standard models [Benson et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.333..177B/abstract), [Sawala et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.2748S/abstract). High-resolution [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] that incorporate these baryonic effects successfully reproduce the Local Group satellite luminosity function and resolve TBTF without modifying dark matter physics [Sawala et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.2748S/abstract), [Libeskind et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.2968L/abstract). However, the literature remains conflicted on the universality of these solutions. Furthermore, resolving the tensions in standard cosmological models might require revisiting [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] themselves; for instance, proposed variations of the stellar initial mass function (like the GWIMF) have been suggested as a way to naturally resolve some of these small-scale discrepancies [Kroupa et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231001473K/abstract). Resolved stellar population studies reveal that many dSphs exhibit extended star formation episodes long after reionization, contradicting models that predict rapid, total quenching [Grebel et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005nfcd.conf....1G/abstract). Furthermore, surveys of isolated systems beyond the Local Group (such as the MATLAS fields) exhibit a "too-many-satellites" problem when compared to hydrodynamical $\Lambda$CDM predictions, suggesting that current feedback calibrations may fail to simultaneously match dwarf abundances across diverse environments [Kanehisa et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A.280K/abstract). Comparisons with the nearby M81 Group reveal that while some members follow Local Group scaling relations, others like F8D1 exhibit anomalously high luminosities for their central surface brightness, highlighting further environmental diversity [Caldwell et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998AJ....115..535C/abstract).

Recent reviews continue to synthesize the diverse properties of the satellite populations of M31 and the Milky Way across stellar masses ranging from $10^3$ to $10^9 M_\odot$ [Doliva-Dolinsky et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206948D/abstract). Furthermore, simulations like IllustrisTNG indicate that low surface brightness galaxies (LSBGs) thrive in different environments depending on whether they are rotation- or dispersion-dominated, with the latter favoring high-density regions where interactions govern their evolution [Pérez-Montaño et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533...93P/abstract).

A separate and highly debated challenge is the "planes of satellites" problem: the observation that dwarf galaxies around both the Milky Way and M31 align in highly flattened, kinematically correlated planar structures. While some authors argue these configurations arise naturally from accretion along cosmic filaments and are adequately reproduced by certain constrained simulations [Sawala et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1412.2748S/abstract), others contend that such organized [[phase-space-dynamics|Phase Space Dynamics]] are highly improbable for collisionless dark matter [Kroupa et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161003854K/abstract). Proponents of modified gravity argue that these planar structures, along with anomalously high radial velocities observed in some dwarfs, strongly suggest a past close flyby of the Milky Way and M31 at $z > 0.8$. Such a close encounter would trigger a merger in $\Lambda$CDM due to dynamical friction, but is permitted and even expected in [[milgrom-relation|Milgrom relation]] models [Zhao et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...557L...3Z/abstract), [Banik et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180509207B/abstract). Self-interacting dark matter (SIDM) models have also been proposed to naturally produce the $\sim 1\text{ kpc}$ dark matter cores inferred from dwarf kinematics, providing a mechanism to alter dwarf satellite structure independent of finely tuned baryonic feedback [Gutcke et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251005258G/abstract). Alternatively, the observation that the properties of galaxies and star clusters are inextricably linked by the "fundamental manifold" has led some researchers to propose that the physics of baryonic settling in gravitational wells operates independently of the presence of a dark matter halo entirely [Zaritsky et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...727..116Z/abstract).

## Galactic Archeology and Chemo-Dynamical Evolution

Galactic archeology utilizes individual resolved [[stellar-component|Stellar component]] units as "fossil records" to reconstruct the star formation histories, chemical evolution, and assembly processes of the Milky Way, Andromeda (M31), and their satellite companions. Under the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] grow via the continuous accretion of smaller building blocks, a process that leaves measurable imprints in their extended [[stellar-substructure|Stellar substructure]] [Klement et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&ARv..18..567K/abstract), [White et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000fist.conf..327W/abstract).

As [[accreted-satellite-galaxies|Accreted satellite galaxies]] fall into massive potentials, they are tidally disrupted into extended stellar streams. Because individual stars keep their common origin imprinted into their kinematic and chemical properties, these halo streams remain identifiable in [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]] long after they have spatially mixed with the background halo [Klement et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&ARv..18..567K/abstract), [Majewski et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..262...99M/abstract). Surveys such as SDSS/SEGUE, APOGEE, and Gaia have systematically mapped these structures in the solar neighborhood, associating them with specific historical accretion events and revealing the highly structured nature of the local phase space [Klement et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&ARv..18..567K/abstract), [Majewski et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..262...99M/abstract). Theoretical insights are heavily bolstered by hybrid models of galaxy formation that combine N-body simulations with [[galform|GALFORM]] techniques to successfully reconstruct the intricate substructure of the Milky Way's stellar halo [De Lucia et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AN....333..460D/abstract). Within this structural evolution, long-term secular evolution driven by internal dynamical interactions between bulges, bars, and dark matter halos also significantly reshapes the disks over cosmic time [Martinet et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995FCPh...15..341M/abstract).

Theoretical models, such as those from the [[apostle-simulation-suite|Apostle simulation suite]], predict strong radial gradients in the distribution of the oldest ($t_{\rm form} < 0.8 \text{ Gyr}$) and most metal-poor (${\rm [Fe/H]} < -2.5$) stars. Counter-intuitively, the fraction of these pristine stars increases outward, with over half located outside the solar circle [Starkenburg et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2212S/abstract). The formation of these metal-poor populations is extended over cosmic time; simulations indicate that half of them formed after $z = 5.3$, and the final 10% formed as late as $z = 2.8$ [Starkenburg et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2212S/abstract). This links local observations directly to [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]], allowing astronomers to estimate the [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] at high redshift directly from these Local Group fossil records [Gilmore et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003RMxAC..17..149G/abstract). Additionally, star clusters serve as fundamental units of stellar feedback and provide unique, compact tracers of their host galaxy's overall assembly history [Adamo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020SSRv..216...69A/abstract). Extending these techniques, researchers utilize globular clusters and planetary nebulae as discrete kinematic tracers to map the assembly history in early-type galaxies well beyond the Local Group [Romanowsky et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ASPC..486..169R/abstract).

While the Milky Way is frequently treated as a universal template for $L^\star$ galaxies, the literature highlights a growing tension with this assumption. Deep spectroscopic studies reveal substantial differences between the assembly histories of the Milky Way and [[andromeda-galaxy-and-satellites|Andromeda Galaxy and Satellites]] [Escala et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhDT........14E/abstract). Notably, M31's stellar halo contains a substantial intermediate-age population, whereas the Galactic halo is almost entirely old, implying divergent major merger histories [Grebel et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005nfcd.conf....1G/abstract). Because of these conflicting evolutionary paths, generalizing the Milky Way's specific chemo-dynamical history to the broader population of disk galaxies requires caution. Looking ahead, the future use of extremely large telescopes will be critical to determine whether the detailed abundance patterns in M31's extended halo truly match the specific predictions of hierarchical assembly [Kirby et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S.156K/abstract).

Modern "chrono-chemo-dynamical" studies utilize high-resolution multi-object spectroscopy to simultaneously measure radial velocities, precise stellar ages, and detailed elemental abundances (e.g., [$\alpha$/Fe]). These diagnostics separate populations based on their varying enrichment timescales. Future endeavors, such as the 4DWARFS survey, aim to map these properties for over 140,000 stars across Milky Way streams and dwarf companions [Skúladóttir et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Msngr.190...19S/abstract). By tightly constraining the chemical and orbital properties of local substructure, galactic archeology continues to rigorously test models of [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]] and [[subhalo-physics-and-galactic-substructure|Subhalo Physics and Galactic Substructure]] on the smallest cosmological scales.

## Dark Matter Distributions and Alternative Gravitational Theories

Determining the total mass and gravitational potential of the Local Group relies heavily on the kinematics of its two dominant members, the Milky Way and Andromeda (M31). While the classical Kahn-Woltjer timing argument established foundational evidence for massive halos [Lynden-Bell et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982ac...proc...85L/abstract), modern mass derivations utilizing multi-body orbital reconstructions [Banik et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.2237B/abstract) and $\Lambda$CDM cosmological N-body analogs [Zhai et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...890...27Z/abstract) converge on a higher total Local Group mass of $M \approx 4.3 - 4.4 \times 10^{12} M_\odot$. This total mass is independently supported by scale-free estimators assuming that [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] act as collisionless tracers of the underlying potential [Di Cintio et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.423.1883D/abstract). However, this simplified two-body assumption may be complicated by historical gravitational perturbations from massive interlopers, such as Maffei 1, which may have traversed the Local Group billions of years ago [Zheng et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991A&A...247...20Z/abstract).

Despite these dynamical constraints, standard $\Lambda$CDM models face severe tensions on local scales. There is an order-of-magnitude discrepancy between the observationally derived dynamical masses of the Milky Way and M31 ($M_{200} \approx 1.4 - 1.6 \times 10^{12} M_\odot$ individually) and the $3 - 20 \times 10^{12} M_\odot$ masses expected from stellar mass-halo mass abundance matching [McGaugh et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230500858M/abstract). Furthermore, the radial peculiar velocities of many Local Group dwarfs systematically exceed $\Lambda$CDM predictions by an average of $\sim 45 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, with extreme cases like DDO 99 showing excesses up to $110 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Banik et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459.2237B/abstract). 

These kinematic anomalies have motivated explorations of alternative gravitational frameworks, most notably Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND). In [[milgrom-relation|Milgrom relation]] dynamics, the high peculiar velocities of dwarf galaxies are naturally explained as the result of a past close flyby between the Milky Way and M31. Integrating galactic orbits backward under MOND suggests this encounter occurred $7 - 11$ Gyr ago ($z > 0.8$) at a pericenter distance of $< 55 \text{ kpc}$ [Zhao et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...557L...3Z/abstract). This event would have acted as a gravitational slingshot, expelling [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] at high velocities. Such a close passage without an ensuing merger is impossible in $\Lambda$CDM due to the strong dynamical friction inherent to massive dark matter halos [Zhao et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...557L...3Z/abstract), [Banik et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv180509207B/abstract). Other novel approaches include non-local interactions modeled via fractional gravity, which have been proposed as an alternative mechanism to reproduce the observed rotation and velocity dispersion curves of dwarf galaxies without requiring standard dark matter halos [Benetti et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Univ....9..478B/abstract). In the realm of exotic dark sector physics, some theorists have advanced the "evanescent matter" proposal, positing an evolving mass component coupled with a fifth force that explicitly assists earlier protogalaxy assembly before decaying over cosmic time [Peebles et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AnP...524..591P/abstract).

Within the dark matter paradigm, the small-scale crisis has alternatively spurred interest in self-interacting dark matter (SIDM). Velocity-dependent SIDM cross-sections naturally generate central dark matter cores of $\sim 1 \text{ kpc}$ in dwarf galaxies, resolving discrepancies in observed rotation curves that collisionless cold dark matter fails to capture [Gutcke et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251005258G/abstract). Furthermore, environmental effects like tidal stripping can trigger SIDM core-collapse in infalling subhalos, leading to a much wider diversity of satellite rotation curves than predicted by standard [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] [Gutcke et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251005258G/abstract). Additional challenges to the strictly collisionless dark matter hypothesis include observations of spatial offsets—up to $1.62 \text{ kpc}$—between stellar centroids and dark matter density peaks in some massive systems (such as the Abell 3827 cluster), an effect difficult to reproduce using purely collisionless $\Lambda$CDM physics [Schaller et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453L..58S/abstract). On galactic scales, astrometric mapping of coherent stellar streams is essential for determining the shape, orientation, and potential "lumpiness" of the dark matter halo, providing a vital test of these substructure predictions [Shaya et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S.274S/abstract). Finally, analysis of the expansion outflow around the nearby M81/M82 galaxy group suggests that dark energy antigravity may dominate dynamics even on local scales of a few megaparsecs [Chernin et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Ap.....50..405C/abstract).

## Environmental Influences: Reionization and the Cosmic Web

The evolution and assembly of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] in the Local Group are heavily modulated by large-scale environmental factors. Chief among these are the cosmic ultraviolet (UV) background established during the epoch of reionization and the hydrodynamical interactions between dark matter halos and the large-scale structure.

The cosmic UV background exerted a profound influence on galaxy formation. This photoionization heated the intergalactic medium, effectively suppressing the formation of small galaxies by preventing gas accretion into shallow dark matter potential wells [Benson et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.333..177B/abstract). Note that relative velocities between baryons and dark matter at early times may have also suppressed star formation, introducing a unique form of luminosity bias in the first stars [Barkana et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PASA...30...36B/abstract). Theoretical models indicate that this form of environmental quenching reduces the expected number of luminous [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] by approximately an order of magnitude compared to the raw abundance of dark matter subhalos predicted by $N$-body simulations, thus partially alleviating the missing satellites problem [Benson et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.333..177B/abstract). Galaxies whose star formation was entirely truncated by this epoch are termed "reionization fossils." Radiative transfer simulations of the Local Group demonstrate that while the region reionized predominantly from the inside out, only about $5\%$ of present-day satellites are pure fossils where the vast majority of star formation concluded prior to the end of global reionization [Dixon et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477..867D/abstract).

However, the literature reveals a significant tension regarding the efficiency of reionization-induced quenching. Resolved stellar population studies of Local Group [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] often reveal extended star formation histories with prominent intermediate-age stellar populations [Grebel et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005nfcd.conf....1G/abstract). This continuing star formation over gigayear timescales is difficult to reconcile with models that predict an abrupt and total cessation of star formation driven by photoionization alone [Grebel et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005nfcd.conf....1G/abstract). 

Beyond radiative feedback, the hydrodynamical interaction between dwarf galaxies and the cosmic web drives severe gas depletion. As the Local Group formed, surrounding matter collapsed into a network of dense filaments and pancakes. Low-mass halos drifting through this moving baryonic web experience intense ram pressure. This process, termed "cosmic web stripping," efficiently removes the gas reservoirs of dwarf galaxies without altering their underlying dark matter kinematics [Benítez-Llambay et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763L..41B/abstract). By unbinding gas from halos, cosmic web stripping serves as a critical environmental quenching mechanism independent of internal stellar feedback or reionization. Despite this, understanding the broader gas cycle remains a challenge; how galaxies continuously accrete new baryons to sustain fuel for star formation on cosmological timescales stands as one of the major unsolved problems in extragalactic astrophysics [Putman et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S.241P/abstract). Cosmic web stripping helps to explain both the scarcity of dwarf galaxies compared to $\Lambda$CDM subhalo predictions and the wide diversity of star formation histories observed in faint galaxies [Benítez-Llambay et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...763L..41B/abstract).

Within the immediate virial volumes of the Milky Way and Andromeda, standard ram pressure stripping and tidal interactions continue to govern [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]]. The observed morphological segregation in the Local Group—where gas-poor dSphs cluster tightly around the [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], while gas-rich dwarf irregulars reside in the lower-density outskirts—is largely attributed to these local environmental stripping effects reshaping the galaxies as they fall into the massive host halos [Pasetto et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003A&A...405..931P/abstract). This mirrors early transformation hypotheses, which originally suggested that dwarf spheroidals were gas-rich irregulars transformed by direct interactions with the extended gaseous halos of their parent galaxies [van den Bergh et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981seng.proc..201V/abstract).

These massive parent halos and the broader Local Group may be embedded within an extended corona of warm-hot intergalactic medium (WHIM). As evidenced by OVI absorption clouds, this hot gas could account for a significant portion of the Universe's "missing" baryons on local scales [Nicastro et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Natur.421..719N/abstract). On even larger scales, the continuous formation of the large-scale structure contributes to gravitational shock heating, which increases gas entropy and prevents accretion, thereby driving global trends in specific star formation rates across different environments [Cen et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...741...99C/abstract).

## Constrained Simulations of the Local Environment

To bridge the gap between global cosmological predictions and the observed local cosmography, researchers employ constrained initial conditions. Instead of generating random realizations of a primordial density field, these models invert observed local large-scale structures—derived from galaxy redshift surveys and peculiar velocity fields—to reconstruct the density perturbations of the early universe. This approach ensures that the resulting [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] evolve to form a landscape mimicking the actual local cosmic web, including structures analogous to the Local Group, the Local Supercluster, and the [[virgo-cluster|Virgo Cluster]]. Accurate catalogs of the [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]], such as the CF4++ dataset, are critical for this process, allowing the dynamical reconstruction of local bulk flows (e.g., $315 \pm 40$ $\text{km s}^{-1}$ at $150\ h^{-1}$ Mpc) and the broader underlying matter density field [Courtois et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.187C/abstract). Generating and testing these models relies heavily on databases such as the HyperLeda and MIGALE projects, which serve as essential virtual instruments for the homogeneous analysis of local galaxy kinematics and dynamics [Theureau et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003astro.ph..9831T/abstract).

The [[clues-project|CLUES project]] (Constrained Local UniversE Simulations) was a pioneering effort in this domain. By constraining the initial power spectrum with observational data of the local galaxy distribution, CLUES provided a numerical laboratory explicitly designed for [[near-field-cosmology|Near-field cosmology]], simulating the Local Group within its correct large-scale environment [Gottloeber et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1005.2687G/abstract), [Yepes et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014NewAR..58....1Y/abstract). Building upon this paradigm, the [[apostle-simulation-suite|Apostle simulation suite]] (A Project Of Simulating The Local Environment) utilized advanced hydrodynamics to model Local Group analogs. APOSTLE simulations track the detailed chemical and dynamical evolution of baryons, successfully reproducing empirical trends such as the strong outward radial gradients of the oldest ($t_{\rm form} < 0.8$ Gyr) and most metal-poor ($[\text{Fe/H}] < -2.5$) stars in Milky Way-sized halos [Starkenburg et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2212S/abstract). 

A more recent milestone in constrained modeling is the HESTIA project (High-resolutions Environmental Simulations of The Immediate Area). The HESTIA simulations target a high-resolution spherical volume of $3$ to $5$ Mpc in radius centered on the Local Group, embedded within a constrained large-scale cosmographic landscape [Libeskind et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.2968L/abstract). Evolved using the AREPO moving-mesh code alongside the AURIGA galaxy formation model for baryonic physics, HESTIA successfully produces Milky Way and Andromeda (M31) analogs that closely match observations. These simulated pairs replicate the empirical halo mass, total mass ratio, relative velocities, satellite galaxy stellar mass functions, and detailed morphology, offering an ideal testing ground for evaluating the impact of the local environment on dwarf galaxy evolution [Libeskind et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.2968L/abstract).

Additionally, constrained setups such as the [[nihao-simulations|NIHAO simulations]] reveal that Local Group dwarfs possess uniquely metal-rich halo gas due to late-time accretion from other dwarfs, distinguishing their chemical evolution from more isolated field systems [Arora et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.6134A/abstract). Other large-scale constrained models, such as the [[magneticum-simulations|Magneticum (simulations)]] Pathfinder efforts, provide complementary insights by successfully reproducing the specific angular-momentum-mass relations characteristic of both disk and spheroid galaxies [Remus et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015IAUS..309..145R/abstract). On larger scales, modern cosmological models such as the [[millenniumtng|MillenniumTNG]] generally match observed stellar mass functions, although they predict higher stellar concentrations in clusters at low redshifts than current observations suggest [Ahad et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.504.1999A/abstract). Constrained initial conditions have also been successfully extended to larger scales to test whether the $\Lambda$CDM paradigm can naturally produce the specific morphological distributions observed in the [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|Local Volume and Nearby Galaxy Studies]]. For instance, the SIBELIUS DARK simulation accurately reproduces the distinct spatial distributions of giant elliptical and disk galaxies across the Local Supercluster. It organically recovers the observed concentration of massive ellipticals along the supergalactic plane, demonstrating that this striking observational feature is a natural consequence of the standard model of galaxy formation interacting with the specific local large-scale structure [Sawala et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024NatAs...8..247S/abstract).

## The Local Bulk Flow and Large-Scale Structure

The Local Group is dynamically embedded within a deeply structured cosmographical landscape that extends far beyond its immediate gravitational boundaries. In its most immediate vicinity, the Local Group resides within the Local Sheet, a flattened array of galaxies that is geometrically and dynamically distinct from the broader Local Supercluster. This sheet has a remarkably low spatial dispersion about the mid-plane of merely $\sim 230 \text{ kpc}$ and is circumscribed by a "Council of Giants" at a radius of roughly $3.75 \text{ Mpc}$, which effectively demarcates the outer limit of the Local Group's realm of influence [McCall et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440..405M/abstract). Measurements of the [[local-cosmic-domain|Local cosmic domain]] indicate that the mean total matter density in the Local Volume ($D < 11 \text{ Mpc}$) is notably sub-critical, yielding $\Omega_m \approx 0.17$ and dropping further when averaged over larger volumes [Karachentsev et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018AN....339..615K/abstract).

On larger scales, the kinematics of the Local Group are dominated by a massive [[peculiar-velocity-field|Peculiar velocity field]] driven by surrounding [[galaxy-overdensities|Galaxy overdensities]]. The Local Group, along with its neighbors, is streaming at high velocities toward the Great Attractor, a massive gravitational anomaly located within the [[laniakea-supercluster|Laniakea Supercluster]]. Observational mapping of this region has historically been severely impeded by the Zone of Avoidance, the dusty disk of the Milky Way that optically obscures approximately $25\%$ of the extragalactic sky [Kraan-Korteweg et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000LNP...556..301K/abstract). However, multi-wavelength surveys in the near-infrared, radio, and X-ray regimes have increasingly penetrated the Zone of Avoidance, revealing complex filamentary and wall-like structures. These observations identify the massive Norma cluster (Abell 3627) as the likely high-density core of the Great Attractor region [Kraan-Korteweg et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000LNP...556..301K/abstract). Complementary multi-wavelength efforts, particularly NIR and FIR surveys, have penetrated this obscuration to discover previously hidden [[high-mass-galaxies|High-mass galaxies]] and populations undergoing active morphological transformation directly within the Great Attractor Wall [Kraan-Korteweg et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008mear.confE...8K/abstract).

The amplitude and extent of this local bulk flow offer critical tests for cosmological models and the assumed scale of cosmic homogeneity. Recent cosmographic reconstructions leveraging highly complete peculiar velocity datasets (such as the CF4++ compendium combining WALLABY, FAST, and DESI surveys) measure an immense bulk flow of 
$$v_{\rm bulk} = 315 \pm 40 \text{ km s}^{-1}$$ 
at a distance of $150\, h^{-1} \text{ Mpc}$ [Courtois et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.187C/abstract). 

Intriguingly, these data indicate that the "dynamical scale of homogeneity"—the theoretical scale beyond which peculiar velocities should average to zero—has not yet been reached, even within the interval of $200 \text{ to } 300\, h^{-1} \text{ Mpc}$ from the observer [Courtois et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250201308C/abstract), [Courtois et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.187C/abstract). The persistence of these flows points to powerful gravitational influences from even more distant massive structures. Refined velocity maps have not only sharpened the boundaries of the supercluster and the Coma cluster but have also uncovered previously hidden mass concentrations, such as the Vela supercluster, further demonstrating that the Local Group's motion is governed by a cosmic web that remains dynamically heterogeneous on surprisingly large scales [Courtois et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...701A.187C/abstract). Furthermore, recent clustering analyses of blue galaxies across narrow redshift shells ($z < 0.12$) serve as powerful empirical tests of cosmic clustering evolution within this highly non-linear local universe [Franco et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202574F/abstract). Mapping this structured environment also benefits other domains of astrophysics. For instance, density reconstructions from the HESTIA and HAMLET simulations have been used to model the cosmic dispersion measure (DM) in the local universe out to $120 \text{ Mpc}$, providing a critical tool for analyzing the distances and environments of Fast Radio Bursts originating in the Local Volume [Huang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.538.2785H/abstract).

## The Galactic Center as a Fundamental Laboratory

As the closest example of a galactic nucleus, [[the-galactic-center|The Galactic Center]] serves as an exquisite astrophysical laboratory for investigating supermassive black holes (SMBHs) and their immediate environments [Ghez et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S..89G/abstract). By tracking the detailed orbits of stars deep within the Galaxy's central potential well, astronomers can conduct precision tests of General Relativity on the mass scale of an SMBH—a regime inaccessible to studies of stellar-mass black holes [Ghez et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S..89G/abstract). Furthermore, high-resolution dynamical measurements provide stringent constraints on the extended dark matter distribution predicted by $\Lambda$CDM to pool around the SMBH, as well as limits on the presence of unseen stellar remnants that could dominate the [[stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|Stellar and Galactic Dynamics]] at the smallest radial scales [Ghez et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S..89G/abstract).

The [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]] typically harbor [[dense-stellar-systems|Dense stellar systems]]. In the Milky Way, the nuclear star cluster provides a unique, highly resolved environment for observing exotic stellar dynamical processes and secular evolution mechanisms that shape galaxies over cosmic time [Ghez et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S..89G/abstract), [Ginsburg et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019BAAS...51c.220G/abstract). Studying star formation and feedback within this localized, extreme environment bridges the gap between resolved Galactic astrophysics and the unresolved physics of extragalactic nuclei [Ginsburg et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019BAAS...51c.220G/abstract).

Although the Milky Way's SMBH is presently under-luminous, the physical mechanisms governing its interaction with local gas and stars are fundamental to the broader paradigm of AGN feedback. In cosmological models, the co-evolution of SMBHs and their host dark matter halos is regulated by a feeding and feedback cycle: gas cooling fuels the central engine, which in turn drives energetic outflows [Eckert et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Galax..12...24E/abstract). This mechanical and radiative feedback tightly couples with the surrounding hot atmospheres of halos, injecting energy that can exceed the local gravitational binding energy and halt runaway gas cooling [Eckert et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Galax..12...24E/abstract). 

The necessity of this process is strongly supported by hydrodynamical simulations such as the [[overwhelmingly-large-simulations-project|OverWhelmingly Large Simulations project]]. Without AGN feedback, models predict that massive galaxies incorrectly remain dominated by central star formation down to redshift $z=0$ [van de Voort et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..778V/abstract). When AGN feedback is properly included, it effectively quenches central star formation, forcing galaxies to grow [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]] through the accretion of [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]] and major mergers at late times [van de Voort et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..778V/abstract). Consequently, detailed observations of the feedback processes at work in the Galactic Center are vital for informing the sub-grid physics required to accurately model massive galaxy evolution across the Universe [Ghez et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S..89G/abstract).

## Future Frontiers in Near-Field Observations

The discipline of [[near-field-cosmology|Near-field cosmology]] is entering a transformative era, driven by a new generation of ground- and space-based observatories. These facilities will exponentially expand our capacity to resolve the faintest galaxies and oldest stars, shifting the observational boundary from the immediate Local Group to broader [[local-volume-and-nearby-galaxy-studies|Local Volume and Nearby Galaxy Studies]].

The *Gaia* mission has fundamentally altered our understanding of galactic assembly by providing high-precision astrometry that maps the [[6d-phase-space|6D phase space]] of local stellar populations and halo streams [Klement et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&ARv..18..567K/abstract), [Chakrabarti et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017IAUS..321..108C/abstract). Building on this foundation, the upcoming Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) at the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, alongside space missions like Euclid and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, will conduct deep, wide-field imaging to discover ultra-faint dwarf galaxies and trace [[stellar-substructure|Stellar substructure]] across the local cosmos [Majewski et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..262...99M/abstract), [Pace et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241107424P/abstract). The influx of massive datasets is motivating the integration of sophisticated [[heuristic-models|Heuristic models]] with survey data to effectively trace Galactic evolution through complex patterns in starlight variability and stellar chemistry [Blancato et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200910661B/abstract). To synthesize these impending discoveries, resources such as the Local Volume Database (LVDB) are being compiled to provide a comprehensive, standardized library of structural, chemical, and kinematic properties for dwarf galaxies within $\sim 3$ Mpc [Pace et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241107424P/abstract).

In space, JWST is poised to observe the most ancient stellar populations in unprecedented detail. It will probe the fossil records of galaxies with present-day stellar masses of $10^3 \lesssim M_\star/\mathrm{M}_\odot \lesssim 10^9$, reaching absolute magnitudes of $M_{1500} > -9$ at $z \sim 7$ [Boylan-Kolchin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462L..51B/abstract). By resolving these populations over volumes comparable to the Hubble Ultra-Deep Field, JWST directly links Local Group relics to the [[early-universe|Early universe]] and the epoch of cosmic reionization.

A major future frontier is extending high-precision stellar archaeology beyond the Local Group. Currently, the unambiguous determination of star formation histories via the oldest main sequence turn-offs is restricted to galaxies within $\sim 1$ Mpc. Extremely Large Telescopes (ELTs), with diameters of 50-150m, operating at the diffraction limit, will extend this capability out to $\sim 17-18$ Mpc. This distance encompasses high-density environments such as the [[virgo-cluster|Virgo Cluster]] and the Fornax cluster [Tolstoy et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006IAUS..232..293T/abstract), [Wyse et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006IAUS..232..140W/abstract). This volumetric expansion is critical for overcoming the sample variance inherent to the Local Group and for testing the environmental drivers of galaxy evolution across diverse cosmic densities. This expansion signifies a strategic shift toward resolving individual stars in the deep outskirts of galaxies far beyond the Local Group to rigorously test models of hierarchical accretion [Crnojević et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ASSL..434...31C/abstract).

Concurrently, massive spectroscopic campaigns will map the chemo-dynamical properties of these faint systems. Surveys such as 4DWARFS on the 4MOST facility will acquire chrono-chemo-kinematical data for 140,000 stars in local streams and satellites [Skúladóttir et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Msngr.190...19S/abstract). On ELT scales, multi-object spectrographs will assemble line-of-sight velocities and metallicities for thousands of individual stars per dwarf galaxy. Such dense spectroscopic sampling is required to robustly measure internal kinematics, strictly constrain the density profiles of dark matter halos, and isolate the effects of baryonic feedback from underlying dark matter physics [Battaglia et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251213827B/abstract). Broadly, upcoming telescopes and advanced instrumentation will profoundly enhance our ability to map internal proper motions and trace detailed baryon cycling within dwarf halos [Haynes et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019IAUS..344....3H/abstract). This stellar spectroscopy will be highly complemented by multi-wavelength observations; for instance, there are profound synergies between extremely large telescope (E-ELT) studies of stars and ALMA/SKA studies of molecular and neutral gas in star-forming regions [Tolstoy et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010amjs.conf..161T/abstract). Additionally, pathfinder radio arrays like MeerKAT harbor immense potential to detect extremely low-mass, gas-rich dwarf galaxies—analogous to Leo T—lurking in nearby structures like the Centaurus region [Adams et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170910369A/abstract).

These observational milestones will be tightly coupled with theoretical advancements. Next-generation [[galacticus|Galacticus]] frameworks trace early star formation and metal enrichment, allowing for direct comparisons between cosmological initial conditions and the detailed local observables mapped by this incoming suite of facilities. Theoretical tracing of these models increasingly relies on refined [[halo-merger-trees|Halo merger trees]] to link primordial density peaks to present-day resolved stellar fossils [Magg et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JOSS....7.4417M/abstract).
