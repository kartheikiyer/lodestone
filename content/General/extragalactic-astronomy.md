#
**Extragalactic astronomy** is the branch of astrophysics devoted to the study of objects and phenomena beyond the Milky Way. As a discipline, it bridges the physical analysis of individual stellar systems with the broader cosmological framework of the expanding universe. The field formally emerged in the 1920s when the identification of [[standard-candles|Standard candles]] in nearby "nebulae" definitively proved they were independent "island universes" rather than local gas clouds [Sandage et al. (1975)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1975gaun.book.....S/abstract). Today, extragalactic astronomy encompasses the formation, evolution, and dynamics of a vast array of objects—ranging from individual [[very-young-galaxies|Very Young Galaxies]] and active galactic nuclei (AGN) to the sprawling "Cosmic Web" of dark matter and the diffuse baryonic gas of the intergalactic medium. By utilizing multi-wavelength [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]], astronomers can reconstruct the hierarchical [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] and the [[chemical-abundances-and-nucleosynthesis|Chemical Abundances and Nucleosynthesis]] of the cosmos across cosmic time [Binney et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998gaas.book.....B/abstract).

A central triumph of modern extragalactic research is the detailed quantification of the cosmic star-formation history. Observations robustly demonstrate that the universe's global star-formation rate density peaked approximately 3.5 billion years after the Big Bang (at $z \approx 1.9$, an epoch colloquially known as "Cosmic Noon") before exponentially declining to its present rate [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Over this timeline, galaxies evolved into a strongly bimodal population—a continuously star-forming "blue cloud" and a passive, early-type "red sequence"—whose morphological and photometric properties are deeply regulated by their local environments [Haines et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021essp.confE..51H/abstract). Crucially, the evolutionary trajectories of these galaxies are inextricably linked to the supermassive black holes at their centers. The co-evolution of black hole accretion and stellar mass assembly is mediated by AGN feedback, a process that injects immense mechanical and radiative energy into the host galaxy, ultimately quenching star formation and shaping the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|Galaxy luminosity function]]. 

In the contemporary era, the field is driven by massively multiplexed, multi-wavelength, and multi-messenger campaigns. These advanced [[cosmological-probes|Cosmological probes]] have opened new observational windows, yet they have also exposed significant theoretical tensions. For example, recent ultra-deep space-based observations of the early universe challenge traditional semi-analytical models by revealing an unexpectedly rapid onset of early stellar assembly and anomalous mid-infrared number counts. Major open questions remain regarding the exact physical properties of the "first stars," the energetic mechanics of the Epoch of Reionization, and the origin of excess diffuse light in the Cosmic Infrared Background. To resolve these tensions, researchers are pushing into [[low-surface-brightness-astronomy|Low Surface Brightness Astronomy]] to uncover the faint fossil records of local galaxy assembly [Brough et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200111067B/abstract), while deploying next-generation facilities like the [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]] and employing advanced astrostatistics to [[sky-mapping-and-surveys|Sky Mapping and Surveys]] the dark sector and survey the unmapped depths of the extragalactic sky [Laureijs et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ASPC..485..495L/abstract), [Joachimi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASPC..507..401J/abstract).

## Historical Development and the Discovery of the Extragalactic Universe

Prior to the 1920s, the physical nature of the diffuse, fuzzy sources documented in early [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]], such as the [[messier-catalog|Messier Catalog]] and John Dreyer's 1888 *New General Catalogue*, remained a subject of intense controversy. The central question was whether these "nebulae" were relatively small clouds of gas residing within the Milky Way or independent, massive stellar systems located at vast distances. This tension culminated in the 1920 "Great Debate" between Harlow Shapley and Heber Curtis. Shapley argued that the Milky Way encompassed the entirety of the known universe, while Curtis championed the "Island Universes" hypothesis, positing that spiral nebulae were external galaxies comparable in scale to our own.

The Great Debate was decisively resolved in 1925 by Edwin Hubble. By identifying Cepheid variable stars—critical [[standard-candles|Standard candles]]—in the Andromeda Nebula (M31) and other local targets, Hubble derived distance estimates that placed these objects far beyond the spatial confines of the Milky Way. This breakthrough marked the formal birth of extragalactic astronomy, proving that galaxies are independent, dynamically bound systems composed of billions of stars. In the subsequent decade, Hubble utilized high-quality photographic plates to systematically classify these newly confirmed galaxies, establishing the morphological sequence that remains foundational today. This taxonomic effort culminated decades later in major data repositories such as the Third Reference Catalogue of [[bright-galaxies|Bright galaxies]] ([[rc3-catalog|RC3 catalog]]), which contains detailed parameters and classifications for over 23,000 galaxies [de Vaucouleurs et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991rc3..book.....D/abstract).

Following the identification of the extragalactic universe, Hubble achieved a second paradigm-shifting breakthrough. In 1929, building on earlier radial velocity measurements, he demonstrated that the recession velocities of galaxies correlate linearly with their distances. This [[redshift-distribution|Redshift distribution]], now widely known as Hubble's Law, provided the first direct observational evidence for an expanding universe. For galaxies at relatively low redshifts ($z \ll 1$), this kinematic relationship is parameterized as:
$$cz = H_0 d$$
where $c$ is the speed of light, $z$ is the [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]], $d$ is the proper distance, and $H_0$ is the Hubble constant. This single empirical relationship bridged observational astronomy and theoretical cosmology, cementing the expanding universe paradigm.

The mid-20th century inaugurated a new era of precision in extragalactic astronomy, largely driven by the commissioning of the 200-inch telescope at Palomar Observatory. Under the direction of astronomers like Walter Baade and Edwin Hubble, ambitious observational programs were launched to systematically map the extragalactic distance scale and refine the expansion rate of the universe [Sandage et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ARA&A..37..445S/abstract). Observations from Palomar successfully recalibrated the Cepheid period-luminosity relation, leading to the discovery of distinct stellar populations (Population I and II) and a drastic revision of the cosmic distance scale [Sandage et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ARA&A..37..445S/abstract). This pursuit of precision cosmology was further accelerated by space-based platforms; for example, the 1994 Wide Field and Planetary Camera 2 (WFPC2) observations of the spiral galaxy M100 aboard the Hubble Space Telescope increased the number of galaxies available for direct Population I star measurement by a factor of 100 [Freedman et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...435L..31F/abstract). Today, this pursuit continues through the Extragalactic Distance Database (EDD), which utilizes the Tip of the Red Giant Branch (TRGB) as a high-quality distance indicator out to $10$ Mpc [Anand et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....162...80A/abstract). While these early surveys were designed to solidify classical cosmological parameters, they serendipitously paved the way for [[extragalactic-phenomena|Extragalactic Phenomena]] by uncovering the first active galaxies and quasars—phenomena entirely unforeseen during the initial formulation of the expanding universe paradigm [Sandage et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ARA&A..37..445S/abstract).

## Galaxy Morphology and Stellar Population Physical Properties

The classification of galaxies has historically relied on visual inspection to place objects along the Hubble sequence, broadly dividing them into early-type (elliptical and lenticular) and late-type (spiral and irregular) systems. Recently, the field of morphological taxonomy has increasingly integrated automated methodologies, utilizing machine learning algorithms to classify galaxies directly from pixel data [Hocking et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.1108H/abstract).

Morphological types correlate strongly with photometric properties, most notably in the color-magnitude diagram. Extensive surveys have confirmed a persistent bimodal distribution of galaxies—a passive "red sequence" and a star-forming "blue cloud"—that extends out to $z \approx 1$ [Haines et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021essp.confE..51H/abstract). A galaxy's position in this parameter space is heavily influenced by its environment; denser regions, such as [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]], preferentially host redder, early-type galaxies, reflecting environmental quenching processes that suppress star formation [Cooper et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..235..167C/abstract). To further constrain these populations, near-infrared surveys like the K20 survey have provided critical insights into the nature of "old and dusty" Extremely Red Objects (EROs) and the broader history of mass assembly within the "redshift desert" [Cimatti et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005mmgf.conf..237C/abstract).

Beyond broad-band photometry, extracting precise [[intrinsic-galaxy-and-stellar-properties|Intrinsic Galaxy and Stellar Properties]] requires integrated-light spectroscopy. A galaxy's integrated spectrum is a superposition of individual stellar spectra, modified by interstellar gas and dust. The [[integrated-light-method|Integrated light method]] through the near-infrared is dominated by bound-free transitions in stellar photospheres, while the infrared captures thermal emission from dust. Absorption lines primarily originate in stellar atmospheres and provide crucial constraints on the age and [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] of the stellar populations. To interpret these integrated spectra, researchers rely on extensive empirical templates, such as the extended MILES library, which provides derived effective temperatures, surface gravities, and detailed chemical abundances (including $[\alpha/\text{Fe}]$ and $[\text{Fe/H}]$) essential for modeling stellar populations [García Pérez et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.4496G/abstract). Conversely, emission lines reveal the temperature, density, and chemical composition of the interstellar medium. Observations reveal striking similarities between the globular cluster systems of spiral and elliptical galaxies, suggesting they formed primarily at early cosmic epochs with limited subsequent contributions from galaxy mergers [Forbes et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASPC..296..517F/abstract). High-resolution spectroscopy of these [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]] also facilitates "extragalactic archaeology," allowing the reconstruction of galaxy assembly histories through chemical tagging [Beasley et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260105849B/abstract).

These spectroscopic and photometric data are synthesized to infer stellar masses and evaluate [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]]. A critical assumption in converting a galaxy's luminosity to its mass is the [[stellar-mass-function|Stellar Mass Function]] (IMF). Observations of the massive end of the IMF in nearby galaxies suggest that it may be broadly universal. Under the assumption of a universal IMF, the observed global stellar mass density at any epoch aligns well with the time integral of the cosmic star-formation rate density [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Homogenized [[astronomical-catalogs|Astronomical catalogs]], such as [[hyperleda|HyperLEDA]] and the HECATE catalog, now provide these derived physical parameters—including morphological classifications, stellar masses, and star formation rates—for hundreds of thousands of galaxies, forming the foundation for large-scale demographic studies of the [[low-redshift-universe|Low-redshift universe]] [Paturel et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997A&AS..124..109P/abstract), [Kovlakas et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.506.1896K/abstract).

## Active Galactic Nuclei and Black Hole-Host Co-evolution

Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) are characterized by extremely luminous, non-thermal emission spanning the entire electromagnetic spectrum, from radio wavelengths to X-ray and very-high-energy (VHE) gamma rays. This emission emanates from a compact central region, typically less than a few parsecs across, and is powered by the accretion of matter onto a supermassive black hole (SMBH) with a mass in the range of $10^6 - 10^9 M_{\odot}$. The efficiency of converting gravitational potential energy into radiation in these environments is so high that an AGN can outshine its host galaxy by up to a factor of a thousand. Observational manifestations include broad and narrow optical/UV emission lines, as well as highly collimated relativistic jets. Taxonomically, this encompasses Seyfert galaxies, quasars, and [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]]. Among [[radio-source-populations|Radio source populations]], AGN-driven radio galaxies are empirically subdivided into steep-spectrum extended sources and flat-spectrum compact sources, the latter typically defined by a spectral index $\alpha < 0.4$ at $\sim 1$ GHz.

A cornerstone of extragalactic astrophysics is the established co-evolution of SMBHs and their host galaxies. Locally, this is evidenced by the tight correlation between the mass of the central black hole and the stellar mass or velocity dispersion of the host's spheroidal component. Across cosmic time, the global comoving rate of central black hole accretion closely mirrors the cosmic star-formation rate density [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Recent JWST observations have extended this census into the early universe, demonstrating that the cosmic AGN luminosity history rises by approximately $1$ dex from $z=10.5$ to $z=5.5$, evolving in tandem with early stellar assembly [D'Silva et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990...44D/abstract). Comprehensive catalogs tracking these earliest supermassive black holes, such as databases compiling all currently known quasars at $z > 5.7$, serve as vital tools for investigating both AGN physics and the timeline of cosmic reionization [Bosman et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020zndo...3634964B/abstract). Programs like the Spitzer Extended Deep Survey (SEDS) further support this by aiming to take a comprehensive census of stellar mass and black hole assembly back to the era of reionization [Fazio et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..446..347F/abstract).

To maintain this co-evolutionary scaling, theoretical models universally invoke AGN feedback—the injection of immense mechanical and radiative energy into the surrounding interstellar and intergalactic media. This feedback is critical for quenching star formation and preventing the formation of [[overly-massive-galaxies|Overly massive galaxies]]. However, recent large-scale surveys of intermediate-redshift groups and clusters over the last $\sim 6$ Gyr complicate standard triggering and quenching models, suggesting that while dense cluster environments can alter the demographic fraction of active galaxies, they may simultaneously suppress their overall power output [Bravo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250913530B/abstract).

Beyond their role in galaxy evolution, AGN are primary laboratories for high-energy astrophysics. Ground-based Cherenkov telescopes and water Cherenkov observatories have detected VHE (TeV) gamma-ray emission and rapid variability from nearby AGN, notably M87 and BL Lacertae objects such as Markarian 421 and Markarian 501 [Horan et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004NewAR..48..527H/abstract), [Ureña-Mena et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231002484U/abstract). Additionally, the MAGIC telescope has achieved notable detections of extragalactic objects, including 3C 279 and S5 0716+714, observed during their optical high states [Kranich et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009arXiv0912.3830K/abstract). Because these VHE photons are subject to energy-dependent attenuation via pair production with the [[metagalactic-radiation-field|Metagalactic radiation field]] (the Extragalactic Background Light), the study of intrinsic blazar spectra acts as a critical indirect probe of the universe's total integrated light [Domínguez et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410.2556D/abstract).

## The Cosmic Star-Formation and Chemical Assembly History

The evolution of the universe's baryonic content is fundamentally characterized by the [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]], often visualized via the cosmic star-formation rate density curve. This framework traces the comoving star-formation rate density (SFRD), commonly denoted as $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$, across cosmic time to provide a global metric for galaxy growth.

Data from [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]] consistently demonstrate that the universe underwent an epoch of peak galaxy growth, termed Cosmic Noon, approximately $3.5$ Gyr after the Big Bang at $z \approx 1.9$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). During this era, global star-formation rates were roughly an order of magnitude higher than present-day values. To further probe this active era, the SMILES survey uses JWST/NIRSpec medium-resolution spectra to investigate obscured AGN and galaxy evolution specifically at Cosmic Noon ($z \sim 1-3$) [Zhu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250812599Z/abstract). Furthermore, the [[help-survey|HELP survey]] and [[herschel-multi-tiered-extragalactic-survey|Herschel Multi-tiered Extragalactic Survey]] (HerMES) project map dust-obscured star formation over $380$ square degrees using the SPIRE and PACS instruments aboard the Herschel Space Observatory [Oliver et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.424.1614O/abstract). These efforts build on early sub-millimeter findings from instruments like SCUBA, which revealed that sub-millimeter galaxies have a median redshift of roughly 2 and may actually dominate the global star formation rate at epochs beyond $z=1$ [Borys et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhDT.........2B/abstract). Following this peak, $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$ declined exponentially with an e-folding timescale of $3.9$ Gyr [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Integrating this history reveals that $50\%$ of the total stellar mass in the local universe had already assembled by $z = 1.3$, while less than $1\%$ formed during the epoch of reionization [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). 

Prior to Cosmic Noon, the SFRD rose rapidly. Recent deep-field observations show that the cosmic star-formation history increased by $\approx 1$ dex between $z=13.5$ and $z=5.5$ [D'Silva et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990...44D/abstract). The onset of this star formation occurred astonishingly early; spectroscopic detections of remarkably mature systems at $z > 9$ indicate that the [[formation-epoch|Formation epoch]] of the very first stellar populations must have occurred just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang [Hashimoto et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.557..392H/abstract).

The conversion of pristine intergalactic gas into stars intrinsically drives [[chemical-abundances-and-nucleosynthesis|Chemical Abundances and Nucleosynthesis]]. As early generations of stars evolved and underwent supernovae, they enriched their host galaxies and the surrounding intergalactic medium. Observational constraints indicate that the mean cosmic metallicity rose to roughly $0.001$ $Z_\odot$ by $z = 6$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). This chemical assembly provides a vital fossil record of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. In the more recent universe, direct observations of chemical enrichment in the interstellar medium are possible through MeV gamma-ray spectroscopy; for example, observations with the INTEGRAL observatory detect radioactive isotope decay across star-forming regions [Panther et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MmSAI..90..276P/abstract).

Within this global evolutionary framework, the majority of star-forming galaxies reside on a tight correlation between their stellar mass and star-formation rate, known as the Main Sequence of Galaxies. However, there is ongoing tension in the literature regarding how the local environment influences this relation. Observations from the HELP survey and others indicate that the main sequence is sensitive to environmental density at Cosmic Noon ($1.5 < z < 2$), where the mean specific [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] is suppressed in denser regions [Duivenvoorden et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..277D/abstract). Conversely, it remains ambiguous whether the main sequence is entirely independent of environment at lower ($z < 1.5$) and higher ($z > 2$) redshifts, as conflicting models and observational limits currently prevent a definitive consensus [Duivenvoorden et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..277D/abstract).

Finally, the global assembly of stars is inextricably linked to the growth of supermassive black holes. The cosmic accretion rate onto central black holes—traced via active galactic nuclei (AGN) luminosities—exhibits a coevolutionary trajectory that closely mirrors the exponential rise and subsequent fall of $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$ across cosmic time [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract)[D'Silva et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990...44D/abstract).

## The High-Redshift Frontier and the Epoch of Reionization

The exploration of the cosmic 'dark ages' and the Epoch of Reionization represents a critical frontier in [[extragalactic-astronomical-surveys|Extragalactic astronomical surveys]]. This era marks the transition from a neutral universe to a fully ionized intergalactic medium, driven by the emergence of the first stars and accreting black holes. Recent observational breakthroughs have forced a paradigm shift in [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]], revealing a universe that reached early maturity much faster than older theoretical models anticipated.

High-Redshift Surveys utilizing the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have pushed the observational horizon to unprecedented epochs, enabling the systematic study of [[very-young-galaxies|Very Young Galaxies]]. Deep spectroscopic programs, such as [[jades|JADES]], have confirmed emission-line targets up to $z=13$, encompassing over 400 galaxies at $z>5$ [D'Eugenio et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240406531D/abstract). These modern capabilities build upon a long history of observational searches for star-forming galaxies at $z > 6$, which have continually refined the selection techniques (such as the Lyman-break method) necessary to construct robust [[nuv-luminosity-function|NUV luminosity function]] estimates for these early galaxy populations [Finkelstein et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PASA...33...37F/abstract). Similarly, the UltraVISTA public survey has enabled accurate determinations of the type-dependent stellar mass function from $z=0$ to $z=4$, alongside robust measurements of high-redshift luminosity functions [McCracken et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Msngr.154...29M/abstract). A cornerstone discovery in the search for First Light is the gravitationally lensed galaxy MACS1149-JD1. It exhibits a [[spectroscopic-redshift|Spectroscopic redshift]] of $z = 9.1096 \pm 0.0006$, but its rest-frame optical colors indicate a dominant stellar component that must have formed approximately $250$ Myr after the Big Bang, or $z \approx 15$ [Hashimoto et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.557..392H/abstract). Through self-consistent census data across [[deep-field-campaigns|Deep field campaigns]], astronomers have determined that the cosmic star-formation rate density rose by approximately $1$ dex between $z=13.5$ and $z=5.5$ [D'Silva et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...990...44D/abstract). Overall, these latest JWST developments provide a new view of [[first-light-galaxies|First-light galaxies]], revealing highly efficient galaxy formation and allowing for the characterization of the very first populations of accreting black holes [Matthee et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251104843M/abstract).

Despite the detection of these first-light galaxies, the precise mechanics of reionization remain highly constrained. The rise of the mean cosmic metallicity to $\sim 0.001 Z_{\odot}$ by $z=6$ was likely accompanied by the production of fewer than ten hydrogen Lyman-continuum photons per baryon. This represents a remarkably tight energy budget for the complete cosmological reionization of the intergalactic medium [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). 

To track the phase transition of the intergalactic medium directly and independently of luminous sources, astronomers increasingly rely on 21-cm Cosmology [Furlanetto et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S..83F/abstract). Observations of the 21-cm spectral line of neutral hydrogen offer the potential to map the neutral intergalactic medium continuously prior to and during reionization. A critical theoretical development in this domain—absent from older textbook structure formation models—is the recognition of a significant relative velocity between baryons and dark matter in the early universe. This velocity difference actively suppresses star formation at very early times ($t \sim 200$ Myr), thereby generating a distinct "luminosity bias" [Barkana et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PASA...30...36B/abstract), [Barkana et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1401.7935B/abstract). 

The spatial variation of this suppression enhances large-scale clustering, predicting a prominent cosmic web in the 21-cm intensity distribution on scales of $\sim 100$ comoving Mpc. This structural signature significantly improves the theoretical feasibility of detecting the era of first stars with forthcoming low-frequency radio arrays [Barkana et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PASA...30...36B/abstract). However, the literature remains cautious regarding several open questions; it is not yet definitively resolved whether 21-cm observations can successfully disentangle the delicate signals of the intergalactic medium from overwhelming galactic foregrounds. Furthermore, the exact physical properties and specific environments of the "first stars" and initial black hole seeds remain an active, unresolved debate in the field.

## Large-Scale Structure and the Intergalactic Medium

The large-scale spatial distribution of matter traces an intricate, web-like network of dark matter known as the Cosmic Web. This structure is characterized by massive nodes, elongated filaments, planar sheets, and expansive underdense voids. The statistical characterization of Galaxy Clustering is a primary tool for mapping this structure and connecting luminous galaxies to their underlying dark matter halos. Additionally, gravitational lensing provides independent and critical insights into the underlying distribution of galactic structure and dark matter halos, allowing astronomers to map mass directly regardless of its light emission [Narayan et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998NewAR..42...73N/abstract). A [[galaxy-redshift-survey|Galaxy redshift survey]] such as VIPERS maps the large-scale distribution of galaxies at $0.5 < z < 1.2$, utilizing samples of $\sim 100,000$ redshifts to track the growth of massive structures over the last 8 billion years [Guzzo et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013Msngr.151...41G/abstract). Similarly, the WAVES survey probes structure growth across scales from $1 \text{ kpc}$ to $10 \text{ Mpc}$ to test the Cold Dark Matter paradigm over a $7 \text{ Gyr}$ timeline [Driver et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Msngr.175...46D/abstract). However, theoretical modeling of Galaxy Clustering remains imperfect; for instance, the clustering amplitude observed at faint flux limits in the SWIRE survey disagrees with standard GALICS semi-analytic models [Waddington et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ASPC..357..267W/abstract). While large-scale mapping efforts trace the broader Cosmic Web, detailed surveys of local analogs provide essential physical ground truth. For instance, observations of the Milky Way's own Galactic Center serve as a crucial laboratory for understanding the physics of galactic nuclei and star formation under extreme conditions, offering high-resolution insights that inform extragalactic models [Schoedel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240604022S/abstract). This detailed study of the local "galactic neighborhood" (up to $z \approx 0.1$) acts as a critical laboratory for studying the complex energy flows and chemical cycles that inform and calibrate high-redshift models [Wang et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011nlaw.confI...5W/abstract).

Within the Cosmic Web, a galaxy's local environment strongly drives its evolutionary trajectory. Denser regions—such as [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]], which represent the largest virialized structures in the universe with masses of $10^{14}$--$10^{15} M_\odot$ and velocity dispersions of $\sim 1000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$—predominantly host redder, passive, early-type galaxies. The exact mechanisms of environmental quenching and active galactic nuclei (AGN) feedback remain debated, with recent multi-wavelength studies highlighting complex, mass-dependent environmental impacts on AGN accretion [Bravo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250913530B/abstract). To map the most massive nodes of the Cosmic Web, astronomers employ the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich (SZ) effect, which allows for redshift-independent mass selection of galaxy clusters [Carlstrom et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ARA&A..40..643C/abstract), [Mroczkowski et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019SSRv..215...17M/abstract). Additionally, the detection of diffuse radio emission in intercluster bridges demonstrates that cosmic-ray electrons and magnetic fields permeate the Cosmic Web on megaparsec scales [Wittor et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Univ....9..319W/abstract). Mapping these vast radio structures increasingly relies on global collaboration; for instance, the development of [[radio-surveys|Radio surveys]] in Ghana has established a crucial node in the African Very Long Baseline Interferometry Network (AVN) [Duah Asabere et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150308850D/abstract), while the South African MeerKAT telescope plays a central role in studying both extended radio structures and the broader history of galaxy formation and evolution [Camilo et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018NatAs...2..594C/abstract). At lower redshifts, radio observatories have laid a foundational legacy; for example, the Arecibo Observatory has been instrumental in mapping local HI structure and measuring extended rotation curves that provide compelling proof for the existence of dark matter [Giovanelli et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AIPC.1035....3G/abstract).

The baryonic material residing between galaxies in these filaments and voids constitutes the Intergalactic Medium (IGM). The IGM contains the bulk of the universe's baryonic mass and serves as the primary reservoir of gas from which galaxies accrete material. Because the IGM is highly diffuse, its properties (density, temperature, chemical composition, and ionization state) are predominantly studied through absorption features in the spectra of background cosmological sources, most notably the Lyman-alpha Forest in the spectra of distant quasars. 

During the epoch of reionization and the preceding cosmic "dark ages," the neutral IGM can be mapped using the redshifted $21\text{-cm}$ line of neutral hydrogen [Furlanetto et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S..83F/abstract). As theoretical models indicate, large-scale clustering in the early IGM was dramatically enhanced by a relative velocity offset between baryons and dark matter, which imprinted a prominent signature on $100$ comoving Mpc scales [Barkana et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PASA...30...36B/abstract), [Barkana et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1401.7935B/abstract).

## Galaxy Environments and Transformation Processes

The properties of galaxies are profoundly shaped by their local surroundings, varying continuously from isolated [[field-populations|Field Populations]] to the dense, virialized environments of [[clusters-of-galaxies|Clusters of galaxies]]. This environmental dependence manifests most clearly in the morphology-density relation: denser regions host systematically larger fractions of early-type (elliptical and lenticular) galaxies that are redder, less gas-rich, and exhibit lower specific star-formation rates compared to their field counterparts. Large-scale mapping efforts, such as the VIPERS program, demonstrate that the transition of galaxies from the star-forming "blue cloud" into the passive "red sequence" is strongly accelerated in these dense regimes up to redshifts of $z \approx 1.2$ [Haines et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021essp.confE..51H/abstract).

The transition of galaxies from actively star-forming to passive states in dense environments is broadly termed environmental quenching. Spectroscopic evidence for this transformation includes an elevated fraction of "k+a" (post-starburst) galaxies in intermediate-redshift clusters, indicating a rapid truncation of star formation following an initial starburst phase. However, the precise role of the environment on the star-formation "main sequence" across cosmic time remains a subject of active debate. Analyses from the [[help-survey|HELP survey]] demonstrate that during "Cosmic Noon" ($1.5 < z < 2.0$), the mean specific star-formation rate declines in regions of higher environmental density. Despite this, the literature remains ambiguous and conflicting as to whether the main sequence is entirely independent of environment at lower ($z < 1.5$) and higher ($z > 2.0$) redshifts [Duivenvoorden et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..277D/abstract).

The influence of environment also extends to the accretion activity of central supermassive black holes. Recent findings from the [[devils-survey|DEVILS survey]], which achieved $90\%$ spectroscopic completeness in the D10-COSMOS field, confirm that the exact impact of environment on AGN is mass-dependent: satellite galaxies residing in massive clusters exhibit increased AGN fractions but decreased overall luminosities, whereas AGN in lower-mass groups remain largely unaltered relative to the field [Bravo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250913530B/abstract).

At the extremes of the environmental spectrum are Clusters of galaxies, the most massive virialized structures in the universe, typically characterized by total masses of $10^{14} - 10^{15} M_\odot$ and galaxy velocity dispersions of $\sigma \approx 1000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$. Because the physical processes transforming cluster galaxies are tied to deep gravitational potentials and the hot intracluster medium, identifying massive clusters uniformly across cosmic time is essential. The Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect provides a highly effective, redshift-independent mass selection technique for detecting these clusters via spectral distortions of the cosmic microwave background [Carlstrom et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ARA&A..40..643C/abstract), [Mroczkowski et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019SSRv..215...17M/abstract).

Finally, environmental transformation processes are not limited strictly to the collapsed halos of massive clusters. The broader cosmic web also leaves observable signatures on the intergalactic scale. For instance, the detection of diffuse radio emission in intercluster bridges provides direct evidence that large-scale magnetic fields and cosmic-ray electrons exist on megaparsec scales, highlighting the dynamic and interconnected nature of galaxy environments across the universe [Wittor et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Univ....9..319W/abstract). On a more localized scale within these environments, multi-wavelength surveys of gas and magnetic fields are actively used to probe the magnetized interstellar medium's small-scale structure during the ongoing star formation cycle, revealing how environmental pressures interact with internal galactic dynamos [Heald et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160304722H/abstract).

## Multi-Wavelength and Multi-Messenger Observational Techniques

Extragalactic astronomy has fundamentally transitioned from optical morphological studies into a highly automated, multi-wavelength, and multi-messenger discipline. Modern survey strategies increasingly rely on massive, synchronized datasets spanning the electromagnetic spectrum to comprehensively characterize the baryonic and dark matter components of the universe.

In the optical and near-infrared regimes, [[integral-field-spectroscopy|Integral Field Spectroscopy]] (IFS) has revolutionized the spatial resolution of galaxy kinematics. Instruments such as SAMI and GMOS allow observers to simultaneously map three-dimensional dynamics, emission-line fluxes, and chemical enrichment histories across spatially extended distant galaxies [Bunker et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AN....325..139B/abstract), [Brough et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1108.4747B/abstract). Concurrently, [[far-infrared-astronomy|Far-infrared astronomy]] and sub-millimeter observations have advanced significantly due to maturing detector technologies deployed on space-based and sub-orbital platforms [Farrah et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017arXiv170902389F/abstract). Facilities like Spitzer, Herschel, and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) are essential for probing the dust-obscured universe, tracking early mass assembly, and resolving mid-infrared aromatic features in high-redshift targets. Ultra-deep space-based observing programs, such as the JWST PEARLS project, have delivered exquisite measurements of $0.9-4.5~\mu\text{m}$ galaxy counts and diffuse light, pushing the limits of near-infrared observational cosmology [Windhorst et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....165...13W/abstract). However, the literature currently reflects a tension in the mid-infrared regime: recent ultra-deep JWST data reveal that observed galaxy number counts in the $5$--$25 \mu$m band significantly diverge from the predictions of traditional semi-analytical models [Stone et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...972...62S/abstract). The Spitzer Extragalactic Representative Volume Survey (SERVS), a medium-deep survey at $3.6$ and $4.5 \mu$m, was specifically designed to put luminous quasars and galaxy clusters at $z > 1$ into a broader cosmological context [Mauduit et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PASP..124..714M/abstract). Similarly, the Spitzer Wide-Area Infrared Extragalactic (SWIRE) survey has provided foundational contributions to our understanding of luminous infrared galaxies (LIRGs) and evolved stellar populations up to $z \sim 3$ [Lonsdale et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003PASP..115..897L/abstract). Complementing Spitzer and Herschel, the AKARI satellite established crucial far-infrared benchmarks for resolved galaxy populations, proving highly utilitarian for measuring high-redshift evolutionary trends [Serjeant et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PKAS...27..305S/abstract). Conversely, while the extreme ultraviolet (EUV) window provides unique diagnostic capabilities, interstellar medium opacity severely restricts most EUV observations to Galactic sources rather than extragalactic targets [Barstow et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AdSpR..53.1003B/abstract). At millimeter wavelengths, phased arrays like ALMA operate at extreme angular resolutions to map the molecular interstellar medium at [[millimeter-wave-astronomy|Millimeter-wave Astronomy]] and even resolve the event horizons of supermassive black holes [Tilanus et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1406.4650T/abstract). In the radio regime, facilities like e-MERLIN provide high-resolution mapping necessary for probing fundamental physics in both local and distant galaxy contexts [Garrington et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&G....57c3.28G/abstract). Furthermore, extragalactic astrochemistry utilizes multi-species molecular line [[radio-surveys|Radio surveys]] (including CO, HCN, and SiO) to establish robust chemical diagnostics for various interstellar medium processes across cosmic time [Bouvier et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251213881B/abstract).

Beyond the electromagnetic spectrum, the field has fully entered the multi-messenger era. Gravitational Wave Astronomy provides a revolutionary and entirely independent class of [[cosmological-probes|Cosmological probes]]. Binary black hole coalescences serve as "standard sirens" that break the mass-redshift degeneracy inherent in many observations. Future third-generation observatories, such as the Einstein Telescope, are projected to constrain the Hubble constant ($H_0$) to $\approx 1\%$ precision without relying on electromagnetic counterparts or traditional [[standard-candles|Standard candles]] [You et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908..215Y/abstract). Looking further ahead, the future space-based detection of massive black hole binaries and Extreme Mass Ratio Inspirals (EMRIs) by observatories like eLISA will provide unprecedented probes of early black hole seeds and the dynamics of galaxy assembly [Porter et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ASSP...40..267P/abstract). When electromagnetic counterparts are expected (e.g., kilonovae), global time-domain networks like GRANDMA coordinate target selection and track transient emission across disparate observatories [Agayeva et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022SPIE12186E..1HA/abstract). In the high-energy regime, the detection of a [[diffuse-neutrino-flux|Diffuse neutrino flux]] by Antarctic observatories provides an entirely new window into the physical conditions of extragalactic cosmic ray accelerators, complementing TeV gamma-ray observations of blazars and radio galaxies [Burton et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&ARv..18..417B/abstract), [Gaisser et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016crpp.book.....G/abstract).

The unprecedented volume and complexity of data generated by upcoming mega-surveys—including those from the [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]] and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory—have precipitated a methodological shift toward advanced data science. A notable paradigm shift in data processing is the deployment of unsupervised machine learning for morphological taxonomy. Operating directly on raw pixel data, these algorithms can automatically segment and classify galaxies without pre-directed training, successfully reproducing and vastly scaling the visual classifications originally established by human-led citizen science initiatives like Galaxy Zoo [Masters et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015HiA....16....1M/abstract), [Hocking et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.473.1108H/abstract). This increasing reliance on cross-disciplinary research—particularly the integration of astrostatistics and astroinformatics—is essential for improving overall research quality and managing the vast scale of modern survey data [Feigelson et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010arXiv1004.4148F/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions in Extragalactic Science

The nature of [[first-light-galaxies|First-light galaxies]] and the initial seeds of supermassive black holes represent major frontiers in extragalactic science. Recent James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observations have highlighted significant tensions with traditional models, particularly revealing discrepancies in mid-infrared ($5-25~\mu\text{m}$) galaxy counts. Traditional semi-analytical models struggle to accurately predict mid-infrared aromatic features, while separate models based on TeV blazar data generally underpredict the observed Cosmic Infrared Background (CIB) fluxes [Stone et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...972...62S/abstract). The exact origin of this excess diffuse light in the CIB remains unresolved. To definitively map the intergalactic medium before and during the epoch of reionization, researchers are increasingly turning to 21-cm cosmology. Future initiatives, including the [[ska-hi-galaxy-survey|SKA HI galaxy survey]], aim to map neutral hydrogen on cosmological scales and detect the unique luminosity bias induced by baryon-dark matter relative velocities in the early universe [Barkana et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PASA...30...36B/abstract), [Barkana et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1401.7935B/abstract).

Another critical observational frontier is [[low-surface-brightness-astronomy|Low Surface Brightness Astronomy]]. Historically, models of galaxy formation have been heavily biased toward optically bright objects, missing a vast, faint population of stellar halos, tidal tails, and dwarf galaxies [Brough et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020arXiv200111067B/abstract). Resolving this incomplete picture requires reaching surface brightness limits of $\mu_V > 27~\text{AB mag arcsec}^{-2}$ [Montes et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230609414M/abstract), [Montes et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251214784M/abstract). Next-generation [[deep-imaging-surveys|Deep imaging surveys]], leveraging 30-40 meter class telescopes and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, will be essential for mapping these extended low-density structures and reconstructing the hierarchical assembly histories of galaxies. Additionally, the anticipated synergy between the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory will be critical in breaking the longstanding age-metallicity degeneracy in extragalactic star clusters, offering unprecedented clarity on stellar population histories [Dage et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230612620D/abstract).

In high-energy astrophysics, the attenuation of very-high-energy $\gamma$-rays via pair-production with the Extragalactic Background Light (EBL) provides a critical link between cosmic star formation and high-energy physics. However, significant uncertainties persist regarding the far-infrared component of the EBL, requiring independent constraints from both infrared and $\gamma$-ray astronomy to resolve conflicting models [Domínguez et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410.2556D/abstract). Concurrently, a highly innovative theoretical direction is the search for Dark Radiation. This "dark astronomy" paradigm proposes using direct dark matter detection experiments (such as XENON) to map non-gravitational dark sector emissions. By leveraging resonant dark-photon-to-photon conversion at the surface of conducting materials, these detectors could provide unique directional sensitivity to dark radiation sources in the cosmos [Alonso-Álvarez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...05..082A/abstract). Another speculative yet intriguing application of extragalactic mid-infrared observations is the search for technosignatures; by analyzing mid-infrared luminosities, researchers evaluate the potential for detecting advanced technological civilizations in external galaxies [Wright et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190707829W/abstract).

Finally, the sheer volume of data from upcoming multi-wavelength missions presents unprecedented computational challenges. For example, the [[euclid-spacecraft|Euclid (spacecraft)]] will survey $15,000~\text{deg}^2$ of the extragalactic sky to measure [[large-area-weak-lensing-surveys|Large area weak lensing surveys]] and galaxy clustering, generating massive, complex datasets [Laureijs et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ASPC..485..495L/abstract), [Joachimi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASPC..507..401J/abstract). To process this deluge of information, the adoption of advanced astrostatistics and machine learning is proving vital. Moving forward, a major goal is the rigorous integration of Bayesian inference and deep learning to handle heteroscedastic measurement errors, classify enormous galaxy populations autonomously, and break parameter degeneracies—such as the mass-redshift degeneracy encountered in standard-siren cosmology [Feigelson et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AnRSA...8..493F/abstract), [You et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908..215Y/abstract).
