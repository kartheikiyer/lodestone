**Baryonic matter evolution** chronicles the physical and chemical transformation of ordinary matter throughout [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]], tracing its journey from a pristine primordial plasma to the complex, chemically enriched structures observed in the present-day universe. Although baryons account for only about 15-20% of the total matter density—with the remainder dominated by non-baryonic [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]]—they are the sole constituents of the universe that emit and absorb light, making them the primary observational tracers of cosmic structure [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). The evolutionary timeline begins with the synthesis of light elements during the [[big-bang|Big Bang]], which established the initial [[total-baryon-fraction|Total baryon fraction]] and [[cosmic-abundances|Cosmic abundances]]. Following recombination and the cosmic dark ages, this diffuse primordial gas within the intergalactic medium (IGM) underwent [[cosmic-accretion|Cosmic accretion]] into the deepening gravitational potential wells of dark matter halos, initiating the epoch of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]].

Within these halos, the evolution of baryonic matter is governed by a highly dynamic "baryon cycle." Infalling gas shock-heats and radiatively cools, losing pressure support to condense into rotationally supported galactic disks. This dense, cold gas provides the fuel for star formation, the efficiency and distribution of which are heavily modulated by the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. However, the process is far from perfectly efficient; energetic feedback from massive stars, core-collapse supernovae, and active galactic nuclei (AGN) continuously reheats and ejects gas back into the circumgalactic and intergalactic media. This regulatory feedback is essential to prevent the overcooling of gas and to reproduce the observed [[mass-distribution-and-functions|Mass Distribution and Functions]] of galaxies. Furthermore, the interplay between dissipational baryonic physics and collisionless dark matter fundamentally alters [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]], driving phenomena such as the adiabatic contraction of the central potential into denser profiles [Blumenthal et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...301...27B/abstract), whereas subsequent strong baryonic feedback can expand these central regions into flattened density cores.

As successive generations of stars live and die, they synthesize heavy elements, driving the [[chemical-enrichment-history|Chemical enrichment history]] of the universe. The resulting [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] serve as a fossil record, encoding the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and gas exchange mechanisms of their host galaxies over billions of years [Tinsley et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980FCPh....5..287T/abstract). On macroscopic scales, the complex physics of gas cooling, star formation, and feedback remarkably condense into tight empirical scaling laws, most notably the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]], which inextricably links a galaxy's luminous baryonic mass to its total [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]]. Yet, the exact physical origins of these universal relations, and why they persist across fundamentally different galactic environments without significant scatter, remain a central theoretical challenge.

Despite the sweeping successes of the standard [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], significant open questions remain at both the high-mass and low-mass extremes of galaxy assembly. The literature highlights ongoing tensions, such as the unexpectedly rapid, quasi-monolithic assembly of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] at high redshifts, the unexpectedly high baryon mass fractions observed in rich galaxy clusters [White et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993Natur.366..429W/abstract), and the elusive nature of the "missing baryons" theorized to reside in the warm-hot intergalactic medium. Contemporary frontiers are aggressively targeting these puzzles using novel observational techniques. Forthcoming capabilities are expected to test classical models of early structure formation, while dispersion measures from Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) are providing unprecedented, direct maps of the ionized IGM, challenging the limits of feedback prescriptions in modern [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]].

## Primordial Nucleosynthesis and the Early Universe

The thermal history of the [[early-universe|Early universe]] is defined by its expansion from a hot, dense initial state known as the [[big-bang|Big Bang]]. Because the Universe was significantly hotter and denser at early times, it provided the necessary conditions for thermonuclear reactions among elementary particles, a process known as Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN). 

First proposed by George Gamow in the 1940s and rigorously calculated by Wagoner et al. in 1967, BBN predicts the [[cosmic-abundances|Cosmic abundances]] of the lightest [[chemical-elements|Chemical elements]]. The primordial plasma synthesized hydrogen, along with a helium mass fraction of approximately one-third that of hydrogen, and trace amounts of isotopes such as deuterium, helium-3, and lithium. The creation of heavier elements during this epoch was halted by the absence of stable nuclei with atomic mass numbers $A=5$ and $A=8$; all subsequent [[heavy-element-enrichment|Heavy element enrichment]] was delayed until the later onset of stellar nucleosynthesis [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

Precise measurements of these primordial abundances provide a crucial constraint on the [[total-baryon-fraction|Total baryon fraction]] of the Universe. Combined with measurements of the cosmic microwave background (CMB), BBN limits the present-day [[cosmic-baryon-density|Cosmic baryon density]] parameter, $\Omega_{b,0}$, to roughly $15-20\%$ of the total matter density [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). However, the literature has historically noted tensions regarding the exact consistency of this value across different scales; for example, White et al. (1993) demonstrated that the baryonic mass fractions observed in rich galaxy clusters appeared significantly higher than the strict predictions derived from standard BBN and inflationary models [White et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993Natur.366..429W/abstract).

Following BBN, the Universe continued to expand and cool. At a cosmic time of $t \sim 2 \times 10^5$ yr, when the temperature dropped to $T \sim 4000$ K, roughly $50\%$ of the baryonic matter transitioned from a plasma into neutral atoms [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). This epoch of recombination drastically reduced the number density of free electrons, causing the Universe to suddenly become transparent to photons. The decoupled radiation field freely expanded and cooled, and is observed today as the CMB with a characteristic temperature of $2.7$ K. Around this same time, the energy density of non-relativistic matter surpassed that of relativistic particles, initiating the matter-dominated epoch.

After recombination, the Universe entered a "dark age" characterized by a highly uniform distribution of dark matter, CMB photons, and a predominantly neutral primordial gas. Because BBN produced negligible heavy elements, this gas was composed almost entirely of hydrogen and helium, with a number ratio of $\text{He/H} \approx 0.07$. Although one might expect the gas temperature to simply scale adiabatically as $T_{\text{gas}} \propto (1+z)^2$ after photon decoupling, a small residual ionization fraction allowed Compton scattering to efficiently transfer energy from the CMB to the baryons, keeping their temperatures coupled until $z \sim 100$ [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). This pristine, neutral epoch persisted until the [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] of overdensities formed the first luminous objects, which ultimately reionized the intergalactic medium approximately one billion years after the Big Bang [Wise et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv190706653W/abstract).

## Hierarchical Structure and Dark Matter Halo Assembly

In the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological framework, the large-scale structure of the Universe is driven by the [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] of non-baryonic [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] (CDM). Initial primordial density perturbations grow linearly with the expansion of the Universe until they reach a critical overdensity. At this point, the regions decouple from the Hubble flow and undergo collapse. Because the dark matter is collisionless, it does not dissipate energy through radiation; instead, the system reaches a quasi-equilibrium state through violent relaxation, a process known as Virialization [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

The resulting structures are extended, gravitationally bound dark matter halos. Because the initial power spectrum of density fluctuations has larger amplitudes on smaller spatial scales, these halos assemble via a [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]]. In this "bottom-up" paradigm, small, dense halos form at high redshifts and subsequently grow by accreting diffuse matter and merging with other halos [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract), [Frenk et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996IAUS..171..247F/abstract). The statistical distribution of these host structures is described by the Halo Mass Function (often modeled using the [[sheth-tormen-mass-function|Sheth-Tormen mass function]]), which predicts the number density of halos as a function of their mass and the background cosmology.

These virialized halos provide the dominant gravitational potential wells necessary for [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]]. As established by foundational two-stage theories of galaxy formation, the dark matter halos assemble first, after which the intergalactic medium undergoes [[cosmic-accretion|Cosmic accretion]] into these wells [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). The trapped baryonic gas can then shock-heat, radiatively cool, and condense toward the halo center to form rotationally supported disks and, eventually, stars.

While dark matter dictates the initial depth and distribution of the potential wells, the relationship between the dark and baryonic components is highly interactive. The condensation of baryons exerts a strong gravitational back-reaction on the dark matter. For instance, the dissipative infall of cooling gas pulls dark matter inward, steepening the central density profile through adiabatic contraction [Blumenthal et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...301...27B/abstract). Conversely, energetic baryonic feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei can rapidly expel gas, violently fluctuating the central potential. This feedback transfers energy to the collisionless dark matter, which can flatten central cusps into cored profiles [Brooks et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AnP...526..294B/abstract). Such baryonic imprints are profound; [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] indicate that galaxy formation physics drives non-monotonic variations across six decades of halo mass, generating systematic deviations in dark matter velocity dispersion and concentration of up to $25\%$ at a Milky Way halo mass of $\sim 10^{12} M_{\odot}$ [Anbajagane et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.3441A/abstract).

Despite the success of the hierarchical merging paradigm in predicting the large-scale distribution of galaxies, tensions remain at the extremes of the halo mass scale. Observations of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] (specifically early-type systems) at high redshifts ($z > 2$) suggest a rapid, quasi-monolithic assembly rather than protracted hierarchical merging, leading to debates over the relative dominance of mergers versus in-situ starbursts driven by rapid [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]] [Chiosi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Galax...2..300C/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract). On sub-galactic scales, the hierarchical CDM model struggles with the "missing satellites" problem and the "diversity problem" of inner [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]] in dwarf galaxies. While baryonic feedback offers plausible solutions to these discrepancies, it remains heavily debated in the literature whether feedback alone is sufficient or if these small-scale tensions indicate the need for modifications to dark matter particle physics [Sales et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6..897S/abstract), [Brooks et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AnP...526..294B/abstract).

## The Intergalactic Medium and Gas Accretion

Before the onset of [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]], all baryonic matter resided within the intergalactic medium (IGM). As the universe expanded and non-linear structures collapsed, the IGM served as the primordial reservoir from which [[cosmic-accretion|Cosmic accretion]] fed growing dark matter halos. Even in the present-day universe, the IGM is believed to contain more than 50% of the [[total-baryon-fraction|Total baryon fraction]], with a substantial portion residing in vast, unvirialized filamentary structures frequently referred to as the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] [Kaastra et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.2324K/abstract). Furthermore, simulations indicate that while baryonic and dark matter follow broadly similar large-scale patterns in the cosmic web, baryonic vorticity develops significantly more strongly at smaller scales, particularly below 2-3 Mpc [Zhu et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...838...21Z/abstract).

The interface between the IGM and a central galaxy is mediated by the circumgalactic medium (CGM), a highly dynamic, multi-phase environment through which gas accretes, cools, and is ultimately recycled by galactic outflows [Peroux et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241107988P/abstract). As gas flows from the IGM into the gravitational potential wells of dark matter halos, its physical state undergoes severe transformation. In standard models of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], infalling gas typically shock-heats to the virial temperature of the halo, forming a [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]]. However, if the local density is sufficiently high, radiative cooling operates on a timescale shorter than the halo age. The gas loses pressure support and dissipates energy, shrinking by approximately a factor of 10 in radius as it falls deeper into the potential well [Blumenthal et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...301...27B/abstract). This dissipative infall actively reshapes the underlying dark matter distribution via adiabatic contraction, pulling dark matter inward and increasing the central density [Blumenthal et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...301...27B/abstract). Assuming angular momentum is conserved during this collapse, the cooling gas spins up and settles into a rotationally supported cold disk in centrifugal equilibrium, providing the necessary fuel for subsequent star formation.

To reconcile theory with nature, researchers increasingly emphasize a "third way" of understanding galaxy formation: utilizing direct observational measures of gas acquisition histories to reveal and resolve discrepancies with standard simulation predictions [Conselice et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..277..267C/abstract). The rate at which galaxies accrete gas from the IGM and CGM is the fundamental driver of their [[galaxy-mass-fractions|Galaxy mass fractions]] and stellar mass assembly. Observations of the 21-cm hyperfine transition of neutral hydrogen (H I) reveal that at $z \sim 1$, the average H I mass of star-forming galaxies is roughly equivalent to their stellar mass [Chowdhury et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Natur.586..369C/abstract). However, without fresh accretion from the IGM, this gas reservoir is insufficient to sustain observed star formation rates for more than 1 to 2 Gyr [Chowdhury et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Natur.586..369C/abstract). This implies that a decline in the efficiency of [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]] at $z < 1$ is directly responsible for the observed drop in the [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] across late cosmic epochs.

Mapping the physical state of the IGM and CGM remains observationally challenging due to the diffuse nature of the gas. Accurately representing complex gas physics and accretion in computational models remains a persistent problem, highlighting the need for detecting the star-formation fuel over diverse redshifts [Putman et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S.241P/abstract). Recently, novel cosmological probes such as Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) and kinematic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (kSZ) measurements have provided direct tracers of the ionized electron density in the IGM [Medlock et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250117922M/abstract), [Kovač et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250707991K/abstract). These measurements have begun to tightly constrain the redistribution of baryons by stellar and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback. Notably, the current literature reflects a tension between these new observations and established theoretical frameworks: early statistical analyses of FRB dispersion measures strongly reject no-feedback scenarios (at $>3\sigma$) and indicate that feedback mechanisms may be significantly more efficient at ejecting gas into the IGM than standard hydrodynamical models currently predict [Reischke et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250717742R/abstract). This underscores the importance of refining our understanding of [[galaxy-and-igm-evolution|Galaxy and IGM evolution]].

## Star Formation and the Initial Mass Function (IMF)

The conversion of cold gas into a [[stellar-component|Stellar component]] bridges immense physical scales, transitioning from the total gas reservoirs of galaxy-sized halos with masses of $\sim 10^{11} M_\odot$ and densities of $\sim 10^{-24}\text{ g cm}^{-3}$ down to individual stars with masses of $\sim 1 M_\odot$ and densities of $\sim 1\text{ g cm}^{-3}$. Because capturing this process from first principles across 11 orders of magnitude in mass and 24 orders of magnitude in density remains computationally prohibitive, cosmological models typically rely on empirical scaling relations. Foremost among these is the Kennicutt-Schmidt Law, which parameterizes the surface density of the star formation rate as a power-law function of the cold gas surface density [Chakraborti et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011arXiv1109.0529C/abstract). This relation implicitly captures the complex physics of gas cooling, gravitational collapse, and the critical transition of neutral atomic hydrogen into the molecular state (${\rm H}_2$) required to fuel dense star-forming cores [Takeuchi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160301938T/abstract). In this context, the multi-phase interstellar medium serves as a vital bridge between large-scale gas accretion and the small-scale regulation of star formation [Saintonge et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250401410S/abstract).

The distribution of stellar birth masses within a newly formed population is described by the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. The IMF is a foundational parameter in astrophysics, as it dictates the photometric evolution, mass-to-light ratio, and chemical yield of a galaxy. While traditionally modeled using a universal Salpeter functional form, the strict universality of the IMF remains a subject of active, unresolved debate in the literature [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...39H/abstract). Some evidence suggests the IMF may vary with the local star-forming environment, gas [[metallicity|Metallicity]], or starburst intensity [Krumholz et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PhR...539...49K/abstract). The high-mass tail of the IMF determines the abundance of [[massive-stars|Massive stars]], which govern the short-term energetic output of a galaxy via ultraviolet radiation, stellar winds, and subsequent core-collapse supernovae [Burkert et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322..489B/abstract). By injecting mechanical energy, ionizing radiation, and nucleosynthetic products into the interstellar medium, the evolution and final fates of these massive stars actively drive overall galaxy transformation [Ekström et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250206614E/abstract). These stars synthesize and eject the bulk of the $\alpha$-elements, directly driving the [[chemical-enrichment-history|Chemical enrichment history]] and providing the necessary baryonic feedback to prevent runaway gas overcooling.

The integration of local star formation processes across the universe yields the cosmic [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]]. The [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] density, $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$, characterizes the average mass converted into stars per unit comoving volume per unit time. Observations reveal that $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$ increased steadily in the early universe, reaching a peak epoch of galaxy assembly at $1 \lesssim z \lesssim 3$ [Chowdhury et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Natur.586..369C/abstract). Since $z \sim 1.5$, the cosmic star formation rate has declined by roughly an order of magnitude to the present day. This suppression is primarily attributed to a transition from an "accretion-dominated" phase—where fresh gas from the intergalactic medium efficiently replenishes galaxies—to a "depletion-dominated" phase, exacerbated by the energetic expulsion of gas via stellar and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback [Pan et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...885L..14P/abstract). In this context, post-starburst (often termed E+A) galaxies serve as ideal observational laboratories for tracing the fastest structural changes and feedback mechanisms that drive star-forming galaxies toward quiescence [French et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASP..133g2001F/abstract). At $z \sim 1$, the neutral hydrogen mass in star-forming galaxies was comparable to their stellar mass, yet this reservoir was insufficient to sustain peak star formation rates for more than 1-2 billion years without continuous cosmological gas infall [Chowdhury et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Natur.586..369C/abstract).

## Feedback Processes and the Baryon Cycle

The evolution of galaxies is fundamentally governed by the "baryon cycle"—the continuous interplay of gas accretion from the intergalactic medium, the cooling and condensation of this gas into stars, and the subsequent ejection of matter and energy back into the surrounding environment. In standard [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] ($\Lambda$CDM) cosmologies, small, dense halos form early, and gas cooling within them is highly efficient. Without regulating mechanisms, these models predict that the vast majority of baryonic material should cool and form stars, severely overpredicting the observed stellar mass fractions of galaxies [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). To reconcile theory with observation, energy injection from stellar and black hole sources must either prevent gas from cooling or reheat it after it has condensed. 

The primary driver of feedback in low-to-intermediate mass systems is supernova (SN) feedback. Energy and momentum injected by dying massive stars reheat the cold interstellar medium, driving powerful [[galactic-outflows-and-inflows|Galactic Outflows and Inflows]] that regulate star formation. This "negative feedback" is crucial for solving the angular momentum and overcooling problems in [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]], as it prevents the premature conversion of gas into stars at high redshifts [Burkert et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322..489B/abstract). Conversely, under certain conditions, SN blast waves can compress surrounding cold gas, triggering a "positive feedback" mode that locally boosts star formation rates [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). 

In more [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]], SN feedback becomes insufficient to unbind gas from the deep gravitational potential wells. In this regime, Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) feedback dominates. Energy released by the accretion of matter onto supermassive black holes heats the [[hot-circumgalactic-medium|Hot circumgalactic medium]], preventing further gas condensation. Recent semi-analytic models and [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] demonstrate that AGN feedback can eject hot gas entirely beyond the virial radius, creating characteristic "U-shaped" baryon fraction profiles and bringing the simulated stellar-to-halo mass relations into agreement with local observations [Contini et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250710673C/abstract), [Croton et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJS..222...22C/abstract).

Baryonic feedback extends beyond regulating star formation; it actively alters the underlying [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]]. While baryonic infall can cause the adiabatic contraction of dark matter, violent feedback episodes can rapidly expel gas, fluctuating the gravitational potential and transferring energy to dark matter particles. This process can transform steep central dark matter cusps into flattened cores [Brooks et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AnP...526..294B/abstract), driving mass-dependent "wiggles" into dark matter property statistics across six decades of halo mass [Anbajagane et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.3441A/abstract). Furthermore, the stellar mass assembly of central galaxies often decouples from the hierarchical assembly history of their host halos, while the disruption timescales of their satellites align closely with dynamical friction timescales, adding yet another layer of dynamic complexity to the baryon cycle [Yang et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...752...41Y/abstract).

Currently, there is active debate regarding the exact efficiency and strength of these feedback mechanisms. Recent cross-correlations of kinematic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (kSZ) data from the Atacama Cosmology Telescope with X-ray gas fractions from eROSITA suggest that feedback may be significantly stronger than what is implemented in baseline hydrodynamical simulations. These energetic processes redistribute matter sufficiently to suppress the matter power spectrum on [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]], with reductions of 20-25% at $k = 5\,h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$ [Kovač et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250707991K/abstract). 

## Galactic Chemical Evolution (GCE)

Galactic Chemical Evolution (GCE) tracks the production and distribution of heavy elements over cosmic time. Because different elements are synthesized on distinct timescales, a galaxy's [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] serve as a fossil record of its [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and its gas exchange with the intergalactic medium [Maiolino et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&ARv..27....3M/abstract), [Curti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250408933C/abstract). 

A foundational metric in GCE is the abundance ratio of $\alpha$-elements (e.g., oxygen, magnesium) to iron, denoted as $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$. The $\alpha$-elements are primarily produced via [[nucleosynthesis|Nucleosynthesis]] in core-collapse (Type II) supernovae from massive stars, enriching the interstellar medium on short timescales ($t \sim 10^7$ yr). Conversely, Type Ia supernovae, which are responsible for producing $\sim 70\%$ of the iron in the Milky Way , operate on delayed timescales compared to the short-lived massive stars responsible for Type II supernovae. Consequently, the $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ ratio acts as a "cosmic clock" [Matteucci et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004oee..symp...85M/abstract): elevated $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ values indicate a rapid, intense starburst where Type II supernovae dominated, while solar or sub-solar values imply an extended star formation period that allowed Type Ia supernovae to eventually contribute [Curti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250408933C/abstract). The precise interpretation of these chemical yields relies heavily on the assumed [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. However, the literature remains in active debate regarding whether the IMF is strictly universal across all environments and cosmic epochs, or if it varies with [[metallicity|Metallicity]] and star formation intensity [Matteucci et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ASPC..117..435M/abstract), [Hopkins et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PASA...35...39H/abstract).

Global metallicity properties are strongly coupled to the depth of a galaxy's gravitational potential well, driving well-established mass-metallicity scaling relations. Massive galaxies can effectively retain the products of successive [[stellar-generations|Stellar generations]], whereas shallower potential wells allow baryonic feedback to efficiently eject metals into the circumgalactic medium. The classical [[closed-box-model|Closed box model]] of chemical evolution assumes that a galaxy evolves in strict isolation. However, this framework generally fails to reproduce observed relative abundance distributions—most notably manifesting as the "G-dwarf problem" in the solar neighborhood [Tinsley et al. (1980)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980FCPh....5..287T/abstract). Accurately reproducing a galaxy's [[chemical-enrichment-history|Chemical enrichment history]] requires models that incorporate continuous infall of pristine or metal-poor gas alongside feedback-driven outflows.

The spatial distribution of metals provides further constraints on this baryonic cycle, particularly in low-mass regimes. Recent spatially resolved observations of star-forming dwarf galaxies highlight a notable divergence from the behavior of more massive systems. Specifically, galaxies in the stellar mass range of $M_\star \approx 10^7$ to $10^{9.5} M_\odot$ exhibit a significant negative correlation between their gas-phase metallicity gradient and their stellar mass [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250417541L/abstract). This contrasts with the flat or positive correlations typically observed in higher-mass galaxies. The presence of these negative gradients, alongside a stronger central suppression of metallicity in lower-mass dwarfs, indicates that their chemical topology is not solely determined by in-situ metal production. Instead, the spatial distribution of metals in these galaxies is heavily modulated by metal mixing and large-scale transport processes, which are primarily driven by stellar feedback and outflows [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250417541L/abstract).

## Scaling Relations and Dynamical Mass Distribution

The relationship between a galaxy's luminous baryonic component and its total [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] is tightly constrained by empirical [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]]. The most prominent of these is the [[baryonic-tully-fisher-relation|Baryonic Tully-Fisher relation]] (BTFR), which establishes a tight correlation between the total baryonic mass $M_b$ (stars plus cold gas) and the characteristic rotation velocity $V_f$ of rotationally supported galaxies. Observations of gas-rich systems demonstrate that the BTFR is a robust, universal law spanning five orders of magnitude in mass, typically parameterized as $M_b = A V_f^4$ with a normalization of $A = 47 \pm 6 \, M_\odot \, \text{km}^{-4} \, \text{s}^4$ [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....143...40M/abstract). Remarkably, the zero-point of the galactic BTFR shows no significant evolution since redshift $z=0.6$, suggesting that the gas reservoirs fueling later star formation were already gravitationally bound to their host galaxies at that epoch [Puech et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..277..138P/abstract). 

A natural consequence of the BTFR is the Radial Acceleration Relation (RAR), which links the observed circular acceleration $g_{\rm obs}$ to the acceleration predicted solely by the baryonic mass distribution, $g_{\rm bar}$ [Wheeler et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...882...46W/abstract). Above a critical acceleration scale of $\sim 10^{-10} \, \text{m} \, \text{s}^{-2}$, the observed acceleration traces the baryonic acceleration in a nearly 1:1 ratio. Below this scale, the relation transitions to $g_{\rm obs} \sim g_{\rm bar}^{1/2}$ [Wheeler et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...882...46W/abstract). In the deeply embedded inner regions of disk galaxies, the central baryonic fraction is often found to be nearly unity. In these regimes, the inner circular-velocity gradients correlate strongly with central surface brightness, and the observed scatter is primarily driven by variations in the stellar mass-to-light ratio and disk thickness, rather than by a dominating dark matter component [Lelli et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Galax...2..292L/abstract). This contrasts sharply with global measurements on the scales of groups and clusters, where the mass-to-light ratio can reach $\sim 350 h \, (M/L)_\odot$, highlighting severe mass discrepancies in the outer halos [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract).

The physical origin of these tight scaling relations remains a subject of active debate in the literature. While alternative theories of modified gravity natively predict the BTFR and RAR, reproducing these precise laws within the standard [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] paradigm requires highly specific formulations of baryonic feedback. To match the lack of scatter and the $V_f^4$ slope within the standard framework, cosmological models must invoke precise feedback efficacy parameters that regulate star formation and gas fractions uniformly across a vast range of halo masses [McGaugh et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AJ....143...40M/abstract). Despite the complexities introduced by gas cooling, star formation, and supernova feedback, the dynamical imprint of the initial conditions persists. High-resolution hydrodynamic simulations indicate that baryonic angular momenta remain reliable tracers of the primordial Lagrangian properties of the initial proto-halo, exhibiting strong spin correlations that survive the highly non-linear assembly process [Sheng et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...943..128S/abstract). 

## Small-Scale Challenges and Baryon-Dark Matter Interactions

The standard [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] (CDM) paradigm successfully predicts the large-scale structure of the Universe but faces persistent theoretical challenges on sub-galactic scales. High-resolution $N$-body simulations historically modeled dark matter halos as rigid, collisionless structures, predicting abundant substructure and steeply rising central density profiles (cusps). However, these dark-matter-only predictions are frequently at odds with observations of [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], leading to tensions such as the "missing satellites" problem, the diversity of inner dark matter content, and the cusp-core problem [Sales et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022NatAs...6..897S/abstract), [Brooks et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AnP...526..294B/abstract).

It is now understood that baryonic physics actively reshapes [[dark-matter-halo-structure|Dark Matter Halo Structure]]. During early [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]], the dissipation and cooling of gas cause baryons to sink toward the center of the dark matter potential well. As the baryonic gas shrinks by roughly a factor of ten in radius, its infall deepens the central gravitational potential, pulling the dark matter inward through a process known as adiabatic contraction [Blumenthal et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986ApJ...301...27B/abstract). 

Conversely, energetic feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei can counteract this contraction. In dwarf ellipticals, rapid fluctuations in the central potential driven by bursty star formation and mass ejection transfer kinetic energy to the dark matter particles. This process can completely erase primordial cusps, producing extended, constant-density isothermal cores that reach out to significant fractions of the virial radius [Carraro et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998dm97.conf..169C/abstract). Furthermore, substantial baryonic mass loss during the initial [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] and expansion turnaround can drastically lower the asymptotic velocity dispersion and [[phase-space-density|Phase space density]] of dwarf spheroidal galaxies, bringing theoretical expectations into better alignment with the observed kinematics of local dwarf satellites [Gritschneder et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...765...38G/abstract). These small-scale tensions on [[small-scale-cosmological-structure|Small-Scale Cosmological Structure]] highlight the intricate relationship between baryonic feedback and dark matter distribution. Environments subject to severe tidal stripping provide another viable pathway for the formation of dark matter-deficient galaxies, particularly within diverse [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|Dwarf Galaxy Populations]] [Arias et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250816759A/abstract).

On cosmological scales, the redistribution of gas via violent feedback heavily suppresses the matter power spectrum at small spatial scales. If we define the suppression factor $S(k)$ as the ratio of the full hydrodynamical power spectrum $P_{\rm full}(k)$ to the dark-matter-only power spectrum $P_{\rm DMO}(k)$, recent constraints reveal that $S(k)$ drops significantly at high wavenumbers. The suppression exceeds the percent-level at $k \sim 0.3-0.6 \, h\text{Mpc}^{-1}$ and reaches $20\%-25\%$ at $k \sim 5-7 \, h\text{Mpc}^{-1}$ [Giri et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021JCAP...12..046G/abstract), [Kovač et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250707991K/abstract). 

## Historical Development of Galaxy Formation Theory

The historical development of galaxy formation theory represents a profound evolution of ideas, transitioning from simple models of isolated gas collapse to a complex, dark matter-regulated cosmological framework. The foundation of modern [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]] was solidified in the mid-20th century. Following the discovery of the cosmic microwave background in 1965, the [[big-bang|Big Bang]] firmly established that the universe emerged from a hot, dense state, and that primordial nucleosynthesis dictated its initial baryonic composition.

By the 1970s, it became evident that the luminous baryonic matter in galaxies was severely insufficient to explain their dynamics. This realization motivated White & Rees (1978) to propose a seminal two-stage theory for [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. In this framework, non-baryonic dark matter halos form first through gravitational clustering, creating deep potential wells. Subsequently, baryonic gas falls into these wells, cools radiatively, and condenses to form the luminous stellar components of galaxies. 

Throughout the 1980s, this concept matured into the standard paradigm of structure formation: the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]] within a [[cold-dark-matter|Cold dark matter]] ($\Lambda$CDM) model. In this "bottom-up" approach, small density perturbations collapse early to form low-mass halos, which continually merge to assemble progressively larger structures over cosmic time [Cox et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..399..284C/abstract). However, early $\Lambda$CDM simulations revealed a critical flaw if only gravity and cooling were considered: gas cooled far too efficiently, converting nearly all baryons into stars and vastly overproducing faint, low-mass galaxies. To reconcile theory with observation, it was recognized that baryonic feedback mechanisms are strictly necessary to reheat or expel cold gas, regulate the star formation rate, and match the observed [[mass-distribution-and-functions|Mass Distribution and Functions]] [Burkert et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASPC..322..489B/abstract).

Despite the broad successes of the hierarchical model, tensions remain regarding the assembly of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]]. A strict hierarchical view anticipates that massive galaxies assemble slowly over a Hubble time via repeated mergers. Yet, observations of high-redshift systems reveal that many were already fully assembled and quenched at $z > 2$ [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract). This has revived interest in "quasi-monolithic" collapse scenarios, where massive halos experience an intense, rapid early starburst driven by rapid gas accretion, effectively assembling their stellar mass early and rapidly rather than strictly relying on late-time merging [Chiosi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Galax...2..300C/abstract).

## Contemporary Frontiers and Future Directions

The study of baryonic matter currently stands at the intersection of precision cosmology and high-resolution astrophysics. While the local inventory of stars and cold gas is well-documented, a historical challenge has been the "missing baryon" problem: the fact that more than 50% of the baryons predicted by Big Bang [[nucleosynthesis|Nucleosynthesis]] and cosmic microwave background observations are not found in galaxies. It is now widely theorized that these baryons reside in the Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM), tracing the vast, unvirialized filamentary structures of the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] [Kaastra et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.2324K/abstract). 

A revolutionary new tool for probing this diffuse ionized gas has emerged in the form of Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs). By analyzing the dispersion measure (DM) of localized FRBs, astronomers can directly map the cosmic distribution of baryons. Recent statistical models have definitively rejected no-feedback scenarios, though the exact strength of this feedback remains a point of active tension. Both early FRB constraints and recent cross-correlations suggest that feedback might be substantially stronger than the baseline implementations in standard [[cosmological-hydrodynamic-simulations|Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations]] [Medlock et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250117922M/abstract), [Kovač et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250707991K/abstract). 

At the high-redshift frontier, understanding [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] requires tracing the assembly of baryons during the Epoch of Reionization. Observations of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] at $z > 2$ have intensified an unresolved debate regarding their formation channels. While the standard [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]] predicts gradual assembly through the coalescence of smaller dark matter halos, the unexpected abundance and maturity of high-redshift systems have led some researchers to argue for a quasi-monolithic formation scenario [Chiosi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Galax...2..300C/abstract), [Conselice et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1212.5641C/abstract). 

Complementing infrared capabilities, advances in 21-cm radio astronomy are opening new windows into the neutral hydrogen content of the universe. Recent stacking experiments successfully measured the average H I mass of star-forming galaxies at $z \sim 1$, revealing that while the neutral gas mass is comparable to the stellar mass, it is entirely insufficient to sustain observed star formation rates for more than $1-2$ billion years [Chowdhury et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Natur.586..369C/abstract). This finding underscores the necessity of continuous [[cosmic-accretion|Cosmic accretion]] from the intergalactic medium to drive [[galaxy-and-igm-evolution|Galaxy and IGM evolution]]. Ultimately, the future of baryonic astrophysics relies on the synergy of these multi-wavelength probes to fully resolve the cosmic baryon cycle across cosmic time [Dayal et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..352...43D/abstract).
