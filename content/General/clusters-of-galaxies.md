**Clusters of galaxies** are the most massive gravitationally bound structures in the universe, occupying the intersecting nodes of the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] and serving as fundamental tracers of the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]]. With total masses typically ranging from $10^{14}$ to $10^{15} M_\odot$, these systems are overwhelmingly dominated by dark matter, which constitutes approximately 80% of their mass budget. The vast majority of the baryonic matter resides not in the hundreds to thousands of luminous member galaxies, but in the hot, highly enriched intracluster medium (ICM)—a diffuse thermal plasma that emits copiously in X-rays via thermal bremsstrahlung [Sarazin et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986RvMP...58....1S/abstract). Because their overall mass composition closely mirrors the universal matter-to-baryon ratio, clusters provide a representative macroscopic sample of the universe's contents, making them indispensable physical laboratories for both astrophysics and cosmology.

The extreme physical conditions within a cluster profoundly influence the evolutionary trajectories of its constituent galaxies. Acting as dense and hostile [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]]s, clusters drive the morphological transformation of star-forming field galaxies into [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] through hydrodynamical and gravitational mechanisms such as ram-pressure stripping, tidal interactions, and [[galaxy-harassment|Galaxy harassment]]. This environmental quenching establishes a stark morphology-density relation in the local universe, culminating in the formation of massive, centrally located [[cd-galaxies|cD galaxies]] [Bautz et al. (1970)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1970ApJ...162L.149B/abstract) surrounded by a diffuse halo of intracluster light and [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]]. However, multi-wavelength observations of [[protoclusters|Protoclusters]] and [[high-redshift-galaxy-clusters|High-redshift galaxy clusters]] reveal a striking reversal of this trend at early cosmic epochs ($z > 1.5$), where cluster cores act as sites of vigorous, merger-driven starbursts rather than regions of suppressed star formation.

Governed by the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]], clusters are dynamically young systems that continuously grow through the accretion of surrounding matter and the merging of smaller [[galaxy-groups|Galaxy groups]]. This ongoing assembly generates significant substructure and macroscopic turbulence, injecting non-thermal pressure components—such as large-scale magnetic fields and relativistic cosmic rays—into the ICM. Understanding these complex dynamical states is critical for [[cluster-cosmology|Cluster cosmology]] and [[galaxy-cluster-lensing|Galaxy Cluster Lensing]], which utilize cluster abundances, Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) signatures, and gravitational shear to tightly constrain fundamental cosmological parameters and the nature of dark energy [Hughes et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998IAUS..188..189H/abstract), [Newman et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PASP..114...29N/abstract). 

This article outlines the foundational physics and modern observations of galaxy clusters. It reviews their morphological classifications, the thermodynamics and chemical enrichment of the ICM, and the profound impact of the cluster environment on galaxy evolution. Finally, it explores current open questions at the frontier of cluster astrophysics, including the elusive gamma-ray emission expected from hadronic cosmic rays, the thermodynamic impact of active galactic nuclei (AGN) feedback in preventing runaway cooling flows, and the precise calibration of [[galaxy-cluster-properties-and-scaling-relations|Galaxy Cluster Properties and Scaling Relations]] to mitigate biases in hydrostatic mass estimates.

## Fundamental Properties and Scaling Relations

Galaxy clusters are the most massive virialized objects in the universe, representing the largest nodes in the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]]. With total masses typically ranging from $10^{14}$ to $10^{15} h^{-1} M_\odot$ and spanning radii of $1-3 h^{-1} \text{ Mpc}$, they serve as critical astrophysical laboratories and cosmological probes [Diaferio et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008SSRv..134....7D/abstract). The total gravitational mass of a cluster is heavily dominated by dark matter, which constitutes approximately 80% of the mass budget. The hot, X-ray emitting thermal plasma of the intracluster medium (ICM) accounts for the vast majority of the baryonic mass ($\lesssim 20\%$), while stars and cold gas locked within member galaxies make up only a few percent. The total baryonic budget and the origin of the heavy elements within this medium have long been subjects of theoretical modeling; for instance, early theories proposed that the stellar progenitors of [[massive-compact-objects|Massive compact objects]] (MACHOs) could potentially account for the observed gas mass and heavy element abundances in rich clusters [David et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...484L..11D/abstract). Because the stellar component contributes such a small fraction to the overall potential, clusters exhibit a remarkably high mass-to-light ratio—typically $M/L \sim 350 h \, (M_\odot/L_\odot)$—which is comparable to the cosmic mean and highlights clusters as representative samples of the universe's matter content.

The spatial extent and mass of a cluster are fundamentally characterized by its virial mass, $M_\Delta$, defined as the mass enclosed within a radius $r_\Delta$ where the mean density is $\Delta$ times the critical density of the universe (commonly $\Delta = 200$ or $500$). While $r_{200c}$ is a standard proxy for the virial radius, recent dynamical analyses emphasize the "splashback radius" ($R_{sp}$) as the true physical boundary of the dark matter halo, representing the apocenter of the most recently accreted shell of matter. Observations map this boundary at $R_{sp} \approx 1.4 r_{200c}$ for massive clusters [Kopylova et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AstBu..77..347K/abstract). Furthermore, when tracing infall patterns to large radii using redshift space caustics, the "ultimate halo mass"—the mass that will remain bound in the far future of a $\Lambda$CDM universe—is measured to be approximately $2.0 M_{200}$ [Rines et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...767...15R/abstract).

The bulk observables of clusters are tightly coupled through [[galaxy-cluster-properties-and-scaling-relations|Galaxy Cluster Properties and Scaling Relations]] that reflect their gravitational collapse. Under the assumption of hydrostatic equilibrium and strict self-similarity (where clusters are simply scaled-up versions of smaller halos), basic physics predicts straightforward scaling relationships between the cluster mass, X-ray temperature ($T$), X-ray luminosity ($L_X$), and the velocity dispersion of the member galaxies ($\sigma$). Optical surveys such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) have further established robust richness-mass scaling relations, identifying that cluster mass scales with optical richness $R$ as $M_{200} \propto R^{1.90}$ [Wen et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJS..183..197W/abstract). However, empirical measurements yield a steeper scaling relation for clusters than simple models predict, approximately $L_X \propto T^{2.79 \pm 0.08}$ [Xue et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...538...65X/abstract). Interestingly, observational comparisons find little dynamical evolution in the velocity dispersion profiles and the $L_X$--$T$ scaling relations of clusters between the local universe and $z \sim 0.3$ [Girardi et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...548...79G/abstract). 

This deviation from simple scaling theory is even more pronounced in lower-mass systems, leading to an observed "similarity break" between clusters and [[galaxy-groups|Galaxy groups]]. For groups, the luminosity-temperature relation steepens drastically to $L_X \propto T^{5.57 \pm 1.79}$ [Xue et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...538...65X/abstract). The physical origin of this similarity break remains an area of active debate. It is generally attributed either to non-gravitational pre-heating (e.g., from AGN or supernovae) that establishes an entropy floor, or to higher star-formation efficiencies in lower-mass systems that selectively remove low-entropy gas from the X-ray emitting phase [Bryan et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...544L...1B/abstract).

Finally, calculations of scaling relations must account for the dynamical complexity of these systems. While hydrostatic equilibrium is a foundational assumption for deriving X-ray masses, a significant fraction of clusters (30-70%) exhibit substantial [[cluster-substructure|Cluster substructure]] and are not fully relaxed. Numerical simulations and multi-wavelength observations demonstrate that clusters are typically triaxial and that turbulent gas motions and cosmic rays provide a significant non-thermal pressure component. In some systems, this non-thermal support can account for $\approx 20\%$ of the total pressure balancing gravity, necessitating careful corrections to avoid systematic biases in hydrostatic mass estimates used for precision cosmology [Morandi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2567M/abstract).

## Historical Development and Morphological Classification

The systematic study of galaxy clusters began in the mid-twentieth century, relying entirely on wide-field optical photographic surveys. While early pioneers like Fritz Zwicky cataloged thousands of systems and first posited the existence of dark matter on cluster scales, the foundational taxonomy for these massive systems was established by George Abell. The [[abell-catalog-of-clusters|Abell Catalog of Clusters]] (1958) identified 1,682 rich clusters from the Palomar Sky Survey using strict richness and compactness criteria. Abell broadly divided these systems into Regular vs. Irregular clusters. Regular clusters, such as the [[coma-cluster|Coma Cluster]], are characterized by a highly concentrated, circularly symmetric spatial distribution of galaxies, historically interpreted as evidence of a dynamically relaxed state. In contrast, irregular clusters, such as the [[virgo-cluster|Virgo Cluster]], lack clear symmetry and exhibit significant spatial [[cluster-substructure|Cluster substructure]], implying they are dynamically young and still undergoing relaxation.

To better quantify these visual properties, several optical morphological classification schemes were developed. The Bautz-Morgan classification (1970) categorizes clusters based on the relative optical contrast of their brightest member galaxy [Bautz et al. (1970)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1970ApJ...162L.149B/abstract). Type I clusters are dominated by a single, centrally located supergiant D or [[cd-galaxies|cD galaxies]] (systems with highly extended, diffuse stellar envelopes), while Type III clusters possess no single galaxy significantly brighter than the general cluster population [Bautz et al. (1970)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1970ApJ...162L.149B/abstract). Observational evidence suggests that the central galaxies in Type I clusters are often highly flattened, a feature potentially driven by mergers and dynamical friction in the cluster core [Leir et al. (1977)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977ApJS...34..381L/abstract). Later, the Rood-Sastry system further refined cluster taxonomy by categorizing the geometric arrangement of the ten brightest members. Interestingly, a cluster morphology-density relation exists on [[superclusters|super-cluster]] scales: flattened or linear clusters reside predominantly in rich supercluster environments, whereas amorphous or round clusters are more prevalent in lower-density regions [Schombert et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ApJ...363..331S/abstract). Furthermore, richer superclusters host denser environments and older stellar populations overall, though their macroscopic morphology (e.g., filamentary versus pancake-like structures) shows little direct influence on galaxy properties compared to local group-scale density effects [Costa-Duarte et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..906C/abstract). To improve cluster identification and minimize background contamination in such optical surveys, robust techniques were later developed using [[galaxy-clustering-and-redshift-estimation|photometrically estimated redshifts]] to determine cluster membership on a probabilistic basis [Brunner et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000AJ....120.2851B/abstract).

With the advent of X-ray astronomy in the 1970s, the paradigm for identifying and classifying clusters shifted dramatically. Rich clusters are highly luminous X-ray sources ($L_X \sim 10^{43} - 10^{45}$ erg s$^{-1}$) due to thermal bremsstrahlung from the hot, diffuse intracluster medium (ICM) [Sarazin et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986RvMP...58....1S/abstract). X-ray selection is fundamentally less susceptible to the line-of-sight optical projection biases that plagued early catalogs [Romer et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.372...75R/abstract). Furthermore, X-ray selection proved vital for mapping regions obscured by the Milky Way; the CIZA survey was the first systematic X-ray search for galaxy clusters located within the Galactic plane region, closing the gap to complete the local [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] map [Ebeling et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000lssx.proc..351E/abstract). Based on *Einstein* observatory data, Jones & Forman introduced an X-ray morphological classification separating clusters into two families: "XD" (X-ray dominant) clusters, which exhibit small core radii ($\lesssim 300$ kpc) centered on a stationary, optically bright dominant galaxy; and "nXD" clusters, which feature larger core radii ($400-800$ kpc) and lack a central dominant galaxy [Jones et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApJ...276...38J/abstract).

Modern multi-wavelength observations have fundamentally altered the historical view of clusters as static, equilibrium structures. Kinematic and X-ray substructure analyses reveal that $30\% - 70\%$ of rich clusters possess significant substructure, demonstrating a paradigm shift: clusters are not entirely relaxed, but rather dynamically young objects actively growing via the hierarchical accretion of groups and subclusters [Bird et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PASP..105.1495B/abstract), [Aguerri et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...521A..28A/abstract). In the contemporary era, cluster identification has been further revolutionized by the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect—the inverse Compton scattering of cosmic microwave background photons by hot ICM electrons. Modern SZ surveys provide a nearly mass-limited selection of clusters, detecting and mapping these massive systems out to $z > 1$ and revealing the large-scale structure far beyond the limits of early optical techniques [Hughes et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998IAUS..188..189H/abstract). This modern application builds upon Sunyaev and Zeldovich's early proposal that combined X-ray and radio (SZ) observations could be used to estimate cluster distances and the Hubble constant [Sunyaev et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981ASPRv...1....1S/abstract).

## The Intra-Cluster Medium and X-ray Astrophysics

The intracluster medium (ICM) is a hot, diffuse plasma that permeates the space between member galaxies and constitutes the vast majority of a cluster's baryonic mass. With characteristic gas densities of $10^{-4} - 10^{-2}$ cm$^{-3}$ and temperatures ranging from $10^7 - 10^8$ K, the ICM represents a mass reservoir that is typically an order of magnitude larger than the total stellar mass contained within the cluster galaxies [Sarazin et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986RvMP...58....1S/abstract). At these temperatures, the gas is fully ionized and cools primarily through thermal bremsstrahlung (free-free emission), producing copious X-ray emission with luminosities typically in the range of $L_X \sim 10^{43} - 10^{45}$ erg s$^{-1}$ [Sarazin et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986RvMP...58....1S/abstract), [Ota et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012RAA....12..973O/abstract).

Far from being pristine primordial gas, the ICM is highly enriched, serving as a fossil record of cosmic nucleosynthesis. X-ray spectroscopy has allowed the detection of up to 13 heavy elements, with iron (Fe) being the most prominent and easily detected tracer [Böhringer et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MmSAI..85..396B/abstract). These chemical abundances indicate that the ICM has been polluted by both core-collapse and Type Ia supernovae over cosmic time. Early statistical studies established the importance of these explosive events, finding an average supernova frequency of approximately one explosion per cluster galaxy per 316 years [Barbon et al. (1968)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1968AJ.....73.1016B/abstract). Observations demonstrate that the global ICM metallicity is typically $\sim 1/3$ of the solar value [Renzini et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...488...35R/abstract). Remarkably, the Iron mass-to-light ratio remains constant across clusters hotter than 2 keV. This constancy implies a universal enrichment efficiency and indicates that the vast majority of the heavy elements synthesized within the cluster potential ultimately reside in the diffuse ICM rather than remaining locked inside the member galaxies [Renzini et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...488...35R/abstract), [Renzini et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999cezh.conf..185R/abstract).

The spatial and thermal structure of the ICM varies significantly among different systems. In [[cool-core-clusters|Cool core clusters]] (historically associated with "XD" morphological classes [Jones et al. (1984)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1984ApJ...276...38J/abstract)), the central gas density is highly peaked around a dominant central galaxy (often [[cd-galaxies|cD galaxies]] or [[brightest-cluster-galaxies|Brightest cluster galaxies]]), leading to localized regions where the radiative cooling time is significantly shorter than the Hubble time. These cool cores frequently exhibit enhanced central metal abundances, largely driven by Type Ia supernova ejecta from the central galaxy [Böhringer et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MmSAI..85..396B/abstract). 

There remain significant tensions in the literature regarding the exact thermodynamic scaling relations of the ICM. While massive clusters follow a relatively tight $L_X$--$T$ scaling relation ($L_X \propto T^{2.8}$), lower-mass systems such as [[galaxy-groups|Galaxy groups]] exhibit a "similarity break," following a much steeper relation ($L_X \propto T^{5.6}$) [Xue et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...538...65X/abstract). The physical cause of this break is actively debated: it may be driven by non-gravitational pre-heating (e.g., from active galactic nuclei) that sets an entropy floor, or alternatively, by a higher efficiency of star formation that selectively depletes low-entropy gas in smaller halos [Bryan et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...544L...1B/abstract), [Xue et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...538...65X/abstract). To properly model these intermediate regimes, cosmological hydrodynamic simulations play a critical role in following the redistribution of baryons via cooling and feedback in the intragroup medium [Oppenheimer et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Univ....7..209O/abstract). 

Finally, while the ICM is predominantly modeled under the assumption of hydrostatic equilibrium governed by thermal pressure, recent observations and hydrodynamic simulations indicate a significant non-thermal pressure component. Turbulence, bulk gas motions, and cosmic rays reaccelerated by merger shocks can provide up to $\sim 20\%$ of the total pressure support [Morandi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2567M/abstract). The exact contribution of this non-thermal component remains an unresolved issue, as it systematically biases X-ray-derived [[galaxy-cluster-mass-profiles|Galaxy-cluster mass profiles]] and cosmological constraints if left uncorrected [Morandi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2567M/abstract), [Ota et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012RAA....12..973O/abstract).

## Hierarchical Assembly and Dynamical Evolution

In the $\Lambda$CDM cosmological paradigm, galaxy clusters represent the most massive gravitationally bound systems in the universe, forming late in cosmic history at the intersecting nodes of the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]] [Diaferio et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008SSRv..134....7D/abstract). The growth of these systems is governed by a "bottom-up" process of [[hierarchical-clustering|Hierarchical clustering]], wherein mass is assembled through the anisotropic, episodic accretion of diffuse dark matter and baryonic gas, along with the ongoing merging of smaller [[galaxy-groups|Galaxy groups]] and subclusters.

Because the Hubble time is comparable to a few dynamical timescales for massive clusters, these systems are frequently dynamically young. Diagnostics applied to galaxy kinematics and X-ray surface brightness maps reveal that between $30\%$ and $70\%$ of well-studied clusters exhibit significant [[cluster-substructure|Cluster substructure]] [Bird et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993PASP..105.1495B/abstract), [Aguerri et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...521A..28A/abstract). Cluster mergers are a primary driver of this structural complexity, with massive systems frequently containing pre-merger companions and post-collision remnants [Wen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.1849W/abstract). Furthermore, N-body simulations demonstrate that the spatial distribution of galaxies within massive clusters is often distinctly lopsided relative to the [[central-galaxies|central galaxy]] [Mangis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RNAAS...8...84M/abstract).

The dynamical boundary of a growing cluster extends well beyond the traditionally defined virial radius, $R_{200c}$. The physical edge of the dark matter halo is more accurately described by the splashback radius ($R_{\rm sp}$), which marks the apocenter of matter on its first orbit following infall. Observations of the galaxy number density profiles in local clusters indicate an average ratio of $\langle R_{\rm sp}/R_{200c} \rangle = 1.40 \pm 0.02$ [Kopylova et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022AstBu..77..347K/abstract). By tracing the velocity infall patterns of galaxies out to these large radii, the "ultimate halo mass"—the total mass that will remain gravitationally bound in the far future of a $\Lambda$CDM universe—is estimated to be $\approx 2.0 M_{200}$ [Rines et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...767...15R/abstract).

This continuous hierarchical assembly introduces profound complexities when determining cluster masses, a critical requirement for using clusters as high-precision probes of the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]]. The classical assumption that the intracluster medium is in strict hydrostatic equilibrium within a spherical potential is heavily challenged by the ubiquity of unrelaxed substructures. N-body simulations and joint X-ray and lensing analyses indicate that clusters are typically triaxial. Furthermore, the turbulence and bulk flows injected by cluster mergers generate a substantial non-thermal gas pressure component, which can account for $\approx 20\%$ of the total pressure in massive systems [Morandi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2567M/abstract). Consequently, there is an active tension in the literature between mass estimates derived from X-ray scaling relations and those obtained from weak gravitational lensing. Resolving these discrepancies requires careful modeling of both halo triaxiality and the non-thermal pressure support induced by the ongoing assembly of the cluster [Morandi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2567M/abstract), [Nagai et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AIPC.1632...88N/abstract).

## Environmental Impact on Galaxy Evolution

Clusters act as dense, hostile environments that drive significant evolutionary changes in their constituent [[satellite-galaxies|Satellite galaxies]]. The classical manifestation of this environmental impact is the morphology-density relation, which quantifies the transition from star-forming [[disk-galaxies|Disk Galaxies]] in the field to [[passive-galaxies|quiescent early-type galaxies]] in dense cluster cores. In local environments, the fraction of spiral galaxies decreases from $\sim 60\%$ in low-density regions to $< 10\%$ in the highest-density cluster cores, with a commensurate increase in the population of elliptical and lenticular (S0) galaxies. Recent observations indicate that this morphological segregation is already established as early as $z = 1.75$, with [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] ($\log(M_*/M_\odot) \lesssim 10.5$) undergoing the most significant structural transformations [Sazonova et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...85S/abstract).

The physical mechanisms driving these transformations are broadly divided into gravitational and hydrodynamical processes. Gravitationally, galaxies within clusters undergo frequent high-speed impulsive encounters—a process known as [[galaxy-harassment|Galaxy harassment]]. As shown by Aguilar & White, while harassment can strip material and induce morphological changes (such as the transformation of spirals into S0 galaxies [Boselli et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PASP..118..517B/abstract)), it generally preserves the $r^{1/4}$ surface brightness profiles of early-type systems without introducing sharp tidal cut-offs. Additionally, detailed studies of the cluster color-magnitude relation (CMR) reveal that the departure of bright-end galaxies from a linear CMR is likely driven by the influence of minor mergers [Jiménez et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASPC..421..261J/abstract). The extreme tidal fields within clusters can also completely disrupt massive galaxies. Observations suggest that a significant fraction of low-mass galaxies in clusters are actually the dynamically stripped remnants of initially higher-mass systems. This process artificially steepens the faint-end slope of the cluster galaxy luminosity function over time, for example shifting from $\alpha = -1.25$ in the field to $\alpha = -1.44$ in the Perseus Cluster [Conselice et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...573L...5C/abstract).

Hydrodynamically, the interaction between the interstellar medium of an infalling galaxy and the hot, dense intracluster medium (ICM) results in ram-pressure stripping. This process efficiently removes the cold gas reservoir necessary for star formation. Consequently, the star formation rate (SFR) of cluster members declines steadily, typically on timescales of a few gigayears, mirroring the gas consumption timescale of field disks [Balogh et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...540..113B/abstract). Empirical studies show a sharp decline in both the SFR and the fraction of star-forming galaxies at clustercentric radii $R \lesssim 0.4 R_{200}$, strongly implicating ram-pressure stripping as the dominant driver of star formation quenching in the inner cluster [Atlee et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...761..114A/abstract). This ongoing quenching leads to distinct evolutionary signatures, such as the [[butcher-oemler-effect|Butcher-Oemler effect]]—the observed increase in the fraction of blue, star-forming galaxies in clusters at [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|high redshifts]]—and a high prevalence of "k+a" (post-starburst) galaxies at intermediate redshifts ($z \sim 0.5$).

While local clusters are dominated by passively evolving systems, the literature highlights a significant tension regarding the environmental dependence of star formation at early cosmic epochs. In [[high-redshift-galaxy-clusters|High-redshift galaxy clusters]] ($z \gtrsim 1.5$), the star formation-density relation appears to reverse. Rather than quenching star formation, the cores of massive high-redshift clusters host massive, dust-obscured starbursts. Mass-normalized SFRs in these early cluster environments scale as $(1+z)^{5.5 \pm 0.6}$, matching or exceeding the activity of average field halos at the same epoch [Smail et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.2290S/abstract). This reversal suggests that the massive early-type galaxies dominating local cluster cores assembled the bulk of their stellar mass via vigorous, [[extreme-starbursts|merger-driven starbursts]] before the cluster environment transitioned into a quenching regime.

## Non-thermal Components and High-Energy Emissions

While the bulk of the intracluster medium consists of a thermal X-ray emitting plasma, it is also permeated by large-scale magnetic fields and populations of relativistic particles, collectively forming a substantial non-thermal component [Feretti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&ARv..20...54F/abstract). These non-thermal components—which include cosmic rays and turbulent gas motions—can account for up to $\sim 20\%$ of the total cluster pressure. Consequently, assuming strict hydrostatic equilibrium based purely on thermal pressure can lead to systematic biases in estimating [[galaxy-cluster-mass-profiles|Galaxy-cluster mass profiles]] and cosmological scaling relations [Morandi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2567M/abstract), [Biermann et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ASPC..301..293B/abstract).

The presence of magnetic fields and relativistic electrons is directly traced by diffuse, megaparsec-scale synchrotron radio emission. These sources, which exhibit extremely low surface brightness and are unassociated with individual member galaxies, are generally classified into [[giant-radio-halos|Giant radio halos]], radio relics, and mini-halos [Feretti et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&ARv..20...54F/abstract), [Feretti et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph.12185F/abstract). Giant radio halos are centrally located, whereas radio relics typically reside in the cluster periphery. Both phenomena are strongly linked to the hierarchical assembly of clusters; the energy dissipated by large-scale shocks and turbulence during cluster mergers reaccelerates the relativistic electron population, powering the synchrotron emission [Feretti et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006astro.ph.12185F/abstract), [Brunetti et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...670L...5B/abstract). Rotation measure studies indicate that intracluster magnetic fields typically have strengths on the order of $\sim 1 \ \mu\text{G}$ [Brunetti et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...670L...5B/abstract). However, the discovery of a candidate radio mini-halo in a $z=1.709$ cool core cluster suggests that magnetic fields can be efficiently amplified to $\sim 10 \ \mu\text{G}$ very early in the cluster formation process [Hlavacek-Larrondo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250619901H/abstract).

In addition to relativistic electrons, clusters act as long-term storage reservoirs for hadronic cosmic rays. High-energy protons accelerated by merger shocks and active galactic nuclei are confined by the intracluster magnetic field for cosmological timescales [Fang et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...828...37F/abstract), [Domainko et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ICRC....3..953D/abstract). Interactions between confined cosmic rays and infrared photons (photopion production) are predicted to generate secondary particles, including high-energy neutrinos and gamma-rays via pion decay [de Marco et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhRvD..73d3004D/abstract). Theoretical models suggest that galaxy clusters could contribute a significant fraction of the diffuse high-energy neutrino flux above $30 \text{ TeV}$ observed by the IceCube Neutrino Observatory [Fang et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...828...37F/abstract), [Nisa et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021arXiv210710080N/abstract).

Despite robust theoretical predictions for hadronic cosmic ray interactions, there is a prominent and unresolved tension in the literature regarding high-energy emissions. Observations by the Fermi-LAT, as well as ground-based Cherenkov arrays like H.E.S.S. and VERITAS targeting massive systems such as the [[coma-cluster|Coma Cluster]], have yet to yield a single confirmed gamma-ray detection from a galaxy cluster [Zimmer et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150202653Z/abstract), [Perkins et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008AIPC.1085..569P/abstract). These non-detections place highly restrictive upper limits on the cosmic ray proton content, indicating that the total energy density of cosmic rays must be $<10\%$ (and in some constraints, such as for Abell 85, $<8\%$) of the thermal energy density of the intracluster medium [Brunetti et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...670L...5B/abstract), [Domainko et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IJMPD..18.1627D/abstract). This observational tension indicates that either the acceleration efficiency of hadronic cosmic rays in clusters is lower than previously assumed, or their energy spectra are significantly steeper than standard models predict.

## Gravitational Lensing and Cosmological Constraints

As the most massive gravitationally bound structures in the universe, clusters of galaxies serve as powerful astrophysical laboratories and cosmological probes. Their extreme masses profoundly warp spacetime, allowing them to act as gravitational telescopes, while their abundance and spatial distribution trace the underlying geometry and expansion history of the universe.

**[[galaxy-cluster-lensing|Galaxy Cluster Lensing]]**
The gravitational deflection of light by a cluster occurs in two primary observational regimes: strong and weak lensing. In the strong lensing regime, the dense cluster core produces highly magnified, distorted multiple images and giant arcs of background galaxies [Kneib et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&ARv..19...47K/abstract). These features are extremely sensitive to the inner [[galaxy-cluster-mass-profiles|Galaxy-cluster mass profiles]] and the dark matter distribution [Natarajan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024SSRv..220...19N/abstract). 

In the weak lensing regime, the cluster's gravitational potential induces small, coherent statistical deformations (shear) in the shapes of background galaxies. This enables the reconstruction of total mass profiles out to the cluster's virial radius. Mass-to-light ratios inferred from lensing are characteristically high, often reaching $\sim 200 \, M_\odot/L_\odot$, confirming that cluster masses are overwhelmingly dominated by dark matter. Additionally, magnification bias and the resulting "depletion area" of lensed sources can be used to probe the [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|redshift distribution]] of faint background galaxies beyond $B=25$ [Mellier et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..146..465M/abstract). 

However, there is an active tension in the literature between cluster mass estimates derived from weak lensing and those calculated via X-ray hydrostatic equilibrium equations. Joint analyses of systems such as Abell 1689 reveal that clusters are frequently triaxial—with line-of-sight to sky-plane axial ratios sometimes exceeding 2—and that non-thermal pressure from turbulence and magnetic fields can account for $\sim 20\%$ of the total pressure support [Morandi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2567M/abstract). Failing to account for this triaxiality and non-thermal pressure can significantly bias cosmological parameters derived from standard X-ray mass proxies [Morandi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2567M/abstract), [Nagai et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AIPC.1632...88N/abstract).

**The Sunyaev-Zel'dovich Effect**
The Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) effect provides an independent method for probing the intracluster medium and measuring cosmic distances. As cosmic microwave background (CMB) photons pass through a cluster, they are inverse-Compton scattered by the hot electrons in the intracluster plasma, distorting the CMB's blackbody spectrum [Hughes et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998IAUS..188..189H/abstract). Because the SZ signal and X-ray bremsstrahlung emission scale differently with the plasma density, combining SZ and X-ray observations of the same cluster breaks geometric degeneracies. This synergy allows for a direct, distance-independent determination of the Hubble constant, $H_0$ [Hughes et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998IAUS..188..189H/abstract), [Natarajan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024SSRv..220...19N/abstract).

**[[cluster-cosmology|Cluster cosmology]] and Large-Scale Structure**
Clusters are the principal nodes of the [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]]. The spatial distribution of X-ray selected clusters exhibits a characteristic correlation length of $13-15 \, h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$ [Romer et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994Natur.372...75R/abstract). Additionally, average cluster sizes and their correlation lengths have been used as a robust test for cosmic homogeneity, confirming the statistical consistency of the universe to within 5-10% over vast scales of $2\ h^{-1}\text{Gpc}$ [Longo et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1305.0464L/abstract). The evolution of the cluster [[mass-distribution-and-functions|mass function]] $n(M,z)$ is highly sensitive to the cosmic matter density $\Omega_m$ and the amplitude of mass fluctuations $\sigma_8$ [Nichol et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph.10231N/abstract). 

Furthermore, by utilizing the simultaneous distribution of clusters in redshift and velocity dispersion, observational surveys can tightly constrain models of dark energy. Specifically, the dark energy equation of state parameter can be constrained to an accuracy of $\pm 0.1$ using cluster data alone [Newman et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PASP..114...29N/abstract):
$$w = \frac{P}{\rho}$$
where $P$ is the pressure and $\rho$ is the energy density of the dark energy fluid. 

Beyond standard cosmology, clusters provide unique environments to test fundamental physics. The stacked phase space density of galaxies around clusters offers a method to test modified gravity theories, such as $f(R)$ models, on scales of $1-30 \, \mathrm{Mpc}$ [Lam et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvL.109e1301L/abstract). Additionally, the measurement of gravitational redshift within the deep potential wells of clusters can be used to test the Weak Equivalence Principle for dark matter, constraining the existence of a hypothetical fifth force to within a few percent [Di Dio et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260119861D/abstract).

## Star Clusters and Intracluster Populations

The deep gravitational potential wells of galaxy clusters host not only galaxies and hot plasma, but also a vast, unbound population of stars and stellar systems. This intracluster population serves as a crucial fossil record, tracing the hierarchical assembly of the cluster and the relentless tidal disruption of its member galaxies over cosmic time [West et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ASPC..245..420W/abstract), [West et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Natur.427...31W/abstract).

During cluster assembly, gravitational interactions and ram-pressure stripping tear material from infalling galaxies. These processes create sprawling tidal streams and diffuse "ghosts" of disrupted systems, which eventually mix to form the intracluster light (ICL) [Gregg et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004IAUS..217...70G/abstract). The ICL is intimately linked to the highly extended, diffuse stellar envelopes of central [[cd-galaxies|cD galaxies]], and together they can account for a substantial fraction of the total optical luminosity in a cluster. 

Embedded within the ICL are thousands of [[extragalactic-globular-clusters|Extragalactic globular clusters]] that have been stripped from their parent halos. These intracluster globular clusters (ICGCs) move freely throughout the cluster core [West et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...453L..77W/abstract). Spatial mapping reveals that ICGC populations can be immense; in the [[virgo-cluster|Virgo Cluster]], for example, the ICGC distribution extends up to 5 million light-years from the cluster center [Lee et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Sci...328..334L/abstract). Furthermore, these intracluster globular clusters serve as unique physical laboratories for studying fundamental dynamical processes like two-body relaxation and mass segregation on timescales shorter than the age of the universe [Meylan et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000chun.proc...76M/abstract).

The properties of these clusters offer distinct clues regarding their origins. The globular cluster systems of massive galaxies generally exhibit a bimodal color distribution: a metal-poor (blue) subpopulation and a metal-rich (red) subpopulation [Brodie et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ARA&A..44..193B/abstract). Globally, when summing across all galaxies, blue (metal-poor) globular clusters outnumber red ones by a ratio of roughly 4 to 1 [Harris et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AJ....151..102H/abstract). The blue GCs formed early in low-mass dark matter halos, while the red GCs trace the later, dissipational buildup of massive early-type galaxies. Estimates indicating that the oldest globular clusters are approximately 13 billion years old provide a key constraint on both the age of the universe and the timeline of early galaxy assembly [Mould et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998Natur.395A..20M/abstract). In the intracluster medium, blue GCs are disproportionately abundant [D'Abrusco et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251101576D/abstract). This overabundance is consistent with ICGCs originating primarily as tidal debris stripped from low-mass [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxies]], which are heavily dominated by metal-poor clusters and are highly susceptible to tidal disruption [Lee et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Sci...328..334L/abstract). In addition, mapping the metallicity and age gradients of globular clusters with clustercentric radius provides an essential tracer for the formation and evolution of the host cluster itself [Park et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...91P/abstract). When comparing structural parameters, it is found that unlike early-type galaxies, the individual half-light radii of globular clusters are independent of their luminosities, a fundamental trend observed across environments from clusters down to M31 and the Milky Way [van den Bergh et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AJ....140.1043V/abstract).

The efficiency of cluster formation is remarkably consistent across different scales. Bound [[star-clusters-and-complexes|star clusters]] typically represent $\approx 10\%$ of the total star formation within galaxies, with some evidence suggesting that this efficiency increases in environments with high star-formation rate densities [Bastian et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASPC..470..287B/abstract). The total mass of a galaxy's GC system scales linearly with its host dark matter halo mass, $M_{\rm GC} \propto M_{\rm halo}$ [Beasley et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020rfma.book..245B/abstract). However, in dense cluster environments, the specific frequency $S_N$—a measure of the number of GCs normalized to the host's luminosity—is notably elevated in cluster ellipticals and nucleated dwarfs compared to field spirals [Seth et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....127..798S/abstract). Furthermore, kinematic and spatial data suggest that the majority of the rich GC populations found in the very centers of clusters were assembled well before the bulk of the stars forming the central cD galaxies [Blakeslee et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000cucg.confE..61B/abstract).

The extreme tidal environment of a cluster also blurs the boundaries between massive star clusters, ultra-compact dwarfs (UCDs), and [[compact-galaxies|Compact Galaxies]] [Zaritsky et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...727..116Z/abstract). UCDs—first identified in the Fornax and Virgo clusters—exhibit structural parameters intermediate between those of large [[globular-clusters|Globular Clusters]] and dwarf spheroidal galaxies. A leading evolutionary scenario for UCDs is "galaxy threshing," wherein the extended stellar disk and halo of an infalling nucleated dwarf galaxy are entirely stripped away by the cluster's tidal field [Drinkwater et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PASA...21..375D/abstract). The surviving remnants are the dense [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]] or [[compact-galactic-nuclei|Compact galactic nuclei]], left to orbit the cluster as ultra-compact, stripped stellar cores [van der Marel et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..241..475V/abstract).

## The High-Redshift Frontier and Protoclusters

The study of the earliest stages of cluster formation at $z > 1.5$ represents a critical frontier in observational cosmology. In this epoch, the progenitors of today's massive clusters—termed [[protoclusters|Protoclusters]]—exist as vast, unvirialized [[galaxy-overdensities|Galaxy overdensities]] embedded within the [[cosmic-web|Cosmic web]]. These extended structures are typically characterized by sheet-like or filamentary geometries that are actively assembling through hierarchical accretion prior to their collapse into spherically relaxed, [[high-redshift-galaxy-clusters|High-redshift galaxy clusters]] [Kurk et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...504..331K/abstract).

Observations at $z > 5$ have revealed extreme environments where the precursors of cluster cores are rapidly building up their mass. A notable example is a giant protocluster discovered at $z \approx 5.7$, which spans a comoving volume of approximately $35^3\ \text{Mpc}^3$ and exhibits a local density of Lyman-alpha emitting galaxies that is $6.6$ times the cosmic average [Jiang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018NatAs...2..962J/abstract). With an estimated final collapse mass of $(3.6 \pm 0.9) \times 10^{15}\ M_\odot$, this structure will eventually evolve into one of the most massive clusters in the universe. However, the existence of such massive structures at a time when the universe was only one billion years old presents a tension in the literature, as they are rarely reproduced in standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological simulations [Jiang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018NatAs...2..962J/abstract).

One of the most striking evolutionary signatures in high-redshift clusters is the reversal of the star formation-density relation. While local clusters at $z < 0.4$ are broadly underdense in star-forming galaxies, an additional population of [[extreme-starbursts|dusty starbursts]] is required to explain the observed cluster luminosities at $z > 0.4$ [Muzzin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...686..966M/abstract). By $z \gtrsim 1.5$, the local relation is completely inverted: cluster cores become active sites of heavily dust-obscured starbursts. Submillimeter observations of clusters at $z = 1.6 - 2.0$ indicate that mass-normalized star formation rates in these environments evolve dramatically, scaling as $(1+z)^{5.5 \pm 0.6}$ [Smail et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.2290S/abstract). By $z \sim 1.8 \pm 0.2$, the star formation rates in cluster halos match those of average field halos, with individual galaxies exhibiting rates of $\text{SFR} \gtrsim 100\ M_\odot\ \text{yr}^{-1}$ [Smail et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.2290S/abstract). The literature currently debates the exact mechanisms responsible for this reversal—specifically the relative roles of gas-rich major mergers versus early AGN feedback—but it is generally agreed that these extreme starbursts form the bulk of the massive early-type population seen in local clusters. 

Despite this intense star formation, the mechanisms that ultimately quench clusters are already activating at these redshifts. The morphology-density relationship is shown to be established as early as $z=1.75$, disproportionately affecting [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] ($\log M/M_\odot \lesssim 10.5$), which are rapidly transformed into compact, bulge-dominated systems [Sazonova et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...899...85S/abstract). The emergence of [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] and the gradual assembly of the red sequence can be observed in structures like the $z=1.6$ overdensity Cl 0332-2742, where a core of passively evolving galaxies is already evident [Kurk et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...504..331K/abstract). Recent multiobject spectroscopic surveys have also discovered clusters at $z \sim 0.95$ with surprisingly contrasting quiescent fractions despite having similar masses. This variance is potentially linked to the different rates at which star-forming galaxies are replenished from the surrounding large-scale structure [Lee et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..272...47L/abstract).

This era of vigorous stellar assembly is accompanied by enhanced central supermassive black hole accretion. Radio activity is significantly elevated in high-redshift clusters; the fraction of clusters hosting a central radio source at $0 < z < 1.5$ increases by a factor of 10 compared to systems at $z < 0.6$ [Mo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...901..131M/abstract). At $z \gtrsim 0.7$, cluster galaxies of a fixed stellar mass are roughly twice as likely to be radioluminous ($L_{\text{1.4GHz}} \geq 10^{25}\ \text{W Hz}^{-1}$) compared to their counterparts in the field [Mo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...901..131M/abstract), underscoring the profound impact of the early protocluster environment on both star formation and active galactic nuclei.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite the success of the [[hierarchical-merger-paradigm|Hierarchical merger paradigm]] in explaining the macroscopic assembly of galaxy clusters, several fundamental questions regarding the complex interplay of their baryonic and dark matter components remain unresolved.

A primary challenge lies in the thermodynamics of the intracluster medium (ICM). X-ray observations of [[cool-core-clusters|Cool core clusters]] reveal central gas cooling times significantly shorter than the Hubble time. In the absence of a heating mechanism, this should result in massive cooling flows and runaway star formation; however, star formation is largely suppressed in these environments [Sarazin et al. (1986)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986RvMP...58....1S/abstract). The consensus posits that *AGN feedback* from central supermassive black holes injects sufficient energy to offset this cooling. This non-gravitational heating is also invoked to explain the "similarity break" in [[galaxy-cluster-properties-and-scaling-relations|Galaxy Cluster Properties and Scaling Relations]] between high-mass clusters and lower-mass [[galaxy-groups|Galaxy groups]], which appear systematically more gas-poor [Xue et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...538...65X/abstract), [Bryan et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000ApJ...544L...1B/abstract). The precise physical mechanisms by which AGN feedback couples mechanically and thermally to the ICM remains an active area of research.

Mass estimation, a cornerstone of [[cluster-cosmology|Cluster cosmology]], is complicated by the presence of *non-thermal gas pressure*. Standard X-ray hydrostatic mass derivations typically assume that thermal gas pressure balances gravity. However, merging clusters exhibit significant non-thermal pressure support driven by macroscopic turbulence, large-scale magnetic fields (up to $\sim 10\ \mu\text{G}$), and relativistic cosmic rays, which can account for $\approx 20\%$ of the total core pressure and subsequently bias mass estimates [Morandi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2567M/abstract), [Hlavacek-Larrondo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250619901H/abstract). The exact composition of this non-thermal sector is heavily contested: while theoretical models predict observable gamma-ray emission from hadronic cosmic ray interactions, observations from Fermi-LAT and H.E.S.S. have consistently failed to detect this emission. This ambiguity places stringent upper limits on the cosmic ray energy density at $<10\%$ of the total thermal energy, suggesting either lower cosmic ray acceleration efficiency or steeper particle spectra than initially predicted [Zimmer et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150202653Z/abstract), [Domainko et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009IJMPD..18.1627D/abstract). 

Furthermore, the *dark matter nature* and the exact distribution of mass within clusters continue to be tested. Triaxiality in the dark matter halo and the presence of complex substructures complicate dynamical mass modelling [Morandi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.416.2567M/abstract). High-precision measurements of the stacked phase-space density and gravitational redshift of cluster members are now being leveraged to test the Weak Equivalence Principle for dark matter and to constrain modified gravity models [Lam et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvL.109e1301L/abstract), [Di Dio et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260119861D/abstract). Additionally, while the ICM contains the bulk of cluster baryons, reconciling the total cluster mass budget with universal nucleosynthesis fractions raises questions regarding *missing baryons*, which are theorized to reside in the Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM) permeating the filamentary [[large-scale-structure-of-the-universe|Large-Scale Structure of the Universe]] at the cluster outskirts [Diaferio et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008SSRv..134....7D/abstract).

Addressing these open questions relies heavily on upcoming multi-wavelength observational campaigns. Next-generation all-sky X-ray surveys, such as *eROSITA*, will provide extensive catalogs of clusters and groups, securely establishing the statistical properties of the ICM across cosmic time [Mints et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...597A...2M/abstract). Concurrently, low-frequency radio arrays like the SKA and ngVLA will trace the evolution of magnetic fields in high-redshift [[protoclusters|Protoclusters]] [Hlavacek-Larrondo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250619901H/abstract), promising a unified picture of cluster assembly and non-thermal physics.
