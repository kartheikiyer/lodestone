**Fundamental astrophysical parameters** are the primary physical quantities required to characterize the state, structure, and evolution of celestial bodies. At the stellar level, these encompass a star's intrinsic mass, radius, [[effective-temperature|Effective temperature]], [[surface-gravity-stellar|Surface gravity (stellar)]], and [[metallicity|Metallicity]]. Together, these variables determine a star's emergent spectrum, internal physics, and placement on the [[hertzsprung-russell-diagram|Hertzsprung-Russell diagram]]. Historically, the derivation of these parameters relied heavily on theoretical mass-luminosity relations and the classical distance ladder, anchored by [[standard-candles|Standard candles]] such as [[cepheid-variables|Cepheid variables]] [Mo et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010gfe..book.....M/abstract). Today, the field has been revolutionized by large-scale space astrometry—most notably the [[gaia-mission-and-data-products|Gaia Mission and Data Products]]—and the advent of asteroseismology. These modern techniques allow for direct, high-precision [[mass-determination|Mass determination]] and atmospheric characterization across hundreds of millions of sources, drastically reducing our reliance on purely empirical models [Gaia Collaboration et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...616A...1G/abstract).

Beyond individual stars, these parameters scale to describe the macroscopic properties of galaxies and unresolved stellar systems. The fundamental parameters of these macro-structures are derived from their integrated light, [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]], and complex [[stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|Stellar and Galactic Dynamics]]. A cornerstone of [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]] is the Fundamental Plane of early-type galaxies, a tight multivariate correlation connecting an elliptical galaxy's effective radius, mean surface brightness, and central velocity dispersion. Deviations from the expected [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|Virial Theorem and Parameters]]—known as the Fundamental Plane "tilt"—remain a subject of active research, probing underlying variations in stellar populations, orbital anisotropy, and [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] [Magoulas et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..262..376M/abstract). Furthermore, the low [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] of such [[astrophysical-mass-scaling-relations|Astrophysical Mass Scaling Relations]] makes them robust distance indicators for mapping peculiar velocities and cosmic structure.

In a cosmological context, fundamental parameters define the origin, composition, and expansion history of the universe. These include the dimensionless [[omega-parameters|Omega parameters]]—which quantify the relative energy densities of [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]], dark matter, and dark energy—and the Hubble constant. Precise measurements of these global quantities rely on diverse [[cosmographic-probes|Cosmographic probes]] tracking the [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]] of structure [Freedman et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..673F/abstract). Simultaneously, the era of multi-messenger astrophysics has extended fundamental parameter inference to [[massive-compact-objects|Massive compact objects]]. For systems like binary black holes and neutron stars, dynamic parameters such as chirp masses, [[mass-ratios|Mass ratios]], and component spins are extracted directly from the phase and amplitude of gravitational-wave signals rather than from electromagnetic emission.

This article reviews the theoretical foundations and observational techniques used to derive fundamental astrophysical parameters across all scales. It traces the historical progression from early distance moduli to modern automated inference pipelines, exploring how tools like asteroseismology provide independent calibration via [[asteroseismic-ages|Asteroseismic ages]] and structural measurements. Finally, the article surveys current open questions and future frontiers. These include unresolved degeneracies between dark matter and baryonic physics, the ongoing debate over the tilt of the Fundamental Plane, challenges in testing modified gravity theories against traditional Newtonian frameworks, and the expected impact of next-generation multi-messenger observatories like LISA and Euclid in minimizing overall [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]].

## Fundamental Stellar Parameters and Atmospheric Characterization

The macroscopic state of a stellar atmosphere is principally defined by its [[effective-temperature|Effective temperature]] ($T_{\rm eff}$), [[surface-gravity-stellar|Surface gravity (stellar)]] (typically expressed as $\log g$), and overall [[metallicity|Metallicity]] ($Z$ or [M/H]). Together with the intrinsic stellar mass and stellar radius, these variables dictate the emergent spectrum of a star and its placement on the [[hertzsprung-russell-diagram|Hertzsprung-Russell diagram]]. Historically, the large-scale derivation of these quantities relied heavily on narrow-band spectroscopy or empirical color-temperature relations, which were often limited in sample size. To break these historical degeneracies in broad-band photometry for individual stars, algorithms utilizing interval-cluster analysis and Kurucz model fitting have been successfully employed to robustly estimate fundamental stellar astrophysical parameters [Belikov et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...489.1107B/abstract).

The advent of space-based astrometry and multi-band surveys has fundamentally shifted the acquisition of these parameters. The [[gaia-mission-and-data-products|Gaia Mission and Data Products]] currently constitute the most extensive homogeneous catalog of stellar parameters to date. Utilizing the Apsis inference pipeline, Gaia Data Release 3 provides $T_{\rm eff}$, $\log g$, and [M/H] for approximately 470 million sources, alongside derived radii for 470 million stars and masses for over 140 million stars [Creevey et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..26C/abstract) [Fouesneau et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..28F/abstract) [Gaia Collaboration et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A...1G/abstract). By combining astrometry, low-resolution BP/RP spectrophotometry, and the Radial Velocity Spectrometer (RVS), degeneracies between temperature, interstellar extinction, and metallicity can be effectively broken [Bailer-Jones et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...559A..74B/abstract) [Anders et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...628A..94A/abstract). Specifically, the Gaia RVS plays a vital role in deriving atmospheric parameters and abundances for stars brighter than $V \sim 15$, providing data that powerfully constrain internal stellar structure [Wilkinson et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.359.1306W/abstract). 

The internal [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] achievable with these automated pipelines is highly dependent on the parameter space, but can reach impressive precision: $\sim 100$-$245$ K in $T_{\rm eff}$, $\sim 0.25$ dex in $\log g$, and $\sim 0.2$ dex in metallicity for relatively bright, unreddened stars [Bailer-Jones et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...559A..74B/abstract) [Anders et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...628A..94A/abstract). Independent photometric algorithms applied to other surveys yield comparable precisions, such as temperature dispersions of $\sim 195$ K and metallicity dispersions of $\sim 0.31$ dex [Sun et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250323067S/abstract). 

Despite the unprecedented internal precision of pipeline-derived parameters, cross-calibrations reveal a persistent tension: absolute accuracy remains systematically tied to the underlying stellar atmosphere models and the rigorous cleaning of unreliable input data [Anders et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...628A..94A/abstract) [Fouesneau et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..28F/abstract). Furthermore, classical treatments dictate that intrinsic stellar mass must be inferred indirectly via [[evolutionary-tracks|Evolutionary tracks]] or empirical mass-luminosity relations. Consequently, robust parameter validation requires independent empirical benchmarks, a need powerfully fulfilled by modern asteroseismology [Casagrande et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..330..206C/abstract). 

These parameterization techniques also extend to stellar aggregates; for example, 2MASS-based studies of 263 open clusters have mapped the correlations between fundamental astrophysical parameters along the Milky Way Galaxy, providing homogeneous catalogs that constrain structural and evolutionary models [Tadross et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014JAsGe...3...88T/abstract). Furthermore, for the final evolutionary stage of approximately 95% of all stars, fundamental parameters manifest entirely differently. In white dwarf stars, parameters such as mass and radius are tightly constrained by the physics of degenerate matter, governed primarily by the Pauli exclusion principle and specific theoretical mass-radius relations [Koester et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013pss4.book..559K/abstract).

## Asteroseismology and the Determination of Stellar Ages

Historically, fundamental parameters such as stellar mass have not been considered direct observables for isolated stars. However, the advent of high-precision, space-based photometry from observatories like the Kepler and CoRoT satellites has established asteroseismology as a revolutionary, independent tool for direct [[mass-determination|Mass determination]] [Kallinger et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...522A...1K/abstract) [Silva Aguirre et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..298..375S/abstract).

Asteroseismology probes internal [[stellar-structure-and-activity|Stellar Structure and Activity]] by analyzing resonant acoustic oscillations. By fitting global models to observed power density spectra, researchers can extract key seismic parameters, most notably the frequency of maximum oscillation power ($\nu_{\rm max}$) and the large frequency separation ($\Delta\nu$) between consecutive modes of the same spherical degree. High-precision time-series data allow for the identification of regular patterns of radial and non-radial oscillation modes (e.g., spherical degrees $l = 0, 1,$ and $2$) while successfully isolating the stellar granulation background signal [Kallinger et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...522A...1K/abstract). Utilizing these global oscillation parameters in established seismic scaling relations yields accurate measurements of stellar mass and radius that are entirely independent of spectroscopy.

This technique has been particularly transformative for the study of red giants, which exhibit clear power excesses in frequency ranges typical for solar-type oscillations [Kallinger et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...522A...1K/abstract). Because the main-sequence lifetime of a star is tightly coupled to its initial mass, determining an accurate asteroseismic mass for an evolved red giant provides a highly constrained estimate of its age. Through Bayesian inference techniques, these [[asteroseismic-ages|Asteroseismic ages]], alongside masses and radii, can be determined with rigorous uncertainty estimates. This enables the precise placement of these stars on the [[hertzsprung-russell-diagram|Hertzsprung-Russell diagram]] and facilitates the study of complex evolutionary phases [Kallinger et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...522A...1K/abstract).

The application of asteroseismology to large samples of red giants has yielded major insights into the stellar populations of the Milky Way. Seismic data have allowed astronomers to map and date different regions of the Galactic disk, revealing distinct structures such as a secondary-clump population of red giants [Kallinger et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...522A...1K/abstract) [Silva Aguirre et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..298..375S/abstract). Furthermore, these measurements have identified spatial gradients in stellar properties, mapping how mass and [[metallicity|Metallicity]] vary within the Galactic red clump and across different vertical heights above the disk midplane [Kallinger et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...522A...1K/abstract) [Silva Aguirre et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..298..375S/abstract).

In the current era of large-scale astrometric surveys, asteroseismic targets serve as crucial [[benchmark-stars|Benchmark stars]]. The fundamental parameters derived from seismic scaling relations provide a robust anchor to validate the outputs of the [[gaia-mission-and-data-products|Gaia Mission and Data Products]] [Casagrande et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..330..206C/abstract). By precisely dating distant components of the Galactic disk and tracking [[metallicity|Metallicity]] gradients as a function of age, asteroseismology supplies unique empirical constraints well outside the solar neighborhood. These results fundamentally inform models of [[chemical-evolution-and-nucleosynthesis|Chemical Evolution and Nucleosynthesis]] and shed light on the high-redshift formation and assembly of the Milky Way [Silva Aguirre et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..298..375S/abstract) [Casagrande et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..330..206C/abstract). Building on these successes, there is a proposed shift toward massive, large-scale time-series spectroscopy programs designed to transition static Galactic maps into comprehensive age-labeled dynamical models [Gruner et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260110240G/abstract).

## Historical Development: From Standard Candles to Space Astrometry

Historically, the determination of fundamental astrophysical parameters relied heavily on geometric and photometric distance indicators. The classical distance ladder was built upon [[standard-candles|Standard candles]], the most notable being [[cepheid-variables|Cepheid variables]]. The tight correlation between a Cepheid's pulsation period and its intrinsic luminosity allowed for the derivation of distances through the [[distance-modulus|Distance modulus]], $\mu = m - M$. In the 1920s, the identification of Cepheids in the Andromeda Nebula by Edwin Hubble definitively proved the extragalactic nature of spiral nebulae, launching the field of [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]]. 

While Cepheids remain critical for local distances—yielding local measurements of the Hubble constant ($H_0 \approx 73$ km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$) [Freedman et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..673F/abstract) that exhibit ongoing tension with early-universe cosmic microwave background derivations—other standard candles extended the distance scale. Historical supernovae and their remnants [Stephenson et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ISAA....5.....S/abstract) provided early constraints on stellar death and the interstellar medium, while Type Ia supernovae eventually allowed cosmological distances to be probed. However, calibrating these photometric methods required accurate fundamental distances to nearby stars. Simultaneously, large-scale extragalactic databases like LEDA played a crucial role in homogenizing diverse astrophysical parameters—such as magnitudes, HI line widths, and velocity dispersions—into unified systems necessary for reliable extragalactic scaling relations [Paturel et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997A&AS..124..109P/abstract).

Ground-based measurements of trigonometric parallax are fundamentally limited by atmospheric seeing and instrumental flexure. The transition to space-based astrometry began in the late 1980s with the European Space Agency's Hipparcos satellite. Hipparcos improved positional accuracies by a factor of 100 compared to previous ground-based efforts, providing multi-epoch astrometric and photometric data for over 100,000 stars [Eyer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012IAUS..285..153E/abstract). This enabled the direct geometric calibration of the nearest standard candles and tightened constraints on theoretical stellar evolutionary models. Meanwhile, in the radio regime, Very Long Baseline Interferometry (VLBI) astrometry of Galactic masers has provided independent constraints on fundamental Galactic parameters, such as the distance to the Galactic center ($R_0 \approx 8.05$ kpc) and the Sun's angular rotation velocity [Honma et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PASJ...64..136H/abstract).

The true revolution in stellar parameterization arrived with the [[gaia-mission-and-data-products|Gaia Mission and Data Products]]. Gaia represents a leap of a factor of 50 to 100 in astrometric precision and a factor of 10,000 in source volume over Hipparcos [Eyer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012IAUS..285..153E/abstract). Operating at microarcsecond precision, Gaia's measurements require a fully relativistic framework, ushering in the era of "gravitational astrometry" where null geodesics in curved spacetime must be explicitly modeled [Crosta et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NCimR..42..443C/abstract). Ultimately, this expansive, uniform dataset has largely supplanted piecemeal ground-based calibrations, establishing a robust empirical foundation for Galactic structure studies and the calibration of extragalactic scaling relations.

## The Fundamental Plane and Galactic Scaling Relations

The Fundamental Plane (FP) is a tight, three-dimensional empirical scaling relation observed in early-type galaxies that connects their effective radius ($R_e$), mean surface brightness within the effective radius ($\langle I \rangle_e$), and central velocity dispersion ($\sigma$). In this parameter space, early-type galaxies do not populate the volume uniformly but instead collapse onto a plane described by the relation:
$$ \log R_e = a \log \sigma + b \log \langle I \rangle_e + c $$
Under the assumption of structural homology and a constant stellar mass-to-light ratio ($M/L$), the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|Virial Theorem and Parameters]] predicts coefficients of $a = 2$ and $b = -1$. However, observations consistently reveal a "tilt" in the FP relative to the virial expectation, a phenomenon that has prompted extensive theoretical and observational debate in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]]. Observations confirm that the Fundamental Plane remains robust at higher redshifts ($z \sim 0.3 - 0.9$), with systematic errors in structural parameters remaining surprisingly small despite widely varying Sérsic indices among cluster galaxies [Chiboucas et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJS..184..271C/abstract).

At optical and near-infrared wavelengths, the measured slope $a$ is significantly shallower than 2. For example, data from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey indicate that the orthogonal fit yields $a \approx 1.40$ in the $g$-band and $a \approx 1.47$ in the $z$-band, with an intrinsic scatter of $0.062$ dex and $0.054$ dex, respectively [Hyde et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.1171H/abstract). The physical origin of this FP tilt remains unresolved, with the literature primarily divided among three non-mutually exclusive explanations:
1. **Systematic non-homology:** Variations in the structural profiles of galaxies, such as a Sérsic index $n$ that correlates with luminosity, alter the dynamical structure independent of stellar mass [Bertin et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...386..149B/abstract).
2. **Variations in $M/L$ ratios:** A systematic increase in the total mass-to-light ratio with galaxy mass. This may be driven by a non-universal, bottom-heavy initial mass function in high-$\sigma$ galaxies, or by varying dark matter fractions [Hyde et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.1171H/abstract) [Mould et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1403.1623M/abstract).
3. **Orbital anisotropy:** Variations in radial orbital anisotropy and the requirements of dynamical stability can drive systems away from the isotropic virial expectation [Nipoti et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.332..901N/abstract).

Substituting luminosity for stellar mass yields a "stellar mass Fundamental Plane," which steepens the velocity dispersion dependence to $a \approx 1.54 - 1.63$ and reduces intrinsic scatter to $\sim 0.048$ dex [Hyde et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.1171H/abstract). Nevertheless, the stellar mass FP still deviates from virial predictions, largely because the ratio of [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] ($M_{\rm dyn}$) to stellar mass ($M_*$) is not constant, scaling approximately as $M_{\rm dyn}/M_* \propto M_{\rm dyn}^{0.17}$ [Hyde et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.1171H/abstract). The relationship between these parameters serves as one of the tightest [[astrophysical-mass-scaling-relations|Astrophysical Mass Scaling Relations]] available for measuring massive galaxies.

Beyond early-type galaxies, scaling relations manifest across diverse scales and morphologies. The Fundamental Plane appears to exhibit an underlying universality across vast mass scales, extending from clusters of galaxies down to globular clusters. At the smallest scales, nuclear star clusters located in galaxy centers share structural properties and positions on the Fundamental Plane that directly link their formation mechanisms to broader galaxy evolution [Böker et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..266...58B/abstract). Galactic globular clusters have been shown to occupy their own Fundamental Plane or a one-dimensional "Fundamental Line," with residuals from these relations potentially indicating underlying bimodal dynamical populations [Pasquato et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...489.1079P/abstract). Mass-to-light ratio variations across such systems strongly indicate underlying changes in dark matter distributions [Ibarra-Medel et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011RMxAC..40...64I/abstract). For rotation-supported systems, researchers have discovered a "Fundamental Line" for disk galaxies that tightly correlates metallicity, central surface brightness, and the dynamical mass-to-light ratio [Simon et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...649..709S/abstract). Furthermore, the classic Faber-Jackson law ($L \propto \sigma^4$)—which fundamentally relates luminosity to velocity dispersion—has been shown to equally apply to Type 1 Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN), exhibiting a similar slope to early-type galaxies despite their entirely different physical contexts [D'Onofrio et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Univ...10..254D/abstract). Extending this to higher redshifts, observations of radio galaxies reveal a distinct redshift evolution of the Fundamental Plane. For Fanaroff-Riley type II (FR-II) sources, the observed evolution in the FP "tilt" from $z \sim 0.5$ to the present day is potentially driven by fundamental size evolution in the host galaxies [Herbert et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.410.1360H/abstract). Furthermore, the role of halo mass and complex environmental selection effects is critical in driving the observed mass scalings and distinct populations within the Fundamental Plane of radio-selected AGNs [Shabala et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.5074S/abstract).

Advanced multivariate Gaussian modeling of the FP, successfully deployed in catalogs like the 6dF Galaxy Survey (6dFGS), allows for the accurate decoupling of observational errors and selection effects [Magoulas et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..262..376M/abstract). Such analyses have revealed that stellar population parameters (such as age and metallicity) vary primarily *through* and *across* the FP, but exhibit zero variation along the specific FP vector corresponding to "luminosity density," a direction closely tied to a galaxy's major merger history [Springob et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.420.2773S/abstract). To further provide an economical distance estimate without the need for resource-intensive spectroscopic velocity dispersions, researchers developed the Photometric Fundamental Plane (PFP). This relation bypasses the velocity dispersion entirely, replacing it with the difference between a galaxy's magnitude and the mode of the host cluster's luminosity function [Scodeggio et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997AJ....113.2087S/abstract).

Beyond probing [[stellar-and-galactic-dynamics|Stellar and Galactic Dynamics]], the low intrinsic scatter of the FP makes it a robust distance indicator for cosmological parameter estimation. The mapping of peculiar velocities for thousands of local galaxies in 6dFGS relies heavily on FP-derived distances [Campbell et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.1231C/abstract). More recently, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) Peculiar Velocity Survey applied the FP to over 6,600 early-type galaxies to constrain the Hubble constant. Utilizing zero-point calibrations from the Coma cluster, the DESI FP analysis yielded an independent measurement of $H_0 = 76.05 \pm 0.35 ({\rm stat}) \pm 0.49 ({\rm sys}) \pm 4.86 ({\rm cal})\, {\rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$, sitting within $2\sigma$ of cosmic microwave background results [Said et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240813842S/abstract).

## Chemical Abundances and Population Synthesis

The fundamental properties of stellar populations depend intricately on the rate of star formation and the chemical composition of the assembling gas. The mass fraction of elements heavier than helium is defined as the [[metallicity|Metallicity]] ($Z$), anchored by the solar value of $Z_\odot \approx 0.02$. Spectroscopic [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] are traditionally expressed logarithmically relative to the Sun:
$$[X/Y] = \log \left( \frac{N_X}{N_Y} \right)_* - \log \left( \frac{N_X}{N_Y} \right)_\odot$$
where $N$ represents the number density of specific elements. In the solar neighborhood, observations of the age-metallicity relation in long-lived G-dwarfs and the relative abundance of $\alpha$-elements (e.g., O/Fe) provide the principal constraints on [[chemical-evolution-and-nucleosynthesis|Chemical Evolution and Nucleosynthesis]]. To calibrate these Galactic chemical evolution models precisely, astronomers rely on large-scale compiled catalogs of specific $\alpha$-element abundances, such as magnesium, cross-matched with accurate stellar parallaxes [Borkova et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ARep...49..405B/abstract). Furthermore, probing distinct and highly obscured environments like the Galactic center requires specialized approaches; researchers have developed dedicated empirical infrared line lists to characterization fundamental abundances where optical spectroscopy fails [Thorsbro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018IAUS..334..372T/abstract). Large-scale surveys now provide homogeneous metallicity maps for millions of stars [Creevey et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...674A..26C/abstract), while asteroseismology reveals distinct mass and metallicity gradients across the Galactic disk [Silva Aguirre et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..298..375S/abstract).

The integrated light and chemical yield of a population are fundamentally driven by the initial mass function (IMF)—the mass spectrum of stars at birth. Observationally, the IMF cannot be measured directly for most populations. Instead, it is derived by constructing a [[stellar-luminosity-function|Stellar luminosity function]], $\Phi(M)$, from volume-limited stellar counts. This luminosity distribution is converted into a mass function using theoretical mass-luminosity relations $m(M)$ and corrected for the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]], $\psi(t)$, to account for stars born earlier that have already evolved into remnants. 

In extragalactic systems where individual stars cannot be resolved, the IMF and population parameters must be inferred from integrated light and scaling relations. Observations of early-type galaxies reveal strong correlations between their central velocity dispersion ($\sigma$) and structural stellar population parameters, such as age and metallicity [Porter et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.445.3092P/abstract). To facilitate these studies, massive datasets like RCSEDv2 have emerged as major modern spectroscopic resources, enabling the homogeneous analysis of kinematics, ages, and chemical composition across more than 4 million galaxy spectra [Goradzhanov et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ASPC..535..175G/abstract). However, the universality of the IMF across different galactic environments remains a subject of active debate. For instance, systematic variations in the stellar mass-to-light ratio of massive elliptical galaxies have been proposed as a primary source of the "tilt" in the Fundamental Plane, potentially indicating a non-universal, "bottom-heavy" IMF in high-velocity dispersion environments [Hyde et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.396.1171H/abstract) [Mould et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1403.1623M/abstract).

Furthermore, macroscopic metallicity scaling relations profoundly impact multi-messenger astrophysics. For instance, the specific choice between empirical models like the Fundamental Metallicity Relation versus the Mass-Metallicity Relation significantly alters the predicted merging rates and chirp mass distributions of double compact object binaries, highlighting the direct link between a population's chemical evolution and its gravitational wave output [Boco et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...907..110B/abstract).

## Cosmological Parameters and the Expansion of the Universe

The expansion history and global geometry of the universe are governed by a set of fundamental cosmological parameters within the framework of the standard cosmological model. These global parameters dictate the overall matter-energy content and thermal history of the universe across cosmic time. Measurements of these quantities rely on a diverse array of [[cosmographic-probes|Cosmographic probes]] that track the [[redshift-evolution-and-distribution|Redshift evolution and distribution]] of cosmic structure.

The Hubble constant, $H_0$, parametrizes the present-day expansion rate of the universe. Historically, $H_0$ has been constrained using empirical distance ladders calibrated by classical [[standard-candles|Standard candles]] such as Cepheid variables and Type Ia supernovae. Extensive surveys utilizing these high-precision local distance indicators typically yield an expansion rate of $H_0 \approx 73 \pm 2 \text{ (random)} \pm 4 \text{ (systematic)} \text{ km s}^{-1}\text{ Mpc}^{-1}$ [Freedman et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ARA&A..48..673F/abstract). However, a widely recognized tension exists between these late-universe measurements and the lower values inferred from early-universe cosmic microwave background (CMB) data, driven in part by lingering systematic errors in the distance ladder. To circumvent these systematics, novel cosmographic methods have emerged. For example, utilizing the fundamental plane of early-type galaxies from the DESI Peculiar Velocity Survey—modeled with a 3D Gaussian maximum likelihood algorithm to handle selection effects—provides an independent, low-redshift constraint of $H_0 = 76.05 \pm 0.35 \text{ (stat)} \pm 0.49 \text{ (sys)} \pm 4.86 \text{ (cal)} \text{ km s}^{-1}\text{ Mpc}^{-1}$, falling within $2\sigma$ of the Planck CMB results [Said et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240813842S/abstract). Additionally, multi-messenger "standard sirens" like binary neutron star mergers offer a direct, purely gravitational measurement of luminosity distance, providing an independent constraint on $H_0$ without requiring intermediate astrophysical calibration [Mastrogiovanni et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AnP...53600180M/abstract). Furthermore, galaxy cluster abundance and the ratios of metric potentials serve as crucial astrophysical tests of modified gravity models, particularly those that include complex screening mechanisms to replicate General Relativity on local scales [Jain et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AnPhy.325.1479J/abstract).

Beyond distance measurements, the intrinsic properties of scaling relations offer novel cosmographic probes. For instance, the residuals of the Fundamental Plane can serve as a robust anchor for measuring weak-lensing magnification. This framework allows cosmologists to simultaneously constrain the foreground mass distribution of galaxy clusters and correct for background redshift errors, mitigating a major source of systematic uncertainty in large-scale structural surveys [Freudenburg et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.496.2998F/abstract). Additionally, gravitational lensing provides independent constraints on galactic structure and cosmological parameters by probing mass distributions through the bulge and halo of the Milky Way and beyond [Narayan et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998NewAR..42...73N/abstract).

The large-scale geometry and ultimate fate of the universe are determined by the [[omega-parameters|Omega parameters]] ($\Omega_m$, $\Omega_\Lambda$, $\Omega_r$), which quantify the fractional energy densities of matter, dark energy, and radiation, respectively. While the cosmological constant ($\Lambda$) is generally assumed to have a negligible impact on local astrophysical scales, recent theoretical investigations indicate that it can qualitatively alter dynamics by establishing outermost stable circular orbits (OSCOs) on the physical scale of galaxy clusters [Boonserm et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.101b4050B/abstract). 

Accurately inferring these cosmological parameters requires tightly constrained astrophysical models, as degeneracies frequently arise between global cosmology and local structure formation. For example, attempting to extract cosmological and astrophysical insights from the 21-cm signal during the epoch of reionization is highly sensitive to the assumed dark matter halo-mass function. Analyses have demonstrated that relying on an incorrect or fixed halo-mass function can significantly bias the inference of fundamental astrophysical parameters by up to $3-4\sigma$, highlighting the critical need to simultaneously model underlying cosmological parameters and local galaxy evolution [Greig et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.2502G/abstract). To tackle this complex parameter space, advanced inference packages like ECHO21 have been developed to explore the cosmos via the 21-cm signal. Such frameworks allow for the joint determination of localized astrophysical parameters—like Lyman-alpha and X-ray emissivity—alongside the standard cosmological parameters [Mittal et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250311762M/abstract).

## Multi-messenger and Dynamic Parameters of Compact Objects

The advent of gravitational wave astronomy has introduced a novel regime for deriving the fundamental parameters of compact astrophysical systems. For merging systems, traditional [[binary-star-parameters|Binary star parameters]] are supplemented or replaced by quantities directly encoded in the phase and amplitude of the inspiral waveform. Chief among these are the chirp mass, [[mass-ratios|Mass ratios]], and the individual spin vectors of the components, notably black hole spin. The dynamics of precessing black-hole binaries in the post-Newtonian regime directly link the evolution of spin cycles during the inspiral to their underlying astrophysical formation histories [Gerosa et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PhRvD..93l4066G/abstract). 

Extracting these dynamic parameters requires high-dimensional Bayesian inference models. While complete relativistic models including spin-precession and higher-order multipole moments provide the most accurate description of the signals, the literature exhibits a practical tension regarding their absolute necessity for population-level inferences. Recent studies suggest that for current and near-future event counts, computationally cheaper models lacking full precession effects yield comparable constraints on cosmological parameters, reducing computational costs significantly without compromising the accuracy of the inferred posteriors [Hoy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251107551H/abstract). Conversely, for future space-based detectors, extreme mass-ratio inspirals (XMRIs)—such as a brown dwarf orbiting a massive black hole—will allow the brown dwarf mass to be constrained to an accuracy of $10^{-1}$, while modifications to General Relativity (such as the Chern-Simons parameter $\zeta$) can be constrained to extreme accuracies between $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-6}$ depending on the orbital eccentricity [Fan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251108221F/abstract).

Compact object mergers also serve as independent [[cosmographic-probes|Cosmographic probes]]. As "standard sirens," the gravitational wave signature provides a direct, calibration-independent measurement of the luminosity distance $d_L$ [Mastrogiovanni et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AnP...53600180M/abstract). By combining individual source inferences using the mass spectrum method, parameters like the Hubble constant ($H_0$) can be constrained even in the absence of direct electromagnetic counterparts [Mastrogiovanni et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024AnP...53600180M/abstract) [Hoy et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251107551H/abstract). 

Beyond transient merger events, the dynamic parameters of compact objects are derived continuously through the observation of radio pulsars. Acting as highly precise cosmic clocks, pulsars in binary systems provide an indispensable laboratory for testing gravity and deriving orbital parameters such as orbital eccentricity, [[semimajor-axis|Semimajor axis]], and companion masses [Kaspi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv160207738K/abstract). The precision of these timing models strongly constrains theories of stellar and binary evolution. Furthermore, ensembles of these clocks—pulsar timing arrays—act as galactic-scale "antennae." Correlated timing residuals across these arrays have successfully identified the stochastic gravitational wave background [Rini et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PhyOJ..16..118R/abstract), opening a new window onto the low-frequency dynamical processes of supermassive black hole binaries. Hierarchical Bayesian modeling of these multi-messenger populations, accounting for highly correlated parameter spaces, is increasingly utilized to characterize the distributions and scale radii of galactic binaries [Adams et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvD..86l4032A/abstract).

While traditional "compact objects" usually refer to stellar remnants, structural parameterization also extends down to planetary mass scales. To define planets quantitatively, researchers have proposed a "Fundamental Plane of Planets." Spanning a mass range from roughly 0.02 Earth masses to 13 Jupiter masses, this relation tightly correlates a planet's mass, radius, and moment of inertia, offering a unified structural framework that complements the scaling relations seen in more massive astrophysical bodies [Kashyap Jagadeesh et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250616063K/abstract). 

At the completely opposite extreme of the mass and size spectrum, comprehensive multi-wavelength parameterization is equally critical for understanding giant radio sources (GRSs) [Bhukta et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220112353B/abstract). Representing the largest known single astrophysical objects in the universe, GRSs require parameterization across the radio, optical, and infrared regimes to fully characterize their host galaxies, enormous physical extents, and the complex energetics of their jet environments [Bhukta et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220112353B/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Frontiers in Parameter Inference

While modern surveys have drastically improved the precision of fundamental astrophysical parameters, significant theoretical and observational challenges remain. A primary frontier involves breaking degeneracies between baryonic physics, dark matter distributions, and fundamental cosmological models. 

Despite advances in [[extragalactic-astronomy|Extragalactic astronomy]], deriving [[dynamical-mass|Dynamical mass]] and structural parameters for early-type galaxies remains entangled with uncertainties in stellar populations. A persistent unresolved question is the physical origin of the "tilt" in the Fundamental Plane, with debate continuing over whether the departure is driven by structural non-homology, mass-dependent variations in the stellar mass-to-light ratio (such as a bottom-heavy initial mass function), or orbital anisotropy [Mould et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1403.1623M/abstract) [Bertin et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002A&A...386..149B/abstract) [Nipoti et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002MNRAS.332..901N/abstract). Complicating this structural non-homology is the strong model-dependence of parameter extraction itself; for instance, the specific choice of the Sérsic index $n$ dramatically affects derived half-light radii, creating an intrinsic mathematical coupling between the effective radius and surface brightness that can artificially skew Fundamental Plane fits [Trujillo et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001MNRAS.326..869T/abstract). These degeneracies propagate to cosmological scales; for example, uncertainties in the halo-mass function can bias the inference of astrophysical parameters during the reionization epoch by up to $3-4\sigma$ [Greig et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.533.2502G/abstract).

Efforts to bypass dark matter entirely via modified gravity theories face significant challenges. While some frameworks like Modified Gravity (MOG) seek parameter-free solutions [Moffat et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009CQGra..26h5002M/abstract), tests of alternatives such as Refracted Gravity (RG) reveal a lack of parameter universality. Kinematic analyses of massive clusters indicate that RG requires a uniquely tuned set of free parameters for each cluster, resulting in a slight statistical preference for Newtonian gravity coupled with dark matter [Pizzuti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241019698P/abstract). Similarly, theoretical constraints on effective parameters—such as the ratio of the two metric potentials—are continuously used to rigorously interpret astrophysical observations against competing modified gravity theories [Jain et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AnPhy.325.1479J/abstract). Furthermore, classical parameterizations often assume the cosmological constant ($\Lambda$) is negligible on local astrophysical scales; however, models indicate that a positive $\Lambda$ qualitatively alters cluster-scale dynamics by introducing Outermost Stable Circular Orbits (OSCOs) [Boonserm et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PhRvD.101b4050B/abstract).

To combat [[measurement-uncertainty|Measurement uncertainty]] and uncover complex correlations, parameter inference is increasingly relying on high-dimensional data analysis. Machine learning algorithms have proven highly effective in reducing the [[intrinsic-scatter|Intrinsic scatter]] of empirical scaling relations. For example, machine learning frameworks applied to Sunyaev-Zeldovich flux-mass data can identify non-linear, multi-variable extensions to standard [[observable-mass-scaling-relations|Observable-mass scaling relations]], producing substantially more robust mass estimates for galaxy clusters [Wadekar et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PNAS..12002074W/abstract). This methodological shift is a key component of the growing importance of astrostatistics in the 21st century. Bayesian inference and likelihood-free computational methods are increasingly essential to correctly handle heteroscedastic measurement errors and complex selection biases across large, heterogeneous cosmic populations [Feigelson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AnRSA...8..493F/abstract).

The next decade of parameter inference will be driven by next-generation space missions, including the electromagnetic Euclid observatory and the gravitational-wave observatory LISA. Euclid will tightly constrain dark energy and dark matter profiles via weak lensing and galaxy clustering. However, extracting unbiased cosmological parameters from these data will require highly accurate, multi-probe analytical frameworks capable of modeling complex astrophysical feedback mechanisms [Amendola et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013LRR....16....6A/abstract) [Pranjal R. et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250713317P/abstract). Concurrently, the space-based gravitational wave observatory LISA will offer parameter constraints completely independent of the electromagnetic distance ladder. By resolving thousands of short-period binaries, LISA is projected to constrain galactic disk radii and chirp mass distributions to within a few percent, vastly outperforming current $\sim 20\%$ limits from optical surveys [Adams et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012PhRvD..86l4032A/abstract). Furthermore, Extreme Mass-Ratio Inspirals (XMRIs) observed by LISA will serve as precision laboratories for fundamental physics, potentially constraining deviations from General Relativity—such as the Chern-Simons parameter—to an accuracy of $10^{-6}$ [Fan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251108221F/abstract). Moreover, as the empirical parameterization of exoplanetary systems rapidly expands, the search for "superhabitable" planets—worlds potentially better suited for life than Earth—will involve optimizing complex ranges within the astrophysical parameter space of star-planet systems, carefully balancing stellar age, stellar mass, and planetary habitability history [Schulze-Makuch et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AsBio..20.1394S/abstract).
