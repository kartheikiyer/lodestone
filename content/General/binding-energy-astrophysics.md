In astrophysics, gravitational binding energy is the minimum energy required to completely disassemble a [[self-gravitating-systems|self-gravitating system]]—such as a star, molecular cloud, or galaxy—and disperse its constituent mass to infinity. Because gravity is an unshielded, long-range force, self-gravitating structures are characterized by a negative heat capacity and lack a stable, extensive thermodynamic equilibrium [Bartelmann et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013SSRv..177....3B/abstract). Instead, the structural stability of these systems is governed by the delicate balance between their total internal kinetic energy and their gravitational potential energy, a relationship formalized by the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|Virial Theorem and Parameters]]. The continuous extraction and dissipation of this binding energy dictate the structural evolution of the universe, ranging from stellar and interstellar medium dynamics [Longair et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994hea..book.....L/abstract) to the catastrophic [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] that forms supermassive black holes [Colgate et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001PhPl....8.2425C/abstract), [Merritt et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013degn.book.....M/abstract).

The concept of binding energy permeates multiple scales of astrophysical inquiry. Within the interstellar medium, local binding criteria dictate whether density perturbations in giant molecular clouds will succumb to [[self-gravity|Self-gravity]] and form stars, or whether they will be dispersed by external tidal stresses and stellar feedback [Hopkins et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.432.2647H/abstract), [Ramírez-Galeano et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515.2822R/abstract). On galactic and cosmological scales, the assembly of massive structures is governed by [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]]. Rather than reaching a classical thermodynamic equilibrium through two-body collisions, dark matter halos and stellar systems achieve a quasi-stationary state via [[violent-relaxation|Violent relaxation]] [White et al. (1977)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977ComAp...7...95W/abstract). Because standard Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics fail to capture the ubiquitous instability inherent to long-range gravitational interactions, modern astrophysical theory frequently employs generalized, non-extensive statistical mechanics (such as Tsallis statistics) to describe galaxy clustering distributions [Nakamichi et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph..4004N/abstract), while localized quasi-equilibrium thermodynamics is used to derive the equation of state for the cosmological many-body problem [Saslaw et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...460...16S/abstract).

Despite its foundational role, the exact accounting of binding energy in large-scale structures remains one of the most profound open questions in modern astrophysics. Kinematic observations consistently demonstrate that the dynamical mass required to bind galaxies and clusters far exceeds their luminous baryonic [[enclosed-mass|Enclosed mass]]. This "missing mass" problem has driven a major theoretical schism between the standard dark matter paradigm—which posits that undetected collisionless particles deepen the [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]]—and alternative frameworks, such as Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) or Modified Gravity (MOG), which propose fundamental alterations to [[newtonian-dynamics|Newtonian dynamics]] at low accelerations [Trippe et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ZNatA..69..173T/abstract), [Moffat et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161006909M/abstract). Furthermore, ongoing research continues to probe how the accelerating expansion of the universe (dark energy) competes with local binding energy to dictate the ultimate fate of [[gravitationally-bound-structures|Gravitationally bound structures]] [Benisty et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250403009B/abstract), while high-energy astrophysicists seek to uncover the exact physical mechanisms—such as relativistic "cannonballs"—responsible for the explosive release of binding energy in supernovae and the origins of super-high-energy cosmic rays [de Rújula et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IJMPA..20.6562D/abstract), [Ginzburg et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996PhyU...39..155G/abstract).

## Fundamental Principles and the Virial Theorem

Gravitational binding energy is the energy required to disassemble a system of interacting masses into a state of infinite separation. In astrophysics, it is the fundamental quantity governing the stability, structure, and evolution of [[self-gravitating-systems|Self-gravitating systems]]. The relationship between the system's total kinetic energy, $K$, and its total gravitational potential energy, $U$, is formalized by the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|Virial Theorem and Parameters]]. For an isolated system in a steady, dynamical equilibrium, the theorem dictates that:
$$ 2K + U = 0 $$

Because gravity is an unshielded, long-range force, these systems possess a negative heat capacity and lack a stable, globally extensive thermodynamic equilibrium [Bartelmann et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013SSRv..177....3B/abstract). Consequently, the classical virial theorem is often supplemented by generalized thermodynamic frameworks and local binding criteria to comprehensively assess structural stability across different scales, grounded in the foundational principles of classical orbital mechanics and statistical thermodynamics [Duric et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003adas.book.....D/abstract).

In practice, the balance between kinetic and binding energy is observationally and numerically quantified using the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|Virial Theorem and Parameters]], specifically the classical virial parameter $\alpha_{\rm vir}^{\rm class} \propto K/|U|$. In numerical models of galaxy evolution, local binding criteria based on this parameter are highly effective at modeling [[self-gravity|Self-gravity]]; they adaptively select local overdensities and naturally predict clustered star formation without relying on arbitrary, resolution-dependent density thresholds [Hopkins et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.432.2647H/abstract).

However, there is significant tension in the literature regarding the general applicability of classical virial estimators, as they often assume isolated, spherical, and constant-density conditions. In the study of molecular clouds, $\alpha_{\rm vir}^{\rm class}$ frequently yields values greater than $2$, erroneously suggesting that the vast majority of clouds are unbound and supported by turbulent motions. When a full virial parameter ($\alpha_{\rm vir}^{\rm full}$) is applied—one that properly accounts for external tidal stresses—approximately 50% of the total molecular cloud population is revealed to be strictly gravitationally bound, while another 20% is gravitationally dominated but actively undergoing tidal disruption from surrounding molecular cloud complexes [Ramírez-Galeano et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515.2822R/abstract). 

Similar discrepancies appear on cosmological scales, where standard virial mass estimators can significantly underpredict the true binding mass of a system. For example, fundamental plane distance estimates of the merging double cluster Abell 2626 indicate that the actual total binding mass is at least $1.65$ times higher than the sum of the subclusters' standard virial masses, suggesting a severe bias in the estimator or the presence of substantial mass extending far beyond the conventionally virialized core [Mohr et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997AJ....114...25M/abstract).

## Historical Development of Gravithermodynamics

The application of thermodynamic principles to astrophysics faced early theoretical hurdles because large-scale [[self-gravitating-systems|Self-gravitating systems]] lack a stable equilibrium state due to their negative heat capacity. Standard [[newtonian-dynamics|Newtonian dynamics]] and classical Gibbs-Boltzmann statistics could not fully capture the macroscopic behavior of systems dominated by long-range gravitational forces. 

To resolve these physical discrepancies, the mid-20th century saw the emergence of 'gravithermodynamics'—an attempt to build a generalized statistical mechanics for gravitationally interacting bodies. A key milestone in historical astrophysics was the development of the statistical mechanics of 'violent agitation', formulated to describe rapidly expanding or collapsing systems [Saslaw et al. (1969)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1969MNRAS.143..437S/abstract). This theoretical framework generalized standard formulations, subsuming Fermi-Dirac, Maxwell-Boltzmann, and Lynden-Bell statistics as special cases. In this model, the physical [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] of a collisional system is mathematically mimicked as a condensation in phase space, akin to the behavior of a degenerate gas [Saslaw et al. (1969)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1969MNRAS.143..437S/abstract).

For a self-gravitating system to reach the quasi-equilibrium states described by these early gravithermodynamic models, it must rapidly "forget" its initial conditions. It was theoretically established that such collisionless systems mix on a macroscopic timescale $\tau \sim \bar{F}/\bar{v}$ (where $\bar{F}$ is a typical fluctuating force per unit mass and $\bar{v}$ is a characteristic velocity), which is significantly shorter than the standard two-body [[relaxation-time|Relaxation time]] [Kandrup et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...366..384K/abstract). This mixing paradigm laid the theoretical groundwork for modern approaches to [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]] and the statistical mechanics of non-extensive systems.

## Binding Energy in Star Formation and Molecular Clouds

The stability of Giant Molecular Clouds (GMCs) and their capacity to form stars are fundamentally governed by the balance between internal kinetic energy, [[self-gravity|Self-gravity]], and external environmental forces. Historically, this energy balance has been evaluated using the classical virial parameter, $\alpha_{\rm vir}^{\rm class}$, an observational proxy defined by the ratio of kinetic energy to gravitational binding energy:

$$ \alpha_{\rm vir}^{\rm class} \propto \frac{E_{\rm kin}}{|E_{\rm grav}|} $$

Evaluations relying on $\alpha_{\rm vir}^{\rm class}$ intrinsically assume that molecular clouds are isolated, spherical, and of constant density. Under these simplified assumptions, observational data frequently suggest that a substantial population of molecular clouds are gravitationally unbound ($\alpha_{\rm vir}^{\rm class} > 2$) and dominated by internal turbulent motions [Ramírez-Galeano et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515.2822R/abstract). 

However, this classical formulation neglects the complex environments of GMCs. Molecular clouds are embedded structures subject to compressive and disruptive tidal forces, exhibiting irregular shapes and substantial substructure. A more rigorous assessment requires the full virial parameter, $\alpha_{\rm vir}^{\rm full}$, which incorporates both [[self-gravity|Self-gravity]] and external tidal stresses, allowing it to take negative (self-gravity dominated) or positive (tide dominated) values. When these full gravitational energy considerations are applied, the dynamical picture shifts considerably: approximately 50% of the molecular cloud population is found to be gravitationally bound. An additional 20% are gravitationally dominated but are actively being torn apart by tidal forces originating from their parent molecular cloud complexes or nearby young stellar structures, rather than large-scale galactic features [Ramírez-Galeano et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515.2822R/abstract).

In numerical simulations of galaxy evolution, accurately modeling star formation requires identifying these gravitationally bound regions. Early models often utilized fixed density thresholds to trigger star formation; however, these thresholds are resolution-dependent and physically fail in high-density environments like starburst galaxies [Hopkins et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.432.2647H/abstract). Instead, applying a local [[self-gravity|Self-gravity]] criterion—based on the local virial parameter and the requirement that gas collapses within a single [[free-fall-time|Free-fall time]]—proves to be highly adaptive. This binding energy criterion naturally selects local overdensities independent of the global galaxy model and automatically predicts the highly clustered nature of star formation [Hopkins et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.432.2647H/abstract). To accurately evaluate this balance, modern large eddy simulations (LES) frequently deploy explicit subgrid-scale models to consistently compute the turbulent velocity dispersion, thereby parameterizing the star formation efficiency driven by local turbulence and binding energy [Schmidt-Brückner et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250906801S/abstract).

Despite the necessity of local gravitational binding to initiate gas fragmentation, the global star formation efficiency is not primarily dictated by the specific small-scale collapse criteria. There is a strong consensus in the literature that integrated star formation rates are overwhelmingly regulated by stellar feedback mechanisms, such as supernovae, radiation pressure, and stellar winds. In the absence of feedback to unbind gas and halt runaway [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]], simulations yield star formation rates orders of magnitude higher than observed. When feedback is present, the galaxy-wide star formation rate aligns with the Kennicutt relation, largely independent of the specific local binding or star formation law applied [Hopkins et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.432.2647H/abstract). Furthermore, when assessing the total mass and binding energy of these structures on a galactic scale, researchers must account for variations in stellar populations. The Integrated Galactic Initial Mass Function (IGIMF) theory suggests that the galaxy-wide IMF is steeper than the canonical IMF found in individual star clusters, fundamentally affecting total binding mass estimates and the scaling of stellar feedback [Pflamm-Altenburg et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..440..269P/abstract).

## High-Energy Phenomena and Compact Object Formation

The most extreme manifestations of gravitational collapse in the universe occur when macroscopic systems transition abruptly into highly compact objects, resulting in the catastrophic release of binding energy. The release of binding energy in these environments is often studied using hard X-ray imaging spectroscopy, which utilizes mosaiced semiconductor arrays (such as CdZnTe) to perform background-limited surveys of transient phenomena in both galactic and extragalactic fields [Rothschild et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995SPIE.2518...13R/abstract).

On stellar scales, the [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] of a massive star represents a sudden, dramatic transition to a more tightly bound quantum state (such as a neutron star or stellar-mass black hole). The resulting supernova explosion releases a prodigious amount of energy, typically on the order of $\sim 10^{51}$ ergs [Burrows et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000Natur.403..727B/abstract). This intense energy release not only drives the chemical enrichment of the interstellar medium but also injects substantial momentum that can regulate star formation and shape the macroscopic evolution of the host galaxy [Burrows et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000Natur.403..727B/abstract). Furthermore, the shock waves generated by this process are the primary drivers of standard galactic cosmic rays [Ginzburg et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996PhyU...39..155G/abstract). However, the literature is currently unresolved regarding the exact mechanics of the central engine in the most extreme cases; for instance, while "cannonball" models propose that catastrophic accretion onto the newly formed compact object emits relativistic plasmoids—potentially unifying supernovae, gamma-ray bursts, and cosmic rays—a definitive theoretical understanding of this accretion "cannon" remains elusive [de Rújula et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005IJMPA..20.6562D/abstract). Similarly, the origin of super-high-energy cosmic rays remains an open question in astrophysics [Ginzburg et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996PhyU...39..155G/abstract).

On macroscopic scales, the deepest [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]] wells are found in galactic nuclei, which host supermassive black holes (SMBHs). The formation and subsequent growth of these massive compact objects involve the extraction of immense amounts of gravitational binding energy. The energy of formation for a typical SMBH of mass $\sim 10^8 M_\odot$ is on the order of $10^{61}$ ergs [Colgate et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001PhPl....8.2425C/abstract). The continuous [[energy-dissipation|Energy dissipation]] during an active galactic nucleus (AGN) or quasar phase not only powers extraordinary photon luminosities but is also hypothesized to generate the vast [[magnetic-energy-density|Magnetic energy density]] observed in the intergalactic medium of galaxy clusters. Dynamo processes operating within the accretion disk can redistribute up to $\sim 10^{59} - 10^{60}$ ergs of magnetic energy into coherent, ordered regions spanning $50 - 100$ kpc across the cluster environment [Colgate et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001PhPl....8.2425C/abstract).

The accumulation of this binding mass is intimately linked to the structural properties of the host galaxy. Recent causal discovery analyses indicate that the central [[mass-density|Mass density]] and velocity dispersion of the host galaxy causally drive the final mass of the supermassive black hole [Pasquato et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231115160P/abstract). The dynamics within these dense nuclear environments are heavily influenced by [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]] and multi-body gravitational encounters. These processes govern the continuous extraction of binding energy through phenomena such as stellar tidal disruptions, loss-cone depopulation, and the orbital decay of binary supermassive black holes, which ultimately culminates in the emission of gravitational waves [Merritt et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013degn.book.....M/abstract).

## Internal Structure of Galaxies and Clusters

The internal structure of galaxies and galaxy clusters is primarily governed by [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]] and the thermodynamics of [[self-gravitating-systems|Self-gravitating systems]]. Because such systems possess a negative heat capacity, they lack a strictly stable equilibrium state; nevertheless, numerical simulations consistently produce quasi-universal [[density-profiles|Density profiles]] [Bartelmann et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013SSRv..177....3B/abstract). These profiles are typically characterized by a relatively flat central region within a scale radius $r_s$, which gradually steepens outward to asymptotically approach a logarithmic slope of approximately $-3$ near the virial radius $R_{\rm vir}$. 

Understanding the complex assembly of these structures relies on diverse observational techniques. On a localized scale, the concept of 'near-field cosmology' utilizes fossil evidence from individual stellar populations within the Milky Way to provide crucial clues about the dissipative processes and the history of binding energy extraction that shaped our Galaxy [Freeman et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ARA&A..40..487F/abstract). Across broader extragalactic populations, analyzing a galaxy's Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) serves as an essential tool to reconstruct its integrated evolutionary history and the underlying physical processes driven by binding energy [Iyer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250217680I/abstract).

A fundamental parameter in classifying these structures is the concentration, defined as the ratio between the virial radius and the scale radius, $c = R_{\rm vir} / r_s$. Theoretical predictions from Cold Dark Matter (CDM) simulations indicate that concentration is closely related to halo mass and redshift, generally yielding low concentration values for massive, cluster-sized halos. However, this remains a point of tension in the literature, as observations of physical clusters often reveal substantially higher concentrations than those predicted by standard CDM frameworks [Bartelmann et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013SSRv..177....3B/abstract).

Estimating the total binding mass of these large-scale structures relies heavily on the [[virial-theorem-and-parameters|Virial Theorem and Parameters]], but standard virial mass estimators can be systematically biased. In merging systems, classical virial estimates may significantly underestimate the true dynamical mass. For instance, fundamental plane distance estimates and kinematic modeling of the double cluster Abell 2626 indicate that the minimum total binding mass is at least $1.65$ times higher than the sum of the standard virial masses of its subclusters [Mohr et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997AJ....114...25M/abstract). Such discrepancies imply either that significant mass resides far beyond the virialized core, or that the cluster possesses an unusually high mass-to-light ratio of approximately $1000h$ [Mohr et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997AJ....114...25M/abstract). Furthermore, applying strict binding energy criteria to identify compact galaxy groups in simulations is often deemed inapplicable due to the complex spatial segregation between luminous galaxies and dark matter particles [Díaz-Giménez et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.409.1227D/abstract).

The extreme mass-to-light ratios required to bind galaxies and clusters under standard [[newtonian-dynamics|Newtonian dynamics]] form the crux of the missing mass problem. While the dominant paradigm attributes this discrepancy to undetected dark matter, there is an active debate regarding the fundamental nature of gravity at low accelerations. Observations reveal a universal mass-discrepancy acceleration relation (characterized by Milgrom's constant) that standard dark matter models cannot fully explain, as the mass discrepancy is a function of gravitational acceleration rather than galaxy size or rotation speed [Trippe et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ZNatA..69..173T/abstract), [Moffat et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161006909M/abstract). Consequently, modified gravity theories—such as Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), Modified Gravity (MOG), and the $\kappa$-model—have been advanced as alternative explanations that can successfully fit galaxy rotation curves and cluster merger dynamics using only the distribution of baryonic matter [Moffat et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150505869M/abstract), [Moffat et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161006909M/abstract), [Pascoli et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Ap&SS.367...62P/abstract). The literature currently remains unresolved between the necessity of elusive dark matter particles and the potential need for a modified gravitational law [Trippe et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ZNatA..69..173T/abstract).

## Statistical Mechanics of Long-Range Systems

The long-range nature of gravity fundamentally alters the thermodynamic behavior of [[self-gravitating-systems|Self-gravitating systems]]. In standard Boltzmann-Gibbs statistical mechanics, systems are assumed to be extensive, meaning their entropy and energy scale linearly with the number of particles. However, because gravity is an unshielded $1/r^2$ interaction, self-gravitating systems exhibit non-extensivity, negative heat capacity, and ubiquitous [[gravitational-instability|Gravitational instability]] [Nakamichi et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph..4004N/abstract), [Bartelmann et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013SSRv..177....3B/abstract). 

To rigorously describe the statistical mechanics of such non-extensive systems, researchers employ generalized frameworks like Tsallis statistical mechanics. By modifying the standard definition of entropy, Tsallis statistics mathematically accommodates the non-additivity inherent in long-range interactions. When applied to the spatial distribution of galaxies—such as in count-in-cell analyses of the CfA redshift survey—this generalized entropy yields a negative [[entropic-parameter|Entropic parameter]], $q$ [Nakamichi et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph..4004N/abstract), [Nakamichi et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002CSF....13..595N/abstract). A negative value for $q$ provides a formal thermodynamic representation of the pervasive clustering that characterizes these systems.

Despite the absence of true global equilibrium, a localized thermodynamic description remains highly effective for modeling the cosmological many-body problem. This approach treats the local fluctuating part of the gravitational field as a many-body system, making a quasi-equilibrium description physically applicable over finite regions [Saslaw et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...460...16S/abstract). This framework successfully derives the functional form of the parameter $b(n, T)$, which represents the ratio of gravitational correlation energy to twice the kinetic energy of peculiar velocities, providing a complete equation of state without relying on empirical ansätze [Saslaw et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...460...16S/abstract).

Analytically derived spatial clustering properties from these thermodynamic models closely match N-body simulations and large-scale survey data. For instance, the theory naturally predicts both the power-law scaling of the two-point correlation function and large-scale periodic oscillations ($\sim 120 \text{ Mpc}$) in cluster distributions [Zhang et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1408.5237Z/abstract). Extensions of this thermodynamic framework to third-order galaxy correlation functions show excellent quantitative agreement with observational data from SDSS and BOSS surveys, offering independent constraints on dark energy and modified gravity that complement standard two-point analyses [Choudhary et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250623829C/abstract).

However, the literature highlights strict limitations to the single-component quasi-equilibrium assumption. While thermodynamic theory accurately models systems with moderate [[mass-ratios|Mass ratios]] (up to $m_2/m_1 \sim 10$), standard formulations break down for extreme mass ratios (e.g., $m_2/m_1 \gtrsim 15$). In these regimes, individual massive galaxies act as non-linear nuclei that accelerate clustering during early stages, violating the collective interaction assumptions that underpin single-component [[equilibrium-models|Equilibrium models]] [Itoh et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ApJ...356..315I/abstract).

## Collisionless Dynamics and Relaxation Mechanisms

Astrophysical objects such as galaxies and dark matter halos are typically modeled as [[self-gravitating-systems|Self-gravitating systems]] operating in the collisionless regime. In this limit, the [[relaxation-timescale|Relaxation timescale]] for two-body encounters is vastly longer than the dynamical free-fall time of the system. The macroscopic dynamics and the evolution of the [[phase-space-density|Phase space density]], $f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v}, t)$, are therefore governed by the [[vlasov-poisson-equation|Vlasov-Poisson equation]] (often referred to as the [[collisionless-boltzmann-equation|Collisionless Boltzmann equation]]):
$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla f - \nabla \Phi \cdot \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{v}} = 0 $$
where $\Phi$ is the self-consistent [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]]. By taking velocity moments of this equation, one derives the Jeans equations, which relate the spatial distribution of mass to the velocity dispersion and provide the basis for constructing [[equilibrium-models|Equilibrium models]] of collisionless structures.

Because collisionless systems cannot rely on two-body scattering to reach thermodynamic equilibrium, they achieve a quasi-stationary state through [[violent-relaxation|Violent relaxation]] [White et al. (1977)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977ComAp...7...95W/abstract). During an initial gravitational collapse, deep and rapid fluctuations in the global potential scatter particle orbits, allowing the system to rapidly mix and forget its initial configuration on a timescale governed by the fluctuating force rather than individual particle collisions [Kandrup et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991ApJ...366..384K/abstract). In the context of galactic disks, [[collisionless-dynamics|Collisionless dynamics]] are also influenced by collective phenomena; density waves and Lindblad resonances act as crucial mechanisms for the generation and maintenance of coherent structures [Mark et al. (1974)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1974ApJ...193..539M/abstract). To thoroughly investigate these complex dynamical scenarios, the astrophysical community increasingly relies on Virtual Observatory (VO) resources. For instance, the GalMer database provides researchers with access to extensive numerical simulations of collisionless galaxy interactions, enabling detailed studies of structural evolution and relaxation mechanisms [Chilingarian et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ASPC..461..307C/abstract).

While the classical paradigm suggests that violent relaxation acts extremely fast—often assumed to be completed within a few crossing times—numerical studies indicate a more complex timeline. Detailed one-dimensional models show that relaxation can actually involve a protracted diffusive phase lasting up to $\sim 1000$ crossing times, followed by a brief violent period of less than $100$ crossing times [Reidl et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995PhRvE..51..884R/abstract). The exact duration is highly sensitive to the population size, with maximum chaotic dynamics and a minimum overall [[relaxation-time|Relaxation time]] observed for systems with roughly $N \sim 30$ interacting mass sheets [Reidl et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995PhRvE..51..884R/abstract).

The ultimate end state of this collapse is a quasi-equilibrium structure typically characterized by a core-halo distribution in phase space [Reidl et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995PhRvE..51..884R/abstract). Because strict global equilibrium is formally unattainable, the physical collapse and subsequent clustering of a collisionless system in phase space is often modeled analytically as a thermodynamic condensation, producing structural signatures analogous to a degenerate gas [Saslaw et al. (1969)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1969MNRAS.143..437S/abstract).

## Dark Matter vs. Modified Gravity Perspectives

Since the 1930s, kinematic observations have revealed a severe discrepancy in the apparent binding energy of galaxies and clusters: the dynamical mass necessary to keep these systems gravitationally bound vastly exceeds their luminous baryonic mass. In typical stellar systems, the observed mass in stars and interstellar gas accounts for only $\sim 10\%$ of the total gravitating mass required to explain the high velocities of their constituents [Trippe et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ZNatA..69..173T/abstract). This "missing mass problem" has fractured theoretical astrophysics into two primary paradigms: the existence of unseen dark matter, and the fundamental modification of gravitational laws.

**The Dark Matter Paradigm**
The standard cosmological consensus ($\Lambda$CDM) resolves the mass deficit by embedding visible galaxies within massive, extended halos of cold, collisionless dark matter (CDM). Under this framework, dark matter dominates the [[enclosed-mass|Enclosed mass]] and provides the deep gravitational potential wells required for [[gravitational-collapse|Gravitational collapse]] and the retention of high-velocity stars and gas [Shaya et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S.274S/abstract). While dark matter models successfully describe the large-scale structure of the universe and galaxy clustering [Dark Energy Survey Collaboration et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.1270D/abstract), they rely on the existence of elusive particles (e.g., WIMPs or axion-like particles) that have evaded direct detection [Galanti et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191109372G/abstract), [Pascoli et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Ap&SS.367...62P/abstract). Additionally, some researchers have explored alternative 'fluid mechanics' theories for dark matter, conceptualizing its distribution through the formation of dark proto-globular-cluster clumps composed of 'primordial fog particles' [Gibson et al. (2000)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000astro.ph..3352G/abstract). Furthermore, standard dark matter models cannot explain the kinematics of galaxies, as the discrepancy between observed and dynamical mass appears to be a function of gravitational acceleration rather than galaxy size or maximum rotation speed [Trippe et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ZNatA..69..173T/abstract).

**Modified Gravity Perspectives**
An alternative solution posits that the missing mass is an illusion caused by a breakdown of [[newtonian-dynamics|Newtonian dynamics]] at low accelerations. Observations consistently demonstrate that the mass discrepancy universally appears below a characteristic acceleration scale, often referred to as Milgrom's constant, independent of a galaxy's physical size or maximum rotation speed [Trippe et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ZNatA..69..173T/abstract). Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) alters the effective gravitational force below this threshold, successfully predicting the Tully-Fisher and Faber-Jackson relations across 14 orders of magnitude in mass without invoking a dark component [Trippe et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ZNatA..69..173T/abstract). Related phenomenological frameworks, such as the $\kappa$-model, link the local mean [[mass-density|Mass density]] to the gravitational field intensity to naturally reproduce MOND-like effects [Pascoli et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Ap&SS.367...62P/abstract).

To address relativistic phenomena and cluster-scale dynamics, covariant theories like Modified Gravity (MOG) have been developed. MOG introduces a massive repulsive vector field $\phi_\mu$ coupled to baryonic matter alongside a running [[gravitational-constant|Gravitational constant]]. In the weak-field, non-relativistic limit, this yields an acceleration law characterized by a modified coupling strength $\alpha$ and an effective vector field mass $\mu$, both of which vary with the length scale $\ell$ via a renormalization group flow [Moffat et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015arXiv150505869M/abstract), [Moffat et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161006909M/abstract). Crucially, MOG has been shown to successfully fit the mass-discrepancy acceleration relation for the SPARC sample of 153 rotationally supported galaxies, as well as the kinematics of merging systems like the Bullet Cluster and the Train Wreck Cluster, using solely baryonic point masses [Moffat et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161006909M/abstract).

**Current Status**
The literature remains deeply conflicted between these two perspectives. While dark matter remains the standard mechanism for providing the binding energy of [[self-gravitating-systems|Self-gravitating systems]] on cosmological scales, modified gravity theories present a compelling challenge by natively predicting galactic rotation curves without the need for undetected mass [Trippe et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ZNatA..69..173T/abstract), [Pascoli et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Ap&SS.367...62P/abstract). The debate over whether the missing mass problem stems from an undiscovered particle or a required revision of gravitational physics remains an unresolved tension in modern astrophysics.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The exact influence of dark energy on the stability of [[gravitationally-bound-structures|Gravitationally bound structures]] remains an active area of research. Observational energy conditions strongly indicate that a phase of accelerated expansion occurred between the epoch of galaxy formation and the present [Gong et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007PhLB..652...63G/abstract). While cosmic expansion drives dynamics on the largest scales, determining the exact threshold at which dark energy dominates over local [[self-gravity|Self-gravity]] requires unified theoretical formalisms. Recent studies utilizing the McVittie spacetime have analyzed invariant scalars, such as the Kretschmann scalar, to map the phase-space boundary where the cosmological constant overcomes the local binding energy of galaxy groups and clusters [Benisty et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250403009B/abstract). Furthermore, to break degeneracies between dark energy and alternative theories of gravity, researchers are increasingly relying on enhanced thermodynamic frameworks. The third-order galaxy correlation parameter $b_3$ extends this thermodynamic framework and provides independent constraints on cosmological parameters that complement standard two-point statistical analyses based on the energy ratio parameter $b(n,T)$ [Saslaw et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...460...16S/abstract), [Choudhary et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250623829C/abstract). 

At galactic and cluster scales, a major unresolved tension exists regarding the "missing mass" required to explain observed binding energies and rotation curves. The literature is strongly divided between the necessity of a paradigm shift toward modified laws of gravity at low accelerations—such as Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), Modified Gravity (MOG), or the $\kappa$-model [Trippe et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ZNatA..69..173T/abstract), [Moffat et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016arXiv161006909M/abstract), [Pascoli et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Ap&SS.367...62P/abstract)—and the existence of elusive dark matter particles that deepen the [[galactic-potential|Galactic potential]]. Among particle candidates, axion-like particles (ALPs) are of significant interest. ALPs are light, neutral, pseudo-scalar bosons predicted to interact primarily with two photons. Future high-energy astrophysics observations aim to detect photon-ALP oscillations in the very-high energy (VHE) band of $100 \, \mathrm{GeV} - 100 \, \mathrm{TeV}$. These oscillations, occurring within the coherent external magnetic fields of galaxy clusters, could partially prevent gamma-gamma absorption and modify the observed spectra of active galactic nuclei, allowing researchers to constrain the ALP parameter space [Galanti et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019arXiv191109372G/abstract).

On smaller scales, precisely modeling the binding energy of forming galaxies and star clusters requires accurately capturing turbulence and [[energy-dissipation|Energy dissipation]]. Because astrophysical fluids span an enormous range of spatial scales, explicit resolution of all interacting eddies is computationally prohibitive. Consequently, researchers increasingly employ large eddy simulations (LES) utilizing spatially filtered magnetohydrodynamic equations. In these frameworks, the effects of unresolved turbulent eddies are approximated using explicit subgrid-scale (SGS) models, such as the Smagorinsky model or turbulence energy equation models [Schmidt-Brückner et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250906801S/abstract). A persistent tension in numerical astrophysics is that many simulation codes suffer from high intrinsic numerical diffusion, which can drown out the physical impact of explicit SGS models. Nonetheless, improving SGS models is a critical future direction; they are essential for consistently computing turbulent velocity dispersions and for adaptively parameterizing star formation efficiencies based on local binding criteria rather than artificial density thresholds [Hopkins et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.432.2647H/abstract), [Schmidt-Brückner et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250906801S/abstract).
