## Article

In modern astrophysics, the **early universe** refers to the high-redshift [[primeval-formation-stages|Primeval formation stages]] of the cosmos, spanning from the [[early-universe-epochs|Early universe epochs]] immediately following the [[big-bang|Big Bang]] through the initial phases of structure formation. Governed by the universe's [[expansion-history|Expansion history]], this period is characterized by the thermal history of a rapidly cooling, expanding plasma. Key milestones in this primordial era include [[nucleosynthesis|Nucleosynthesis]], where the first light elements were forged in a [[radiation-dominated-universe|Radiation-dominated universe]] [Sato et al. (1971)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1971PThPS..49...11S/abstract), and the subsequent recombination era at $z \approx 1300$. During recombination, the cosmic temperature dropped sufficiently for electrons and protons to bind into neutral hydrogen, effectively decoupling [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]] from the radiation field and releasing the cosmic microwave background. This transition marked the beginning of a dark period in [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]] known as the Dark Ages, establishing the initial conditions for [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|Galaxy and Universe evolution]].

The Dark Ages concluded with the onset of [[first-star-formation|First star formation]] as primordial density fluctuations—seeded during cosmic inflation—grown and collapsed into [[dark-matter-halo-scales|Dark Matter Halo Scales]]. Within these pristine environments, the cooling of trace molecular hydrogen triggered the formation of metal-free Population III stars and the initial [[massive-black-hole-seeds|Massive black hole seeds]] [Greif et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ComAC...2....3G/abstract), [Haemmerlé et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020SSRv..216...48H/abstract). The emergence of these first luminous sources fundamentally transformed the cosmos by flooding the neutral intergalactic medium (IGM) with ultraviolet radiation. This drove the [[epoch-of-reionization|Epoch of Reionization]], a profound phase transition that culminated roughly one billion years after the Big Bang ($z \approx 6$), leaving the IGM in a warm, highly ionized state [Bouwens et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..423..111B/abstract). Concurrently, supernovae from these massive early stars initiated the [[universal-enrichment-history|Universal enrichment history]], establishing a chemical "metallicity floor" and paving the way for more mature [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]].

Today, the study of the early universe is a rapidly evolving frontier characterized by both remarkable observational breakthroughs and significant theoretical tensions. Recent data from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have revealed an unexpected overabundance of [[bright-galaxies|Bright galaxies]] at $z > 10$. This "Impossibly Early Galaxy" problem challenges the strict halo mass limits of the standard $\Lambda$CDM hierarchical growth paradigm, prompting intense debate over whether these findings necessitate radical revisions to [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] models—such as invoking [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|High-redshift cosmic star formation efficiencies]] and top-heavy [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]—or modifications to fundamental cosmology, like Early Dark Energy [Menci et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...07..072M/abstract), [Shen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919427S/abstract). Resolving these questions, alongside determining the primary drivers of reionization and the origin of supermassive black holes, are the primary goals of next-generation facilities. In particular, the advent of [[21-cm-cosmology|21-cm Cosmology]] promises to tomographically map the pristine neutral hydrogen of the IGM, offering unprecedented insights into the universe's first luminous sources and the underlying nature of dark matter dynamics [Furlanetto et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S..83F/abstract).

## Historical Development and the Expanding Universe

The modern cosmological paradigm traces its origins to the 1920s, culminating in Edwin Hubble’s demonstration that [[extragalactic-phenomena|Extragalactic Phenomena]] are distant galaxies participating in a global [[expansion-history|Expansion history]] [Kirshner et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004PNAS..101....8K/abstract). The empirical relationship between a galaxy's distance and its redshift—codified in Hubble's diagram—established that the cosmic scale factor $a(t)$ is increasing with time. This realization underpins the [[big-bang|Big Bang]] framework: if the universe is expanding, it must have been significantly denser and hotter in the past. This hot early phase provides the necessary conditions for primordial [[nucleosynthesis|Nucleosynthesis]] and recombination, inherently linking the macroscopic expansion of the universe to the microscopic laws of particle and nuclear physics.

For decades, cosmological models debated the ultimate fate of the universe, often assuming a deceleration driven by the self-gravity of its matter content. However, age estimates of ancient [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|High-mass early-type galaxies]], such as a $\sim 3.5$ Gyr old galaxy at $z=1.55$, initially created severe tension with matter-dominated flat models, favoring the presence of a non-zero cosmological constant [Krauss et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...480..466K/abstract). Correspondingly, measurements of the evolution of the universe's luminosity density provided early historical evidence favoring a flat, $\Lambda$-dominated universe over [[cosmogony|Cosmogony]] based on Einstein-de Sitter frameworks [Totani et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997ApJ...483L..75T/abstract). Late-twentieth-century observations of Type Ia supernovae confirmed an unexpected [[late-time-cosmic-acceleration|Late-time cosmic acceleration]] [Zehavi et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Natur.401..252Z/abstract). This required the reintroduction of Einstein's Cosmological constant ($\Lambda$) to drive the accelerated expansion, though modified theories of gravity also provide distinct paradigms for this cosmic acceleration compared to simple dark energy models [Avelino et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Symm....8...70A/abstract). Kinematic analyses of supernovae and radio galaxies indicate that the transition from a decelerating to an accelerating universe occurred at a [[pivot-redshift|Pivot redshift]] of $z \approx 0.42 \pm 0.08$ [Daly et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007NuPhS.173...19D/abstract). Consequently, the field converged on the $\Lambda$CDM Standard model, describing a spatially flat universe dominated by approximately $75\%$ dark energy, $21\%$ cold dark matter, and $4\%$ [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]]. Complementing these discoveries, the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect has emerged as a powerful, redshift-independent tool for surveying galaxy clusters and constraining the mass density and expansion history of the universe [Carlstrom et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph..3480C/abstract). 

Future observatories will test this expansion history even further; for instance, third-generation gravitational-wave observatories functioning as standard sirens from binary black holes will be able to measure the Hubble constant to within 1%, offering a powerful method to distinguish between the standard $\Lambda$CDM model and alternatives such as the $R_h=ct$ cosmology [You et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...908..215Y/abstract). While the Standard model has been remarkably successful at reproducing the large-scale structure and the [[cosmological-age|Cosmological age]] of the [[local-universe-and-local-group|Local Universe and Local Group]]—an age additionally corroborated by independent methods, such as using white dwarf luminosity distributions to estimate the age of the [[galactic-disk-evolution|Galactic disk evolution]] and the universe [Winget et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987ApJ...315L..77W/abstract)—it is currently facing rigorous "stress tests" from [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] studies [Boylan-Kolchin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7..731B/abstract). Recent early data from the JWST have uncovered an unexpected abundance of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] at $z > 10$, some exhibiting stellar masses $M_* \ge 10^{10.5} M_\odot$ and stellar [[mass-evolution|Mass evolution]] approaching $\rho_* \approx 10^6 M_\odot \mathrm{Mpc}^{-3}$ [Menci et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L...5M/abstract). 

The literature is currently highly conflicting regarding the implications of these early, massive systems. Some researchers argue that this rapid assembly strictly violates the theoretical limits of dark matter halo growth in the $\Lambda$CDM framework. To resolve this tension, alternative cosmologies have been proposed, including Early Dark Energy (EDE) models that accelerate early structure formation and yield a higher Hubble constant of $H_0 \approx 74.6 \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$ [Jiang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240919941J/abstract), [Shen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919427S/abstract), or an evolving dark energy sector with a negative Cosmological constant [Menci et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...07..072M/abstract). Conversely, other studies maintain that there is no fundamental crisis for the Standard model. These authors argue that high-resolution hydrodynamic simulations, which incorporate locally enhanced star formation efficiencies or a top-heavy [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]], can naturally reproduce these [[very-young-galaxies|Very Young Galaxies]] without requiring modifications to the underlying cosmology [McCaffrey et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..47M/abstract), [Inayoshi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L..10I/abstract).

## Primordial Phases: From the Planck Epoch to Recombination

The thermal history of the infant universe is dictated by its continuous expansion following the [[big-bang|Big Bang]]. Because the cosmic scale factor $a(t)$ increases monotonically with time, the early universe was exceedingly denser and hotter, providing the requisite [[primeval-formation-stages|Primeval formation stages]] for the creation and transmutation of fundamental particles [Sato et al. (1971)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1971PThPS..49...11S/abstract).

### The Radiation-Dominated Era and Nucleosynthesis
During the earliest evolutionary phases, the cosmic energy budget was overwhelmingly controlled by relativistic particles, characterizing the [[radiation-dominated-universe|Radiation-dominated universe]]. In this epoch, the temperature of the cosmic bath scaled inversely with the expansion of space ($T \propto a^{-1}$). When the universe cooled to temperatures of $T \sim 0.1 \text{ MeV}$ (approximately $10^9 \text{ K}$), it entered the brief but critical [[early-universe-epochs|Early universe epochs]] of primordial [[nucleosynthesis|Nucleosynthesis]]. 

At this stage, the universe consisted primarily of a tightly coupled plasma of protons, helium nuclei, electrons, and photons, alongside a background of decoupled neutrinos. Because the thermal energy of the universe had dropped below the electron rest mass ($m_e c^2 \approx 0.51 \text{ MeV}$), both baryons and electrons were firmly non-relativistic [Peacock et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999coph.book.....P/abstract). The application of nuclear and particle physics to this dense, hot medium allows for precise predictions regarding the primordial abundances of light [[chemical-elements|Chemical elements]]. These early fusion processes fundamentally established the initial chemical composition that would govern all subsequent [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|Baryonic Matter and Evolution]]. Following nucleosynthesis, the cosmic plasma was maintained in thermal equilibrium through continuous electromagnetic interactions, most notably free-free emission and Compton scattering between photons and charged particles.

### Transition to Matter Domination and Recombination
As the universe continued to expand and cool, the energy density of relativistic radiation ($\rho_{\rm rad}$) dropped faster than the rest-mass energy density of non-relativistic matter ($\rho_{\rm m}$):
$$ \rho_{\rm rad} \propto a^{-4}, \quad \rho_{\rm m} \propto a^{-3} $$
This differential scaling eventually led to matter-radiation equality, a pivot point after which the universe became matter-dominated. 

The falling temperature subsequently permitted the formation of stable, neutral atoms—a phase transition known as Recombination. When the cosmic temperature reached $T \sim 4000 \text{ K}$ (at a cosmic time of $t \sim 2 \times 10^5 \text{ yr}$), the thermodynamic balance shifted such that roughly 50% of the baryonic matter had combined into neutral atoms. By a redshift of $z_{\rm rec} \sim 1300$, electrons and protons had predominantly recombined to form neutral hydrogen gas.

The rapid depletion of free electrons abruptly decoupled the baryonic matter from the radiation field. Without a dense screen of free electrons to frequently scatter them, photons began to stream freely through space, causing the universe to suddenly transition from an opaque plasma to a transparent state. These relic photons, adiabatically cooled by the continuing expansion of the universe, are observed today as the Cosmic Microwave Background. High-resolution measurements of small-angular-scale CMB anisotropies can even be used to distinguish between secondary contributions, such as the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect, and nonstandard inflationary scenarios [Cooray et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002PhRvD..66h3001C/abstract). Following this decoupling, the universe entered a prolonged dark age characterized by a highly uniform, neutral intergalactic medium devoid of luminous sources, thereby setting the initial stage for [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] and the eventual [[epoch-of-reionization|Epoch of Reionization]] [Wise et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ConPh..60..145W/abstract).

## The Cosmic Dark Ages and Dark Matter Halo Assembly

Following the [[formation-epoch|Formation epoch]] of recombination at $z \simeq 1300$, when electrons and protons combined to form neutral hydrogen, the universe became transparent to radiation. Devoid of luminous sources, the cosmos entered a period known as the Dark Ages. During this era, the universe was filled with cosmic microwave background (CMB) photons, a highly uniform [[cosmic-h-i-density|Cosmic H I density]], and dark matter. Without active heating mechanisms, the gas cooled via adiabatic expansion, and the CMB temperature decreased linearly with redshift: 
$$ T(z) = T_0(1 + z) $$
where $T_0$ is the present-day CMB temperature. 

Throughout the Dark Ages, primordial density fluctuations seeded during inflation amplified via gravitational growth. In the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological framework, this growth proceeds via hierarchical [[galaxy-and-universe-evolution|Galaxy and Universe evolution]], where small-scale overdensities collapse first and subsequently merge to form progressively larger bound structures [Dayal et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhR...780....1D/abstract). Interestingly, recent findings indicate that galaxy stellar spins retain a reoriented memory of the initial linear tidal field, offering a potential probe of [[characteristic-astrophysical-scales|Characteristic Astrophysical Scales]] from this early epoch [Moon et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...952..101M/abstract). The underlying dark matter dictates this architecture, providing the gravitational potential wells into which baryonic matter later falls. This continuous process of [[halo-accretion|Halo accretion]] drives the accumulation of mass required to eventually ignite the first luminous sources.

The first structures to fully collapse and virialize were [[dark-matter-halo-scales|Dark Matter Halo Scales]], which possessed typical masses in the range of $10^5$ to $10^6 \, M_\odot$ [Greif et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ComAC...2....3G/abstract), [Haemmerlé et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020SSRv..216...48H/abstract). The [[early-universe-epochs|Early universe epochs]] and the mass limits of these first galaxies were fundamentally dictated by the dynamics of nonspherical collapse and rotation [de Araujo et al. (1994)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...437..556D/abstract). To probe the thermodynamics and chemical evolution of the intergalactic medium during this elusive Dark Age, astronomers are increasingly emphasizing the diagnostic power of Lyman-alpha and [C II] 158 $\mu$m emission lines [El Bourakadi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241213090E/abstract). In the metal-free environments, the lack of heavy elements meant that the primordial gas could not cool efficiently via atomic transitions. Instead, cooling was governed by trace amounts of molecular hydrogen ($\text{H}_2$), which allowed the gas to dissipate thermal energy and condense within the dense centers of these minihalos [Greif et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ComAC...2....3G/abstract), [Yamasawa et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...735...44Y/abstract). This critical phase of [[pre-galactic-evolution|Pre-galactic evolution]] set the stage for the formation of the first [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] (Population III), eventually bringing the Dark Ages to a close and initiating the [[epoch-of-reionization|Epoch of Reionization]].

While the standard model of dark matter halo assembly has long been the consensus, recent observations have introduced significant tensions into this narrative. Early data from the JWST have revealed an unexpected abundance of [[very-young-galaxies|Very Young Galaxies]] at $z > 10$. The high stellar masses observed in these early systems, with some candidates reaching $M_* \gtrsim 10^{10.5} \, M_\odot$, severely challenge the limits of the predicted $\Lambda$CDM dark matter halo mass function at such early epochs [Menci et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L...5M/abstract), [Boylan-Kolchin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7..731B/abstract). 

The literature is currently conflicting on how to resolve this discrepancy. It remains highly debated whether these observations can be reconciled within standard cosmology by invoking [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|High-redshift cosmic star formation efficiencies]] and modified stellar feedback models [Inayoshi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L..10I/abstract), or if they necessitate fundamental revisions to [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. Proposed non-standard solutions include the introduction of Early Dark Energy to accelerate early structure formation [Shen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919427S/abstract), [Menci et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...07..072M/abstract), seeding by primordial supermassive black holes [Dolgov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240106882D/abstract), or alternative structure formation seeds such as axion domain walls formed via spontaneous symmetry breaking in the early universe [Sikivie et al. (1982)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1982PhRvL..48.1156S/abstract). Additionally, non-Gaussian density perturbations generated by cosmic string loops could potentially trigger nonlinear structure and star formation significantly earlier than standard inflationary seeds [Shlaer et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JCAP...05..026S/abstract).

## The First Luminous Sources: Population III Stars and SMBH Seeds

The end of the cosmic dark ages was marked by [[first-star-formation|First star formation]], specifically the emergence of the pristine, metal-free Population III (Pop III) stars. Theoretical models and numerical simulations establish that these first luminous sources assembled within dark matter minihalos of masses $M \sim 10^5 - 10^6 M_\odot$, where molecular hydrogen ($H_2$) cooling was the dominant mechanism allowing the primordial gas to condense [Greif et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ComAC...2....3G/abstract), [Haemmerlé et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020SSRv..216...48H/abstract). Due to the higher gas temperatures and the inefficiency of $H_2$ cooling compared to metal-line cooling, the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] of Pop III stars is widely theorized to have been top-heavy, favoring [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] over the standard Salpeter-like distribution observed locally, though the exact mass scale remains an active subject of debate [Larson et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.301..569L/abstract), [Inayoshi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L..10I/abstract), [Blackwell et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240802781B/abstract). 

These intensely luminous sources had a profound impact on the surrounding [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] and intergalactic medium. Radiation from supermassive Pop III.1 stars may have triggered an early "Flash" ionization at $z \sim 20-30$, prior to the main [[epoch-of-reionization|Epoch of Reionization]], potentially contributing a scattering optical depth of $\tau \sim 0.04$ to the cosmic microwave background [Tan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250618490T/abstract). The subsequent supernovae of these stars provided the universe's first heavy elements, establishing a chemical "metallicity floor" (e.g., $[O/H] \approx -4.0$) that dictated the transition from Pop III to Population II star formation [Brauer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016366B/abstract), [Greif et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ComAC...2....3G/abstract).

The remnants of these massive early stars are intimately linked to the origin of [[massive-black-hole-seeds|Massive black hole seeds]]. However, the discovery of luminous quasars powered by supermassive black holes (SMBHs) at $z > 6$ presents a severe timescale challenge for [[high-redshift-black-hole-growth|High-redshift black hole growth]] and hierarchical models [Volonteri et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..267...26V/abstract). There is significant theoretical tension between the two primary seeding paradigms. "Light seeds" ($\sim 100 M_\odot$) left behind by Pop III core-collapse cannot easily reach $10^9 M_\odot$ by $z > 6$ if restricted to the Eddington accretion limit. Conversely, "heavy seeds" ($\sim 10^4 - 10^5 M_\odot$) may have formed through the direct collapse of massive primordial gas clouds, bypassing the stellar phase entirely [Haemmerlé et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020SSRv..216...48H/abstract). An intermediate pathway suggests that dense primeval clusters could have fostered repeated stellar collisions among massive stars, yielding a very massive central star that directly collapsed into an SMBH seed [Katz et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ffbh.book..125K/abstract). To untangle these scenarios, future space-based gravitational-wave detectors like eLISA will be critical, as they will be able to observe massive black hole binaries at high redshifts to track the formation of seed black holes [Porter et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ASSP...40..267P/abstract).

Alongside SMBHs, the remnants of the first stars also included an evolving population of X-ray binaries. Over cosmological timescales, high-mass X-ray binaries (HMXBs) became the dominant source of X-ray emissivity at $z > 2.5$, significantly contributing to the [[universal-enrichment-history|Universal enrichment history]] as these binary populations evolved [Fragos et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764...41F/abstract). Reconciling these seed mechanisms with the over-massive black holes increasingly detected by JWST in high-redshift [[host-galaxies|Host galaxies]] remains a major frontier. To bridge the mass gap rapidly, recent models suggest that early [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] underwent episodic, super-Eddington accretion. Triggered by early major mergers, these extreme accretion bursts (lasting $0.5 - 3 \text{ Myr}$) can dramatically inflate both light and heavy seeds. This evolutionary pathway predicts an active duty cycle of just $1-4\%$, leaving the vast majority of these over-massive early black holes in a quiescent, low-Eddington state ($\lambda_{\rm Edd} < 0.05$), consistent with recent high-redshift spectroscopic surveys [Trinca et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241214248T/abstract).

## The Epoch of Reionization (EoR) and Intergalactic Medium Dynamics

The [[epoch-of-reionization|Epoch of Reionization]] (EoR) marks a profound cosmological phase transition during which the intergalactic medium (IGM) evolved from a cold, neutral state into a warm, highly ionized plasma [Ferrara et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1409.4946F/abstract). This transformation was triggered by the onset of [[first-star-formation|First star formation]] and the emergence of early galaxies and quasars, which flooded the early universe with ultraviolet ionizing radiation. 

While there is strong observational consensus that [[global-reionization|Global reionization]] concluded approximately one billion years after the Big Bang at $z \approx 6$ [Shapiro et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AIPC..586..219S/abstract), [Bouwens et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..423..111B/abstract), the exact timeline of its onset and the dominant sources driving it remain subjects of active debate. The literature currently conflicts over whether low-mass star-forming galaxies alone provided sufficient emissivity to complete the process, or if early active galactic nuclei were required to supplement the photon budget. In support of a stellar-driven scenario, observations of high-redshift [[extragalactic-phenomena|Extragalactic Phenomena]] such as gamma-ray bursts provide an independent measure of the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] at $z > 8$, suggesting that the integrated star formation across this epoch was indeed sufficient to reionize the universe [Kistler et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705L.104K/abstract). A critical uncertainty in resolving this tension is the [[escape-fraction|Escape fraction]] ($f_{\rm esc}$) of Lyman-continuum photons from dark matter halos into the IGM. Determining the precise value of $f_{\rm esc}$ is complex, as it appears to scale non-linearly with a galaxy's baryonic fraction and the rate of ionizing photons [Hattab et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.3307H/abstract).

The temporal progression of the EoR may also have featured multiple phases rather than a single monotonic increase in the ionization fraction. Theoretical models suggest an early "Flash" ionization event could have occurred at $z \sim 20-30$, driven by Population III.1 supermassive stars. This early phase may have contributed a Thomson scattering optical depth of $\tau_{\rm PopIII.1} \sim 0.04$ before the IGM underwent partial [[re-ionization|Re-ionization]] later by subsequent galaxy populations [Tan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250618490T/abstract).

Observationally, the state of the IGM during the EoR is constrained using the absorption spectra of high-redshift sources. For instance, observations of the gamma-ray burst GRB 210905A at $z \sim 6.3$ place a robust upper limit on the IGM neutral hydrogen fraction of $x_{\rm HI} < 0.15$, confirming that the medium was predominantly ionized by this epoch [Fausey et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240313126F/abstract). However, the phase transition was highly patchy. This inhomogeneous topology is evidenced by the discovery of massive reservoirs of cold, neutral atomic hydrogen permeating a galaxy proto-cluster at a post-EoR redshift of $z=5.4$. These reservoirs exhibit extreme column densities, a finding that challenges simplified models of a uniform reionization end-date and underscores the complex topology of the IGM [Heintz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240706287H/abstract). This patchy topology is further driven by large-scale [[galaxy-overdensities|Galaxy overdensities]] that lead to cosmic scatter in galaxy abundances [Barkana et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...609..474B/abstract).

Finally, while the reionization of hydrogen and neutral helium concluded by $z \sim 6$, the second ionization phase of helium, known as [[he-ii-reionization|He II reionization]], required substantially harder photon energies. Consequently, He II reionization was decoupled from the hydrogen EoR, occurring at significantly lower redshifts and driven almost exclusively by the harder radiation spectra of quasars rather than stellar sources [Basu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251022043B/abstract).

## Chemical Enrichment and Star Formation History

The transition of the early universe from a primordial mix of hydrogen and helium to a metal-enriched environment is fundamentally tied to the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] and the life cycles of the first generations of stars. The [[global-star-formation-rate|Global star formation rate]] density, $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$, is known to rise sharply from $z=0$ to a peak at $z \sim 1.5$ before declining toward the high-redshift universe [Madau et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..148...88M/abstract). However, early observations from the JWST have revealed an unexpectedly high abundance of UV-bright, [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] at $z > 10$. This presents a tension with standard $\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}$ hierarchical growth limits, sometimes referred to as the "impossibly early galaxy" problem [Boylan-Kolchin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7..731B/abstract). Reconciling these observations requires highly efficient star formation, with gas-to-star conversion efficiencies potentially reaching $10\%-30\%$ [Inayoshi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L..10I/abstract). In these embryonic, gas-rich environments, galaxies likely experienced [[instantaneous-star-formation-rate|Instantaneous star formation rate]] bursts that systematically deviated from standard Schmidt-Kennicutt relations [Papadopoulos et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...717.1037P/abstract).

Early metal enrichment was driven by the rapid evolution and subsequent supernovae of metal-free Population III and early Population II stars. This [[universal-enrichment-history|Universal enrichment history]] established a "metallicity floor" in the intergalactic medium, with theoretical yields predicting baseline abundances [Brauer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016366B/abstract). These early evolutionary stages can also be traced by the brightest events in the universe, such as gamma-ray bursts (GRBs). Observations of GRB host environments reveal that the interstellar medium was already chemically evolved to roughly one-tenth of solar metallicity by $z > 2$ [Savaglio et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..265..139S/abstract). The measurement of a low carbon-to-oxygen ratio in a $z=6.23$ galaxy indicates that early enrichment was dominated by core-collapse supernovae rather than intermediate-mass stars, implying the rapid buildup of the [[stellar-populations-by-metallicity|Stellar Populations by Metallicity]] in $\lesssim 100 \text{ Myr}$ [Jones et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951L..17J/abstract). As galaxies continued to evolve from these primordial stages, interactions at lower redshifts ($z \sim 2-4$) played an increasingly crucial role; these interactions can destabilize cold gas to trigger both circumnuclear starbursts and black hole accretion [Menci et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...604...12M/abstract).

A major unresolved question is whether the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] in the early universe was significantly "top-heavy." Theoretical models of [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|Galaxy chemical enrichment mechanisms]] often require a flatter IMF—disproportionately favoring [[massive-stars|Massive stars]]—to reproduce the observed metallicities in the intracluster medium and the abundances of ancient metal-poor stars [Larson et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.301..569L/abstract), [Blackwell et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240802781B/abstract). While top-heavy IMFs naturally produce the intense UV radiation required to match early JWST observations [Inayoshi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L..10I/abstract), there is conflicting evidence. Variations in the assumed IMF parameterizations during spectral energy distribution fitting can reduce inferred stellar masses, leaving the exact shape of the early IMF ambiguous [Woodrum et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231018464W/abstract).

Dust formation and [[nucleosynthesis|Nucleosynthesis]] in these early epochs also challenge existing models. In the primordial universe, dust was primarily formed in the ejecta of supernovae. The survival of these grains against shocks dictated the formation rate of molecular hydrogen, which in turn strongly regulated subsequent star formation rates [Yamasawa et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...735...44Y/abstract). Finally, the origin of rapid neutron-capture r-process [[chemical-elements|Chemical elements]], such as europium, remains in tension with standard delay-time distributions. The delayed coalescence of neutron star mergers cannot easily account for the observed trends at low metallicities. This discrepancy strongly suggests that up to $50\%$ of the early universe's r-process enrichment must have been driven by an alternative, prompt source, such as a rare class of core-collapse supernovae [Côté et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...875..106C/abstract).

## Feedback Mechanisms and Galaxy-IGM Interaction

Early galaxies were not closed systems; their evolution was fundamentally coupled to the surrounding intergalactic medium through a variety of feedback mechanisms. Energy, mass, and heavy elements injected by [[massive-stars|Massive stars]] and [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] regulated both the internal star-formation efficiency of these halos and the thermal state of the external gas.

**Stellar Feedback and Outflows**
Mechanical feedback from stellar winds and core-collapse supernovae drove massive outflows that evacuated gas from shallow potential wells. The conceptual importance of these massive outflows has long been recognized; historical frameworks like the 'early explosive era' model posited that 10-40% of a galaxy's initial mass could be ejected as [[hot-gas|Hot gas]] into intergalactic space [Ikeuchi et al. (1977)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977PThPh..58.1742I/abstract). Today, observations of gravitationally lensed emitters confirm that powerful outflows were ubiquitous in early low-mass star-forming galaxies [Nianias et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241021788N/abstract). Internally, supernova-driven forward and reverse shocks destroyed dust grains, substantially suppressing star-formation rates in early minihalos [Yamasawa et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...735...44Y/abstract). By expelling chemically processed gas, these outflows drove the [[universal-enrichment-history|Universal enrichment history]], establishing a metallicity floor in the early IGM [Brauer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241016366B/abstract). Damped Lyman-$\alpha$ absorbers at high redshift trace this ongoing pollution of [[pre-galactic-evolution|Pre-galactic evolution]] matter by massive stars [Fenner et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...606..116F/abstract). Furthermore, early gas stripping via tidal shocks and ram pressure in these gas-dominated progenitors helps explain the properties observed today in [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]] [Mayer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Natur.445..738M/abstract).

**AGN Feedback**
In parallel with stellar processes, accretion onto early supermassive black holes injected vast amounts of kinetic and thermal energy into the interstellar and intergalactic environments. Theoretical models suggest that early black hole seeds grew via short, repeated episodes of super-Eddington accretion [Trinca et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241214248T/abstract). The resulting [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] triggered powerful winds that could sweep cold gas out of the host galaxies or shock-heat the surrounding halo gas, suppressing future accretion and quenching early star formation. The importance of these combined processes is supported by the successful validation of comprehensive feedback models [Torrey et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.438.1985T/abstract).

**Photoionization Feedback**
Radiation from the first luminous sources dramatically altered the IGM by initiating the [[epoch-of-reionization|Epoch of Reionization]]. Ultraviolet photons heated the intergalactic gas from a cold neutral state to temperatures of roughly $10^4$ K, which raised the local thermal Jeans mass. This photoheating effectively photoevaporated gas from [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|Dwarf Galaxy Evolution and Interactions]] and suppressed subsequent gas accretion, restricting new star formation in halos near the atomic cooling limit [Shapiro et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AIPC..586..219S/abstract). To fully benchmark theoretical models of radiative feedback from massive stars in such environments, the JWST Early Release Science program is currently providing essential observations of Photo-Dissociation Regions [Berné et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASP..134e4301B/abstract). The global impact of this photoionization feedback depended critically on the [[escape-fraction|Escape fraction]] of ionizing photons [Hattab et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.3307H/abstract). 

While there is broad consensus that star-forming galaxies were the primary drivers of reionization ending at $z \approx 6$ [Bouwens et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ASSL..423..111B/abstract), some models propose an earlier "flash" ionization as high as $z \sim 20-30$ driven by Population III supermassive stars [Tan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250618490T/abstract). Ultimately, the long-term impact of these cascading feedback mechanisms and cosmic expansion governed the thermal history of large-scale structures. For instance, [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] in clusters only achieved full thermal equilibrium much later, a delayed thermalization directly linked to the transition of the universe's overall density to dark energy domination [Sereno et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.5214S/abstract).

## The 'Impossibly Early Galaxy' Problem and JWST Observations

The advent of the JWST has fundamentally challenged the standard $\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}$ model's timeline for early structure formation. Early observations have revealed an unexpected overabundance of [[bright-galaxies|Bright galaxies]] at $z > 10$ [Boylan-Kolchin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7..731B/abstract). Furthermore, early release observations have discovered a surprisingly large population of dropout galaxy candidates potentially spanning $z \approx 11 - 20$, pushing the theoretical boundary of early structure formation significantly further into the cosmic dark ages [Yan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...942L...9Y/abstract). Under standard hierarchical growth, the [[number-density-evolution|Number density evolution]] of dark matter halos imposes a strict upper limit on the stellar mass density of galaxies at any given epoch. However, observations suggest a stellar mass density for [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] exceeding $M_* \ge 10^{10.5} \, M_\odot$ at $z \approx 10$ [Menci et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L...5M/abstract). These objects precipitate what is known as the "Impossibly Early Galaxy" problem [Yennapureddy et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PDU....20...65Y/abstract), [Boylan-Kolchin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7..731B/abstract). 

The literature remains highly conflicted on whether this tension represents a true failure of the $\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}$ cosmology or merely points to an incomplete understanding of early-universe baryonic physics. From an astrophysical perspective, reconciling these sources with standard halo mass functions requires [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|High-redshift cosmic star formation efficiencies]] that are more than an order of magnitude higher than the efficiencies observed in low-redshift systems [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...790L..17M/abstract), [Inayoshi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L..10I/abstract). Alternatively, a shift toward a top-heavy [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] populated by [[luminous-blue-stars-and-galaxies|Luminous Blue Stars and Galaxies]], or significant helium enhancements in early star clusters, could boost the rest-frame UV luminosity [Inayoshi et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...938L..10I/abstract), [Katz et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E.106K/abstract). Indeed, several high-resolution hydrodynamic models—analogous to massive simulation suites like [[bluetides|BlueTides]]—assert that localized, intense starbursts can reproduce the JWST observations without invoking non-standard cosmology [McCaffrey et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023OJAp....6E..47M/abstract). Similarly, high-resolution zoom-in simulations illustrate [[accelerated-galaxy-evolution|Accelerated galaxy evolution]], revealing that the massive galaxies detected at $z \sim 9$ may actually be the direct progenitors of massive systems within protoclusters [Rennehan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...975..114R/abstract).

Conversely, if these astrophysical calibrations prove insufficient, the "Impossibly Early Galaxy" problem may necessitate modifications to the standard cosmological paradigm. Early Dark Energy (EDE) models have gained significant traction as a potential solution, modifying the pre-recombination expansion history and naturally predicting a higher abundance of massive galaxies [Menci et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...07..072M/abstract), [Shen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919427S/abstract). Other non-standard frameworks, including $R_h = ct$ cosmology [Yennapureddy et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019EPJC...79..571Y/abstract) and the seeding of early halos by supermassive primordial black holes [Dolgov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240106882D/abstract), have also been proposed as mechanisms to rapidly accelerate early galaxy assembly. Ultimately, distinguishing between a required paradigm shift in cosmology and necessary revisions to high-redshift star formation theory will rely on forthcoming spectroscopic confirmations and precise metallicity measurements of these [[nascent-galaxies|Nascent galaxies]].

## Open Questions and Future Frontiers in 21-cm Cosmology

The upcoming decade of astrophysics in the early universe will be heavily driven by [[21-cm-cosmology|21-cm Cosmology]], which provides a direct tomographic map of the [[cosmic-h-i-density|Cosmic H I density]] across the cosmic dark ages and the [[epoch-of-reionization|Epoch of Reionization]] [Furlanetto et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S..83F/abstract). By observing the 21-cm hyperfine transition, future radio facilities aim to measure both the spatial fluctuations and the thermal state of the pristine IGM [Dayal et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..352...43D/abstract). A major theoretical frontier is the detection of structures at extremely early epochs, corresponding to a [[cosmological-age|Cosmological age]] of $t \sim 200$ Myr. At these times, the relative streaming velocity between baryons and dark matter is predicted to significantly suppress star formation in low-mass halos, imprinting a unique signature on the cosmic web [Barkana et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013PASA...30...36B/abstract), [Barkana et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1401.7935B/abstract).

A pressing open question is whether the recent discovery of unexpectedly massive [[bright-galaxies|Bright galaxies]] at $z > 10$ requires a fundamental revision of the standard $\Lambda$CDM paradigm. The high stellar mass densities inferred from early JWST data strain the available baryonic reservoirs permitted by standard hierarchical halo assembly [Boylan-Kolchin et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023NatAs...7..731B/abstract). Next-generation observations will be crucial for testing alternative [[cosmological-history|Cosmological history]] scenarios and dark matter models that attempt to resolve this tension. For instance, theoretical models can tightly constrain alternative dark matter models with non-zero equations of state that attempt to accelerate early structure formation [Davari et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231115083D/abstract). Furthermore, the 21-cm signal will serve as an independent test to determine whether the overabundance of high-redshift galaxies is better resolved by Early Dark Energy models or by invoking [[high-redshift-cosmic-star-formation-efficiencies|High-redshift cosmic star formation efficiencies]] in primordial halos [Menci et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...07..072M/abstract). 

Additionally, 21-cm tomography will serve as a highly sensitive probe for exotic early-universe physics, notably primordial magnetic fields. Generated during inflation or post-Big Bang phase transitions, these fields influence the collapse of dwarf galaxies and dissipate energy into the IGM, leaving measurable thermal and structural signatures on the redshifted 21-cm line [Subramanian et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016RPPh...79g6901S/abstract). Determining whether these fields exist, and distinguishing their effects from standard astrophysical feedback, is a major priority. 

Ultimately, realizing the full potential of 21-cm cosmology requires cross-correlating measurements with rest-frame UV and optical data from JWST. This synergy will be essential to decipher whether the anomalies currently observed in [[early-galaxy-formation|Early galaxy formation]] point to novel fundamental physics, or merely to complex and previously underestimated baryon astrophysics in the early universe [Dayal et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020IAUS..352...43D/abstract).
