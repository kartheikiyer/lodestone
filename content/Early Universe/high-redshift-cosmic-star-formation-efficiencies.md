**High-redshift cosmic star formation efficiency** quantifies the fraction of available [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|baryonic mass]] within a dark matter halo that is successfully converted into stars during the [[early-universe|early epochs of the Universe]]. Formally defined as the baryon conversion efficiency $\epsilon_\star \equiv M_\star / (f_b M_{\rm halo})$, this metric is a fundamental parameter for understanding [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]]. Historically, the global evolution of stellar mass growth has been tracked via the [[global-star-formation-rate|cosmic star formation rate density]], which maps a universal [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] that rose sharply in the early universe, peaked around $z \approx 2$ during an epoch known as "Cosmic Noon" ([[peak-star-formation-history|Peak star formation history]]), and subsequently declined by an order of magnitude toward the present day [Madau et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.283.1388M/abstract), [Kennicutt et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ARA&A..36..189K/abstract), [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Across most of cosmic time, empirical techniques such as [[luminosity-functions-and-scaling-relations|abundance matching]] reveal that this [[average-star-formation-efficiency|star formation efficiency]] is strongly mass-dependent. It peaks at a characteristic [[pivot-mass|Pivot mass]] of $M_{\rm halo} \approx 10^{12} M_\odot$, where [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]] achieve their maximum baryon conversion efficiency [Behroozi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770...57B/abstract). 

The physical processes governing this mass-dependent efficiency are frequently conceptualized through quasi-equilibrium or [[bathtub-models|Bathtub models]]. In this framework, galaxies exist in a slowly evolving balance between cosmological [[galaxy-accretion|gas accretion]], gas consumption via star formation, and feedback-driven outflows. In [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], intense stellar feedback—driven by [[extragalactic-supernovae|supernovae]], radiation pressure, and cosmic rays—readily ejects gas from shallow potential wells, keeping the overall star formation efficiency low. Conversely, in [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] hosted by halos well above $10^{12} M_\odot$, [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|active galactic nucleus (AGN)]] feedback and virial shock heating prevent hot circumgalactic gas from cooling, sharply quenching further star formation. These regulatory baryon cycles establish tight [[fundamental-scaling-relations|universal scaling relations]], such as the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]], and dictate the [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|chemical evolution]] encoded in the [[abundance-gradients|Abundance gradients]] and [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|mass-metallicity relations]] of early [[star-forming-galaxies|Star-forming galaxies]].

Recently, the paradigm of high-redshift star formation has been fundamentally challenged by observations from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). Pre-launch extrapolations of the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology predicted a steep decline in the abundance of bright galaxies at $z > 10$ due to lower halo masses and assumed low efficiencies. Instead, JWST has revealed an unexpected overabundance of extraordinarily massive and [[luminous-blue-stars-and-galaxies|UV-luminous galaxies]] at cosmic dawn. This tension has ignited intense debate over whether early galaxies operated with a drastically evolving star formation efficiency—potentially reaching $\epsilon_\star \approx 0.5-1.0$ through feedback-free [[extreme-starbursts|starbursts]] in dense, gas-rich environments. Resolving this discrepancy is a central frontier in [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]], with open questions focusing on whether these observations require entirely new efficiency physics, or if they can be explained by bursty star formation histories, reduced dust attenuation, and variations in the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] biased toward massive, [[stellar-populations-by-metallicity|metal-poor stars]] [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954L..48W/abstract), [Mauerhofer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202647M/abstract).

## Conceptual Framework of Cosmic Star Formation Efficiency

Star formation efficiency (SFE), often parameterized as the [[average-star-formation-efficiency|baryon conversion efficiency]] $\epsilon_\star \equiv M_\star / (f_b M_{\rm halo})$, quantifies the fraction of available cosmic [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|baryons]] that a dark matter halo successfully converts into stars [Behroozi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770...57B/abstract), [Shuntov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008290S/abstract). This metric is central to understanding [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|Stellar Mass Assembly and Functions]] across cosmic time. By mapping the observed [[galaxy-luminosity-function|galaxy stellar mass function]] to the underlying dark matter halo mass function, models establish the empirical [[astrophysical-mass-scaling-relations|stellar-to-halo mass relation]] (SHMR). A ubiquitous finding across redshifts is that the SFE is a strong, non-monotonic function of halo mass, peaking at approximately $M_{\rm halo} \approx 10^{12} M_\odot$ where galaxies achieve their maximum conversion efficiency [Behroozi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770...57B/abstract), [Furlanetto et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.3394F/abstract). 

The shape of the SHMR and the resulting SFE are primarily governed by the balance of [[galaxy-accretion|gas accretion]] and feedback mechanisms rather than the small-scale physics of star formation itself [Furlanetto et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.3394F/abstract). Indeed, observations of HI-selected galaxies reveal a remarkably constant atomic gas star formation efficiency across five orders of magnitude in [[stellar-component|stellar mass]], consistent with models of marginally stable disks [Wong et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.1106W/abstract). In the context of quasi-equilibrium or [[bathtub-models|Bathtub models]], galaxies exist in a slowly evolving balance between cosmological gas inflow, star formation, and feedback-driven outflows [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract), [Mitra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1184M/abstract). In low-mass halos ($M_{\rm halo} < 10^{11} M_\odot$), stellar feedback driven by supernovae and radiation pressure, potentially amplified by cosmic rays, ejects gas and suppresses the SFE [Hanasz et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777L..38H/abstract), [Diesing et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvL.121i1101D/abstract). Conversely, in massive halos ($M_{\rm halo} > 10^{12} M_\odot$), [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|active galactic nucleus (AGN)]] feedback and virial shock heating prevent gas from cooling, causing the SFE to decline sharply [Shin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711..268S/abstract), [Katsianis et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472..919K/abstract).

While the local and intermediate-redshift universe is well-described by this paradigm, the evolution of SFE at high redshifts ($z > 10$) is currently a subject of intense debate. Pre-JWST extrapolations of the standard $\Lambda$CDM model predicted a precipitous drop in the abundance of bright galaxies. However, recent JWST observations have revealed an unexpected abundance of [[massive-galaxies|massive]] ($M_\star \gtrsim 10^{10} M_\odot$), [[luminous-blue-stars-and-galaxies|UV-luminous galaxies]] at $z > 10$ [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954L..48W/abstract), [Shuntov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008290S/abstract). To reconcile this, some theoretical models propose a rapidly evolving SFE, where the baryon conversion efficiency approaches unity ($\epsilon_\star \approx 0.5-1.0$) in early, dense environments [Li et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231114662L/abstract), [Shuntov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008290S/abstract), [Shuntov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A..20S/abstract). These feedback-free [[extreme-starbursts|starburst]] modes suggest that high gas densities at cosmic dawn enable star formation to outpace feedback, fundamentally altering [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] in the early universe [Li et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231114662L/abstract). 

The literature remains divided on whether such extreme efficiencies are strictly necessary. Alternative models demonstrate that standard structure formation can reproduce the JWST data through a combination of younger [[galactic-stellar-populations|stellar populations]], evolving IMFs, or altered dark matter halo collapse models [Donnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250103217D/abstract), [Yung et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250418618Y/abstract), [Fakhry et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.112l3503F/abstract), compounded by the asymmetric scatter introduced by bursty [[star-formation-history|star formation histories]], as well as systematic SED uncertainties and Eddington bias from the steep high-mass tail of the halo mass function [Krishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251113708K/abstract). Consequently, the true trajectory of cosmic SFE in the first billion years remains ambiguous, hinging on resolving these systematic and astrophysical uncertainties.

## Historical Evolution of the Cosmic Star Formation History

The quest to map the [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] of the Universe underwent a revolution in the 1990s with the advent of deep multi-wavelength imaging. Early efforts utilizing the Hubble Deep Field established the foundational "Madau-Lilly plot," which traces the evolution of the [[global-star-formation-rate|cosmic star formation rate density]], $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$, across cosmic time [Madau et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.283.1388M/abstract), [Madau et al. (1997)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998ASPC..148...88M/abstract). By employing [[bx-bm-galaxies|UV-dropout (Lyman-break)]] techniques to identify distant [[star-forming-galaxies|star-forming galaxies]], these pioneering studies demonstrated that the global luminosity density rose sharply from the local universe to a peak at intermediate redshifts.

Subsequent decades of multi-wavelength synthesis have solidified this picture. It is now well-established that the global $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$ peaked approximately $3.5\text{ Gyr}$ after the Big Bang at $z \approx 1.9-2.5$, an epoch often termed "Cosmic Noon" ([[peak-star-formation-history|Peak star formation history]]), and has since declined exponentially with an e-folding timescale of $3.9\text{ Gyr}$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Consequently, half of the [[stellar-component|stellar mass]] observed today was formed before $z \approx 1.3$ [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). Furthermore, analysis of the 'cosmic spectrum' from large galaxy surveys places a strong upper limit on early star formation, suggesting that no more than 80% of stars formed before $z=1$ [Baldry et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...569..582B/abstract). Interestingly, [[quiescent-galaxies|quiescent galaxies]] at a fixed stellar mass exhibit more rapid historical evolutionary timescales compared to galaxies that are still forming stars [Pacifici et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...824...45P/abstract). Notably, the luminosity density evolution of the star-forming population closely mirrors the accretion history of supermassive black holes, suggesting shared regulatory mechanisms driving both galactic and nuclear growth [Boyle et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.293L..49B/abstract), [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract). In these early epochs, the cosmic luminosity density was significantly dominated by [[elliptical-and-early-type-galaxies|massive ellipticals]] undergoing [[extreme-starbursts|intense starbursts]], which may have ejected a large fraction of 'missing metals' into the [[galaxy-cluster-mass-profiles|intra-cluster medium]] [Calura et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...596..734C/abstract).

Historical galaxy formation models initially attributed the redshift evolution of $\dot{\rho}_\star(z)$ to simple gas exhaustion. Modern theoretical frameworks have since shifted toward [[bathtub-models|Bathtub models]] representing a quasi-equilibrium between cosmological gas inflow, star formation, and feedback-driven outflows [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). Despite the success of these equilibrium models in reproducing general [[luminosity-functions-and-scaling-relations|Luminosity Functions and Scaling Relations]], a persistent observational tension remains: the time integral of the cosmic star formation rate density over-predicts the directly observed [[stellar-mass-assembly-and-functions|stellar mass density]] by approximately $\sim 0.3$ dex at $z < 3.5$ [Choi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.419.1280C/abstract), [Shuntov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008290S/abstract), [Shuntov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A..20S/abstract). The literature remains ambiguous as to whether this discrepancy arises from missing faint [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]] in high-redshift surveys, or if it implies a non-universal [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] that evolves with redshift [Davé et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ASPC..440..353D/abstract), [Choi et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.419.1280C/abstract). Furthermore, recent observations highlight that [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|dust-obscured galaxies]] found via [[radio-luminosity-function|radio emission]] can account for up to $40\%$ of the SFRD at $z > 4.5$, a substantial contribution that is often missed by UV surveys [Talia et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...909...23T/abstract).

Most profoundly, recent observations by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have challenged extrapolations calibrated to the historical Madau-Lilly plot, revealing an unexpected abundance of extraordinarily UV-luminous and massive galaxies at $z > 10$ [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954L..48W/abstract), [Ceverino et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240402537C/abstract). Current literature presents conflicting interpretations of this tension. Some models suggest that the star formation efficiency must increase sharply to values approaching $\epsilon \approx 0.5-1.0$ in early dark matter halos, possibly driven by feedback-free starbursts at extreme gas densities [Shuntov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008290S/abstract), [Shuntov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A..20S/abstract). Conversely, other studies argue that $\Lambda$CDM models remain intact if one accounts for evolving stellar populations or observational systematics. The resolution of this high-redshift efficiency debate remains an active and highly contested frontier in extragalactic astrophysics.

## The Mass-Dependency of Efficiency Peaks

The efficiency with which dark matter halos convert their cosmological baryon accretion into stars is a strong, non-monotonic function of halo mass. Evidence from [[luminosity-functions-and-scaling-relations|abundance matching]], gravitational lensing, and [[satellite-stellar-mass-functions|satellite kinematics]] consistently demonstrates that the [[integrated-star-formation|Integrated star formation]] efficiency peaks at a characteristic [[pivot-mass|Pivot mass]] of $M_{\rm halo} \approx 10^{12} M_\odot$ [Behroozi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770...57B/abstract). At this optimal mass scale, galaxies achieve their maximum [[high-star-formation-efficiency|baryon conversion efficiency]] [Behroozi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770...57B/abstract), [Mitra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1184M/abstract).

This mass-dependency arises from the varying dominance of feedback mechanisms that regulate the "bathtub" equilibrium of gas inflows, outflows, and consumption [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). In low-mass halos ($M_{\rm halo} \ll 10^{12} M_\odot$), star formation is highly inefficient due to intense regulation by stellar feedback. In fact, matching the observed UV luminosity function requires a strict minimum halo mass constraint for galaxy formation of approximately $10^{9.4} M_\odot$ at $z=7-8$ [Muñoz et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...729...99M/abstract). [[dwarf-galaxy-physical-processes|Supernova-driven winds]], potentially amplified by cosmic ray momentum deposition, readily eject gas from the shallow potential wells of these systems [Hanasz et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777L..38H/abstract), [Diesing et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvL.121i1101D/abstract). Conversely, in massive halos ($M_{\rm halo} \gg 10^{12} M_\odot$), the efficiency is sharply suppressed by a combination of virial shock heating—which prevents hot halo gas from cooling rapidly—and mechanical feedback from [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|active galactic nuclei (AGN)]] [Shin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711..268S/abstract). The $10^{12} M_\odot$ scale thus represents the transitional "sweet spot" where the potential well is deep enough to retain cold gas against stellar feedback, but not yet dominated by AGN quenching.

This paradigm is fundamentally driven by the evolution of [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]], which reside at the bottom of the halo potential well and are the primary recipients of cosmic gas streams. By contrast, galaxies that fall into larger halos are subject to [[dwarf-galaxy-evolution-and-interactions|satellite quenching]]. Once accreted, these satellites lose their cosmological gas supply to strangulation and suffer ram-pressure stripping, causing their specific star formation rates to plummet as they transition into [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] [Quadri et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...744...88Q/abstract), [Schawinski et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.440..889S/abstract). In lower-mass and intermediate environments, nearly 50% of galaxies with [[besan-on-galaxy-model|Milky Way-like]] rotation velocities are estimated to have quenched their star formation since $z=1$ [Boissier et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...522A..18B/abstract). While the growth of a [[stellar-and-galactic-core-structure|stellar bulge]] correlates with reduced star formation efficiency, additional processes beyond disk stabilization are necessary to explain the quenching of these [[late-type-galaxies-and-stellar-populations|late-type galaxies]] [Eales et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491...69E/abstract). As a result, the global correlation between halo mass and star formation efficiency is overwhelmingly dictated by the central galaxy population.

Empirical models show that this characteristic halo mass remains the most efficient site for star formation across most of cosmic history, from the local universe out to at least $z \sim 8$ [Behroozi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770...57B/abstract). At intermediate redshifts, approximately 40% of massive galaxies at $z \sim 1$ undergo significant star formation episodes that double their stellar mass between $z=0.4$ and $1.4$, often accompanied by [[specific-merger-rate|major mergers]] [Conselice et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..399..190C/abstract). However, cosmological simulations suggest that smooth gas accretion, rather than mergers, is the dominant driver of stellar mass growth overall, with mergers directly contributing only $\sim 25\%$ of today's stellar mass [Martin et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472L..50M/abstract). In dense environments, the classical [[butcher-oemler-effect|Butcher-Oemler effect]] is naturally explained in [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|hierarchical clustering]] scenarios where high-redshift clusters undergo more rapid assembly and frequent merging than their low-redshift counterparts [Kauffmann et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.274..153K/abstract). The absolute value of this peak efficiency is not temporally constant; the baryon conversion efficiency of massive halos drops markedly after $z \sim 2.5$, coinciding with the global, exponential decline in the [[global-star-formation-rate|cosmic star formation rate density]] [Behroozi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770...57B/abstract), [Madau et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ARA&A..52..415M/abstract).

At the most extreme redshifts ($z > 10$), the universality of this mass-efficiency relationship is currently heavily debated in light of recent JWST discoveries. Whether the integrated star formation efficiency increases dramatically to $\epsilon \approx 0.5-1.0$ in early halos [Shuntov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008290S/abstract), or if the underlying efficiency remains standard while other factors boost luminosity [Donnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250103217D/abstract), is a central question in modern galaxy evolution studies.

## Feedback Regulation and Baryon Cycling

The regulation of star formation in high-redshift galaxies is fundamentally governed by [[baryonic-matter-and-evolution|baryon cycling]], wherein galaxies exist in a slowly evolving quasi-equilibrium state. In these [[bathtub-models|Bathtub models]], the continuous inflow of cosmological gas is balanced by the consumption of gas through star formation and its expulsion via feedback-driven outflows [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). Consequently, the [[average-star-formation-efficiency|global star formation efficiency]] and the [[normalized-star-formation-rate|specific star formation rate]] are heavily dictated by the accretion rate of the host dark matter halo, with the condition that gas accretion roughly equals the sum of the star formation rate (SFR) and the outflow rate [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract).

To maintain this equilibrium and reproduce observed [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]], powerful feedback loops are required to suppress the otherwise rapid collapse of cooling gas. In [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], this suppression is predominantly driven by [[dwarf-galaxy-physical-processes|stellar feedback]]. Supernovae and stellar winds inject energy and momentum into the [[stellar-distributions|interstellar medium]], regulating the [[gas-fraction|Gas fraction]] and driving ubiquitous galactic outflows. In fact, ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs) serve as extreme testbeds for efficiency physics, often exhibiting low star formation efficiencies relative to their atomic gas mass down to 500 pc scales [Kado-Fong et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...11K/abstract). In the smallest systems with star formation rates below $1 M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$, supernova feedback can effectively quench star formation after just $\sim 10$ Myr, significantly lowering their star formation efficiency and limiting their overall contribution to [[early-universe|cosmic reionization]] [Wyithe et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.2741W/abstract). Recent theoretical advances emphasize the critical role of non-thermal components, specifically [[dwarf-galaxy-physical-processes|cosmic ray feedback]], in this regime. Cosmic rays accelerated in supernova remnant shocks can boost the effective momentum deposition of the blast waves by factors of $2-3$, which is particularly impactful in environments with severe radiative cooling losses [Diesing et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvL.121i1101D/abstract). Additionally, cosmological gas infall onto a galaxy may contribute substantially to the acceleration of these cosmic rays, providing further non-thermal feedback pressure [Hedrick et al. (1977)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977ApJ...215..208H/abstract). The inclusion of cosmic rays drives fast ($> 10^3$ km s$^{-1}$), multiphase winds and yields a mass loading factor—the ratio of the gas mass outflow rate to the SFR—of order unity in high-redshift, [[luminous-gas-rich-disc-galaxies|gas-rich disks]] [Hanasz et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777L..38H/abstract). Furthermore, cosmic ray pressure can significantly reduce star formation efficiencies in low-mass halos, an effect that becomes progressively stronger at smaller mass scales [Pfrommer et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ASPC..379..221P/abstract). Models incorporating cosmic rays and magnetic fields naturally produce the bursty star formation histories characteristic of early dwarf galaxies while effectively retaining metals within the host system [Owen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221206469O/abstract), [Martin-Alvarez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603245M/abstract).

At the opposite end of the mass spectrum, stellar feedback is insufficient to unbind gas from the deep potential wells of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] (typically those residing in halos exceeding $\sim 10^{12} M_\odot$). In these systems, [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|AGN feedback]] becomes the dominant regulatory mechanism, heating or ejecting the circumgalactic and interstellar media to effectively quench star formation and transition objects into [[quiescent-galaxies|Quiescent galaxies]] [Mitra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1184M/abstract). Alongside AGN, stellar endpoints inject substantial energy; notably, the X-ray luminosity in the early universe is predicted to transition from being dominated by low-mass X-ray binaries to high-mass X-ray binaries at redshifts $z > 2.5$ [Fragos et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764...41F/abstract). However, formulating a consistently robust physical model for AGN feedback remains challenging; for instance, purely mechanical AGN feedback models often struggle to simultaneously satisfy empirical constraints for X-ray luminosities and the [[astrophysical-mass-scaling-relations|scaling relations between black hole mass and stellar mass]] without over-evacuating the halo gas; combining radiative and mechanical feedback is proposed as the likely solution to satisfy these simultaneous constraints [Shin et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711..268S/abstract).

Ultimately, the stellar mass-halo mass relation across cosmic time is largely decoupled from the small-scale physics of individual star-forming clouds, depending instead almost entirely on the macroscopic balance of these feedback mechanisms [Furlanetto et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.3394F/abstract). Fitting these equilibrium models to observed scaling relations suggests that galactic outflow scalings lie intermediate between purely energy-driven and momentum-driven winds, highlighting the complex, multi-scale nature of baryon cycling during [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] [Mitra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1184M/abstract). Furthermore, evidence from high-velocity-dispersion disks suggests that star formation, rather than cosmic accretion, acts as the primary energetic driver of [[galactic-disk-evolution|galaxy disk turbulence]] across all cosmic epochs [Green et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.467..684G/abstract). This is corroborated by recent models indicating a tight correlation exists between galactic star formation efficiency and the product of gas turbulent velocity dispersion and the host galaxy's angular velocity [Vollmer et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241118420V/abstract).

## The JWST Challenge: High-Redshift Efficiency Evolution

The advent of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has revealed an unexpected abundance of bright, [[massive-galaxies|massive galaxies]] at [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|redshifts $z > 10$]]. Pre-launch [[semi-empirical-galaxy-evolution|semi-analytic models]] based on standard $\Lambda$CDM extrapolations predicted a rapid decline in the [[galaxy-luminosity-function|UV luminosity function]] at these early epochs, operating under the assumption that star formation efficiencies remained comparable to those observed at lower redshifts [Somerville et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250505442S/abstract). Instead, the high stellar mass densities inferred from early JWST data present a significant challenge to standard models of [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]].

To reconcile these observations with theoretical dark matter halo mass functions, many models now invoke a strongly redshift-dependent [[average-star-formation-efficiency|star formation efficiency]] (SFE). Abundance matching and hydrodynamical simulations suggest that the integrated star formation efficiency, defined as the fraction of accreted baryons converted into stars ($\epsilon_{\star} \equiv M_{\star} / (f_{\rm b} M_{\rm halo})$), must increase dramatically from $\sim 0.1$ at $z \sim 3.5$ to values approaching $0.5 - 1.0$ at $z \sim 10$ [Shuntov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008290S/abstract), [Shuntov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A..20S/abstract). The cosmic baryon fraction sets theoretical lower limits on the dark matter halo masses required for a given stellar mass; exploring these limits across the redshift ranges probed by JWST and WFIRST (now the Roman Space Telescope) is critical for testing whether $\Lambda$CDM-consistent halo densities can actually support such observed massive galaxies [Behroozi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.5382B/abstract). Furthermore, reconciling these extreme UV luminosity functions with large-scale [[galaxy-overdensities|galaxy clustering]] and bias measurements at $z > 11$ requires a mass-dependent star formation duty cycle that approaches unity at these extreme redshifts [Chakraborty et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250307590C/abstract).

One physical mechanism proposed to drive this extreme efficiency is the onset of feedback-free [[extreme-starbursts|starbursts]]. In this regime, the extremely high gas densities present in massive halos at cosmic dawn allow star formation to proceed rapidly before stellar feedback (such as supernova-driven winds) can effectively disperse the star-forming clouds [Li et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231114662L/abstract). This feedback-free phase is predicted to last $\sim 100$ Myr, yielding highly compact systems with low [[gas-fraction|gas fractions]] and [[low-metallicity-galaxies|low metallicities]] [Li et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231114662L/abstract), [Ceverino et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240402537C/abstract). Other models achieve similar consistency with observations by implementing a density-modulated star formation efficiency, where the fraction of dense gas directly dictates the efficiency, thereby boosting the predicted abundance of UV-luminous galaxies at $z > 10$ [Somerville et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250505442S/abstract).

Alternatively, the discrepancy between the observed UV light and the predicted stellar mass could be an artifact of assuming a universal [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]]. If early galaxies harbored a top-heavy IMF or significant populations of metal-poor Population III stars, their UV luminosities and [[first-star-formation|ionizing photon production efficiencies]] would be significantly enhanced for a given stellar mass [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...954L..48W/abstract), [Mauerhofer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202647M/abstract). Indeed, discrepancies between integrated star formation and observed stellar mass density can be partially resolved by invoking a 'bottom-light' evolving IMF that weights toward massive stars at high redshift [Kang et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...713.1301K/abstract). Reconciling the [[early-universe|cosmic reionization history]] with the observed galaxy luminosity function may also require such a bimodal IMF or the presence of a significant population of unseen faint, active star-forming galaxies [Vangioni et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447.2575V/abstract). To complement traditional galaxy surveys, [[transient-host-galaxies|gamma-ray burst (GRB)]] rates provide independent constraints on the cosmic star formation history, suggesting that high-redshift star formation was sufficient to reionize the universe [Kistler et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...705L.104K/abstract). Recent semi-analytic 'abcd' models demonstrate that Population III star formation can persist down to $z \sim 5$ in massive halos by jointly following the evolution of pristine and metal-enriched gas [Hegde et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250719581H/abstract). This scenario naturally explains the bright UV luminosity function during the [[early-universe|epoch of reionization]] without requiring a near-unity conversion of baryons into stars. Conversely, minimalist feedback models predict that while global star formation declines by a factor of 20 between $z=6$ and $15$, the abundance of bright galaxies accessible to current instruments declines even more steeply, exacerbating the tension if feedback remains efficient [Furlanetto et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.1576F/abstract).

The literature currently reflects active debate regarding whether this efficiency evolution or new physics is strictly necessary. Several studies argue that the standard cosmological framework is not broken, proposing that the apparent excess of massive galaxies can be entirely explained by assuming exceptionally young stellar populations, bursty star formation histories, and elevated light-to-mass ratios [Donnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250103217D/abstract), [Yung et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250418618Y/abstract). Furthermore, systematic uncertainties in SED-derived stellar masses play a critical role. Asymmetric scatter induced by the steeply-falling high-mass tail of the halo mass function can artificially inflate the inferred abundance of massive galaxies, largely resolving the tension when modeled rigorously [Krishnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251113708K/abstract). Hierarchical assembly biases—where observers incorrectly assume that all stars in a [[descendant-galaxies|descendant galaxy]] formed *in situ*—can also lead to overestimates of historic stellar masses by over an order of magnitude [Cochrane et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250902700C/abstract). Finally, some authors suggest that modifications to the underlying dark matter halo collapse physics or enhanced small-scale power spectra [Fakhry et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025PhRvD.112l3503F/abstract), as well as introducing primordial non-Gaussianity [Biagetti et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944..113B/abstract), might alleviate the tension without requiring extreme astrophysical efficiencies. 

Whether the JWST luminous galaxy excess is driven by a fundamental shift in early-universe star formation efficiency, an evolving IMF, or observational systematics remains an open question at the forefront of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]].

## Scaling Relations and the Star-Forming Main Sequence

The tight [[sfr-stellar-mass-correlation|SFR-Stellar Mass correlation]] observed across much of cosmic time defines the [[star-forming-main-sequence|Star-Forming Main Sequence]] (SFMS). The normalization of the SFMS, conventionally parametrized via the [[normalized-star-formation-rate|specific star formation rate]] ($\text{sSFR} \equiv \text{SFR}/M_*$), evolves strongly as a function of redshift. While low-redshift observations show a gradual decline in sSFR, high-redshift measurements indicate that the median sSFR at a fixed stellar mass flattens *compared* to the specific mass accretion rate of the host dark matter halos [Behroozi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...799...32B/abstract). This tracking behavior forms the theoretical foundation for [[bathtub-models|Bathtub models]], where galaxies are modeled as evolving in a quasi-equilibrium state regulated by a balance between cosmological gas inflow, star formation, and feedback-driven outflows [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract), [Furlanetto et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.3394F/abstract).

The evolution of the SFMS and its scatter is not uniform across all mass scales, reflecting a [[morphological-downsizing|"downsizing"]] scenario in early galaxy assembly [Juneau et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...619L.135J/abstract). Overall, specific star formation rates decline with stellar mass at all redshifts $z < 1.1$, indicating that most massive galaxies assembled the bulk of their mass before redshift unity [Feulner et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MNRAS.358L...1F/abstract). Measurements of the specific star formation rate function demonstrate that the redshift evolution of the median sSFR scales approximately as $(1+z)^b$, where the exponent $b$ is highly [[mass-scaling-relations|mass-dependent]]: it increases from $b \approx 2.88$ for lower-mass galaxies ($\log_{10}(M_*/M_\odot) \approx 9.75$) to $b \approx 3.78$ for more massive systems ($\log_{10}(M_*/M_\odot) \approx 11.1$) [Ilbert et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...579A...2I/abstract). Furthermore, the intrinsic scatter of the specific star formation rate function broadens significantly at higher masses (ranging from $0.28$ dex to $0.46$ dex), indicating an increasing diversity of [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] profiles as massive galaxies gradually exhaust their gas reservoirs or undergo [[mini-quenched-galaxies|quenching]] [Ilbert et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...579A...2I/abstract). Deep JWST observations spanning $z \sim 3-7$ further emphasize this mass dependence; the SFMS is already well-established for [[high-mass-galaxies|High-mass galaxies]] ($\log_{10}(M_*/M_\odot) > 9$) by $z \sim 5-7$, whereas it only becomes a distinct, significant sequence for lower-mass systems at $z < 4$ [Rinaldi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240613554R/abstract).

Environmental factors also play a subtle but crucial role in these scaling relations. The scaling relations between color, mass, sSFR, and metallicity are sensitive to the large-scale geometric environment, such as [[galaxy-overdensities|filaments versus sheets]], even when matching stellar mass distributions [Nandi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024JCAP...02..012N/abstract). Furthermore, galaxies within large-scale structures (LSS) at $z \sim 0.7$ can maintain field-level star formation rates regardless of local density, suggesting the LSS environment can override local quenching mechanisms [Ren et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.3071R/abstract).

At the extreme low-mass end of these scaling relations ($\log_{10}(M_*/M_\odot) < 7$), the smooth quasi-equilibrium paradigm breaks down, and galaxies are found predominantly in a starburst regime [Rinaldi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240613554R/abstract). In these [[nascent-galaxies|nascent]] [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], star formation is highly stochastic, regulated by rapid cycles of intense star formation followed by delayed supernova and cosmic ray feedback that temporarily quenches the system [Martin-Alvarez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603245M/abstract). This bursty star formation has profound implications for interpreting the high-redshift universe. Bursty cycles introduce an asymmetric scatter in light-to-mass ratios and systematically boost the time-averaged [[instantaneous-star-formation-rate|Instantaneous star formation rate]] alongside associated far-infrared emission lines. Consequently, semi-analytic models and line intensity mapping (LIM) power spectra—such as those targeting [C II] 158 $\mu$m—must explicitly account for burst-driven large-scale clustering excesses, which cannot be accurately captured by invoking a simple mass-independent logarithmic scatter [Liu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...974..175L/abstract), [Liu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240104204L/abstract). The tension between traditional smooth equilibrium models and the unexpected abundance of UV-bright galaxies at $z > 10$ highlights that stochastic, burst-driven feedback is a critical component of the high-redshift SFMS. Despite this stochasticity, certain universal regulatory mechanisms appear to persist across cosmic time. For instance, a tight linear correlation between HCN luminosity (a tracer for dense gas) and infrared luminosity (tracing the star formation rate) holds across nine orders of magnitude, connecting local dense cores to high-redshift [[extreme-starbursts|extreme starbursts]] [Gao et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007IAUS..237..331G/abstract). Furthermore, the peak star formation intensity limit in these starburst systems remains approximately constant from the local universe out to $z \approx 6$, suggesting that universal physical mechanisms regulate maximum star formation densities regardless of epoch [Hathi et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...673..686H/abstract).

## Chemical Evolution and Metallicity-Efficiency Scaling

The [[galaxy-chemical-enrichment-mechanisms|chemical enrichment]] of the interstellar medium serves as a fossil record of a galaxy's integrated star formation history, encoding the delicate balance between cosmological gas accretion, star formation, and metal-enriched outflows. In the framework of [[bathtub-models|Bathtub models]], galaxies are posited to exist in a slowly evolving quasi-equilibrium state where the cosmological gas inflow rate approximately balances gas consumption and ejection [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract). This mass-conservation principle can be expressed as:

$$ \dot{M}_{\rm in} \approx \dot{M}_* + \dot{M}_{\rm out} $$

where $\dot{M}_{\rm in}$ is the gas accretion rate, $\dot{M}_*$ is the [[global-star-formation-rate|star formation rate]], and $\dot{M}_{\rm out}$ is the feedback-driven outflow rate. This equilibrium directly shapes the cosmic evolution of the [[metallicity-distributions-and-scaling-relations|mass-metallicity relation]]. Analytical formulations demonstrating this balance successfully reproduce observed chemical trends at $z \sim 2$, illustrating that sustained high star formation rates at [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|cosmic noon]] require continuous accretion that is heavily regulated by outflow rates comparable to the star formation rate [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract). Crucially, global galaxy properties from high redshifts down to the local universe are best fit by a linear star formation-molecular gas relation, characterized by a universal [[h2-regulated-star-formation|gas depletion time]] of approximately 2.5 Gyr [Feldmann et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.433.1910F/abstract).

The depth of a galaxy's potential well and its star formation efficiency (SFE) fundamentally govern its chemical evolution. A stellar Fundamental Metallicity Relation (FMR) has been identified (part of the broader [[fundamental-scaling-relations|Fundamental scaling relations]]), establishing a smooth continuous surface that correlates stellar mass, star formation rate, and light-weighted stellar metallicity across populations ranging from starbursts to [[passive-galaxies|Passive galaxies]] [Looser et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.2832L/abstract). At high redshifts, chemical scaling relations highlight the profound role of varying efficiencies. Observations indicate that at a fixed stellar mass, smaller [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]] exhibit systematically higher stellar metallicities. This anti-correlation between galaxy size and metallicity is driven by elevated star formation efficiencies, which accelerate chemical enrichment compared to more extended, lower-efficiency systems [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251002573W/abstract). Simulations tracking the [[early-universe|epoch of reionization]] similarly predict that mass-metallicity relations flatten at high stellar masses—a feature attributed to the assumed subgrid physics for star formation and feedback—while the ratio of stellar to gas metallicity is shown to decrease as stellar age increases [Noel et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221016750N/abstract).

Spatially resolved [[chemical-abundances|Chemical Abundances]] offer critical insights into the physical mechanisms driving galaxy assembly, such as [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]] and radial gas mixing. Recent high-resolution observations spanning $1.5 \lesssim z \lesssim 9$ reveal a dynamic, V-shaped evolution in radial [[abundance-gradients|Abundance gradients]]. At cosmic dawn ($z > 5$), galaxies typically exhibit strong negative abundance gradients of approximately $-0.4 \text{ dex kpc}^{-1}$, indicating intense, centralized star formation. As galaxies evolve toward $z \approx 2$, these gradients systematically flatten, reflecting enhanced gas mixing driven by vigorous feedback and gas accretion. Moreover, localized accretion events can spur rapid chemical and evolutionary changes; spectroscopic evidence from local extremely metal-poor galaxies (XMPs) suggests that localized starbursts can be triggered by the recent infall of low-metallicity gas from the cosmic web [Sánchez Almeida et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...810L..15S/abstract). Following the peak of the cosmic star formation rate density, gradients gradually become negative again as the universe approaches $z=0$ [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250612129L/abstract).

However, the literature exhibits clear tensions between these observations and theoretical models regarding spatial chemical evolution. Many modern cosmological simulations that employ relatively smooth stellar feedback models invariably predict strong negative metallicity gradients at early cosmic times. While these models yield gradients at $z > 6$ that are in mild tension with the flatter profiles seen in recent observations, they struggle significantly to reproduce the flat or occasionally positive gradients robustly observed during the $1 < z < 4$ epoch. This discrepancy indicates that smooth feedback models may not adequately mix metals throughout the interstellar medium, implying that either more explosive, bursty stellar feedback or additional subgrid turbulent metal diffusion is required to accurately model the spatial distribution of metals in high-redshift starbursts [Garcia et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250303804G/abstract).

## Empirical and Theoretical Modeling Approaches

The interpretation of high-redshift star formation efficiencies relies on a combination of empirical scaling relations, [[semi-empirical-galaxy-evolution|semi-analytical modeling]], and cosmological hydrodynamical simulations. These approaches aim to reconcile the underlying dark matter halo assembly with the observed baryonic properties of galaxies across cosmic time.

A foundational theoretical framework is the "bathtub" or equilibrium model, which posits that galaxies exist in a slowly evolving state of balance between cosmological gas inflow, star formation, and feedback-driven outflows [Davé et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.421...98D/abstract), [Mitra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452.1184M/abstract). Under this assumption, [[equilibrium-star-formation-rates|Equilibrium star formation rates]] and specific star formation rates (sSFR) are primarily governed by the dark matter halo accretion rate [Furlanetto et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.3394F/abstract). To reproduce observed chemical trends, the gas accretion rate must approximately equal the combined sum of the star formation rate and the mass outflow rate [Erb et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...674..151E/abstract).

Empirical models frequently utilize abundance matching to link observed galaxy stellar mass functions to theoretical dark matter halo mass functions. Across multiple epochs, this technique reveals that the conversion of baryons into stars is most efficient in halos near $\sim 10^{12} M_{\odot}$ [Behroozi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770...57B/abstract). Historically, early comparisons with hierarchical models indicated that a constant baryon conversion efficiency of approximately 2% could explain the observed star formation rate intensity distribution at high redshifts [Barkana et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002NewA....7..337B/abstract). However, abundance matching applied to recent JWST observations at $z > 5$ implies [[integrated-star-formation|integrated star formation efficiencies]] of $\epsilon_* \equiv M_* f_{\rm b}^{-1} M_{\rm halo}^{-1} \gtrsim 0.5$ for massive systems, indicating a significant departure from the efficiencies typically observed in the low-redshift universe [Shuntov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008290S/abstract), [Shuntov et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A..20S/abstract). When interpreting high-redshift data, it is crucial to contrast 'universal' star formation efficiency models against feedback-regulated ones; distinguishing between these models requires improved constraints on galaxy clustering and dust duty cycles [Mirocha et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.4534M/abstract). Additionally, the StAGE semi-empirical framework utilizes stellar archaeology to trace evolutionary histories, successfully alleviating tensions between the integrated SFRD and stellar mass density by accounting for NIR-dark [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|progenitors]] [Bosi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250322543B/abstract).

[[semi-empirical-galaxy-evolution|Semi-analytical models (SAMs)]] track [[galaxy-formation-and-assembly|Galaxy Formation and Assembly]] by overlaying simplified baryonic prescriptions onto dark matter merger trees. Recent SAMs exploring the [[early-universe|early universe]], such as DELPHI, indicate that reproducing the unexpected abundance of UV-bright galaxies at $z \gtrsim 11$ requires either a density-modulated star formation efficiency that increases sharply with redshift or a top-heavy [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] [Mauerhofer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...696A.157M/abstract), [Somerville et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250505442S/abstract). SAMs also highlight discrepancies in quenching pathways; models such as L-GALAXIES successfully predict that star-forming central galaxies are systematically more efficient at converting baryons into stars than quiescent centrals of the same halo mass, a feature currently missed by several state-of-the-art hydrodynamical simulations [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240807743W/abstract), [Wang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980..233W/abstract). Moreover, the evolution of the star-forming sequence in hierarchical galaxy formation models reveals that predicted specific star formation rates can be offset from observations at $z < 2$ by factors of $2-3$, suggesting that the gas reincorporation timescales from feedback need significant modification [Mitchell et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.2637M/abstract).

Hydrodynamical simulations explicitly resolve gas dynamics, cooling, and feedback. Historically, simulations such as the EAGLE suite have successfully reproduced the growth of the cosmic stellar mass density and the evolution of the galaxy stellar mass function out to $z=7$ [Furlong et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.450.4486F/abstract). More recent cosmological zoom-in suites like FirstLight and THESAN-ZOOM demonstrate that the halo-scale efficiency follows a double power-law dependence on halo mass, while undergoing a mild positive redshift evolution driven by the extreme gas densities present at cosmic dawn [Ceverino et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240402537C/abstract), [Shen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250301949S/abstract). [[extragalactic-supernovae|Supernova]] and [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|active galactic nuclei feedback]] are the primary regulators of the [[global-star-formation-rate|cosmic star formation rate density]] within these simulations [Katsianis et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472..919K/abstract). For instance, the Illustris simulation successfully reproduces the co-evolution of black holes and host galaxies at the massive end across cosmic time, while simultaneously noting a lack of tight scaling relations in low-mass, star-forming systems [Sijacki et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.452..575S/abstract). However, the implementation of feedback remains an area of active debate. Simulations employing smooth stellar feedback models struggle to reproduce the flat or positive metallicity gradients observed at $z \sim 1-4$, suggesting that early galaxy formation may be heavily regulated by highly bursty, non-thermal processes such as cosmic ray-driven winds [Garcia et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250303804G/abstract), [Martin-Alvarez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603245M/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Frontiers

The recent detection of unexpectedly massive and UV-luminous galaxies at $z > 10$ has invigorated debates surrounding the physical drivers of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. Reconciling these observations with standard $\Lambda$CDM models requires determining whether these early systems underwent extreme feedback-free [[extreme-starbursts|starbursts]] with efficiencies approaching $\epsilon \approx 0.5-1.0$ [Li et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv231114662L/abstract), [Shuntov et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241008290S/abstract), or if the data can be explained by younger stellar populations, bursty star formation histories, and top-heavy variations in the [[initial-mass-function-imf|Initial Mass Function (IMF)]] [Donnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250103217D/abstract), [Mauerhofer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250202647M/abstract). Several critical frontiers must be addressed to resolve this tension.

A primary observational obstacle is understanding the nature of evolution in dust attenuation. Interpreting the rest-frame UV luminosities of nascent galaxies relies heavily on assumptions regarding dust production and geometry. Theoretical models suggest that the unexpectedly high number density of UV-bright galaxies might not require fundamentally broken physics, but rather could emerge from a combination of density-modulated star formation efficiencies and a rapid decrease in [[high-redshift-dusty-star-forming-galaxies|dust attenuation]] at very high redshifts [Somerville et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250505442S/abstract). At $z \gtrsim 11$, dust may be too dispersed to significantly impact UV luminosity [Mauerhofer et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...696A.157M/abstract). Furthermore, disentangling stellar light from the emission of high-redshift active galactic nuclei remains challenging; recent data indicate that early supermassive black hole accretion may actually outpace stellar mass assembly, complicating the derivation of purely stellar efficiencies [Volonteri et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...849..155V/abstract), [D'Silva et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250303431D/abstract). Finally, translating these observations into global luminosity functions is fundamentally limited by [[galaxy-overdensities|cosmic variance]], which dominates over Poisson noise for most high-redshift surveys outside of the brightest sources or at $z \gtrsim 12$ [Trapp et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499.2401T/abstract).

On the theoretical side, the exact role of non-thermal feedback mechanisms remains a major open question. Standard hydrodynamical simulations relying purely on mechanical supernova feedback often struggle to simultaneously reproduce realistic stellar mass assembly, metal retention, and observed outflow rates. Cosmic rays (CRs) are increasingly recognized as vital regulatory agents; they can boost supernova momentum deposition by factors of $2-3$ [Diesing et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhRvL.121i1101D/abstract). Incorporating CRs, magnetic fields, and radiation pressure into galaxy formation models produces fast, multi-phase mass-loaded outflows that help explain the highly bursty [[star-formation-history|Star Formation History]] observed in early dwarf galaxies, all without overly suppressing their [[metallicity|metal content]] [Owen et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv221206469O/abstract), [Martin-Alvarez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250603245M/abstract).

Ultimately, the global cosmic star formation rate is governed by the microphysics of [[massive-star-formation|giant molecular clouds (GMCs)]]. A persistent puzzle is the diversity of SFE within individual molecular clouds. While local observations imply that star formation is generally fast and inefficient—limited to cloud lifetimes of $\sim 10$ Myr with an integrated efficiency of $2-3\%$ due to rapid pre-supernova feedback —simulations reveal a broad $\sim 0.5$ dex scatter in the efficiency per free-fall time ($\epsilon_{\rm ff}$). This scatter does not correlate simply with global cloud properties like mass or velocity dispersion, but rather arises from the unique evolutionary path of each cloud [Grisdale et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.5482G/abstract).
