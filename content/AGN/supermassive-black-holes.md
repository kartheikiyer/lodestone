## Article

**Supermassive black holes** (SMBHs) are the most massive class of [[massive-compact-objects|Massive compact objects]] in the Universe, classically defined by masses ranging from $10^5$ to $10^{10}$ solar masses ($M_\odot$). Recent observational and theoretical constraints have expanded this demographic to include both [[ultramassive-black-holes|Ultramassive black holes]] exceeding $10^{11} M_\odot$ and lower-mass precursors that bridge the mass gap to [[intermediate-mass-astrophysical-objects|Intermediate-Mass Astrophysical Objects]]. SMBHs reside at the barycenters of virtually all [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] that possess a spheroidal stellar component [Kormendy et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ARA&A..33..581K/abstract), [Ferrarese et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005SSRv..116..523F/abstract). Through highly efficient [[accretion-processes|Accretion Processes]], where gravitational binding energy is dissipated by a surrounding accretion disk, SMBHs power the most luminous persistent phenomena in the cosmos. This energy conversion drives [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]], producing intense radiation, relativistic jets, and wide-angle winds that profoundly influence the thermodynamic state of their host environments.

The assembly of an SMBH is intimately tied to the evolutionary history of its host galaxy. In the local Universe, this connection is manifested through tight empirical scaling relations—such as the $M-\sigma$ relation—between the black hole mass and the structural properties of the host's [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] [Kormendy et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Natur.469..374K/abstract). These correlations strongly suggest a history of [[synchronized-coevolution|Synchronized coevolution]] driven by AGN feedback. As the black hole accretes, it injects massive amounts of mechanical and radiative energy into the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]], heating the gas, quenching large-scale star formation, and self-regulating further black hole growth [Di Matteo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005Natur.433..604D/abstract). Over cosmic time, hierarchical galaxy mergers further drive mass assembly by funneling gas into the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]] and creating bound [[supermassive-black-hole-binaries|Supermassive black hole binaries]]. The eventual coalescence of these binaries via gravitational wave emission can profoundly reshape the [[stellar-and-galactic-core-structure|Stellar and Galactic Core Structure]], occasionally imparting anisotropic momentum kicks that produce [[recoiling-supermassive-black-holes|Recoiling supermassive black holes]] or long-lived [[wandering-black-holes|Wandering black holes]].

Despite robust physical frameworks for late-time SMBH growth and feedback, the origins and early evolution of these central engines remain major open questions in modern astrophysics [Volonteri et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&ARv..18..279V/abstract). The existence of [[luminous-intermediate-redshift-quasars|Luminous intermediate redshift quasars]] within the first billion years of the Universe ($z > 6$) requires the rapid, early growth of [[massive-black-hole-seeds|Massive black hole seeds]]. Current theoretical debates center on whether these early seeds originated from the remnants of metal-free Population III stars, the monolithic gas collapse of [[direct-collapse-black-holes|Direct collapse black holes]], or runaway stellar collisions in dense primordial clusters. Furthermore, recent James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observations have revealed populations of [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]], gas-enshrouded active nuclei operating at an extreme [[eddington-ratio|Eddington ratio]]. These early systems frequently host an [[overmassive-black-hole|Overmassive black hole]] whose mass growth drastically preceded the stellar assembly of its host, directly challenging the paradigm of synchronized co-evolution and opening new observational frontiers in understanding black hole demographics across both massive and [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]].

## Defining the Supermassive Regime and Observational Demographics

Supermassive black holes are classically defined by masses ranging from $10^5$ to $10^{10} M_\odot$. While historical textbook definitions often cite $\sim 10^{10} M_\odot$ as an absolute upper mass limit, recent observational and theoretical work has expanded this boundary to include [[ultramassive-black-holes|Ultramassive black holes]] (UMBHs) that can reach or exceed $10^{11} M_\odot$ [Brockamp et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...585A.153B/abstract). Record-breaking discoveries, such as a 9.7 billion solar mass black hole in NGC 3842, demonstrate that some SMBHs significantly exceed predictions from standard scaling relations [McConnell et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Natur.480..215M/abstract). Furthermore, the detection of a 17 billion solar mass black hole in the relatively isolated galaxy NGC 1600 indicates that such ultramassive black holes are not restricted strictly to the centers of rich cluster environments [Thomas et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.532..340T/abstract). More recently, the discovery of an extreme 36 billion solar mass ultramassive black hole at the center of the Cosmic Horseshoe gravitational lens ($z = 0.44$) demonstrates that some central engines sit significantly above standard scaling relations [Melo-Carneiro et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250213788M/abstract). However, extreme mass estimates can sometimes face observational tension; for instance, adaptive optics spectroscopy of NGC 1277 revised its black hole mass down to $4.9$ billion solar masses, significantly lower than earlier claims [Walsh et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...817....2W/abstract). Theoretical models suggest a physical limit to mass growth via luminous accretion at $M_{\rm max} \simeq 5 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$ for typical parameters, potentially reaching up to $2.7 \times 10^{11} M_\odot$ for maximal prograde spin; above this threshold, the black hole cannot grow via a standard radiating accretion disk [King et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456L.109K/abstract). Theoretical arguments also suggest a mass limit between $10^{10}$ and $6 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$, driven by the transition of the accretion flow into an advection-dominated state that generates powerful, growth-halting outflows [Inayoshi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...828..110I/abstract).

Observationally, SMBHs are ubiquitous in the local Universe, residing in the galactic nuclei of virtually all [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] that possess a spheroidal component or [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]]. In these massive systems, the SMBH sits deep within the gravitational potential well, tightly anchored to the galaxy's barycenter. However, the demographics become markedly different—and the literature more ambiguous—at lower mass scales.

The occupation fraction—the percentage of galaxies hosting a central black hole—is observed to approach unity for high-mass ellipticals and early-type spirals. In contrast, the occupation fraction remains highly uncertain in [[low-mass-galaxies|Low-mass galaxies]], [[dwarf-spheroidal-and-elliptical-galaxies|Dwarf Spheroidal and Elliptical Galaxies]], and low-surface-brightness galaxies [Hodges-Kluck et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...898..106H/abstract). While intermediate-mass and [[low-mass-black-holes|Low-mass black holes]] do exist in some dwarf galaxies, their environments and growth mechanisms can be unconventional. Even in gas-poor systems, local massive black holes can be found; for example, a 3 million solar mass black hole was detected in the dwarf spheroidal galaxy Leo I, which is hypothesized to be accreting from the winds of evolved red giant branch stars [Pacucci et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...940L..33P/abstract). Cosmological simulations suggest that the dynamical evolution of these smaller systems is complex. In the dwarf regime, up to $50\%$ of massive black holes may fail to sink to the barycenter following galaxy mergers due to inefficient dynamical friction in shallow potential wells. This results in an elusive population of [[wandering-black-holes|Wandering black holes]] located several kiloparsecs away from the galactic center [Bellovary et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.2913B/abstract), [Bellovary et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.5129B/abstract). This theoretical prediction is supported by the eROSITA all-sky survey census of 74 AGN-dwarf galaxy pairs, which confirms that approximately 50% of AGN in these low-mass systems are indeed off-nuclear [Sacchi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240601707S/abstract).

Furthermore, a complete demographic census of SMBHs must account for objects that have been stripped of their original extended host galaxies. In dense cluster environments, tidal forces can disrupt satellite galaxies, leaving behind stripped, [[compact-galactic-nuclei|Compact galactic nuclei]] and ultra-compact dwarf galaxies (UCDs). Dynamical mass measurements and elevated mass-to-light ratios suggest that a significant fraction of massive UCDs host central black holes. This population of hidden SMBHs in stripped nuclei is predicted to increase the total supermassive black hole number density in galaxy clusters by $30\%$ to $100\%$ [Voggel et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...871..159V/abstract), [Mayes et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.4643M/abstract), revealing that the cosmic census of SMBHs extends well beyond the centers of intact, isolated galaxies.

## Early Formation Scenarios and Seed Models

The discovery of [[luminous-intermediate-redshift-quasars|Luminous intermediate redshift quasars]] at $z > 6$ implies extremely rapid growth within the first billion years of cosmic history [Smidt et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...865..126S/abstract), [Wise et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.566...85W/abstract). Explaining these early masses requires either sustained [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion and Feedback]] or the formation of large [[massive-black-hole-seeds|Massive black hole seeds]]. The literature currently debates several primary models for [[smbh-seeding|SMBH seeding]], which generally fall into light, intermediate, and heavy seed categories.

**Light Seeds**
The earliest massive stars formed in metal-poor environments and are expected to leave behind Population III remnants with masses of $100 - 1000 M_\odot$ [Bellovary et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...742...13B/abstract), [Wise et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230409311W/abstract). Growing a $\sim 100 M_\odot$ light seed to $10^9 M_\odot$ by $z \sim 7$ requires continuous accretion near the Eddington limit, a process that may be severely bottlenecked by radiative feedback or metal diffusion in the intergalactic medium [Bellovary et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...742...13B/abstract). However, episodic hyper-Eddington accretion, wherein photon trapping suppresses negative feedback, might allow these stellar remnants to reach intermediate masses of $10^3 - 10^5 M_\odot$ in early galactic nuclei [Ryu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.4122R/abstract).

**Heavy Seeds**
To circumvent the stringent timescale constraints of light seeds, heavy seed models propose the formation of [[direct-collapse-black-holes|Direct collapse black holes]] (DCBHs) with initial masses of $10^4 - 10^6 M_\odot$ [Matsumoto et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...810...64M/abstract), [Pacucci et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...850L..42P/abstract). This mechanism typically involves the monolithic collapse of a pre-galactic gas cloud or a supermassive star. DCBH formation requires preventing gas fragmentation, which can be achieved through strong Lyman-Werner radiation suppressing molecular hydrogen cooling, or via substantial dynamical heating from rapid mass infall in overdense regions [Wise et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.566...85W/abstract). Recent JWST observations revealing an [[overmassive-black-hole|Overmassive black hole]] at $z \approx 10$ with an estimated mass of $\sim 8 \times 10^7 M_\odot$ provide strong observational support for a heavy seeding origin, as such extreme masses at early epochs are difficult to reconcile with light seed models [Kovács et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...965L..21K/abstract).

**Runaway Stellar Collisions**
An intermediate pathway relies on stellar dynamics in the dense cores of [[protogalaxies|Protogalaxies]]. Through mass segregation, massive stars rapidly concentrate in the center of a nuclear star cluster, leading to runaway collisions that form a very massive star. This star can subsequently collapse into a seed black hole of $10^3 - 10^4 M_\odot$ [Ebisuzaki et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...562L..19E/abstract), [Latif et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016PASA...33...51L/abstract). The efficiency of this central mass formation is largely regulated by the ratio of the cluster velocity dispersion to the speed of light [Gaete et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240613072G/abstract), [Askar et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251003766A/abstract).

**Tensions and Alternative Models**
The field currently lacks consensus on the dominant origin channel, and there is active debate regarding the relative importance of light versus heavy seeds. Adding to the tension, some researchers propose that primordial black holes (PBHs)—originating from the direct collapse of density perturbations in the early universe—could provide initial seeds of $\sim 1000 M_\odot$, entirely bypassing astrophysical formation bottlenecks [Dolgov et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020mbhe.confE..13D/abstract), [Yuan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230309391Y/abstract). Whether the earliest quasars grew primarily from Population III remnants, direct collapse mechanisms, or primordial origins remains a major open question.

## Accretion Physics and Mass-Energy Conversion

The primary mechanism for the mass assembly of SMBHs is the accretion of baryonic matter via [[accretion-processes|Accretion Processes]]. For a hot, diffuse interstellar or circumgalactic medium, this process is often approximated by Bondi accretion, a spherical inflow where the black hole's gravitational potential overcomes the specific thermal energy of the gas. This occurs within an accretion radius defined as 
$$r_A \sim \frac{GM_{\rm BH}}{c_s^2},$$ 
where $c_s$ is the sound speed of the gas. However, infalling gas typically possesses significant angular momentum, causing it to settle into a flattened accretion disk. Accretion can also be driven by local asymmetries; for example, observations of NGC 4593 reveal an "m=1" feeding mode where lopsided young stellar components exert torques on molecular gas to sustain high black hole accretion rates [Winkel et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250619902W/abstract). Furthermore, high-resolution observations indicate that accretion is not exclusively a smooth, hot flow; it can also be dominated by a stochastic rain of cold, clumpy molecular clouds infalling at velocities of $\sim 300\ {\rm km\ s^{-1}}$ [Tremblay et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016Natur.534..218T/abstract). Subparsec-scale interferometric observations, such as those of the Circinus Galaxy, reveal that only a small fraction ($<3\%$) of these molecular gas inflows are actually accreted by the black hole, with the vast majority being expelled back out via powerful outflows [Izumi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Sci...382..554I/abstract). Observational constraints indicate that estimated mass inflow rates toward the parsec-scale nucleus can be up to 1000 times higher than the actual black hole accretion rate, leading to the buildup of significant nuclear gas reservoirs rather than immediate consumption [Storchi-Bergmann et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019NatAs...3...48S/abstract).

Accretion disks convert gravitational binding energy into radiation with an efficiency $\epsilon_{\rm rad}$ determined by the innermost stable circular orbit, which is tightly coupled to the black hole's dimensionless spin parameter $a$. X-ray reflection spectroscopy of moderate-redshift quasars has yielded high spin estimates (e.g., $a > 0.66$ at the $5\sigma$ confidence level for a lensed quasar at $z=0.658$), indicating that SMBH growth can proceed via prolonged, coherent accretion episodes rather than chaotic merging [Reis et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.507..207R/abstract). Average radiative efficiencies for quasars spanning $0.4 < z < 2.1$ are estimated to be $\sim 30\%-35\%$, suggesting that many actively accreting SMBHs rotate very rapidly ($a \sim 1$) [Wang et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...642L.111W/abstract). In contrast, continuity equation models characterizing local relic SMBHs suggest time-averaged efficiencies of $\epsilon_{\rm rad} \approx 0.04-0.16$ [Marconi et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004IAUS..222...49M/abstract). The literature currently reflects an active debate over whether maximal spins are set natally or achieved incrementally via coherent accretion.

The structure and radiative output of the accretion flow are strongly governed by the [[eddington-ratio|Eddington ratio]], $\lambda = L_{\rm bol}/L_{\rm Edd}$. At $\lambda \gtrsim 0.02$, the flow typically takes the form of a geometrically thin, optically thick, and radiatively efficient disk. However, below a critical threshold of $\sim 0.02 L_{\rm Edd}$, the standard accretion disk collapses into an X-ray hot, radiatively inefficient plasma [Hagen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240606674H/abstract). Most local SMBHs, including the one at [[the-galactic-center|The Galactic Center]], spend the majority of their evolutionary history in this low-luminosity, radiatively inefficient state, where energy is primarily dissipated via kinetic jets and winds rather than escaping radiation.

Conversely, rapid mass growth in the early universe frequently necessitates [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion and Feedback]]. High-redshift JWST observations have recently revealed a population of compact, red sources (often dubbed "Little Red Dots") at $z \gtrsim 6$, which may represent early SMBHs accreting at or above the Eddington limit. The physical nature of these objects remains a subject of active tension: some literature interprets them as highly obscured, overmassive standard active galactic nuclei, while others propose they are gas-enshrouded "Black Hole Stars," where an extremely dense, turbulent, and dust-free atmosphere envelopes the SMBH [Naidu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250316596N/abstract). Regardless of the exact morphology, super-Eddington accretion phases drive powerful, multi-phase outflows. This efficient mass-to-energy conversion ultimately self-regulates the growth of the SMBH by expelling the cold gas supply from the host galaxy's nuclear region, thereby terminating the active growth phase and maintaining scaling relations. Across varying accretion states, observational linkages span massive ranges in scale. The recent discovery of a "millimetre fundamental plane of black hole accretion" demonstrates a tight correlation linking nuclear 1mm luminosity, X-ray luminosity, and black hole mass, unifying accretion physics across both [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]] and supermassive scales [Elford et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260104007E/abstract).

## Black Hole Spin and Relativistic Effects

The dimensionless spin parameter, $a$, is a fundamental property of SMBHs that quantifies their intrinsic angular momentum. According to the Kerr bound, a black hole cannot rotate faster than the limit where its event horizon vanishes, restricting the spin to $a \le 1$ (or $J \le M$ in geometric units). Theoretical models suggest that if astrophysical black holes were to violate the Kerr bound and become "super-spinning" ($J > M$), their apparent shadow would shrink by roughly an order of magnitude, offering a clear observational signature [Bambi et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009PhRvD..79d3002B/abstract). 

In practice, the spin of an actively accreting SMBH is most robustly measured by analyzing X-ray spectra, specifically through the profile of the fluorescent iron K$\alpha$ line. As gas in the accretion disk approaches the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO), its emission is subject to extreme Doppler boosting and gravitational redshift, producing a characteristic, relativistically broadened red wing in the iron K$\alpha$ profile [McClintock et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011CQGra..28k4009M/abstract). Because the radius of the ISCO is strictly determined by the black hole's mass and spin, modeling these relativistic reflection features provides a direct constraint on $a$ [McClintock et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011CQGra..28k4009M/abstract). For instance, X-ray observations of a gravitationally lensed quasar at $z = 0.658$ revealed reflection originating from within three gravitational radii of the black hole, setting a strict lower limit on the spin parameter of $a > 0.66$ at a $5\sigma$ confidence level [Reis et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.507..207R/abstract).

The spin of an SMBH encodes its integrated growth history, differentiating between pathways governed by [[accretion-processes|Accretion Processes]] that are either coherent or chaotic [Reis et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.507..207R/abstract), [Volonteri et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0709.1722V/abstract). Coherent accretion—in which gas continuously feeds the black hole from a stable, co-rotating disk—efficiently transfers angular momentum to the black hole, rapidly spinning it up toward $a \sim 1$ [Wang et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...642L.111W/abstract). Conversely, chaotic, episodic accretion—where gas clouds fall in with randomly oriented angular momentum vectors—tends to spin the black hole down, resulting in lower net spin values [Wang et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697L.141W/abstract), [Reis et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.507..207R/abstract). Semi-analytical models suggest that a mixture of coherent accretion and kinematically chaotic feeding (anchored to the velocity dispersion of the host galaxy's bulge) is necessary to accurately reproduce observed spin distributions [Sesana et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...794..104S/abstract).

There is strong evidence that the dominant mode of SMBH growth evolves over cosmic time. During the peak of quasar activity ($z > 1.5$), black holes are inferred to have high radiative efficiencies ($\eta \approx 0.30 - 0.35$), implying that they are rapidly rotating ($a \sim 1$) and primarily assembled via coherent accretion [Wang et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...642L.111W/abstract), [Reis et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.507..207R/abstract). However, estimates of the average radiative efficiency indicate a sharp decline toward the local universe, dropping by an order of magnitude to $\eta \approx 0.03$ at $z \approx 0$ [Wang et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697L.141W/abstract). This cosmological evolution is interpreted as a shift from coherent disk accretion to episodic random accretion, which systematically spins down the local SMBH population [Wang et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...697L.141W/abstract).

Spin also plays a critical role in [[supermassive-black-hole-binaries|Supermassive black hole binaries]] and their eventual coalescence. The spins of merging black holes strongly influence the anisotropic emission of gravitational waves, which can impart a recoil velocity to the remnant. For equal-mass black holes with opposite spins aligned and anti-aligned with the orbital axis, the kick velocity scales as $V = 475a \text{ km s}^{-1}$, which is sufficient to produce [[recoiling-supermassive-black-holes|Recoiling supermassive black holes]] that are ejected from the cores of their host galaxies [Herrmann et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...661..430H/abstract). Furthermore, while high spin is often invoked in models of relativistic jet launching in [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]], observational measurements indicate that the presence or absence of jets is not entirely determined by the black hole's spin parameter alone [McClintock et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011CQGra..28k4009M/abstract).

## Co-evolution and Scaling Relations

The tight empirical correlations between the mass of a central SMBH ($M_{\rm BH}$) and the large-scale properties of its host galaxy provide the primary framework for understanding [[galaxy-black-hole-assembly|Galaxy-black hole assembly]]. In the local Universe, $M_{\rm BH}$ is observed to correlate strongly with the stellar velocity dispersion ($\sigma$) of the host—known as the $M-\sigma$ relation—as well as with the total spheroid mass and luminosity [Kormendy et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ARA&A..33..581K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ARA&A..51..511K/abstract). Across many orders of magnitude, the central black hole comprises approximately $0.1\%$ to $0.5\%$ of the host's stellar bulge mass [Kormendy et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001AIPC..586..363K/abstract), [Trakhtenbrot et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...349..168T/abstract).

Historically, these scaling relations were thought to apply universally to all central massive objects and host galaxies. However, refined demographic studies demonstrate a strong component specificity: $M_{\rm BH}$ correlates tightly with classical [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] and core elliptical galaxies, but shows little to no correlation with galaxy disks, pseudobulges, or the total dark matter halo mass [Kormendy et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Natur.469..374K/abstract), [Kormendy et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Natur.469..377K/abstract). However, significant black hole growth can still occur in bulgeless galaxies that lack a history of major mergers; these systems are often found to follow a broader correlation between black hole mass and the total stellar mass of the galaxy rather than classical bulge properties [Simmons et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.429.2199S/abstract). This dichotomy suggests that tightly coupled co-evolution is driven by global, merger-induced violent relaxation and gas inflows that build classical bulges, rather than by the local, secular processes responsible for growing pseudobulges and disks [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ARA&A..51..511K/abstract). 

The physical origin of these scaling relations is widely attributed to self-regulated growth via AGN feedback. As gas accretes onto the black hole, the ensuing radiation and kinetic winds couple to the surrounding interstellar and circumgalactic medium. Once $M_{\rm BH}$ reaches a critical threshold, the feedback energy exceeds the gravitational binding energy of the galaxy's gas, driving massive outflows that quench both star formation and further black hole accretion [Di Matteo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005Natur.433..604D/abstract), [Zubovas et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019GReGr..51...65Z/abstract). Furthermore, the $M-\sigma$ relation may naturally emerge as galaxies relax toward virial equilibrium, balancing kinetic energy and the host's gravitational potential [Sherman et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1410.8514S/abstract). At the highest masses, successive dissipationless major mergers effectively average out variations, further tightening the scatter in the observed scaling relations [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ARA&A..51..511K/abstract). Moreover, selection biases in local dynamically measured black hole masses may explain the apparent discrepancy between local and active black hole scaling relations [Shankar et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1500S/abstract).

Despite the success of the synchronized co-evolution paradigm in the local Universe, substantial tensions remain at higher redshifts. An observation of an early star-forming galaxy at $z \sim 3.3$ revealed a system hosting an [[overmassive-black-hole|Overmassive black hole]], where the $M_{\rm BH}/M_{\rm star}$ ratio was 10 to 100 times higher than local relations predict, reaching a value of $1\%$ to $10\%$ [Trakhtenbrot et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...349..168T/abstract). These findings challenge the assumption of strictly synchronous growth across all epochs, suggesting instead that in compact or early-universe systems, black hole assembly can drastically precede the growth of the host galaxy's stellar population.

## AGN Feedback and Galaxy Evolution

Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) play a critical role in the cosmological evolution of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] by regulating the exchange of gas between the interstellar medium and the surrounding [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]]. As matter undergoes [[accretion-processes|Accretion Processes]] onto a central supermassive black hole, vast amounts of mechanical and radiative energy are released. This energy injection, collectively termed AGN feedback, modifies the thermodynamic state of the host galaxy's baryon cycles, preventing gas from cooling and thereby suppressing the star formation rate [Donahue et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PhR...973....1D/abstract). Without this self-regulating mechanism, cosmological models fail to reproduce the observed red sequence of massive elliptical galaxies and vastly overpredict their present-day stellar masses [Di Matteo et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005Natur.433..604D/abstract), [Sijacki et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.380..877S/abstract).

AGN feedback is generally categorized into two distinct regimes. During phases of rapid accretion (near the Eddington limit), black holes operate in a "quasar" or radiative mode. In this state, radiation pressure drives highly ionized winds with velocities approaching $v \sim 0.1c$, which sweep up interstellar gas into large-scale outflows [King et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SSRv..183..427K/abstract), [Kaastra et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Sci...345...64K/abstract). As the accretion rate drops, the black hole transitions into a radiatively inefficient state known as the kinetic mode (or radio mode). In this regime, the primary feedback mechanism shifts to mechanical energy injected via powerful relativistic jets and localized winds [Sijacki et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007MNRAS.380..877S/abstract).

The kinetic mode is considered essential for the long-term quenching of star formation in massive halos. By injecting mechanical energy, kinetic feedback raises the entropy of the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]], lengthening local gas cooling times from $10-100 \text{ Myr}$ to $1-10 \text{ Gyr}$ [Zinger et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499..768Z/abstract). This process is both "ejective," physically blowing cold gas out of the central regions, and "preventative," halting the cosmological infall of new gas [Dubois et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428.2885D/abstract), [Zinger et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.499..768Z/abstract). Observations and analytical estimates suggest these outflows can reach large-scale velocities of $\sim 1000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, delivering a mechanical luminosity of approximately $L_{\rm mech} \simeq 0.05 L_{\rm Edd}$, where $L_{\rm Edd}$ is the Eddington luminosity [King et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SSRv..183..427K/abstract).

Observational evidence robustly links the mass of the supermassive black hole to the suppression of its host's star formation rate. Integrated spectral histories of nearby galaxies reveal that systems hosting more massive central black holes quenched their star formation earlier and more efficiently [Martín-Navarro et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.553..307M/abstract). Furthermore, the specific star formation rate of a galaxy decreases smoothly as a function of the specific black hole mass, $M_{\rm BH}/M_*$, suggesting that quenching is a gradual process tied to accumulated feedback energy [Terrazas et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844..170T/abstract). At higher redshifts ($z \sim 2-6$), vigorous starbursts are common, but submillimeter and X-ray observations show a marked absence of intense star formation in hosts where the AGN X-ray luminosity exceeds $L_X > 10^{44} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$ [Page et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.485..213P/abstract).

Despite a broad consensus on the necessity of AGN feedback, tensions remain regarding its exact timing and energy coupling efficiency. The literature reflects an ongoing debate over whether feedback is strictly coeval with starbursts or acts with a significant time delay of $\sim 100 \text{ Myr}$ [Schawinski et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...692L..19S/abstract). Additionally, observations timing the starburst-AGN connection measure a time delay of roughly 250 to 300 Myr between the peak of a nuclear starburst and the subsequent rise in black hole accretion rates, driven largely by stellar mass loss [Wild et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.405..933W/abstract). While hydrodynamic simulations predict a sharp transition to quiescence above a specific $M_{\rm BH}$ threshold, observations display a broader scatter, indicating that the precise physical mechanisms by which wind energy couples to the multiphase interstellar medium remain incompletely understood [Terrazas et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1888T/abstract).

## Galaxy Mergers and Supermassive Black Hole Binaries

The hierarchical assembly of galaxies inherently leads to the pairing of their central supermassive black holes, driving the formation of [[supermassive-black-hole-binaries|Supermassive black hole binaries]] [Colpi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SSRv..183..189C/abstract). Simultaneous accretion is generally rare, but strong [[dual-active-galactic-nuclei|Dual active galactic nuclei]] activity can be triggered during the late phases of a galaxy merger when the black holes are at small separations (<1-10 kpc) [Van Wassenhove et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748L...7V/abstract). As the merger progresses, the black holes sink toward the center of the remnant galaxy via dynamical friction until they form a bound Keplerian binary at parsec scales.

At this separation, dynamical friction becomes inefficient. The subsequent orbital decay requires alternative mechanisms to shed angular momentum, presenting a theoretical bottleneck known as the "final parsec problem." The literature presents several competing resolutions to this problem, and it remains an active area of debate. In gas-rich major mergers, hydrodynamic drag from a massive, turbulent circumbinary disk can drive the binary to sub-parsec scales in $\lesssim 10^6 \text{ yr}$ [Mayer et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007Sci...316.1874M/abstract). In gas-poor environments, the binary hardens via the three-body scattering of ambient stars (the slingshot mechanism). This process ejects stars from the galactic core, a phenomenon known as [[scouring-astrophysics|Scouring (astrophysics)]], which decreases the central stellar density by up to an order of magnitude and is thought to create the flattened density cores observed in massive elliptical galaxies [Meiron et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.433.2502M/abstract), [Khan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250307711K/abstract). However, for the most massive systems, ultramassive black hole binaries can bypass the typical three-body stellar scattering phase entirely, with their coalescence driven directly by efficient dynamical friction and gravitational wave emission [Khan et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...798..103K/abstract). Alternative models suggest that interactions with ultralight dark matter quasiparticles could also extract sufficient orbital energy to facilitate the merger [Bromley et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PhRvD.110b3517B/abstract).

Once the binary separation shrinks to milliparsec scales, the emission of gravitational waves (GWs) drives the system to rapid coalescence. The cosmic population of inspiraling [[supermassive-black-hole-binaries|Supermassive black hole binaries]] generates a stochastic gravitational wave background in the nanohertz frequency regime. Recent limits and common-spectrum signals from Pulsar Timing Arrays constrain the chirp mass and local number density of these systems [Arzoumanian et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...914..121A/abstract). Models based on these signals estimate a characteristic strain amplitude of $\sim 2 \times 10^{-16}$ at a frequency of $1 \text{ yr}^{-1}$, implying a local binary number density that may be up to five times larger than previous theoretical predictions [Chen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...897...86C/abstract), [Casey-Clyde et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...924...93C/abstract). The final coalescence of these binaries will be primary targets for the future space-based interferometer LISA, which is expected to detect events with rates of $\gtrsim 1 \text{ yr}^{-1}$ out to high redshifts [Menou et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...558..535M/abstract), [Chen et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...897...86C/abstract). Furthermore, steady-state models of stellar dynamics around these central engines predict approximately 2,000 resolvable extreme mass-ratio inspirals (EMRIs) that will also be detectable by LISA over a 4-year mission [Rom et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240619443R/abstract). In addition to generating gravitational waves, the population of merging black holes significantly contributes to the cosmological entropy budget, potentially surpassing the entropy from cosmic microwave background photons starting around $z \sim 12$ [Chen et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260113621C/abstract). In the local Universe, the remnant population resulting from these binary black hole mergers is substantial, with theoretical models estimating a number density of approximately $660 \text{ Mpc}^{-3}$ [Doctor et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...914L..18D/abstract).

The anisotropic emission of GWs during the final plunge imparts a momentum flux to the remnant black hole, resulting in a GW recoil or "super-kick" with velocities reaching up to $\sim 4000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ [Li et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748...65L/abstract). Such kicks can displace the black hole from the galactic center, creating [[recoiling-supermassive-black-holes|Recoiling supermassive black holes]] that may appear as offset or wandering active galactic nuclei over timescales of up to several gigayears [Komossa et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AdAst2012E..14K/abstract), [Chu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522..948C/abstract). Strong observational evidence for this process is found in the powerful quasar 3C 186, where the broad-line gas bound to the black hole is significantly blue-shifted relative to the host galaxy's systemic velocity [Chiaberge et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250118730C/abstract). The existence of such coalesced and recoiling systems confirms that the final parsec problem is successfully overcome in nature.

## Dynamical Interactions and Recoil Phenomena

The coalescence of supermassive black hole binaries is accompanied by the intense, anisotropic emission of gravitational waves. This asymmetry imparts a net linear momentum to the remnant black hole, resulting in a recoil velocity or "kick" that can reach up to $\sim 4000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, depending on the mass ratio and spin alignment of the progenitors [Li et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...748...65L/abstract), [Komossa et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AdAst2012E..14K/abstract). Such extreme velocities are sufficient to eject the remnant completely from the host galaxy or displace it into long-lived, damped oscillations through the galactic core. Observationally, these [[recoiling-supermassive-black-holes|Recoiling supermassive black holes]] can manifest as [[displaced-active-galactic-nuclei|Displaced active galactic nuclei]]—quasars that are spatially or kinematically offset from their host galaxies. A prominent candidate for this phenomenon is the powerful quasar 3C 186, which exhibits broad emission lines significantly blue-shifted relative to the host galaxy's systemic velocity, consistent with a massive gravitational wave recoil [Chiaberge et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250118730C/abstract).

Both the binary inspiral phase and the subsequent recoil profoundly impact the central stellar distribution of the host galaxy. Before emitting significant gravitational radiation, a hardening binary sheds orbital energy by ejecting ambient stars via the three-body slingshot mechanism. This process, known as [[scouring-astrophysics|Scouring (astrophysics)]], creates a central mass deficit and excavates a flattened density core in the stellar distribution [Volonteri et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...593..661V/abstract). Recent $N$-body simulations suggest that this slingshot ejection, combined with the subsequent recoil of the merged remnant, can decrease the central stellar surface density by an order of magnitude. This mechanism has been proposed to explain the evolutionary transition from extremely dense, high-redshift "Little Red Dots" to the less dense elliptical galaxies observed in the local universe [Khan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250307711K/abstract). When a supermassive black hole is ejected, it carries a retinue of tightly bound stars; the occasional tidal disruption of these stars by the recoiling black hole is predicted to produce distinctive off-nuclear X-ray flares [Komossa et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...683L..21K/abstract). More broadly, the census of tidal disruption events (TDEs) serves as a primary method for illuminating [[dormant-black-holes|Dormant black holes]] in quiescent galaxies across the X-ray, ultraviolet, and optical bands [Gezari et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013BrJPh..43..351G/abstract). Furthermore, if a tidal disruption event occurs in a system hosting a yet-unmerged binary, the secondary black hole can dynamically interrupt the accretion of stellar debris. This interruption produces characteristic dips in the TDE light curve, providing a unique observational signature for the presence of supermassive black hole binaries [Liu et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...706L.133L/abstract).

Supermassive and intermediate-mass black holes can also become displaced without a gravitational wave kick, primarily through galaxy-galaxy mergers. Following a merger, the timescale for a secondary black hole to sink to the center of the newly formed potential well is governed by dynamical friction. In less massive systems, such as dwarf galaxies, dynamical friction is highly inefficient due to shallow potential wells and low stellar densities. Cosmological simulations indicate that up to $\sim 50\%$ of massive black holes in dwarf galaxies may be [[wandering-black-holes|Wandering black holes]], stalled at distances of several kiloparsecs from the galactic center and unable to sink within a Hubble time [Bellovary et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.2913B/abstract), [Bellovary et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.5129B/abstract). Even in massive systems like brightest cluster galaxies, mergers with satellite galaxies on highly radial orbits can eject or displace the central black hole by parsecs to hundreds of kiloparsecs, sustaining an off-center orbit for up to several gigayears [Chu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522..948C/abstract). Because they reside outside the dense nuclear gas reservoirs, these wandering black holes typically exhibit very low accretion luminosities, making them exceptionally difficult to detect observationally [Bellovary et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.2913B/abstract), [Chu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522..948C/abstract).

## Evolution of SMBHs through Cosmic Time

The assembly history of supermassive black holes is deeply intertwined with the evolution of their host galaxies, a relationship that is traced observationally across cosmic time by the quasar luminosity function. The volume-averaged accretion history indicates that the peak of SMBH growth occurred at $z \sim 2$, an epoch that coincides with the maximum in the cosmic star formation rate. By integrating the quasar luminosity function over time, the total mass accreted during these active phases is found to be consistent with the local mass density of [[dormant-black-holes|Dormant black holes]] residing in the centers of quiescent galaxies today [Volonteri et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ralc.conf..174V/abstract). Moreover, for typical star-forming galaxies, the average ratio of black hole accretion rate to star formation rate (BHAR/SFR) has been shown to remain relatively constant over the last 11 billion years since $z \sim 2$ [Calhau et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464..303C/abstract).

The redshift evolution of the [[active-black-hole-mass-functions|Active black hole mass functions]] reveals a distinct "cosmic downsizing" or anti-hierarchical growth pattern [Merloni et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.388.1011M/abstract). While the standard cosmological paradigm dictates that dark matter halos assemble hierarchically (smaller structures merging to form larger ones), the most massive black holes ($M_{\rm BH} \gtrsim 10^9 M_\odot$) grew extremely rapidly and were largely in place by $z \sim 2$. In contrast, the space density of lower-mass black holes continued to undergo strong evolution at later epochs, dominating the overall accretion density at $z < 1$ [Li et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...742...33L/abstract). This shift—from the rapid early growth of high-mass black holes to the late-time growth of lower-mass black holes—mirrors the downsizing observed in galactic star formation histories.

However, at $z > 4$, the assembly history frequently deviates from the synchronized co-evolution seen at lower redshifts. Observations of [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] reveal that billion-solar-mass black holes were already assembled within the first billion years of the Universe. Recent JWST data has identified a prevalent population of early systems where [[high-redshift-black-hole-growth|High-redshift black hole growth]] drastically preceded the assembly of the stellar host, resulting in an [[overmassive-black-hole|Overmassive black hole]] relative to the local scaling relations. In these early galaxies, the $M_{\rm BH}/M_\star$ ratio can be 10 to 100 times higher than in the local Universe [Juodžbalis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Natur.636..594J/abstract), [Pacucci et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..154P/abstract). Some models suggest that this mass ratio evolves with redshift proportionally to $(1+z)^{5/2}$ [Pacucci et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..154P/abstract). This implies that the earliest SMBHs may have followed a "black hole-first" evolutionary pathway before runaway star formation and subsequent mergers eventually brought them onto the local $M_{\rm BH}-M_\star$ relation. Adding to the complexity, the discovery of a massive black hole at $z = 7.04$ (also presenting as a "Little Red Dot") residing in a near-pristine galaxy with extremely low metallicity ($\sim 4\%$ solar) challenges both heavy seed formation pathways and standard accretion models [Maiolino et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522567M/abstract). Despite these early deviations, some scaling relations appear remarkably stable over cosmic time. The first stellar-dynamical mass measurement of an inactive black hole at a high redshift of $z = 1.95$ suggests that the $M-\sigma$ relation may have been strictly in place for the past 10 billion years, even while the relation between black hole mass and bulge mass continued to evolve [Newman et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250317478N/abstract).

The [[specific-black-hole-accretion-rate|Specific black hole accretion rate]] and the average [[eddington-ratio|Eddington ratio]] also exhibit strong redshift evolution. At higher redshifts, the characteristically high gas fractions of early galaxies sustained near-Eddington or super-Eddington accretion flows, which were necessary to rapidly build up the early SMBH population [Shirakata et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487..409S/abstract). Over cosmic time, the average accretion efficiency and Eddington ratio steadily declined. By $z \sim 0$, the majority of the most massive SMBHs have transitioned into radiatively inefficient, low-accretion states characterized by kinetic feedback modes. In this late-time regime, self-regulated accretion severely limits further gas consumption, and any residual mass growth for the largest black holes is driven primarily by dry mergers involving [[supermassive-black-hole-binaries|Supermassive black hole binaries]] rather than by continuous gas accretion [Weinberger et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.4056W/abstract).

## Open Questions: The IMBH Link and Future Frontiers

Modern research actively seeks to bridge the [[mass-gap|Mass gap]] between stellar-mass and supermassive black holes through the study of [[intermediate-mass-astrophysical-objects|Intermediate-Mass Astrophysical Objects]] (IMBHs). Typically defined in the mass range of $10^2 - 10^5 M_\odot$, IMBHs are hypothesized to be the local relics of early black hole seeds. They are predominantly sought in dwarf galaxies and globular clusters; however, while smaller massive black holes of $\sim 10^6 M_\odot$ serving as low-mass analogues have been confirmed in some local [[star-forming-dwarf-galaxies|Star-forming dwarf galaxies]] such as [[henize-2-10|Henize 2-10]] [Reines et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Natur.470...66R/abstract), the detection of true $10^2 - 10^5 M_\odot$ IMBHs in globular clusters remains highly controversial and largely elusive [Greene et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ARA&A..58..257G/abstract). However, compelling evidence has emerged from time-domain astronomy; for instance, a luminous X-ray outburst interpreted as a tidal disruption event flare has revealed the presence of a $\sim 10^4 M_\odot$ IMBH residing in an off-centre massive star cluster [Lin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018NatAs...2..656L/abstract).

The dynamics of IMBHs in dwarf systems present a significant challenge to classical co-evolution models. Cosmological simulations indicate that up to $50\%$ of IMBHs in dwarf regimes may be [[wandering-black-holes|Wandering black holes]] at kiloparsec distances from the galactic center rather than settling into the nucleus [Bellovary et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.482.2913B/abstract), [Bellovary et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.505.5129B/abstract). This off-center distribution limits their gas accretion and mass growth, which may explain why IMBHs frequently deviate from the standard $M-\sigma$ relation. Furthermore, the broader relationship between central massive objects reveals distinct scaling behaviors: while supermassive black holes correlate tightly with the classical bulge mass, [[dense-stellar-systems|Dense stellar systems]] correlate more strongly with the total stellar mass of the host galaxy . This divergence challenges the paradigm that a single set of universal scaling relations governs all central compact objects [Kormendy et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ARA&A..51..511K/abstract).

At the high-redshift frontier, next-generation observatories like JWST are probing the earliest growth phases of supermassive black holes. The existence of [[luminous-intermediate-redshift-quasars|Luminous intermediate redshift quasars]] at $z > 6$ requires extremely rapid early growth, leading to active debate over the initial seeding mechanism. "Light seeds" ($\sim 100 - 1000 M_\odot$) are proposed to form from the remnants of Population III stars [Ryu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.4122R/abstract), while "heavy seeds" ($\sim 10^4 - 10^6 M_\odot$) may arise from the direct collapse of massive, pristine gas clouds into [[direct-collapse-black-holes|Direct collapse black holes]] [Wise et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019Natur.566...85W/abstract). Alternative models also suggest that primordial black holes (PBHs) could account for these early seeds, potentially contradicting standard astrophysical remnant theories [Yuan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230309391Y/abstract).

Crucially, recent JWST observations have fundamentally challenged the assumption of synchronized co-evolution between black holes and their host galaxies in the early universe. Data reveal a population of compact, high-redshift systems—often appearing observationally as "Little Red Dots"—hosting an [[overmassive-black-hole|Overmassive black hole]] whose mass growth drastically precedes the assembly of its host galaxy. In these systems, the black hole-to-stellar mass ratio ($M_{\rm BH}/M_*$) can reach $\sim 0.1$, which is orders of magnitude higher than the local universe average of $\sim 0.001$ [Trakhtenbrot et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015Sci...349..168T/abstract), [Juodžbalis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Natur.636..594J/abstract), [Pacucci et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..154P/abstract). Whether these overmassive sources represent a novel phase of gas-enshrouded accretion—sometimes theorized as exotic "black hole stars"—or are simply standard active galactic nuclei heavily obscured by dust remains an unresolved and active frontier of research.
