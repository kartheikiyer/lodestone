## Article

**Galaxy-black hole assembly** describes the coupled, co-evolutionary growth of [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] (SMBHs) and their [[host-galaxies|Host galaxies]] across cosmic time. Embedded within the broader framework of hierarchical structure formation, this field bridges sub-parsec accretion physics with the large-scale cosmological evolution of dark matter halos. The cornerstone of this [[synchronized-coevolution|Synchronized coevolution]] paradigm is the emergence of tight empirical scaling relations in the local universe, most notably the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ correlation between the central black hole mass and the stellar velocity dispersion of the host's bulge [Volonteri et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&ARv..18..279V/abstract), [King et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502L...1K/abstract). These relations suggest a fundamental, causal link regulating mass assembly, wherein the energy liberated by [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|AGN feedback]] couples the black hole's growth to the gravitational potential of the galaxy, effectively self-regulating both star formation and further gas accretion.

The trajectory of this assembly process is governed by a diverse set of mechanisms that shift in dominance depending on the cosmic epoch and the host's mass. In the pre-reionization era, initial conditions are set by the formation of [[massive-black-hole-seeds|Massive black hole seeds]], which may originate from the remnants of Population III stars or the monolithic collapse of pristine gas clouds into [[direct-collapse-black-holes|Direct collapse black holes]]. As these early halos assemble, black hole growth is driven by a combination of gas-rich [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] and steady-state secular [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]]. While violent mergers are highly efficient at driving rapid gas inflows and triggering luminous quasar phases, secular processes and torque-limited accretion are critical for maintaining scaling relations in structurally calmer, disk-dominated systems. As massive galaxies mature, the immense energy injected by AGN feedback expels and heats the circumgalactic medium, quenching star formation and driving a structural transition. This results in a [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] model, characterized by an early phase of rapid, in-situ starbursts followed by late-time, ex-situ mass accretion via dry mergers ([[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]]).

Investigating galaxy-black hole assembly requires probing phenomena across a vast range of physical scales and mass regimes. The structural impacts of this co-evolution are evident everywhere from [[active-dwarf-galaxies|Active dwarf galaxies]] to the [[ultramassive-black-holes|Ultramassive black holes]] residing in the cores of [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|High-mass early-type galaxies]]. Remnants of this complex assembly history are preserved in fossil tracers like the [[galactic-globular-cluster-system|Galactic globular cluster system]] and nuclear star clusters, as well as in the dynamical signatures of massive black hole binaries, triple systems, and recoiling [[wandering-black-holes|Wandering black holes]]. Despite a robust theoretical framework, significant tensions remain. Recent discoveries by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have revealed a population of early, near-pristine galaxies hosting an [[overmassive-black-hole|Overmassive black hole]] well before significant stellar assembly has occurred [Scoggins et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.2155S/abstract). These extreme high-redshift systems, alongside persistent questions regarding binary stalling, the precise physical mode of AGN feedback, and the influence of halo assembly bias, drive the current frontier of astrophysical research and set the stage for upcoming multi-messenger observatories.

## Foundations of Galaxy-Black Hole Co-evolution

The paradigm of [[synchronized-coevolution|Synchronized coevolution]] between [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] (SMBHs) and their host galaxies is fundamentally anchored by a set of tight empirical scaling relations observed in the local universe. The origin and early growth of supermassive black holes remain a central question in astrophysics, as these early stages dictate the initial conditions for subsequent galaxy and black hole co-evolution [Djorgovski et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008mgm..conf..340D/abstract). Locally, black holes typically account for approximately $0.1\%$ of their host's bulge mass [Volonteri et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&ARv..18..279V/abstract). A strictly linear relation between black hole and bulge mass emerges naturally in theoretical models if the accreted mass tracks star formation closely over cosmic time [Cattaneo et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.308...77C/abstract). However, the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ correlation is widely considered the more fundamental scaling relation, typically scaling as $M_{\rm BH} \propto \sigma^4$ [King et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502L...1K/abstract).

The tightness of these relations suggests a causal link regulating co-evolutionary growth. Classical models frequently attribute this to [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|AGN feedback]], wherein the energy liberated by accretion regulates the cold gas supply, coupling the black hole's growth to the gravitational potential well of the host [Schawinski et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012arXiv1206.2661S/abstract). Imprints of this feedback are visible in the stellar populations of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]]: systems hosting black holes that sit above the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ relation tend to be older and more $\alpha$-element enhanced, reflecting an earlier, feedback-driven quenching of star formation [Martín-Navarro et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832L..11M/abstract). Supporting the role of the black hole as a cumulative feedback tracer, studies demonstrate that host galaxy star formation rates correlate more strongly with black hole mass than with instantaneous X-ray luminosity [Mountrichas et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...679A.151M/abstract). Conversely, some models demonstrate that these scaling relations can emerge without strong self-regulation, driven instead by gravitational torque-limited accretion that scales dynamically with the host galaxy's global star formation rate [Anglés-Alcázar et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770....5A/abstract), [Anglés-Alcázar et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.2840A/abstract). Further reinforcing internal baryonic processes, semi-analytical models like GECO demonstrate that the characteristic "downsizing" of star formation can emerge as a natural outcome of hierarchical structure formation even in the absence of explicit AGN feedback [Ricciardelli et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...518A..14R/abstract).

While standard theory often emphasizes [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] as the primary driver of both bulge formation and SMBH fueling—suggesting a pattern of "punctuated equilibrium" where merger-driven transitions create a chronological evolutionary sequence from spirals to lenticulars, and finally to ellipticals [Graham et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.535..299G/abstract)—recent observations challenge the absolute necessity of this pathway. Broadly, analysis of star formation and nuclear activity in large samples suggests that stellar mass is the critical parameter in the evolutionary sequence of host galaxies [La Mura et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426.1893L/abstract). Significant black hole growth is observed in bulgeless, pure-disk galaxies that have experienced calm, merger-free histories [Simmons et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.429.2199S/abstract). In these systems, central black holes scale with the total stellar mass of the galaxy rather than a bulge component, implying that secular processes and internal [[galaxy-accretion|Galaxy accretion]] mechanisms are sufficient to maintain co-evolution [Simmons et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.1559S/abstract).

To bridge these physical phenomena over cosmic time, researchers increasingly turn to diverse theoretical frameworks. For example, some models successfully reproduce the coupled evolution of cold gas and quasar luminosities by assuming black hole and bulge formation occur strictly simultaneously during major mergers . Meanwhile, Bayesian empirical modeling successfully reproduces galaxy stellar mass functions and quasar luminosity functions by linking dark matter structure to simple baryonic prescriptions [Boettner et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230311368B/abstract). Additionally, utilizing the [[central-galaxies|central-to-halo mass ratio]] $f_c$ serves as an observable proxy for halo assembly time, linking larger ratios to earlier structural assembly [Lim et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.455..499L/abstract). 

Despite general consensus, a major ongoing debate in the literature is whether these scaling relations genuinely evolve with redshift. Direct large-scale hydrodynamic simulations show that the ratio of black hole to stellar mass density evolves moderately over time, with black hole growth typically peaking at lower redshifts than global star formation [Di Matteo et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...676...33D/abstract). The AGN duty cycle itself exhibits strong morphological dependencies; the fraction of growing black holes declines with mass in early-type galaxies but paradoxically increases with mass in late-type systems [Schawinski et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..267..438S/abstract). Accurately reproducing observed AGN fractions necessitates models where Eddington ratios systematically decrease with both cosmic time and black hole mass [Shankar et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..421S/abstract). This Eddington ratio distribution likely evolves with redshift, with its slope increasing at lower gas fractions in late-time galaxies [Jones et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881..110J/abstract). Furthermore, self-regulated models rooted purely in merger simulations often over-predict early black hole accretion while under-predicting late-time growth compared to robust observational data [Somerville et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ASPC..399..391S/abstract). At high redshifts ($z \sim 2$), studies of infrared-selected AGNs show that neither radiative nor radio-mode feedback appears to significantly regulate star formation during peak activity epochs [Polletta et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...480L..41P/abstract). 

Observations of high-redshift quasars and recently discovered [[massive-black-hole-seeds|Massive black hole seeds]] suggest the existence of [[overmassive-black-hole|over-massive]] black holes where the $M_{\rm BH}/M_*$ ratio significantly exceeds the local average, sometimes reaching values greater than unity [Scoggins et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.2155S/abstract). This structural extreme is occasionally mirrored in the local universe, exemplified by the compact lenticular galaxy NGC 1277, which hosts a black hole comprising roughly $59\%$ of its bulge mass [van den Bosch et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.491..729V/abstract). However, the literature remains strongly divided on whether these offsets reflect a genuine evolutionary pathway—where black hole assembly precedes stellar mass growth—or if they are predominantly the result of observational selection biases that favor the detection of highly luminous, overly massive black holes at early cosmic epochs [Shankar et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1500S/abstract), [Tanaka et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...979..215T/abstract), [Farrah et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...981...71F/abstract). Systematic uncertainties in local scaling relations inherently introduce significant impact into the determination of the local black hole mass function [Shankar et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013CQGra..30x4001S/abstract). For instance, modeling UV/optical variability suggests that single-epoch virial black hole mass estimators may be systematically biased by 0.3 to 0.6 dex [Sun et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521.2954S/abstract), while comparisons of virial and host-based mass estimates for nearly 400 AGNs reveal that Eddington ratios can vary by up to three orders of magnitude for a given black hole mass [Woo et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...579..530W/abstract). Similarly, new SINFONI adaptive optics measurements of early-type galaxies emphasize that properly accounting for radial mass-to-light ratio variations can decrease dynamically determined black hole masses by up to 30% [Thater et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...625A..62T/abstract). However, studies of mass-limited galaxy samples in environments such as the Virgo Cluster suggest that black hole masses in early-type galaxies are not strongly biased by traditional selection effects, upholding the validity of their scaling relations [Byrne et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.1095B/abstract). Ultimately, empirical models of black hole growth from $z=2$ to $z=0$ reveal diverse co-evolutionary pathways: in some galaxies, stellar mass firmly assembles prior to black hole growth, while in others, the exact opposite occurs [Terrazas et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241108838T/abstract), [Abuter et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024Natur.627..281A/abstract). Furthermore, characterizing these host galaxies relies heavily on accurate spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting; incomplete AGN templates can lead to systematic overestimates of host stellar mass by up to 0.5 dex, a bias that new SED fitting techniques like GRAHSP are designed to mitigate [Buchner et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...692A.161B/abstract). Quiescent black holes that fall entirely off active AGN relations may alternatively be identified via sharp light profile spikes in the galactic nucleus or through the "slingshot" ejection of stars stemming from binary interactions [Rees et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990SciAm.263e..56R/abstract).

## Initial Seeding and High-Redshift Assembly

The existence of $\sim 10^9\, M_\odot$ [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] within the first billion years of the Universe ($z > 6$) requires highly efficient formation and growth mechanisms for [[massive-black-hole-seeds|Massive black hole seeds]] [Volonteri et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&ARv..18..279V/abstract), [Wise et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230409311W/abstract). Theoretical models for [[smbh-seeding|SMBH seeding]] generally divide the initial mass distribution into two primary channels: "light" seeds and "heavy" seeds. In all frameworks, the dynamical evolution of early, ultra-dense star systems inevitably leads to extreme gas concentrations that collapse into black holes, establishing the initial centers for active nuclei [Dokuchaev et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991SvPhU..34..447D/abstract). These early seeds likely form in small proto-galaxies before being sequentially processed through hundreds of smaller components in a hierarchical cosmology [Volonteri et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010IAUS..267...26V/abstract).

Light seeds originate from the remnants of Population III stars, typically forming with initial masses of $10$--$100\, M_\odot$. In dense, early star clusters, repeated stellar collisions and rapid gas accretion might elevate these initial masses. Models incorporating the background gas potential and stellar mass loss in Population III clusters demonstrate that black holes of $> 400\, M_\odot$ can reliably form, potentially exceeding $10^4\, M_\odot$ if high accretion rates ($\sim 10^{-3}\, M_\odot\, \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$) can be sustained [Katz et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ffbh.book..125K/abstract), [Reinoso et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250320415R/abstract). Moreover, semi-analytic models suggest that smaller initial seed masses ($\sim 10^3\, M_\odot$) may better reproduce the observed empirical dispersion in scaling relations for local dwarf galaxies [Shirakata et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461.4389S/abstract).

Conversely, heavy seeds are theorized to form as [[direct-collapse-black-holes|Direct collapse black holes]] (DCBHs) originating from the monolithic collapse of pristine gas clouds. This process requires environments where molecular hydrogen cooling is strongly suppressed, typically by a Lyman-Werner UV radiation field from a nearby star-forming galaxy. DCBHs bypass the stellar remnant phase entirely, forming initial seeds generally modeled in the $10^4$--$10^5\, M_\odot$ range [Wise et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230409311W/abstract), [Trinca et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260114370T/abstract). Direct gas collapse can also occur in gas-rich [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]], where multiscale gas inflows trigger the formation of a supermassive star that rapidly collapses into an equivalent heavy seed [Bonoli et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.437.1576B/abstract). Furthermore, massive black hole seeds may form via collisions within dense black hole clusters that develop inside nuclear star clusters through mass segregation [Gaete et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...690A.378G/abstract). Seeding models that implement black hole formation based on local gas properties rather than strict halo mass thresholds suggest that the detection of higher masses at higher redshifts is fundamentally indicative of early downsizing in the universe [Wang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.483.4640W/abstract). 

Regardless of the exact seeding mechanism, rapid subsequent growth is necessary to match the luminosities of [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]]. Such growth is likely driven by short, repeated episodes of super-Eddington accretion triggered by global galactic instabilities or massive gas inflows [Trinca et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241214248T/abstract), [Chen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250903283C/abstract). Direct observational evidence for merger-driven fueling in the early universe is found in the hyperluminous, obscured quasar WISE J2246-0526 at $z=4.6$; spectroscopic imaging reveals a multiple merger event with tidal dusty bridges directly feeding the central black hole [Díaz-Santos et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Sci...362.1034D/abstract). Limits on the integrated X-ray emission at $z > 6$ place critical constraints on this epoch, confining the total accreted mass density of supermassive black holes to $< 1000\, M_\odot$ Mpc$^{-3}$ [Treister et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...778..130T/abstract). 

Recent observations by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have profoundly impacted our understanding of this high-redshift assembly. JWST has uncovered a population of compact, dust-obscured active galactic nuclei, often referred to as "Little Red Dots" (LRDs) [Chen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250903283C/abstract). These systems frequently host an [[overmassive-black-hole|Overmassive black hole]] relative to their host galaxy's stellar mass. In standard local scaling relations, the black hole mass is roughly $0.1\%$ of the bulge mass; however, in these high-redshift systems, the ratio $M_{\rm BH}/M_*$ frequently exceeds $10^{-3}$ and can be much larger. In extreme cases, the black hole mass may even rival or exceed the stellar mass ($M_{\rm BH}/M_* > 1$) from birth until they merge with more massive halos [Scoggins et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.2155S/abstract). This apparent offset is corroborated by radiation-hydrodynamics simulations, which suggest that high-redshift black holes formed in overdense regions frequently initially fall below local scaling relations, as their host stellar assembly significantly precedes initial accretion [Bulichi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.544..355B/abstract). Comprehensive models, such as the Delphi framework, successfully track this hierarchical assembly of gas, stars, and black holes at $z > 5$, predicting that the epoch of reionization significantly impacts the structural stellar mass density of these high-redshift hosts [Dayal et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.2336D/abstract).

Furthermore, JWST spectroscopy of an LRD at $z=7.04$ reveals a near-pristine environment with a metallicity of just $\sim 4\%$ solar [Maiolino et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522567M/abstract). The presence of a massive black hole in such a chemically un-enriched galaxy challenges standard Population III seeding models, DCBH scenarios, and super-Eddington accretion models, suggesting that exotic primordial black hole formation may be required to explain their existence without concurrent metal pollution from star formation [Maiolino et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522567M/abstract).

Despite the prevalence of over-massive black holes in early JWST data, the literature remains conflicted on whether this represents a universal phase of early galaxy evolution. Alternative selections have identified a population of massive galaxies at $z \sim 3$--$5$ hosting central black holes with mass ratios ($\sim 0.1\%$) that are strictly consistent with local scaling relations [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205048L/abstract). This suggests that diverse pathways for early supermassive black hole assembly coexist in the young Universe: some systems experience runaway black hole growth prior to stellar assembly, while others co-evolve more synchronously.

## Growth Channels: Hierarchical Mergers and Secular Accretion

The assembly of supermassive black holes is inextricably linked to the structural evolution of their host galaxies. Broadly, the mechanisms driving this mass assembly can be divided into two primary channels: violent, merger-driven growth and steady-state internal processes. The relative dominance of these channels is an area of active debate, heavily dependent on galaxy mass, morphology, and redshift.

In the standard paradigm of structure formation, hierarchical mergers are considered the primary drivers for the most extreme episodes of black hole growth. Genetic modification experiments in advanced cosmological suites like the EAGLE model definitively show that [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] are fundamentally essential for disrupting disk angular momentum support in order to fuel significant black hole assembly [Davies et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.4705D/abstract). During major mergers—typically defined by progenitor mass ratios greater than $1:3$—strong non-axisymmetric gravitational torques disrupt stellar orbits and strip angular momentum from the interstellar medium. Particularly in gas-rich mergers, this results in massive gas inflows toward the galactic nucleus, fueling rapid, often super-Eddington [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] phases [Bonoli et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.437.1576B/abstract), [Farrah et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.513.4770F/abstract). For example, observations of CO(3-2) in hyper-luminous quasars at $z=2.6$ reveal massively extended molecular gas reservoirs, with black hole gas consumption timescales fully consistent with high-redshift merger-driven evolutionary sequences [Feruglio et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...565A..91F/abstract). This merger channel naturally links the growth of the central black hole to the formation of [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]] and the transition into [[high-mass-early-type-galaxies|High-mass early-type galaxies]], providing a compelling explanation for tight empirical scaling relations observed in the local universe [Croton et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.369.1808C/abstract). Conversely, certain interactions can disrupt this fueling; head-on galaxy collisions, such as those inferred in the Andromeda galaxy, can strip the central gas torus, effectively shutting down the primary fueling source and strongly regulating the AGN duty cycle [Miki et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatAs...5..478M/abstract). Similarly, black holes are often "starved" in halos below a critical mass, with subsequent gas accretion episodes crucially and permanently linked to the joint assembly history of the overarching host halo [Piana et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.2146P/abstract).

However, relying exclusively on mergers to explain SMBH assembly is increasingly challenged by observations of massive black holes in bulgeless, pure-disk galaxies. Systems lacking classical bulges—which are the anticipated remnants of major mergers—can nonetheless host central black holes with masses exceeding $10^6 - 10^7\, M_\odot$ [Simmons et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.429.2199S/abstract), [Simmons et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.470.1559S/abstract). Semi-analytic models find that while merger-triggered bursts elegantly explain high-redshift growth spikes, an additional steady accretion mode is absolutely necessary to reproduce the observed low-redshift black hole luminosity function [Ricarte et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.1995R/abstract). This secular evolution sees steady-state processes such as galactic bars, spiral arms, and minor disk instabilities channeling cold gas inwards without destroying the galactic disk. Such stochastic, non-merger accretion suggests that black holes co-evolve directly with the total stellar mass or global dark matter halo. Notably, ROMULUS simulations indicate that long-term black hole accretion rates closely trace star formation rates directly across stellar masses ranging smoothly from $10^8$ to $10^{12}\, M_\odot$ [Ricarte et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.489..802R/abstract). Recent studies of star-forming galaxies reveal that the average black hole accretion rate correlates more strongly with host-galaxy compactness—specifically, the central surface mass density, $\Sigma_1$—than with total stellar mass or global star formation rate, reinforcing the importance of concentrated nuclear gas supplies in fueling secular growth [Ni et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.4989N/abstract). Furthermore, centrophilic orbit chaos induced by the central black hole can dynamically drive massive triaxial galaxies toward axisymmetry, fundamentally transforming large-scale galactic structure from the inside out [Holley-Bockelmann et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001astro.ph..4423H/abstract).

The physical modeling of these accretion channels in cosmological simulations remains a major theoretical challenge. Historically, simulations have relied on the spherical Bondi-Hoyle prescription to estimate the accretion rate onto the central singularity, but comparison tests show that implementing discrete Accretion Disc Particles (ADP) may sometimes capture excessive gas compared to carefully modified Bondi prescriptions [Muldrew et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1306.4327M/abstract). To reproduce observed black hole masses and scaling relations, Bondi-Hoyle models typically require extreme, artificially strong AGN feedback to self-regulate the gas supply and quench the [[specific-black-hole-accretion-rate|Specific black hole accretion rate]] [Anglés-Alcázar et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770....5A/abstract). Furthermore, recent general relativistic magnetohydrodynamic (GRMHD) studies demonstrate that magnetic stresses can suppress Bondi accretion by up to two orders of magnitude [Porras-Valverde et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251108683P/abstract), [Su et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251108668S/abstract). 

As an alternative, torque-limited accretion models have been proposed to better capture secular and merger-driven inflows. In this framework, angular momentum transport—driven by galactic-scale gravitational torques rather than spherical hydrodynamics—dictates the gas supply reaching the sub-parsec scale. Remarkably, torque-limited accretion models can successfully reproduce black hole-host scaling relations without the strict requirement of strong self-regulation by AGN feedback [Anglés-Alcázar et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...770....5A/abstract), [Anglés-Alcázar et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.2840A/abstract). Early remnants can also display unique dynamical signatures; for instance, orbiting black holes inherited from early Population III remnants may be detectable via the distinct three-arm spiral patterns they generate as they perturb central gaseous discs [Etherington et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.367.1003E/abstract). 

The current literature remains conflicted on the precise boundary between these growth channels. While gas-rich major mergers are required to trigger the most luminous quasars and assemble the most massive black holes at high redshift, secular evolution and torque-driven accretion likely dominate the long-term, moderate-luminosity growth of the broader AGN population across cosmic time.

## AGN Feedback and Quenching Mechanisms

In the standard paradigm of galaxy evolution, the energy liberated by [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] during their active accretion phase is the primary mechanism responsible for quenching star formation in [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] [Martín-Navarro et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832L..11M/abstract), [Davies et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501..236D/abstract). Without this AGN feedback, cosmological [[galaxy-models|Galaxy models]] consistently predict that massive galaxies would continue to cool gas and form stars at high rates down to $z=0$, failing to reproduce the observed population of quiescent, early-type galaxies [van de Voort et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..778V/abstract). The NIHAO cosmological simulation suite demonstrates this successfully, showing that realistic feedback mechanisms quench halos definitively above $10^{12}\, M_\odot$, effectively transforming rapidly star-forming systems into visually "red and dead" galaxies [Blank et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487.5476B/abstract).

Feedback becomes critically effective when the energy injected by the accreting black hole exceeds the gravitational binding energy of the host halo's gas. Galaxy groups serve as ideal astrophysical laboratories for testing this feedback phenomenon precisely because the cumulative energy injected by the central AGN can often exceed the gravitational binding energy of the entire intra-group gas [Eckert et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Univ....7..142E/abstract). Simulations indicate that this threshold is typically crossed when the emitted AGN energy reaches approximately $1\%$ of the halo binding energy, corresponding to halo masses of $M_{\rm h} \sim 10^{11.75}\, M_\odot$ at $z=4$ [Piana et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.5661P/abstract). In this regime, AGN-driven outflows can be up to two orders of magnitude more energetic than supernovae-driven winds [Piana et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.510.5661P/abstract). Observational surveys corroborate this gradual transition; specific star formation rate is shown to be a smoothly decreasing function of the $M_{\rm BH}/M_*$ ratio, indicating a continuous, gradual adjustment of the galaxy's gas reservoir to sustained central heating [Terrazas et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...844..170T/abstract).

The physical modes of feedback are traditionally divided into a highly luminous "quasar mode" at high accretion rates and a kinetic "radio mode" at low accretion rates. Kinetic winds become dominant in shutting down star formation when their energy injection exceeds the binding energy of gas within the galaxy's stellar radius [Terrazas et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1888T/abstract). Even in the quasar mode, kinetic feedback efficiencies as low as $0.1\%$ relative to the bolometric luminosity can profoundly alter the thermal state of the circumgalactic medium and suppress both galaxy and black hole growth [Anglés-Alcázar et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.2840A/abstract). 

A primary consequence of this energy injection is the expulsion and heating of the circumgalactic medium. By clearing the halo gas, [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|AGN feedback]] reconfigures the medium at a lower density, substantially extending its cooling time and starving the interstellar medium of fresh star-forming gas [Davies et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501..236D/abstract). This gas expulsion leaves distinct chemical and observational signatures. For instance, galaxies hosting over-massive black holes relative to the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ relation tend to be older and more $\alpha$-element enhanced, bearing the imprint of rapid, feedback-driven truncation of their star formation histories [Martín-Navarro et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...832L..11M/abstract). Furthermore, the highly metal-enriched material driven into the halo elevates column densities of O VI, which serves as a sensitive observational tracer of SMBH feedback [Sanchez et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...882....8S/abstract).

Interestingly, the ejective nature of AGN feedback produces large-scale anisotropic effects. Outflows powered by the central engine preferentially clear gas along the host galaxy's minor axis. Consequently, satellite galaxies located along the minor axis experience reduced ram pressure stripping, allowing them to preserve their star formation. Because of this, quenched satellites are observed to be significantly less frequent along the minor axis of [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]] [Martín-Navarro et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021Natur.594..187M/abstract).

Despite the strong consensus on the necessity of AGN feedback, the precise timing and physical mechanisms remain debated in AGN physics. Observations of intermediate optical colors in moderate-luminosity AGN hosts suggest a potential time delay of $\sim 100$ Myr between the cessation of star formation and detectable black hole accretion, complicating the causal link between activity and quenching [Schawinski et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...692L..19S/abstract). Moreover, while typically suppressive (negative feedback), AGN blast waves may occasionally induce positive feedback by compressing surrounding cold gas clouds and triggering localized starbursts. Intriguingly, positive feedback during the early, vigorous stages of supermassive black hole growth could physically explain the ubiquitous overabundance of Magnesium relative to Iron seen in local bright elliptical galaxies [Pipino et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.392..475P/abstract). The efficiency and impact of feedback are strongly mass-dependent; observations indicate that less massive AGN host galaxies ($\log M_* \sim 10$) often exhibit enhanced star formation rates relative to the star-forming main sequence, whereas more massive hosts lie below it, marking the transition where feedback successfully stalls star formation [Cristello et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...962..156C/abstract).

## Structural Evolution and the Two-Phase Formation Framework

The structural and morphological evolution of [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]] is best understood through the framework of [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]]. This paradigm dictates that massive systems assemble their baryonic mass through two distinct modes separated in both time and physical mechanism, directly linking the growth of the galaxy to the hierarchical assembly of its host dark matter halo [Johansson et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..354J/abstract), [Ma et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250916138M/abstract). Identifying individual massive galaxies at $z > 1.5$ allows astronomers to directly study these early elliptical and disk progenitors through their raw observed structures prior to late-time contamination [Conselice et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ASSL..301...47C/abstract). To test these complex galaxy assembly models beyond pure cosmological simulations, directly observing the historical baryon acquisition path provides an essential, empirical "third way" forward [Conselice et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..277..267C/abstract). 

**Phase 1: In-Situ Star Formation and Core Assembly**
At high redshifts ($z \gtrsim 2$), galaxy growth is dominated by the rapid, highly dissipative collapse of gas within fast-assembling dark matter halos. This massive influx of cold gas triggers intense, in-situ starbursts that build a dense, dynamically hot central core [Ma et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250916138M/abstract). During this highly dissipative phase, gas accretion efficiently fuels both star formation and the rapid concurrent growth of [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] [van de Voort et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..778V/abstract). Spatially resolved chemical properties, such as those cataloged by the MASSIV survey at $z \sim 1-2$, provide a vital statistical baseline for probing this exact high-redshift mass assembly phase [Contini et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011IAUS..277..134C/abstract). Furthermore, Mutual Information analysis on simulation suites rigorously confirms a strong co-evolutionary relationship exists between galactic star formation efficiency and the underlying assembly time of the dark matter halo in low-mass [[central-galaxies|Central galaxies]] [Camargo et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.tmp..248C/abstract).

Observations using integral field spectroscopy of local massive ellipticals and brightest cluster galaxies (e.g., NGC 3311) confirm the survival of these compact progenitors in the local Universe. The surviving cores are typically restricted to small radii ($R \lesssim 2$ kpc) and are characterized by extremely old stellar populations ($\gtrsim 11$ Gyr), high metallicities ($[Z/H] \sim 0.2$), bottom-heavy initial mass functions, and relatively low velocity dispersions ($\sigma_* \approx 180$ km s$^{-1}$) [Barbosa et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...649A..93B/abstract), [Jegatheesan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250109827J/abstract). Detailed progenitor tracking of massive red galaxies reveals an inverted timeline across mass bins: while the absolutely most massive central systems assemble very late via mergers, intermediate-mass galaxies consume gas quickly and assemble their cores much earlier [Stoppacher et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241205745S/abstract). For disk-dominated systems, analytical descriptions like the "leaky accretion disk" model have proven highly successful in reproducing the specific radial profiles of chemical abundances and star formation histories over time, such as those mapping the Milky Way's distinct disk assembly [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...970...34W/abstract).

**The Transition Point**
The early in-situ phase is truncated when the host dark matter halo transitions into a slow-accretion regime and AGN feedback becomes highly efficient. Discrepancies between theoretical model predictions and observations of rapidly declining specific star formation rates below $z=2$ suggest that fundamental modifications to assumed gas reincorporation timescales are required to match falling stellar assembly histories properly [Mitchell et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444.2637M/abstract). As the central black hole reaches a critical mass, the energy liberated by accretion regulates the host galaxy by heating or expelling the circumgalactic medium [van de Voort et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..778V/abstract), [Davies et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.501..236D/abstract). While the literature broadly agrees that AGN feedback drives this quenching, the precise timing and physical mode remain subjects of active debate [Schawinski et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...692L..19S/abstract). 

**Phase 2: Ex-Situ Accretion and Inside-Out Growth**
Once the galaxy becomes gas-poor and in-situ star formation stalls, structural evolution enters its second phase. At lower redshifts ($z \lesssim 1$), mass assembly is dominated by [[ex-situ-galaxy-growth|Ex-situ galaxy growth]] via "dry" (gas-poor) minor and [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] [Johansson et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..295..354J/abstract), [Jegatheesan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250109827J/abstract). However, theoretical work asserts that multiple dry mergers cannot simultaneously satisfy observed mass-metallicity and mass-alpha relations unless the individual progenitor galaxies already tightly obeyed these macroscopic scaling laws prior to their collision [Pipino et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...486..763P/abstract). Because accreted satellite galaxies generally possess higher specific angular momentum, their stellar material is stripped and deposited primarily in the galactic outskirts. The thermodynamic properties of the encompassing Intracluster Medium (ICM) act as highly reliable indicators of the multifaceted dynamical state and diverse ex-situ accretion histories of these large-scale clusters [Vallés-Pérez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A...1V/abstract).

This mechanism drives [[inside-out-formation|Inside-out formation]], which drastically increases the galaxy's effective radius without significantly altering the stellar mass of the dense core [van de Voort et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..778V/abstract). The transition to this ex-situ dominated phase is highly mass-dependent; cosmological simulations indicate that the ex-situ stellar fraction increases sharply for central galaxies with stellar masses $\log(M_*/{\rm M}_\odot) \gtrsim 11$, and mergers become the primary channel for growth once the host halo mass exceeds $\log(M_{200}/{\rm M}_\odot) \sim 13$ [Lee et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...836..161L/abstract). Recent observational constraints confirm that the majority of stellar mass growth since $z=1.1$ in both star-forming and quiescent galaxies occurs in their extended stellar halo regions ($2\text{--}10 R_e$), driven largely by the steady accretion of minor mergers [Williams et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241203662W/abstract). However, assembly at the extremes can stall; observations indicate that brightest cluster galaxies in highly massive clusters at $z \sim 0.4$ grow very little dynamically toward $z \sim 0.2$, a stark result that challenges current semi-analytic model predictions for continuous hierarchical growth [Inagaki et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.446.1107I/abstract). During this extended phase, substantial black hole growth can still occur even within extended quiescent galaxies, helping maintain local scaling relations [Aird et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515.4860A/abstract).

This spatially segregated assembly history leaves distinct chemo-kinematic signatures. While the in-situ core remains metal-rich, the accreted outer envelopes exhibit broader line-of-sight velocity distributions and are typically $\alpha$-enhanced, reflecting their origins in rapidly quenched satellite galaxies [Barbosa et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...649A..93B/abstract). Ultimately, this inside-out morphological evolution accounts for the observed size-mass relation of dynamically hot galaxies, where the initial core size scales homologously with the radius of its fast-assembled parent halo ($r_{\rm f} \approx 100 r_{\rm bulge}$) before subsequent merger-driven expansion freezes the final structure [Chen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.532.4340C/abstract).

## Assembly Across Mass Regimes: From Dwarfs to Ultramassive Systems

The connection between [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] (SMBHs) and their [[host-galaxies|Host galaxies]] exhibits pronounced morphological and dynamical variations across the full spectrum of galactic masses. From gas-rich dwarf galaxies to giant ellipticals, the mechanisms governing mass assembly and co-evolution shift dramatically. In the broader context of compact object astrophysics, studying expansive Galactic stellar-mass black hole populations directly provides critical contextual grounding for the evolution of more massive systems and the heavily varied pathways surrounding general compact object assembly [MacLeod et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230409368M/abstract). 

**Dwarf Galaxies and the Low-Mass Regime**
In the low-mass regime, the assembly of galaxies and their central black holes is heavily influenced by secular processes, stochastic gas accretion, and environmental effects. Recent observational campaigns have significantly expanded the sample of black holes in [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxies]]. Surprisingly, many of these systems host black holes that closely adhere to the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ scaling relations established for more massive galaxies, following an $M_{\rm BH} \propto \sigma^4$ power law rather than flattening out as predicted by some merger-driven assembly models [King et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.502L...1K/abstract). 

But the occupation fraction of intermediate-mass black holes and small SMBHs in this regime is highly sensitive to the [[galaxy-environment|Galaxy environment]]. Cosmological simulations predict that dwarf galaxies ($M_* < 10^9\, M_\odot$) residing in dense cluster environments possess an occupation fraction approximately twice that of field dwarfs. This discrepancy arises because cluster dwarfs typically formed earlier in the universe, whereas the field population includes many late-forming dwarfs that pull the average occupation fraction down [Tremmel et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024OJAp....7E..26T/abstract). While cluster-level environments dramatically alter occupation rates, global stellar mass growth in large central galaxies appears largely insensitive to the wider, large-scale cosmological environment, despite mild structural effects introduced by assembly bias in smaller halos [Jung et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...794...74J/abstract). Furthermore, while close dwarf-dwarf interactions can drive accretion—with integral field surveys demonstrating that dwarf galaxy mergers can trigger AGN at physical separations below $20$ kpc—surveys also find that the overall AGN fraction in dwarf galaxies paradoxically increases in lower-density environments, highlighting the complex interplay between merger triggers and environmental quenching [Eróstegui et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...699A.330E/abstract).

Observations of [[active-dwarf-galaxies|Active dwarf galaxies]] such as [[henize-2-10|Henize 2-10]] demonstrate that $\sim 10^6\, M_\odot$ black holes can grow efficiently in the absence of a classical bulge, indicating that black hole accretion can precede bulge assembly [Reines et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Natur.470...66R/abstract). Furthermore, scaling relations for dwarf galaxies exhibit significant intrinsic scatter. This dispersion is attributed to inefficient black hole formation in low-mass halos with high angular momentum, as well as to dynamical disruptions. For instance, asymmetric gravitational wave emission during black hole mergers can impart recoil kicks that displace the black hole, creating a population of [[wandering-black-holes|Wandering black holes]]. Supporting this dynamic picture, X-ray surveys of local dwarf galaxies reveal that up to $50\%$ of accreting black holes are spatially offset from the photometric centers of their hosts [Sacchi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240601707S/abstract), [Volonteri et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...737...50V/abstract).

**Ultramassive Systems and the High-Mass Regime**
At the opposite extreme, the assembly of the most [[massive-galaxies|Massive galaxies]]—such as brightest cluster galaxies and massive ellipticals with $M_* \gtrsim 2 \times 10^{12}\, M_\odot$—is dictated by the [[two-phase-galaxy-growth|Two-phase galaxy growth]] framework described above [van de Voort et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..778V/abstract), [Nguyen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.3548N/abstract). This regime serves as the host environment for [[ultramassive-black-holes|Ultramassive black holes]] with masses exceeding $10^{10}\, M_\odot$. Observations of X-ray AGN suggest that these most massive black holes show little relative mass gain since $z=4$, implying that they required rapid, intense growth episodes prior to this epoch to reach their observed masses [Guetzoyan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.536...79G/abstract).

In some cases, the resulting mass ratios strongly deviate from standard co-evolutionary scaling relations. A notable example is the compact lenticular galaxy NGC 1277, which hosts an [[overmassive-black-hole|Overmassive black hole]] of $1.7 \times 10^{10}\, M_\odot$ that accounts for roughly $59\%$ of the galaxy's entire bulge mass [van den Bosch et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.491..729V/abstract). Such extreme outliers suggest that some ultramassive black holes assembled the bulk of their mass during an early, highly efficient accretion phase, but their host galaxy was subsequently deprived of the late-time, merger-driven stellar accretion that typically builds the extended halos of giant ellipticals. Overall, the scaling relations at the highest masses reflect a transition where black hole feedback efficiently quenches star formation, decoupling late-time black hole accretion from the suppressed stellar mass growth of the host [van de Voort et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462..778V/abstract).

## Fossil Tracers: Globular Clusters and Nuclear Star Clusters

Stellar clusters serve as durable chronological records of a galaxy's structural and chemical assembly. Because they survive the violent dynamical processes that shape their host galaxies, globular clusters and nuclear star clusters provide distinct "fossil" signatures of both early in-situ star formation and ex-situ accretion. 

In massive galaxies, the metallicity bimodality of the [[galactic-globular-cluster-system|Galactic globular cluster system]] is widely understood to be a natural consequence of hierarchical assembly [Tonini et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...762...39T/abstract). Theoretical models indicate that the metal-rich subpopulation typically forms in-situ within the main galaxy progenitor at $z \sim 2$, whereas the metal-poor subpopulation consists of clusters that formed earlier ($z \sim 3-4$) and were subsequently accreted from satellite halos [Tonini et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...762...39T/abstract). The absolute oldest globular clusters, with confirmed ages exceeding 12.5 Gyr, provide direct observational insight into galaxy assembly during the cosmological epoch of reionization [Forbes et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018RSPSA.47470616F/abstract). This structural bimodality is a mass-dependent phenomenon, generally disappearing in [[dwarf-galaxy-populations|dwarf galaxies]] with $M_* \lesssim 10^9\, M_\odot$ [Tonini et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...762...39T/abstract). However, high-speed collisions between dense subhalos during early assembly phases may dynamically create unique "naked" globular clusters as initial gas pools violently decouple from their dark matter and undergo profound shock compression [Madau et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...890...18M/abstract).

Cosmological simulations demonstrate that a globular cluster system's age-metallicity distribution is a highly sensitive probe of its host's mass growth and minor merger history. By mapping these distributions, researchers can reconstruct a host galaxy's merger tree and identify progenitor halos with stellar masses $M_* \gtrsim 10^8\, M_\odot$ out to $z = 2.5$ [Kruijssen et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.3134K/abstract). At a macroscopic level, the total mass of the globular cluster system scales linearly with the host's dark matter halo mass above $5 \times 10^{11}\, M_\odot$, yielding a remarkably constant ratio of $M_{\rm GC}/M_{\rm halo} \sim 5 \times 10^{-5}$ [Bastian et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.1050B/abstract). Extrapolating to the outskirts of clusters, extremely large telescopes will soon enable precision observations of individual stars directly in external halos, definitively testing hierarchical assembly processes through precise chemical abundance patterns [Kirby et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009astro2010S.156K/abstract). 

Kinematic data further decouple a galaxy's accreted and in-situ populations. The discovery of pronounced mass stratification in the globular cluster system of the S0 galaxy NGC 3115 rigorously reflects the hierarchical assembly and sequential inside-out growth that built its massive stellar halo [Dou et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250921975D/abstract). In the [[milky-way|Milky Way]], the distinct orbital kinematics of metal-poor and metal-rich clusters point to a rapid early assembly involving more than a dozen low-mass mergers and $1.4 \pm 1.2$ early [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]] around $z \approx 3.1$ [Trujillo-Gomez et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503...31T/abstract). Theoretical frameworks coupling galaxy formation explicitly with cluster synthesis predict that up to 30% of the Milky Way's halo globular clusters may actually originate from these accreted satellite galaxies [Angeloni et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251211059A/abstract). Additionally, precise asteroseismic dating of Milky Way stars provides indisputable evidence that a substantial thick disk population already existed long before the famous Gaia-Enceladus/Sausage merger impacted the system over 10 billion years ago [Montalbán et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatAs...5..640M/abstract). Within [[galactic-bulges|Galactic Bulges]], complex fossil fragments such as the Liller 1 and Terzan 5 clusters host distinct multi-age stellar populations (e.g., 1-3 Gyr and 12 Gyr), providing direct proof that the Milky Way spheroid was built through the coalescence of massive primordial structures [Ferraro et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatAs...5..311F/abstract). Conversely, models of the Andromeda galaxy feature highly energetic in-situ clusters and ex-situ clusters at large radii, bearing the kinematic scars of a much more recent major merger [Chen et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.3692C/abstract). Beyond distinct cluster populations, the bulk structure of the galactic disc itself serves as a fossil record; for example, the vertical age-thickness relation of the Milky Way stellar disk identifies an abrupt increase in thickness approximately $11.13$ Gyr ago, directly linked to the ancient Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus merger event [Thulasidharan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241212304T/abstract).

In the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]], nuclear star clusters (NSCs) sit at the bottom of the galactic potential well and share a complex co-evolutionary relationship with central supermassive black holes. The dense Milky Way center itself serves as an unparalleled laboratory for studying how extreme local tidal fields and high stellar densities influence the steady growth of a central massive black hole [Alexander et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005PhR...419...65A/abstract). NSC assembly is driven by two mechanisms: in-situ star formation triggered by infalling gas, and the ex-situ migration of globular clusters that spiral into the center via dynamical friction [Antonini et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812...72A/abstract), [Gao et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.7731G/abstract). The relative contribution of in-situ versus ex-situ formation to the NSC remains a subject of active debate; however, semi-analytical models suggest that in-situ formation can account for up to $\sim 80\%$ of the total NSC mass [Antonini et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812...72A/abstract). The assembly timeline of the host halo heavily influences this process: galaxies that assemble earlier tend to host more massive and spatially concentrated globular cluster systems, driving greater stellar mass deposition into the NSC [Gao et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.527.7731G/abstract). Furthermore, in luminous galaxies hosting binary supermassive black holes, dynamical heating from the black holes can partially erode the NSC, producing NSC-host scaling relations that are shallower than those observed for the black holes themselves [Antonini et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...812...72A/abstract).

## Dynamical Signatures and Binary Evolution

When galaxies undergo [[major-mergers|Major Mergers]], their central black holes sink toward the center of the newly merged gravitational potential, leading to the formation of massive black hole binaries (MBHBs). In gas-rich environments, dissipation and gravitational torques from a massive, self-gravitating nuclear disc promote rapid inspiral down to sub-parsec scales, potentially triggering [[kiloparsec-scale-binary-agns|double active galactic nuclei]] on timescales of $< 1$ Myr [Colpi et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0710.5207C/abstract). Historically, it has been proposed that distinct, observable AGN classes may actually represent four distinct, sequential evolutionary stages of supermassive black hole binaries transitioning securely from early large-scale mergers through to pre-coalescence [Lobanov et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005MmSAI..76..164L/abstract). Observational evidence of such dual systems includes the Seyfert galaxy NGC 3393, which hosts two active massive black holes separated by roughly $150$ pc [Fabbiano et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Natur.477..431F/abstract). In the time domain, a systematic search for close supermassive black hole binaries in the Catalina Real-time Transient Survey (CRTS) has identified over 100 candidate massive black hole binaries characterized by periodic variability signals, such as the 5.2-year period observed in PG 1302-102 [Graham et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.1562G/abstract). In these coalescing environments, nuclear stellar discs (NSDs) act as fragile dynamical tracers of a galaxy's merger history. While major mergers typically disrupt NSDs entirely, these structures often survive intermediate and minor mergers, preserving a detailed photometric and kinematic record of the galaxy's recent assembly mode [Sarzi et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.1070S/abstract).

A classical theoretical hurdle in binary evolution is the "final parsec problem," wherein binary hardening stalls following the depletion of the local loss cone of stars. Crucially, the exact physical timescales required for black hole pair coalescence in these late-stage mergers remain poorly constrained, introducing severe overarching uncertainties in the expected event rates for next-generation gravitational wave observatories [Dotti et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AdAst2012E...3D/abstract). Current research suggests this stalling problem can be overcome by gas-driven hardening or the complex dynamics of triple SMBH systems. Because galaxy assembly is highly hierarchical, subsequent mergers can introduce a third black hole into the nucleus before the initial binary coalesces. $N$-body simulations indicate that in triaxial host potentials, the two most massive SMBHs typically form a rapidly hardening binary that coalesces within a fraction of a Hubble time, while the third remains on a wider orbit or forms a stable hierarchical triple [Saha et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251020466S/abstract). Furthermore, secular Kozai-Lidov mechanisms in triple SMBH systems can drive the inner binary to extreme eccentricities ($e \gtrsim 0.9$) [Bonetti et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.4044B/abstract). In the stellar-mass domain, merging binaries also mirror their hosts' evolutionary tracks; simulations suggest that rare low-metallicity star formation occurring during high-redshift galaxy assembly significantly boosts the production rate of LIGO-detectable stellar binary black hole mergers compared to more typical cosmic star formation periods [O'Shaughnessy et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.464.2831O/abstract). These stellar binary black hole mergers at low redshifts overwhelmingly occur preferentially in highly massive galaxies ($M_* \gtrsim 2 \times 10^{10}\, M_\odot$), yielding a uniquely bimodal distribution for the stellar mass of their galactic hosts [Cao et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.474.4997C/abstract). Modern chemical evolution models that carefully match cosmic star formation histories successfully predict the mass distribution and merger rates for these entire binary populations [Dvorkin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.461.3877D/abstract).

Upon the final coalescence of the binary, the anisotropic emission of gravitational waves imparts a momentum kick to the remnant black hole. This gravitational wave recoil can reach velocities of up to $4000$ km/s, sufficient to displace or entirely eject the black hole from its host galaxy [Komossa et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...678L..81K/abstract), [Dunn et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...896...72D/abstract). Such events give rise to [[wandering-black-holes|Wandering black holes]] or off-nuclear AGNs. Actively accreting recoiling black holes physically appear as distinct quasars that are kinematically or spatially offset from the centers of their hosts, a phenomenon possessing massive potential to dramatically alter or challenge strictly unified AGN theoretical models [Komossa et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AdAst2012E..14K/abstract). Theoretical models predict that non-rotating recoiled black holes may settle $1\text{--}30$ kpc from their galactic centers, while rapidly rotating ones can be displaced by hundreds of kiloparsecs [Volonteri et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...687L..57V/abstract). A prominent candidate for a recoiling supermassive black hole is the quasar SDSS J092712.65+294344.0, which exhibits an emission line velocity offset of $2650$ km/s [Komossa et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...678L..81K/abstract). While recoil events can drastically reduce the mass of the central black hole in high-redshift ($z \sim 5$) galaxies by up to an order of magnitude, the overall occupation fraction remains largely unaffected due to subsequent seeding and merging episodes [Dunn et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...896...72D/abstract).

The inspiral and merger of MBHBs are among the most energetic phenomena in space, and coalescing supermassive binaries are broadly expected to be the loudest absolute sources of gravitational waves in the Universe, carrying significant astrophysical implications for hierarchical structure formation and galaxy merger physics [Komossa et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016IAUFM..29B.292K/abstract). The stochastic background from these unresolved binary populations is a primary target for Pulsar Timing Arrays (PTAs). Current upper limits from PTA observations have placed constraints on the population parameters of MBHBs, though these limits do not necessarily imply that binary evolution is universally stalled [Middleton et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018NatCo...9..573M/abstract). At higher frequencies, the mergers of individual MBHBs ($10^4\text{--}10^7\, M_\odot$) will be directly detectable by the upcoming Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA). Cosmological models predict that LISA will detect $25\text{--}75$ events per year, with triple-induced coalescences potentially accounting for $\sim 30\%$ of these detections [Bonetti et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.4044B/abstract). Electromagnetic precursors to these LISA events, manifesting as periodically varying AGNs with $5\text{--}12$ day orbital periods, are expected to be identified by time-domain surveys in the coming decade [Haiman et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230614990H/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Perspectives

The co-evolution of [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] (SMBHs) and their host galaxies remains framed by significant observational and theoretical tensions, particularly at the extremes of cosmic time and mass. The recent JWST detection of over-massive black holes ($M_{\rm BH}/M_* > 10^{-3}$) in highly un-enriched ($4\%$ solar metallicity) galaxies at $z>6$ has fundamentally challenged $\Lambda$CDM seeding models [Scoggins et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.2155S/abstract), [Maiolino et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250522567M/abstract). Reconciling these high-redshift detections remains an open question. Proposed solutions include heavy initial seeds—such as [[direct-collapse-black-holes|Direct collapse black holes]]—or short, repeated bursts of super-Eddington accretion lasting $0.5-3$ Myr [Trinca et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241214248T/abstract). Intriguingly, such super-Eddington growth models predict a distinct gap in the black hole mass function within the intermediate-mass range ($10^4-10^6\, M_\odot$), which may serve as a critical observational test for high-redshift accretion scenarios [Piana et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.530.1732P/abstract). Fundamentally, varying initial seed mass models lead directly to widely different predicted halo occupation fractions down the timeline, which serves as a primary population constraint that upcoming space-based gravitational wave detections by LISA and ground-based PTA campaigns must inevitably test [DeGraf et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.491.4973D/abstract).

The apparent early assembly of black holes relative to their hosts is further complicated by selection bias in dynamical mass measurements, which may artificially inflate the normalization of local scaling relations [Shankar et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.493.1500S/abstract). Determining whether the $M_{\rm BH}-\sigma$ relation genuinely evolves with redshift, or if observed high-redshift offsets are purely selection effects, is a major ongoing effort [Farrah et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...981...71F/abstract). Furthermore, theoretical attempts to model these populations must grapple with galaxy assembly bias—the dependence of galaxy clustering and occupation fractions on dark matter halo properties other than mass. Assembly bias introduces significant systematic errors into interpretations of the galaxy-halo-black hole connection, making it difficult to isolate the physical drivers of SMBH growth [Zentner et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.443.3044Z/abstract), [García-Moreno et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250406770G/abstract). To address these challenges, new frameworks such as StAGE (Stellar Archaeology-driven Galaxy Evolution) use stellar archaeology and near-infrared-dark high-redshift progenitors to reconstruct the mass assembly history of quiescent galaxies and their central black holes [Bosi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...984..117B/abstract).

In the nanohertz gravitational wave regime, Pulsar Timing Array (PTA) observations initially placed stringent upper limits on the stochastic background, prompting debates over whether massive black hole binaries stall prior to coalescence [Middleton et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018NatCo...9..573M/abstract). However, recent parameterized models successfully reconcile PTA limits with the enhanced high-redshift black hole luminosity function observed by JWST, provided that early super-Eddington accretion episodes are common [Toubiana et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241017916T/abstract). 

Upcoming observatories will be critical in resolving these tensions and exploring the dynamical fate of SMBHs. The Roman Space Telescope is expected to identify hundreds of active, short-period binary systems acting as LISA precursors [Haiman et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023arXiv230614990H/abstract). LISA itself will detect the coalescences of $10^{4-7}\, M_\odot$ massive black hole binaries, directly testing seed formation models, measuring binary eccentricity driven by triple SMBH interactions [Bonetti et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.4044B/abstract), and probing the frequency of gravitational wave recoil [Dunn et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...896...72D/abstract). In the electromagnetic domain, off-nuclear AGN signatures resulting from these recoil events will be targeted by observatories such as the James Webb Space Telescope and the Hubble Space Telescope [Volonteri et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...687L..57V/abstract). Future premier missions, such as the proposed X-ray Surveyor, explicitly aim to probe the environments of the very first galaxy groups and directly map the growth of primordial black hole seeds with significantly higher sensitivity than Chandra could achieve [Gaskin et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015SPIE.9601E..0JG/abstract). Additionally, the Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) will provide sub-parsec kinematic resolution to weigh [[ultramassive-black-holes|Ultramassive black holes]] in local massive galaxies [Nguyen et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.3548N/abstract), the next-generation Very Large Array (ngVLA) will probe obscured accretion out to cosmic dawn [Rujopakarn et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018arXiv181007098R/abstract), and the Athena X-ray observatory will map the large-scale impact of [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] and feedback on the circumgalactic medium [Castellani et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022arXiv220902539C/abstract).
