**Extreme AGN and quasar populations** encompass outlier subsets of active galactic nuclei (AGN) that push the boundaries of supermassive black hole (SMBH) accretion physics and host galaxy co-evolution. While standard textbook models often describe AGN and [[quasars|Quasars]] as a relatively uniform class separated primarily by bolometric luminosity and viewing angle [Beckmann et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012agn..book.....B/abstract), extreme populations represent distinct, highly dynamic phases of galaxy evolution. These subsets include hyper-luminous sources at cosmic noon, heavily obscured (dust-reddened) systems, and rapidly varying or super-Eddington accretors. Understanding these extreme outliers is essential for testing [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|AGN Classification and Unified Models]] [Urry et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0712.1041U/abstract), as modern multi-wavelength observations increasingly demonstrate that features like extreme obscuration frequently trace specific, gas-rich evolutionary stages rather than simple geometric orientation effects. 

The comprehensive identification and characterization of these sources require synthesizing X-ray, mid-infrared, radio, and time-domain surveys, as no single wavelength can capture the full census of nuclear activity. For example, radio and infrared selections are critical for uncovering heavily obscured, Compton-thick systems—such as [[hot-dog-hot-dust-obscured-galaxy|Hot DOG (Hot Dust-obscured Galaxy)]] populations and [[reddened-quasars|Reddened quasars]]—that are systematically missed by deep X-ray surveys. Furthermore, extreme AGN provide the most stringent observational tests of feedback mechanisms. In these systems, radiatively driven multiphase outflows and mechanically driven radio jets inject immense kinetic energy into the host's interstellar and [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]]. Cross-matching the multi-wavelength properties of these AGN with their host environments reveals distinct clustering behaviors and accretion modes, fundamentally linking SMBH fueling to both secular internal processes and large-scale dark matter halos [Hickox et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...696..891H/abstract), [Goulding et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783...40G/abstract). Additionally, the discovery of extreme temporal variability, such as Changing-Look AGN and Tidal Disruption Events, highlights the highly stochastic nature of black hole fueling and challenges assumptions of steady-state accretion [LaMassa et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800..144L/abstract).

In recent years, the high-redshift frontier has fundamentally challenged legacy models of [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]] [Small et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992MNRAS.259..725S/abstract). The advent of JWST has unveiled a vast population of ultra-compact, heavily obscured sources at $z > 5$ known as [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]], which are over a hundred times more abundant than traditional UV-selected [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]]. If these sources are predominantly powered by supermassive black holes, they appear to be overmassive by up to 4 dex compared to local host-mass scaling relations, contradicting the classical assumption that early SMBH growth was restricted to rare, highly luminous quasars. This article details the multi-wavelength taxonomy of extreme AGN, explores the physics of extreme accretion modes and multiphase feedback, and examines their environmental triggering. Finally, it outlines the open questions driving modern research, including the true physical nature of Little Red Dots, the substantial contribution of faint AGN to cosmic reionization, and the unresolved controversy of whether AGN feedback actively quenches star formation or merely maintains prior quiescence.

## Taxonomy and Multi-wavelength Selection of Extreme AGN

The accurate classification and census of active galactic nuclei (AGN) require a synthesis of multi-wavelength diagnostics, as no single observational band can capture the full diversity of accretion states, obscuration levels, and host galaxy properties [Azadi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835...27A/abstract), [Radcliffe et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...649A..27R/abstract). Historically, [[quasars|Quasars]] and Seyfert 1 galaxies were divided by an arbitrary absolute magnitude threshold of $M_B = -21.5 + 5\log h$, with standard optical identification relying on broad emission lines or narrow-line flux ratios mapped on the [[multiple-diagnostic-active-galactic-nuclei|BPT diagram]]. However, optical and UV surveys are highly sensitive to dust extinction and host-galaxy contrast. Indeed, optical sub-type classification is frequently driven by the AGN-to-host galaxy luminosity contrast, where a high host galaxy luminosity can dilute and hide broad emission lines even in the absence of high intrinsic extinction [Barquín-González et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...687A.159B/abstract).

In the X-ray regime, high-energy emission is a relatively unbiased tracer of radiatively efficient accretion. The 5-10 keV band is particularly valuable for constraining the X-ray luminosity function, as it minimizes the need for absorption corrections up to column densities of $N_H \sim 10^{23} \text{ cm}^{-2}$ [Fotopoulou et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...587A.142F/abstract). Furthermore, hard X-ray selections such as the NuSTAR 80-month serendipitous survey detect an excess of redder broad-line AGN compared to optical surveys, highlighting populations hidden by moderate dust [Greenwell et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJS..273...20G/abstract). However, standard X-ray selection systematically misses heavily obscured, Compton-thick AGN ($N_H > 10^{24} \text{ cm}^{-2}$). Mid-infrared (MIR) color selection and radio continuum observations are critical for identifying these hidden populations. Recent observations using JWST's MIRI instrument, such as the SMILES survey, have further revolutionized this selection, revealing that approximately 80% of mid-infrared-selected AGN candidates were previously unknown due to extreme obscuration [Lyu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966..229L/abstract). The surface density of Compton-thick sources detected via radio emission is predicted to be on average ten times higher than those detected in X-rays, and even greater at high redshifts [Mazzolari et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...687A.120M/abstract). Furthermore, multi-wavelength cross-matching reveals a stark environmental dichotomy: radiatively efficient X-ray and MIR-selected AGN typically reside in star-forming or "green valley" galaxies, while mechanically-dominated, radio-loud AGN are predominantly hosted by massive, passive elliptical galaxies [Hickox et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...696..891H/abstract), [Goulding et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783...40G/abstract). Detailed radio observations, such as the VLA-COSMOS 3 GHz Large Project, demonstrate distinct host-galaxy properties between high-luminosity (HLAGN) and moderate-luminosity (MLAGN) radio sources, further emphasizing the environmental dependence of different accretion regimes [Delvecchio et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...602A...3D/abstract).

Time-domain astronomy provides another powerful selection mechanism. Variability selection via optical and MIR light curves is highly effective at identifying low-luminosity AGN whose emission might otherwise be outshone by stellar light [Villforth et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426..360V/abstract). This technique is also the primary method for discovering Changing-Look AGN (CLAGN)—extreme transients that rapidly transition between Type 1 and Type 2 states. Spectroscopic and MIR monitoring indicates that these dramatic transitions are primarily driven by intrinsic changes in the accretion rate rather than variable line-of-sight obscuration [LaMassa et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800..144L/abstract), [Sheng et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889...46S/abstract).

Robustly characterizing the host galaxies of extreme AGN requires advanced Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) fitting to de-blend the nuclear emission from the surrounding galaxy. Failing to properly model the AGN contribution can lead to the systematic overestimation of host stellar masses by up to $0.5$ dex and significantly skew derived star formation rates [Florez et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497.3273F/abstract), [Buchner et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...692A.161B/abstract). Furthermore, when the AGN contribution exceeds 20% of the total optical light, it can significantly bias morphological measurements, often causing the host galaxy to appear artificially more bulge-dominated and bluer than its true underlying stellar population [Pierce et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.405..718P/abstract). To aid in this separation, tools like the model-free 'Index Diagram' have been developed to robustly identify spectra with significant host-galaxy stellar light contributions without requiring full SED modeling [Negrete et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.543.4272N/abstract). Modern SED fitting codes (such as CIGALE, PROSPECT, and GRAHSP) dynamically incorporate AGN templates, proving indispensable for studies of [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|AGN Classification and Unified Models]] [Thorne et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.4940T/abstract). For infrared wavelengths, tools such as the IRAGNSEP spectral energy distribution fitting code have been instrumental in constructing templates to accurately separate intrinsic AGN mid-to-far-infrared emission from host galaxy dust components, particularly within hard [[swift-bat-agn-spectroscopic-survey|X-ray Swift-BAT selected samples]] [Bernhard et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.2598B/abstract). In recent DESI data, for instance, SED fitting effectively identifies approximately 87% of [[wise-selected-quasars|WISE-selected AGN]] and provides complementary detections to traditional BPT diagnostics [Siudek et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609143S/abstract).

Despite these advances, new multi-wavelength discoveries continue to challenge established paradigms. For example, SED fitting of the highly abundant $z > 5$ [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]] population yields ambiguous results; there is significant, unresolved debate regarding whether their red continuum is dominated by dust-obscured AGN or [[extreme-starbursts|extremely compact starbursts]]. If they are AGN-dominated, their bolometric luminosities suggest they harbor exceptionally massive black holes relative to their hosts [Akins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240610341A/abstract), [Durodola et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240610329D/abstract).

## The High-Redshift Frontier and JWST Discoveries

JWST has revolutionized our view of the $z > 5$ universe, severely challenging classical textbook paradigms that assumed early supermassive black hole (SMBH) growth was dominated exclusively by rare, highly luminous quasars. Most prominently, JWST has uncovered a vast population of compact, extremely red sources at $z > 4$, colloquially termed [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]] (LRDs). These sources are over 100 times more abundant than traditional UV-selected [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] and quasars at comparable redshifts [Akins et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991...37A/abstract).

The true physical nature of LRDs remains a subject of intense debate. While their spectral energy distributions (SEDs) can theoretically be fit by either dusty, compact star-forming populations or obscured AGN, detailed multi-wavelength SED modeling strongly favors a hybrid scenario where AGN emission contributes between 40% and 85% of the rest-frame optical luminosity [Durodola et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240610329D/abstract). However, stacking of deep X-ray, mid-infrared, and radio observations currently yields non-detections, leaving the purely AGN-dominated interpretation contested [Akins et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991...37A/abstract).

If the LRD population is indeed AGN-dominated, it poses a severe challenge to standard models of [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]]. Black hole mass estimates for these sources indicate that they are overmassive relative to their host galaxies by 2 to 4 dex when compared to the local $M_\bullet - M_\star$ relation [Durodola et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240610329D/abstract). For instance, a highly obscured LRD at $z = 7.3$ ($A_V \approx 3.26$ mag) hosts an estimated $M_{\rm BH} = 7 \times 10^8 M_\odot$ and exhibits a spatial cross-correlation length with nearby galaxies of $r_0 = 9 \pm 2 \ h^{-1} \, \mathrm{cMpc}$. This strong clustering requires a minimum dark matter halo mass of $\log_{10}(M_{\rm halo, min}/M_\odot) = 12.3_{-0.8}^{+0.7}$, implying a near 100% AGN duty cycle in these halos—drastically different from the $<1\%$ duty cycle of UV-luminous quasars [Schindler et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241111534S/abstract). 

The unexpected abundance of these [[low-luminosity-quasars|faint, high-redshift AGN]] has also prompted a reevaluation of the sources driving cosmic reionization. While textbooks historically attributed the Epoch of Reionization almost entirely to star-forming galaxies, recent joint constraints on the rest-UV AGN luminosity function suggest that faint AGN (including unobscured Type I subsets of the LRD population) injected Lyman-continuum escape photons at a rate of $\dot{N}_{\rm ion, AGN} = 3.77_{-0.95}^{+1.08} \times 10^{51} \, \mathrm{s}^{-1} \, \mathrm{Mpc}^{-3}$. This implies that AGN supplied 31% to 75% of the total ionizing photon budget required to maintain hydrogen reionization at $z > 5$ [Singha et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251026990S/abstract).

In addition to LRDs, JWST observations of luminous $z > 5$ quasars reveal extreme feedback mechanisms and complex accretion histories. Early quasars exhibit exceptionally powerful [O III] outflows reaching velocities up to $8400 \, \mathrm{km \, s}^{-1}$, with kinetic energy outflow rates occasionally reaching up to $\sim 260\%$ of the quasar's bolometric luminosity, $L_{\rm bol}$ [Liu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250908793L/abstract). Initial analyses of JWST observations further show that many X-ray-luminous AGN at $3 < z < 5$ reside in spheroidal or bulge-dominated hosts with colors consistent with quenched or post-starburst populations, showing little evidence of major merger disturbances [Kocevski et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...946L..14K/abstract). Concurrently, high-resolution spectroscopy has identified early quasars, such as HSC J2239+0207 at $z \sim 6.25$, that are accreting at sub-Eddington rates ($\lambda_{\rm Edd} \sim 0.4$) despite already hosting a $3 \times 10^8 M_\odot$ black hole [Lyu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...981L..20L/abstract). The existence of such massive, sub-Eddington black holes so early in cosmic history suggests a fading quasar phase and provides strong circumstantial evidence for heavy initial black hole seeding during the cosmic dark ages.

## Heavily Obscured Populations and Hot DOGs

The traditional view of [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|AGN Classification and Unified Models]] posits that obscuration is primarily an orientation effect. However, observations of hyper-luminous, dust-reddened systems suggest that extreme obscuration often traces a distinct, short-lived evolutionary phase associated with the rapid, concurrent growth of the supermassive black hole and its host galaxy [Andonie et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.2577A/abstract).

Among the most extreme of these sources are the [[hot-dog-hot-dust-obscured-galaxy|Hot DOG (Hot Dust-obscured Galaxy)]] population and hyper-luminous infrared galaxies (HLIRGs). Hot DOGs are typically identified as "W1W2 dropouts" in the mid-infrared, rendering them visible as [[wise-selected-quasars|WISE-selected quasars]] but largely invisible in optical bands. These systems emit the bulk of their bolometric luminosity in the mid-infrared due to intensely heated dust ($\sim$ hundreds of Kelvin) driven by deeply embedded, hyper-luminous AGN. While prevalent at $z \sim 2-4$, rare low-redshift analogs ($z < 0.5$) have recently been identified with a surface density of $2.4 \times 10^{-3} \text{ deg}^{-2}$. These rare local Hot DOGs feature accretion rates close to the Eddington limit and harbor black holes that sit above the local host-mass scaling relations [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250205813L/abstract). Similarly, spectral energy distribution (SED) decomposition of HLIRGs reveals that while the overall bolometric output is usually AGN-dominated, modeling their infrared excess often requires substantial composite templates, indicating that $>50\%$ of their emission can originate from [[extreme-starbursts|extreme, concurrent starburst activity]] [Ruiz et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...515A..99R/abstract), [Ruiz et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ASSP...14..347R/abstract).

Broad selections of highly luminous infrared quasars ($L_{\rm AGN, IR} > 10^{45} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$) show that the majority ($\approx 61\%$) are heavily obscured, possessing column densities $N_{\rm H} > 10^{22} \text{ cm}^{-2}$. These obscured systems exhibit star formation rates approximately three times higher than their unobscured counterparts, alongside a $0.2 \text{ dex}$ enhancement in radio-loudness. This multi-wavelength behavior directly contradicts purely orientation-based unification models, favoring a scenario where [[reddened-quasars|Reddened quasars]] represent a specific gas- and dust-rich evolutionary stage [Andonie et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.2577A/abstract). Furthermore, identifying the most heavily obscured, Compton-thick AGN ($N_{\rm H} > 10^{24} \text{ cm}^{-2}$) within these populations remains a multi-wavelength challenge. Extrapolations of AGN population synthesis models demonstrate that radio selections can uncover a surface density of Compton-thick AGN at high redshift that is an order of magnitude larger than what is detected in deep X-ray surveys, which frequently miss these sources entirely [Mazzolari et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...687A.120M/abstract).

At the highest redshifts ($z > 4$), JWST has unveiled a vast population of compact, dust-reddened systems known as [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]]. These sources exhibit rest-frame optical colors that mimic both [[extremely-red-quasars|Extremely red quasars]] and compact starbursts, and they are remarkably abundant, outnumbering UV-selected quasars by factors of $>100$ [Akins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240610341A/abstract). If their emission is indeed AGN-dominated, their bolometric luminosities reach $10^{45-47} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$, challenging local scaling relations as noted above [Durodola et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240610329D/abstract), [Akins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240610341A/abstract).

## AGN Feedback: Outflows, Jets, and Gas Depletion

Active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback regulates star formation and establishes the co-evolution of supermassive black holes and their host galaxies. This feedback typically operates in two primary regimes: a radiatively efficient "quasar mode" driven by radiation pressure, and a mechanically dominated "radio mode" governed by relativistic jets.

**Radiative feedback** is particularly prominent in luminous [[quasars|Quasars]] in the early Universe. During this phase, extreme ionized gas outflows can be driven into the host galaxy's interstellar medium (ISM) and out into the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]]. Recent JWST observations of $z \sim 5-6$ quasars reveal [O III] outflow velocities reaching up to $8400 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, with kinetic energy outflow rates estimated to be up to $\sim 260\%$ of the observed bolometric luminosity [Liu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250908793L/abstract). In individual extreme systems, such as the quasar J1402+2330, absorption-line studies indicate mass outflow rates exceeding $1000\ M_\odot \text{ yr}^{-1}$ at kiloparsec scales, contributing a kinetic energy output that is $>5\%$ of the Eddington luminosity ($L_{\rm Edd}$) [Dehghanian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250118034D/abstract), [Dehghanian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A...4D/abstract). Extreme kinematics can also manifest uniquely in individual sources; for example, the broad-line quasar SDSS 0956+5128 displays unexplained extreme velocity offsets reaching up to $-4100 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, which are postulated to be post-shock outflows driven by strong shock waves from supernova-like events occurring within the nuclear region [Rusakov et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...944..217R/abstract). Moreover, there is an observed evolutionary transition in quasars from galaxy-scale gas inflows to massive outflows, a shift that correlates with the increasing strength of the narrow [O III] emission line [He et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240804458H/abstract). As these winds propagate, they generate Narrow-line region (NLR) shocks. Near-infrared spectroscopic tracers, such as elevated $[\text{Fe II}]\lambda12570 / [\text{P II}]\lambda11886$ line ratios ($>10$), spatially correlate with blueshifted ionized gas, confirming that AGN outflows sweep through the NLR and shock-heat the surrounding ISM [Mizumoto et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...960...41M/abstract). The spatial extent of these feedback signatures is critical; the CARS survey has discovered that the maximum projected size of the extended narrow-line region (ENLR) correlates strongly with black hole mass, potentially acting as an indicator of the overall AGN lifetime [Husemann et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...659A.124H/abstract).

Conversely, **Radio-mode AGN** are predominantly found in massive, early-type host galaxies and are strongly linked to "inside-out" quenching of star formation, as evidenced by negative stellar age gradients with galactocentric distance [Comerford et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...901..159C/abstract). Even low-power radio jets can drive multiphase outflows and inflate expanding bubbles in the ISM, as observed in the $z=1.59$ obscured quasar XID2028 [Cresci et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...672A.128C/abstract). Spatially resolved integral field spectroscopy demonstrates that radio jets heavily ionize the surrounding nuclear ISM, indicated by enhanced $[\text{N II}]/\text{H}\alpha$ emission line ratios in the central regions of radio AGN hosts [Jin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...694A.309J/abstract), [Jin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240901279J/abstract). Furthermore, high-resolution e-MERLIN observations of the Quasar Feedback Survey reveal that even formally "radio-quiet" quasars frequently exhibit diverse sub-kiloparsec radio morphologies, including distinct cores, knots, and compact jets, driving feedback on nuclear scales [Njeri et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250103433N/abstract).

The ultimate theoretical consequence of these feedback mechanisms is the suppression of star formation via **Molecular gas depletion**. At cosmic noon ($z=1-2.6$), ALMA observations from the KASHz and SUPER surveys demonstrate that AGN host galaxies exhibit significant CO depletion. When matched in stellar mass and far-infrared luminosity, AGN hosts show systematically lower molecular gas fractions compared to non-active galaxies [Bertola et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240816821B/abstract), [Bertola et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...691A.178B/abstract). 

Despite clear evidence of gas depletion and extreme outflows, the literature remains highly conflicted regarding whether AGN feedback actively quenches star formation (negative feedback) or merely maintains prior quiescence. While Radio-mode AGN are firmly associated with quiescent hosts [Comerford et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...901..159C/abstract), [Jin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...694A.309J/abstract), some studies reveal complexities in this relationship; for example, radio-loud quasars have been shown to exhibit a factor of 1.4 star formation rate excess over their radio-quiet counterparts, potentially indicating instances of jet-induced positive feedback [Kalfountzou et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471...28K/abstract). Many radiatively efficient quasars reside in hosts that lie squarely on the star-forming main sequence [Zhuang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934..130Z/abstract). Furthermore, some studies find that the average star formation rate is broadly flat across wide ranges of AGN luminosity, suggesting that the short timescales of AGN variability mask underlying long-term feedback correlations [Stanley et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453..591S/abstract). Other high-redshift X-ray luminous AGN actually show enhanced star formation rates, indicating that accretion and star formation may be simultaneously fueled by a shared gas reservoir rather than immediately suppressed [Florez et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497.3273F/abstract), [Shehata et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250900612S/abstract). Resolving this tension remains a major objective for [[high-redshift-galaxy-evolution|High-Redshift Galaxy Evolution]] studies.

## Co-evolution and the Host Galaxy Connection

Detailed morphological and physical characterization of galaxies hosting extreme active galactic nuclei (AGN) reveals a complex picture of co-evolution. While textbook paradigms historically associated powerful quasar activity exclusively with major mergers and massive elliptical galaxies, recent high-resolution imaging and bulge-disk decomposition have challenged this view. At $z \sim 0.3$, approximately 40% of quasar hosts reside in disk-dominated (late-type) galaxies, demonstrating that secular processes are sufficient to drive quasar-level accretion without requiring major mergers [Zhuang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934..130Z/abstract). Indeed, broad-line AGN hosts at $z \sim 1.2-1.7$ often show intermediate structural sizes that place them between larger star-forming disks and compact quiescent galaxies, consistent with evolutionary pathways driven by secular compaction processes [Silverman et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...887L...5S/abstract). However, at higher redshifts ($z > 1.5$), AGN hosts exhibit significantly higher bulge fractions than the non-active galaxy population, indicating that the dominant triggering mechanisms may evolve over cosmic time [Bruce et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.458.2391B/abstract).

The position of AGN hosts relative to the star-forming main sequence remains a subject of active debate in the literature. Many luminous, radiatively efficient AGN and [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] are hosted by massive galaxies ($M_{\star} > 10^{10} M_{\odot}$) that populate the "green valley" between the blue cloud of star-forming galaxies and the red sequence of passive galaxies [Ishino et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PASJ...72...83I/abstract), [Klesman et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442..314K/abstract). This clustering in the green valley is frequently interpreted as evidence of a transition phase wherein AGN feedback actively quenches star formation. Conversely, studies utilizing strictly mass-matched control samples suggest that this apparent clustering is driven by the color-mass correlation of the underlying galaxy population, finding that AGN host colors and star formation rates are largely indistinguishable from inactive galaxies of comparable mass [Xue et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...720..368X/abstract). Furthermore, investigations mapping X-ray AGN on the UVJ diagram find a lack of a clear bimodal star-forming versus quiescent distribution, with luminous sources instead preferring star-forming regions [Cionitti et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023RNAAS...7..165C/abstract). Furthermore, multiwavelength morphological studies of ultra-hard X-ray detected AGN in the BAT-SWIFT sample reveal complex feedback effects; contrary to standard quenching models, many of these powerful sources continue to reside squarely on or even above the star-forming main sequence [Bilata-Woldeyes et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021IAUS..356..295B/abstract). The ambiguity extends to the effects of obscuration; heavily obscured AGN exhibit star formation rates approximately three times higher than unobscured quasars, implying a potential evolutionary sequence rather than the pure orientation effects traditionally assumed in [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|AGN Classification and Unified Models]] [Andonie et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.2577A/abstract). A further complication to simple evolutionary pathways is the rare population of 'cold quasars'. Sources such as CQ 4479 host highly luminous, unobscured X-ray AGN yet simultaneously maintain massive cold gas reservoirs and high star formation rates, appearing to stall or circumvent standard gas depletion models [Cooke et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...903..106C/abstract). 

Host galaxy properties also depend sensitively on the specific method of AGN selection. For example, among dwarf galaxies, variability-selected AGN hosts are typically found to harbor older stellar populations than those identified via mid-infrared color selections [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024RAA....24f5006L/abstract). Additionally, obscuration-based selections, such as Type II AGN, reveal a bimodal star formation behavior: while lower-mass hosts typically reside on the star-forming main sequence, massive hosts exhibit suppressed star formation that is directly linked to gas removal and quenching [Vietri et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...659A.129V/abstract). Crucially, demographic studies of transient events indicate that Type 2 AGN hosts exhibit significantly higher core-collapse supernova counts than Type 1 hosts, suggesting fundamentally different stellar population ages between the two classes and providing a critical missing ingredient that challenges purely geometric AGN unification.

Clearer distinctions emerge when separating AGN by accretion mode. Mechanically dominated, radio-loud AGN are almost exclusively found in massive, passive elliptical galaxies [Goulding et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783...40G/abstract), [Hickox et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...696..891H/abstract). An analysis of the stellar population age in these hosts reveals older stellar populations and negative stellar age gradients, indicative of inside-out quenching associated with radio-mode maintenance feedback [Comerford et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...901..159C/abstract).

The scaling between supermassive black holes and their hosts, typically parameterized by the $M_{\rm BH}-M_{\star}$ relation, is a cornerstone of co-evolution models. At low and intermediate redshifts, quasar hosts generally track the local scaling relations, though some samples suggest their central black holes may be slightly under-massive relative to their host galaxies [Ishino et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PASJ...72...83I/abstract), [Cloonan et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240803379C/abstract). This paradigm, however, is being profoundly challenged at the high-redshift frontier. Recent JWST observations of the compact, heavily reddened population known as [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]] at $z > 4$ have revealed black holes that strongly violate this local relation, as discussed previously [Durodola et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240610329D/abstract), [Schindler et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241111534S/abstract). Whether these sources represent an early epoch of heavy black hole seeding that precedes stellar mass assembly, or if their stellar masses are systematically underestimated due to extreme AGN outshining, remains an open and pivotal question in the literature.

## Extreme Accretion Modes and Temporal Variability

The standard paradigm of supermassive black hole (SMBH) growth often posits relatively steady accretion phases. However, the discovery of extreme temporal variability and super-Eddington accretion modes highlights the highly stochastic nature of black hole fueling. These phenomena provide unique probes into accretion disk physics and the short-term duty cycles of active galactic nuclei.

**Super-Eddington Accretion**
While the bulk of the AGN population accretes at sub-Eddington rates, the high-luminosity tail of the Eddington ratio distribution extends well into the super-Eddington regime. Phenomenological models connecting the AGN population to host galaxies require a steep power-law tail for super-Eddington sources, with an inferred slope of $\gamma_2 = -2.1^{+0.3}_{-0.5}$ [Aird et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...775...41A/abstract). Furthermore, the demographics of rapid accretion are highly dependent on the host galaxy state; the occurrence rate of narrow-line AGN above a fixed Eddington ratio remains constant or increases with stellar mass in star-forming galaxies, but declines sharply in quiescent systems, reflecting the necessity of a readily available cold gas supply [Blanton et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251114575B/abstract). Observationally, extreme Population A (xA) quasars are proposed as candidate super-Eddington accretors. They are classically selected by their strong optical Fe II emission relative to broad H$\beta$, specifically defined by the flux ratio $R_{\rm FeII} > 1$. However, identifying true super-Eddington sources at low to intermediate redshifts is fraught with observational tensions. Careful host galaxy subtraction is critical; evolved stellar populations can produce deep absorption lines that artificially mimic Fe II emission, leading to severe misclassification of sources as xA quasars when traditional selection criteria are applied without stringent continuum modeling [Bon et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...635A.151B/abstract). Despite these observational challenges, extreme Population A (xA) quasars are highly valuable; due to their proposed stability in Eddington ratio across high redshifts, they have been suggested as potential cosmological distance indicators [Dultzin et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020FrASS...6...80M/abstract).

**Changing-Look AGN**
Historically, the dichotomy between broad-line (Type 1) and narrow-line (Type 2) sources was attributed strictly to viewing angle, as described in [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|AGN Classification and Unified Models]]. This paradigm is challenged by "changing-look" AGN (CLAGN), which exhibit rapid, dramatic transitions between these spectral classes on timescales of months to years. Multi-wavelength monitoring, particularly tracking mid-infrared and X-ray variability, has established a consensus that these transitions are primarily driven by intrinsic changes in the accretion rate rather than variable line-of-sight obscuration [LaMassa et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800..144L/abstract), [Sheng et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...889...46S/abstract). For instance, the first identified changing-look quasar, SDSS J015957.64+003310.5, exhibited a factor of six drop in continuum flux as it transitioned from a Type 1 to a Type 1.9 state, dimming the supply of ionizing photons available to the broad-line region [LaMassa et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800..144L/abstract). 

Conversely, large samples of "turn-on" CLAGN have also been discovered, which transition from a dim state to a highly accreting bright state. Interestingly, these turn-on sources tend to reside in massive host galaxies with star formation properties resembling those of Type 2 AGN, yet they exhibit lower bright-state Eddington ratios than typical quasars of similar mass [Yang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...91Y/abstract). To distinguish accretion-driven CLAGN from cases of true variable absorption, the literature defines "frozen-look" AGN as systems that maintain constant emission line profiles despite dramatic changes in the ionizing continuum, indicating physical line-of-sight obscuration changes rather than accretion disk fluctuations [Komossa et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AN....34430015K/abstract).

**Tidal Disruption Events and Extreme Transients**
At the absolute extreme of stochastic accretion variability are Tidal Disruption Events (TDEs), which occur when a star ventures too close to an SMBH and is torn apart, forming a transient accretion disk. TDEs frequently manifest as giant-amplitude X-ray or optical flares in otherwise quiescent galaxies, showing luminosity increases by factors of $>1000$ to $6000$ [Komossa et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AN....34430015K/abstract). X-ray selected TDEs are characterized by an extreme spectral softness, with thermal temperatures of $T \sim 50-100 \text{ eV}$, directly probing the newly formed, compact accretion flow feeding the black hole [Komossa et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AN....34430015K/abstract), [Kara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ARA&A..63..379K/abstract). Related to these extreme accretion phenomena are Quasi-Periodic Eruptions (QPEs), repeating high-amplitude X-ray flares that are posited to be the electromagnetic signatures of Extreme Mass Ratio Inspirals (EMRIs) orbiting accreting SMBHs [Kara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ARA&A..63..379K/abstract). Together, CLAGN and TDEs represent the physical extremes of black hole accretion variability, shedding light on the rapid assembly, instability, and dissipation of accretion disks. Beyond individual black hole accretion, such variability and the extreme nature of quasars highlight them as unique systems; for instance, quasars are up to seven times more likely than random galaxies to host supermassive black hole binaries, making them primary targets for pulsar timing arrays aiming to detect low-frequency gravitational waves [Casey-Clyde et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240519406C/abstract).

## Environmental Impact and Large-Scale Structure

The clustering strength and local environment of active galactic nuclei (AGN) provide critical insights into the triggering mechanisms of supermassive black hole accretion. Multi-wavelength studies reveal a strong dependence of the typical dark matter halo mass on the dominant accretion mode. [[multiple-radio-galaxy-populations|Radio-selected, mechanically dominated AGN]] are the most strongly clustered, typically residing in massive halos of $M_{\rm halo} \sim 10^{13} - 3 \times 10^{13} \ h^{-1} M_\odot$ [Hickox et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...696..891H/abstract), [Hickox et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ASPC..408..341H/abstract). However, the comprehensive LOFAR Two-Metre Sky Survey (LoTSS) reveals that the majority of low-redshift radio-loud AGN actually inhabit intermediate halos with masses below $10^{14} M_\odot$, though the probability of true cluster association strongly increases with higher radio luminosity [Croston et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019A&A...622A..10C/abstract). In contrast, X-ray-selected AGN occupy intermediate halos ($\sim 10^{13} \ h^{-1} M_\odot$), and infrared-selected, radiatively efficient AGN are found in less massive halos ($M_{\rm halo} \lesssim 10^{12} \ h^{-1} M_\odot$) [Hickox et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...696..891H/abstract). Furthermore, spatial cross-correlation with luminous red galaxies indicates that unobscured (Type 1) AGN occupy significantly more massive halos ($M_{\rm halo} \approx 10^{13.3} \ h^{-1} M_\odot$) than their obscured counterparts ($M_{\rm halo} \approx 10^{12.6} \ h^{-1} M_\odot$). This difference suggests that obscuration may be linked to the evolutionary stage and environmental density, rather than purely the line-of-sight viewing angle posited by standard [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|AGN Classification and Unified Models]] [Córdova Rosado et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240908314C/abstract), [Córdova Rosado et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..158C/abstract).

Within massive galaxy clusters, the literature presents a somewhat ambiguous and conflicting picture regarding AGN triggering. While luminous [[quasars|Quasars]] are frequently associated with major mergers and small-scale overdensities, lower-luminosity AGN often appear to be driven by secular processes [Villforth et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.426..360V/abstract). Several studies find no significant difference in the fraction of AGN between cluster and field environments, and observe a weak or absent correlation between the AGN fraction and cluster mass or richness [Klesman et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.1215K/abstract), [Klesman et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442..314K/abstract), [Bufanda et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2531B/abstract). This implies that internal host galaxy processes and cold gas availability frequently outweigh the large-scale influence of the intracluster medium. Conversely, other high-resolution surveys indicate that the cluster AGN fraction decreases with distance from the center, with radio-selected AGN acting as the primary contributors to the increased density in cluster cores [Hashiguchi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023PASJ...75.1246H/abstract). At the scale of galaxy groups, quasars are roughly $1.5$ times more likely to be found at the group center than matched inactive galaxies [Wethers et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..192W/abstract). In older, relaxed systems such as isolated fossil groups, extreme AGN feedback can profoundly impact the local environment. For example, the fossil group SDSSTG 4436 exhibits a giant AGN outburst of $\sim 1.5 \times 10^{61}$ erg that has significantly heated the surrounding gas, demonstrating how nuclear activity can disrupt cool cores even in the absence of recent merging events [Eckert et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250613907E/abstract).

At earlier cosmic epochs, [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] and extreme quasar populations act as crucial signposts for large-scale structure and protoclusters. The discovery of a candidate quadruple AGN system at $z \sim 3$, spanning roughly $480$ kpc, highlights the extreme density peaks characteristic of forming clusters [Herwig et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241000974H/abstract), [Herwig et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...694L..12H/abstract). Massive overdensities of quasars, such as the $z \sim 2$ "Cosmic Himalayas," initially appeared to challenge the $\Lambda$CDM cosmological framework due to their extreme clustering amplitude. However, recent analyses demonstrate that when evaluated with non-Gaussian statistics—such as an asymmetric generalized normal distribution—these overdensities naturally arise within standard $\Lambda$CDM structure formation [Kuwayama et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251224966K/abstract). 

At $z > 5$, the newly discovered population of [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]] (LRDs) has been shown to anchor substantial galactic overdensities. Spectroscopic clustering estimates of a $z = 7.3$ LRD reveal a cross-correlation length of $r_0 = 9 \pm 2 \ h^{-1}\ \text{cMpc}$, comparable to that of UV-luminous quasars, implying a minimum dark matter halo mass of $\log_{10}(M_{\rm halo}/M_\odot) \approx 12.3$ [Schindler et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241111534S/abstract). This clustering strength suggests that nearly all halos above this mass threshold must host actively accreting supermassive black holes during the epoch of reionization.

## Historical Development and Evolution Models

The global accretion history of supermassive black holes traces the rise and fall of active galactic nuclei across cosmic time, peaking at $z \sim 1-2$, an epoch often referred to as cosmic noon. During this "golden age" of nuclear activity, both the universal star formation rate and black hole accretion reached their cosmic maxima [Fiore et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MmSAI..77..694F/abstract), establishing the foundations of modern [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]].

Historically, the evolution of the [[quasars|quasar]] and AGN populations was modeled using simple parametric shifts of the local luminosity function. Early frameworks relied on either pure density evolution (PDE), where the comoving number density of AGN scales uniformly with redshift as $\Phi(L, z) \propto \Phi(L, 0) (1+z)^k$, or pure luminosity evolution (PLE), where the characteristic luminosity shifts while the number density remains constant. Early theoretical attempts to reconcile these observations with physical models assumed [[backward-evolution-models|simple analytical accretion histories]]; for instance, postulating an average accretion rate that decays as $\langle \dot{M} \rangle \propto M^{-1.5} t^{-6.7}$ [Small et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992MNRAS.259..725S/abstract). Studies of [[multiple-radio-galaxy-populations|low-redshift radio AGN]] have attempted to fit evolution out to $z = 0.5$ using such simple parametrizations, yielding $k = 0.92 \pm 0.95$ for PDE and $k = 2.13 \pm 1.96$ for PLE, though both measurements suffer from considerable statistical uncertainty .

As deep multi-wavelength surveys expanded, it became evident that simple PDE or PLE models could not capture the complex, differential evolution of the broader AGN population. The consensus shifted toward luminosity-dependent density evolution (LDDE). In the LDDE framework, the peak of the AGN comoving number density depends strictly on luminosity, with less luminous AGN peaking at lower redshifts than their highly luminous counterparts—a phenomenon known as AGN downsizing. Bayesian model selection applied to hard X-ray (5-10 keV) surveys—which are largely unbiased by obscuration up to column densities of $N_{\rm H} \sim 10^{23}\, {\rm cm}^{-2}$—demonstrates that LDDE best describes the population out to $z = 4.0$, with no evidence of a rapid decline at these epochs [Fotopoulou et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...587A.142F/abstract). 

However, the literature exhibits some tension regarding the universal applicability of LDDE, particularly at the highest redshifts and in different wavebands. While LDDE firmly describes X-ray populations across a broad redshift baseline, recent measurements of the ultraviolet quasar luminosity function at $2 \lesssim z \lesssim 6$ suggest that a pure density evolution model provides an adequate and simpler approximation at these early epochs. In this high-redshift regime, the evolution of the UV luminosity function can be understood primarily through the evolution of the underlying dark matter halo mass function [Kim et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910L..11K/abstract).

These empirical luminosity functions ultimately reflect a fundamental shift in the physical drivers of accretion history. In modern evolutionary models, extreme quasar activity is often triggered when dark matter halos reach a critical mass threshold of $M_{\rm halo} \sim 10^{12} - 10^{13} h^{-1} M_\odot$ [Hickox et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...696..891H/abstract), [Hickox et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ASPC..408..341H/abstract). Following an initial, rapid phase of radiatively efficient accretion near the Eddington limit, the AGN eventually transitions into a lower-accretion, radiatively inefficient phase driven by secular processes and hot-halo maintenance [Shen et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...654L.115S/abstract), [Draper et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...751...72D/abstract). The apparent shift from PDE at high redshifts to LDDE at lower redshifts likely traces this exact transition, evolving from an early universe dominated by rapid halo assembly to a late universe governed by the gradual depletion of cold gas reservoirs. When interpreting these accretion histories, it is critical to account for observational constraints. Luminosity-limited surveys often introduce severe selection biases that fail to capture the full, complex range of black hole growth, whereas utilizing strict Eddington ratio limits provides a significantly more complete view of host galaxy properties and the actual distribution of accretion states over cosmic time [Jones et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...843..125J/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

While multi-wavelength surveys have fundamentally advanced our understanding of extreme active galactic nuclei (AGN), several critical mysteries regarding black hole assembly, triggering mechanisms, and feedback remain unresolved. 

**Early Universe and Black Hole Seeds**
The recent discovery of a highly abundant population of compact red sources at $z > 4$—termed [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]]—has introduced significant tension into models of early supermassive black hole (SMBH) assembly [Akins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240610341A/abstract). If these sources are dominated by [[reddened-quasars|dust-obscured AGN]], their inferred black hole masses are orders of magnitude larger than expected from local relations [Durodola et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240610329D/abstract). This dramatic deviation strongly favors models invoking heavy black hole seeds during the cosmic dark ages. However, the literature remains strongly divided on whether these objects are universally powered by AGN or if they represent a hybrid population with massive, compact starbursts [Durodola et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240610329D/abstract), [Akins et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...991...37A/abstract).

**Triggering and Environments**
The mechanisms that trigger extreme accretion are still debated, particularly the dichotomy between merger-driven and secular fueling. While the standard paradigm assumes that gas-rich major mergers drive the most luminous quasars, up to $40\%$ of $z \sim 0.3$ quasars reside in disk-dominated galaxies, challenging the necessity of major mergers for quasar-level accretion [Zhuang et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...934..130Z/abstract). At higher redshifts, the recent discovery of a dual AGN system at $z = 5.4$ [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250809749L/abstract), the highest-redshift kiloparsec-scale close quasar pair J2329-0522 at $z = 4.85$ [Yue et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AJ....165..191Y/abstract), and a candidate quadruple AGN at $z \sim 3$ [Herwig et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241000974H/abstract) suggests that multiple AGN triggering in dense protocluster environments might be significantly more common than current theoretical frameworks predict.

**The Feedback Controversy**
The physical impact of AGN feedback on the host galaxy's interstellar medium and the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] remains ambiguous. While massive, multi-phase outflows can carry kinetic energy exceeding $5\%$ of the Eddington luminosity [Dehghanian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...695A...4D/abstract), the literature is in active conflict regarding their ultimate impact on [[cosmic-evolution-and-star-formation-history|Cosmic Evolution and Star Formation History]]. It is heavily debated whether these winds actively suppress star formation (negative feedback) or merely act in a "maintenance mode" to preserve quiescence in galaxies that have already ceased forming stars [Stemo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888...78S/abstract), [Florez et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497.3273F/abstract).

**High-Resolution Spectroscopy and the Intergalactic Medium**
Luminous quasars also serve as indispensable background beacons to probe the intergalactic medium (IGM). In the near future, high-resolution observational campaigns, such as the EQUALS legacy survey utilizing the ESPRESSO spectrograph, will be pivotal in determining the temperature of the IGM and quantifying early stellar chemical contributions through the detailed study of quasar absorption lines [Berg et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251206159B/abstract).

**Time-Domain Astrophysics and the Hidden Census**
Exploring the extremes of accretion variability is a primary objective for the coming decade. The rise of time-domain astrophysics has revealed rapid, large-amplitude variations in Changing-Look AGN and Tidal Disruption Events (TDEs), where transitions are driven by intrinsic accretion rate changes rather than variable obscuration [Komossa et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AN....34430015K/abstract), [Yang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...91Y/abstract). Upcoming wide-field multi-wavelength surveys by LSST, Roman, and Euclid will provide unprecedented benchmark datasets to study AGN host morphology and determine how these dramatic environments tie into all SMBH lifecycles [Parra Tello et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250621800P/abstract).

Finally, obtaining a complete census of the most heavily obscured, Compton-thick AGN continues to challenge current paradigms of [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|AGN Classification and Unified Models]]. X-ray surveys frequently miss these sources, which can exhibit star formation rates three times higher than unobscured systems [Andonie et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.517.2577A/abstract). Next-generation radio facilities, notably SKA/ngVLA, will be critical to overcoming this barrier. Forecasts suggest that the SKAO will detect thousands of AGN at $z > 6$, more than half of which are expected to be Compton-thick, providing a crucial, obscuration-independent tracer of extreme accretion in the early Universe [Mazzolari et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...687A.120M/abstract), [Radcliffe et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...649A..27R/abstract). Furthermore, the predicted synergy between the future mid-infrared PRIMA observatory and the X-ray NewAthena mission will be uniquely powerful in uncovering and characterizing Compton-thick AGN at $z > 6$, offering a new hope for fully tracing early obscured accretion [Barchiesi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250319915B/abstract).
