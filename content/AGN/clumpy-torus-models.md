## Article

In the study of [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]], **clumpy torus models** are theoretical frameworks that describe the obscuring dusty material surrounding a supermassive black hole as a localized distribution of discrete, optically thick clouds, rather than a smooth, continuous fluid. Historically, standard [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|AGN Classification and Unified Models]] posited that a homogeneous, geometrically thick "donut" of dust and gas was responsible for the observational dichotomy between Type 1 (unobscured) and Type 2 (obscured) active galaxies [Blandford et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992AIPC..254....3B/abstract), [Osterbrock et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993RMxAA..26...65O/abstract). However, smooth density distributions consistently failed to reproduce the mid-infrared spectra of AGNs, most notably over-predicting the depth of the $10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ silicate absorption and emission features—a discrepancy widely known as the "silicate feature problem." By modeling the [[circumnuclear-environment|Circumnuclear Environment]] as a stochastic collection of discrete clumps, these updated frameworks allow thermal radiation from the hot inner regions to "leak" through optically thin sightlines, naturally reconciling theoretical spectral energy distributions (SEDs) with observed multi-wavelength data [Hönig et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...452..459H/abstract), [Schartmann et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...482...67S/abstract).

A profound consequence of the clumpy paradigm is that AGN unification becomes inherently probabilistic. Instead of relying on a strict geometrical viewing-angle cutoff, line-of-sight obscuration is governed by the statistical escape probability of a photon avoiding intersection with any discrete clouds [Alonso-Herrero et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...736...82A/abstract). Utilizing 3D Monte Carlo radiative transfer simulations and infrared spectral energy distribution fitting, researchers have established that these obscuring structures are remarkably compact, typically confined to the inner $1$ to $6\text{ parsecs}$ of the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]]. 

Recent advancements in parsec-scale interferometry utilizing facilities such as VLTI/MIDI, ALMA, and JWST have spatially resolved these clumpy structures, catalyzing a shift from a static geometrical model to a highly dynamic, multi-component picture [Jaffe et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Natur.429...47J/abstract), [Lopez-Rodriguez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250608077L/abstract). This article explores the theoretical foundations of clumpy radiative transfer, the macroscopic geometric parameters defining these models, and their multiwavelength observational signatures. It further examines the emerging consensus that the classical equatorial torus is deeply coupled to extended polar dusty outflows, forming a complex disk-wind system. Finally, the article surveys ongoing open questions in the field, including unresolved degeneracies between competing SED modeling libraries, the physical origin of the near-infrared excess in Type 2 Seyferts, and the challenge of accurately decoupling intrinsic torus emission from [[composite-astrophysical-models-and-populations|Composite Astrophysical Models and Populations]] in the surrounding host galaxy.

## Historical Evolution from Smooth to Clumpy Media

The foundational framework of [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|AGN Classification and Unified Models]] originally posited that the central engine of an active galaxy was obscured by a geometrically thick, homogeneous "donut" of dust and gas [Blandford et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992AIPC..254....3B/abstract), [Osterbrock et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993RMxAA..26...65O/abstract). While this continuous smooth torus elegantly explained the basic viewing-angle dichotomy between Type 1 and Type 2 AGNs, it eventually encountered severe theoretical and observational limitations that forced a paradigm shift toward discrete, clumpy media.

The primary observational catalyst for this shift was the "silicate feature problem." Radiative transfer models of a smooth torus consistently failed to reproduce the mid-infrared spectra of active galactic nuclei. Specifically, strong temperature gradients in a continuous medium theoretically produce excessively deep $10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ silicate absorption features in edge-on (Type 2) sources, and overly pronounced silicate emission in face-on (Type 1) sources [Hönig et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...452..459H/abstract), [Schartmann et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...482...67S/abstract). To resolve these discrepancies, theorists developed three-dimensional models where the dust is sequestered into discrete, optically thick clouds. In a clumpy medium, the shadowing effects between clouds fundamentally alter the internal temperature distribution, allowing photons from the hot inner radii to "leak" through low-density sightlines, naturally dampening the silicate features to match observations [Hönig et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...452..459H/abstract). Furthermore, incorporating the presence of discrete clouds in the narrow-line region has been shown to successfully explain silicate emission even in specific edge-on sources like FSC 10214+4724 [Efstathiou et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.371L..70E/abstract). 

The NGC 1068 history serves as the archetypal case study for this transition. Early attempts to model the infrared continuum of NGC 1068 with a smooth tapered disk required ad-hoc assumptions, such as introducing optically thin dust into the ionization cone, to reconcile the data [Efstathiou et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.277.1134E/abstract). The definitive breakthrough occurred when mid-infrared interferometry using VLTI/MIDI spatially resolved the [[circumnuclear-environment|Circumnuclear Environment]] of NGC 1068. The observations revealed a compact structure of warm ($T \approx 320\ \mathrm{K}$) dust, approximately $3.4\ \mathrm{pc}$ in diameter and $2.1\ \mathrm{pc}$ thick [Jaffe et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Natur.429...47J/abstract). 

However, as noted by Jaffe (2004), maintaining a geometrically thick distribution of discrete dust clouds poses a severe dynamical challenge: inelastic cloud collisions would rapidly dissipate kinetic energy, causing the clouds to quickly collapse into a thin disk. Therefore, the observed thick clumpy torus cannot be a purely static structure; it requires a continuous injection of kinetic energy—such as from stellar feedback or AGN winds—to avoid dynamical collapse and remain geometrically thick [Jaffe et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Natur.429...47J/abstract). In support of this dynamic requirement, hydrodynamic simulations have demonstrated that stellar feedback originating from a young nuclear star cluster can physically sustain such a geometrically thick, turbulent torus structure [Schartmann et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.393..759S/abstract). This revelation fundamentally altered the field, moving theories away from stable donuts toward dynamic, energy-driven models.

## Geometrical Parameters and Statistical Unification

In clumpy torus models, the circumnuclear obscurer is parameterized as an ensemble of discrete dusty clouds rather than a uniform fluid. The macroscopic structure of this distribution determines the escape probability ($P_{\rm esc}$) of an AGN photon, shifting traditional [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|AGN Classification and Unified Models]] from a strict viewing-angle dichotomy to a probabilistic framework. A Type 1 classification simply implies a clear line of sight exists through the cloud distribution (with typical unobscured probabilities of $P_{\rm esc} \approx 12\% - 44\%$), while a Type 2 indicates intersection with at least one optically thick cloud [Alonso-Herrero et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...736...82A/abstract).

This geometry is typically characterized by several key variables:
* **Equatorial Clouds ($N_0$):** The average number of clouds along a radial line of sight in the equatorial plane. Fits to infrared spectral energy distributions (SEDs) typically yield $N_0 \approx 5-8$ [Hönig et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...515A..23H/abstract), [Lira et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764..159L/abstract). 
* **Opening Angle ($\sigma$):** The angular dispersion of the cloud distribution away from the midplane. While some SED modeling libraries (like CLUMPY) suggest characteristic opening angles of $\sigma \gtrsim 40^\circ$ [Lira et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764..159L/abstract), multi-wavelength X-ray fits to specific sources like the Circinus galaxy indicate much narrower profiles, such as $\sigma \approx 14.7^\circ$ [Tanimoto et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877...95T/abstract).
* **Radial Thickness ($Y$) and Extent:** The ratio of the outer boundary to the inner dust sublimation radius is typically constrained to $Y \lesssim 50$ [Lira et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764..159L/abstract). High-resolution mid-infrared observations (e.g., using SOFIA) demonstrate that tori are remarkably compact, with outer radii restricted to $<1$ to $8.4\text{ pc}$ of the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]] [Fuller et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.2618F/abstract). Interestingly, observations suggest the existence of an "Intermediate Line Region" (ILR) situated between the broad-line and narrow-line regions, which may trace the physical transition into this inner torus boundary [Zhu et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...700.1173Z/abstract).

A macroscopic consequence of these structural parameters is the geometrical covering factor, which dictates the overall probability that the active nucleus is obscured. A major application of this framework is testing the *receding torus model*, which posits that the covering factor decreases as the central AGN luminosity increases and dust is sublimated at larger radii. The literature remains actively divided on this topic: some multi-wavelength surveys support a luminosity-dependent covering factor dropping from $f \approx 0.9-1.0$ at low luminosities to $f \approx 0.1-0.3$ at quasar luminosities [Alonso-Herrero et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...736...82A/abstract). Conversely, other large radio-selected studies report a relatively constant Type 1 fraction of $\sim 20\%$ across broad [O III] luminosity ranges [Lu et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010MNRAS.404.1761L/abstract). Further estimates using $[\mathrm{O~III}]/\mathrm{IR}$ ratios place an upper bound around $f = 0.5$ for powerful quasars [Dempsey et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.4615D/abstract). 

Disentangling this intrinsic torus covering factor from broader galactic obscuration—such as an [[extended-dust-component|Extended dust component]] or the host galaxy's [[cosmic-media-and-interstellar-medium|Cosmic Media and Interstellar Medium]]—remains a complex challenge. This is particularly relevant in the early Universe; recent studies demonstrate that [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] ($z=3-6$) obscuration is driven by a strong combination of the clumpy torus and the dense interstellar medium of the host galaxy [Pouliasis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...685A..97P/abstract). Consequently, recent findings indicate an increasing Compton-thick fraction at higher redshifts ($z > 0.75$), linking the nature of obscuration strongly to host-galaxy evolution [Yan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951...27Y/abstract). Despite the heavy obscuration at these distances, high-redshift quasars (up to $z=5$) display dust abundances and metallicities that are at least as high as their local counterparts, indicating remarkably rapid early enrichment [Andreani et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.306..161A/abstract).

## Multiwavelength Observational Signatures

Clumpy torus models have proven essential for interpreting the broadband continuum and discrete spectral features of [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]]. Beyond resolving the silicate feature depth, observations reveal that the $9.7\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ silicate feature often shifts to $\sim 10.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. This shift implies that dust grains in the AGN environment are systematically larger and more porous than standard [[cosmic-media-and-interstellar-medium|Cosmic Media and Interstellar Medium]], yielding a flatter, "gray" extinction curve [Hönig et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...515A..23H/abstract). Spectral fitting of these features frequently necessitates micron-sized grains ($\sim 1.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) [Xie et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJS..228....6X/abstract), [Mehdipour et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A&A...619A..20M/abstract). Discrete emission features also act as crucial diagnostics; for instance, the Fe II emission line's high sensitivity to the presence of dust makes it a potential tracer of the dusty transition region between the broad-line region and the torus itself [Adhikari et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...856...78A/abstract). Additionally, silicate emission is occasionally observed in [[x-ray-absorbed-agn|X-ray absorbed AGN]] like NGC 2110. Rather than requiring ad-hoc optically thin dust extensions, this emission can be successfully modeled as originating directly from the clumpy torus itself, particularly when there are few clouds along the observer's line of sight [Mason et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...693L.136M/abstract). Furthermore, for the hyperluminous galaxy FSC 10214+4724, reconciling silicate emission with a Type 2 classification requires an additional $100\text{ K}$ dust component situated within the narrow-line region [Efstathiou et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.436.1873E/abstract).

A persistent challenge in modeling the multiwavelength SED is the near-infrared excess observed in many Type 2 Seyferts. Purely clumpy torus libraries often underpredict the continuum flux in the $1-5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ regime. The literature remains divided on the physical origin of this emission. Some authors suggest the data necessitate a [[two-phase-model|Two-phase model]], wherein discrete high-density clumps are embedded in a smooth, continuous dusty medium [Lira et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764..159L/abstract), [Siebenmorgen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...583A.120S/abstract). Alternatively, the excess may originate from hot ($\sim 1700-2500\ \mathrm{K}$) pure-graphite dust located interior to the sublimation radius of silicates, potentially co-spatial with the broad-line region [Mor et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.420..526M/abstract), [Lira et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764..159L/abstract).

In the time domain, infrared reverberation mapping leverages the delayed response of dust to central UV/optical continuum variations to probe torus structure. Radiative transfer models demonstrate that anisotropic illumination and cloud self-shielding significantly reduce the observed lag times between optical and IR variations compared to predictions from a smooth geometry [Almeyda et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...843....3A/abstract). The reverberation response is also highly wavelength-dependent due to steep surface temperature gradients across individual irradiated clumps, with the innermost dust increasing in temperature rather than instantly sublimating during accretion disk flares [Schnülle et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...557L..13S/abstract), [Almeyda et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...843....3A/abstract). 

At higher energies, clumpy obscuration leaves distinct imprints on X-ray reflection spectra. In a patchy medium, X-ray photons reflected from the illuminated back-side of the torus can "leak" through low-density gaps. Consequently, the observed fluorescent $\mathrm{Fe}\ \mathrm{K}\alpha / \mathrm{Si}\ \mathrm{K}\alpha$ line ratios in Type 2 AGNs span values between $5$ and $60$—substantially lower than the predictions of smooth torus simulations. Direct X-ray measurements of sources like Mkn 3 show that the primary continuum can be seen through the rim of the torus, suggesting a reprocessor size of $\ge 2\text{ pc}$ [Cappi et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999A&A...344..857C/abstract). These ratios independently confirm cloud covering factors of $N_0 \le 5$, with some sources suggesting $N_0 < 2$, in strong agreement with derivations from the infrared [Liu et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459L.100L/abstract). Deep Chandra observations of specific active galaxies, such as ESO 428-G014, provide evidence for significant X-ray transmission in the "cross-cone" direction (perpendicular to the torus axis), suggesting that tori are not completely opaque even in the equatorial plane [Fabbiano et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...855..131F/abstract). Additionally, the dynamic radiation-driven [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]] model can account for multiphase warm absorbers and produce specific emission line features (e.g., O VIII) that are observed in Type 1 AGNs [Ogawa et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...925...55O/abstract). Furthermore, multi-epoch hard X-ray modeling of Compton-thick sources, such as the Circinus galaxy, reveals column density variations on timescales of days, providing direct empirical support for the highly clumpy, discrete nature of the circumnuclear material [Kayal et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.4098K/abstract). Similarly, in narrow-line Seyfert 1s like Mrk 766, X-ray spectral variations are driven by the [[variable-double-partial-covering-model|Variable double partial covering model]] of the central source by multilayer clumpy absorbing clouds [Mochizuki et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.525..922M/abstract). Looking forward, X-ray polarimetry is emerging as a key tool for testing [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|AGN Classification and Unified Models]] and the receding torus model by measuring expected changes in the polarization fraction near the Type 1 / Type 2 transition [Marin et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...591A..23M/abstract).

## Computational Radiative Transfer and SED Modeling

Modern theoretical frameworks employ 3D Monte Carlo simulations to model radiative transfer through discrete dusty clouds [Hönig et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006A&A...452..459H/abstract). These codes probabilistically trace photon packets emitted by the central engine as they undergo absorption, thermal re-emission, and multiple scattering events. Contemporary investigations frequently employ advanced packages, such as the SKIRT code, to synthesize expected SEDs. SKIRT has been utilized to model complex geometries, finding that fitting the mid-infrared spectra of prototypical objects like NGC 1068 benefits significantly from incorporating maximum dust grain sizes of $\sim 1\,\mu\text{m}$ [Victoria-Ceballos et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...926..192V/abstract). 

These simulations have been compiled into extensive grid-based SED libraries, most notably CLUMPY and the Siebenmorgen [[two-phase-model|Two-phase model]] [Lira et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764..159L/abstract), [Siebenmorgen et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...583A.120S/abstract). X-ray spectral synthesis is also being integrated into these Monte Carlo frameworks; tools are used to model Compton-thick reflection and fluorescent line emission in models such as XCLUMPY and IMPACTX [Tanimoto et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...877...95T/abstract), [Fujiwara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220993F/abstract). Recent advancements in physically motivated X-ray reflection spectroscopy further demonstrate that for specific sources like the Circinus galaxy, complex warped disk models are favored over both the radiative fountain model and traditional clumpy or smooth tori [Buchner et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...651A..58B/abstract).

Coupled with continuum radiative transfer, photoionization tools like [[cloudy-code|CLOUDY (code)]] are critical for understanding the gaseous component of the clumpy torus and its transition into the broader [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]]. By calculating the thermal and ionization equilibrium of discrete clouds under intense AGN irradiation, CLOUDY simulations help predict emission line ratios, map the spatial kinematics of coronal lines, and quantify the impact of the ionizing radiation field on the host galaxy's [[cosmic-media-and-interstellar-medium|Cosmic Media and Interstellar Medium]] [Mullaney et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.394L..16M/abstract), [Roos et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800...19R/abstract), [Dempsey et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.477.4615D/abstract). To properly interpret molecular line intensities in active nuclei like NGC 1068, researchers also frequently rely on X-ray Dominated Region (XDR) and Photon Dominated Region (PDR) models [Meijerink et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PhDT........11M/abstract). Additionally, the clumpy structure of [[molecular-gas-and-cloud-evolution|Molecular Gas and Cloud Evolution]] in compact high-redshift galaxy discs can lead to enhanced CO spectral line energy distribution (SLED) excitation for $J > 5$ due to X-ray radiation from the central AGN [Vallini et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.490.4502V/abstract).

To confront models with observations, researchers use Bayesian SED fitting routines (e.g., AGNFITTER-RX and SMART) to decompose broad-band photometry into contributions from the host galaxy, starbursts, and the AGN [Martínez-Ramírez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...688A..46M/abstract), [Varnava et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250214101V/abstract). To improve estimates of star formation rates and host properties within these routines, it is increasingly essential to self-consistently couple [[non-stellar-emission|Non-stellar emission]] to the ionizing spectrum in [[heavily-dust-enshrouded-stellar-populations|Heavily dust-enshrouded stellar populations]] synthesis models [Byler et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...840...44B/abstract). Moreover, advances in Monte Carlo radiative transfer codes like COLT allow for self-consistent modeling of nebular line and continuum emission across cosmic time by balancing photoionization heating and metal cooling [McClymont et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251013952M/abstract). Furthermore, the ARXSED framework has been introduced for modeling comprehensive radio-to-X-ray SEDs, which explicitly accounts for [[non-stellar-continuum|Non-stellar continuum]] jet contributions that can otherwise contaminate star-formation estimates [Azadi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945..145A/abstract). Other frameworks, such as [[cygnus-models|CYGNUS models]], utilize Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) fitting to test self-consistent radiative transfer templates against local ultraluminous infrared galaxies [Efstathiou et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.5183E/abstract). Moreover, these comprehensive frameworks have facilitated the development of robust bolometric luminosity correction recipes for AGNs across different epochs, seamlessly integrating accretion disk SED templates with detailed torus obscuration corrections [Azadi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919666A/abstract). However, the literature reveals significant systematic tensions: different clumpy and two-phase libraries can yield derived AGN fractions and bolometric luminosities that differ by up to an order of magnitude for the same source, although derived star formation rates and stellar masses are robustly estimated irrespective of the assumed torus model [Papadopoulos et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.536.2433P/abstract), [Varnava et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250214101V/abstract).

## Interferometry and the Emerging Disk-Wind Paradigm

Direct interferometric imaging and submillimeter arrays have revolutionized the study of the [[nuclear-regions-of-galaxies|Nuclear Regions of Galaxies]], providing parsec-scale resolution that breaks the degeneracies inherent in broadband SED modeling. These observations have catalyzed a major paradigm shift: rather than being confined to a single, static equatorial plane, circumnuclear dust is now understood to be a dynamic, multi-component system.

Initial mid-infrared interferometry using VLTI/MIDI on the archetypal Seyfert 2 galaxy NGC 1068 resolved warm ($T \approx 320\text{ K}$) dust into a compact structure approximately $2.1\text{ pc}$ thick and $3.4\text{ pc}$ in diameter [Jaffe et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004Natur.429...47J/abstract). Operating at longer wavelengths, ALMA mapped the cold ($T \lesssim 100\text{ K}$) [[molecular-gas-and-cloud-evolution|Molecular Gas and Cloud Evolution]] in NGC 1068, confirming a $3.5\text{ pc}$ radius disk with a torus gas mass of $M_{\rm gas}^{\rm torus} = (1 \pm 0.3) \times 10^5 M_\odot$ [García-Burillo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...823L..12G/abstract). Further multi-band mid-infrared thermal imaging of NGC 1068 utilizing the MATISSE instrument reaffirmed the presence of a thick edge-on disk obscuring the central engine, comprehensively mapping the dust temperature distribution across the structure [Gámez Rosas et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Natur.602..403G/abstract). Beyond NGC 1068, evidence for extensive rotating tori exists on larger scales; for example, OH maser observations in the active galaxy Markarian 231 trace a rotating molecular torus on scales of $30$ to $100\text{ pc}$ [Klöckner et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Natur.421..821K/abstract).

However, subsequent interferometric campaigns revealed that the mid-infrared emission in many active galaxies is highly asymmetric and extended along the polar axis. In NGC 1068, warm dust located $5-10\text{ pc}$ from the core along the polar region contributes four times more to the $12\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ flux than the central equatorial dust, effectively tracing the heated inner wall of a dusty cone [López-Gonzaga et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014A&A...565A..71L/abstract). Similar structures are observed in the Circinus galaxy, where VLTI/MIDI data are best fit by a two-component model comprising a compact ($0.2\text{ pc}$) thin dusty disk and a parsec-scale hyperboloid shell representing a polar wind [Tristram et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007PhDT.........3T/abstract), [Stalevski et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.3334S/abstract). 

The current consensus increasingly favors this "disk+wind" framework. The equatorial component governs black hole feeding and provides the bulk of the line-of-sight obscuration. ALMA imaging of the Circinus galaxy at $2-4\text{ pc}$ resolution uncovered a cold molecular disk extending to $25\text{ pc}$, featuring spiral arms that channel material inward at a rate of $0.3-7.5\ M_\odot \, {\rm yr}^{-1}$ [Tristram et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...664A.142T/abstract), [Goesaert et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251005199G/abstract). Simultaneously, radiation pressure drives a hollow polar cone of dust that dominates the extended mid-infrared emission. High-ionization coronal line gas is also likely launched directly from the inner edge of the torus and accelerated outward by this radiation pressure, effectively tracing the kinematic transition from the nuclear region into the host galaxy bulge [Mullaney et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.394L..16M/abstract). Recent JWST aperture-masking interferometry of Circinus confirmed this partitioning, showing that the bulk of the dust mass resides in the equatorial feeding disk, while a small fraction ($<1\%$) of the emission originates from hot dust entrained in a molecular and ionized outflow [Lopez-Rodriguez et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250608077L/abstract). 

This dynamic view is also supported by X-ray spectral modeling. Self-consistent fits for [[x-ray-absorbed-agn|X-ray absorbed AGN]] like Circinus require a Compton-thick flared equatorial disk ($N_{\rm H} \approx 10^{25}\text{ cm}^{-2}$) paired with a Compton-thin polar hollow cone ($N_{\rm H} \approx 2 \times 10^{23}\text{ cm}^{-2}$) [Andonie et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511.5768A/abstract). Consequently, next-generation spectral libraries, such as the IMPACTX model, now explicitly combine a classical clumpy equatorial distribution with a paraboloid-shell representing polar dusty gas [Fujiwara et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251220993F/abstract). The clumpy torus is thus no longer viewed as a static "donut", but rather as a dynamic, radiation-driven [[galactic-fountains|Galactic fountains]] where dust is continuously fed, uplifted, and expelled. Furthermore, this broadens the context of obscuration; in galaxies such as NGC 6860, X-ray absorption and reddening are found to originate from dust within the extended narrow-line region and host galaxy, rather than a compact torus alone [Psaradaki et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241102270P/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

Although clumpy torus models successfully resolve several observational tensions inherent to smooth dust distributions, the field faces significant challenges related to model degeneracies and the physical origin of the obscuring material. The universal applicability of these frameworks is also debated; for instance, clumpy torus models are favored in only a small fraction (e.g., $\sim 10\%$) of nearby FR I radio galaxies, suggesting diverse obscuration mechanisms operate across different AGN classes .

Current theoretical frameworks are challenged by structural and parametric degeneracies. As noted, different modeling libraries can yield derived active galactic nucleus (AGN) fractions and bolometric luminosities that differ significantly for the same source [Varnava et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250214101V/abstract). A persistent, unresolved issue in [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] is the origin of the "near-IR excess." While certain [[two-phase-model|Two-phase model]] libraries naturally accommodate this excess, others require an ad hoc hot blackbody component, which challenges pure orientation-based unification since such hot dust should theoretically be hidden from the line of sight in Type 2 objects [Lira et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...764..159L/abstract).

The relationship between the clumpy torus and global galaxy dynamics is another active frontier, particularly regarding co-evolution. This necessitates looking at broader evolutionary frameworks, such as a proposed evolutionary path spanning from heavily obscured star-forming galaxies, transitioning into Type 2, and finally culminating in unobscured Type 1 AGNs, suggesting that unification models must inherently consider host galaxy evolution [Spinoglio et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021IAUS..356...29S/abstract). This complexity also extends outward to the galaxy halo; recent simulations suggest the clumpy nature of the [[circumgalactic-medium|Circumgalactic Medium]] involves mist-like cloudlet structures that blend absorption lines through turbulent broadening [Singh Bisht et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241117173S/abstract). While star formation and AGN activity are often concurrent, the direct impact of AGN feedback on host star formation efficiency is highly debated. Some [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] simulations suggest that despite driving significant outflows, AGN ionizing radiation only marginally reduces the global star formation rate [Roos et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...800...19R/abstract). While disentangling the exact fraction of infrared emission contributed by the AGN torus is a complex challenge when building accurate [[composite-astrophysical-models-and-populations|Composite Astrophysical Models and Populations]], recent SED modeling studies demonstrate that star formation rates and stellar masses can actually be robustly estimated irrespective of the assumed torus model, although the derived AGN fraction remains highly model-dependent [Papadopoulos et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.536.2433P/abstract). This is further complicated by the challenge of AGN-powered heating of diffuse dust beyond the torus itself; in galaxies like NGC 1068, this extended heated dust can severely contaminate the derivation of host galaxy physical properties if not properly isolated [Viaene et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...638A.150V/abstract).

Next-generation facilities are poised to break many of these model degeneracies. Synthetic spectral studies predict that high-resolution observations with JWST/MIRI and NIRSpec can distinguish between competing clumpy geometries and constrain torus parameters to within a $15\%$ error margin, provided the host galaxy's fractional contribution to the mid-infrared flux is kept below $50\%$ [González-Martín et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884...10G/abstract). These unprecedented spatial constraints, alongside ALMA kinematics, will be critical in determining whether parsec-scale tori are long-lived, stable structures or transient phenomena fed by local spiral arms in the [[circumnuclear-environment|Circumnuclear Environment]] [García-Burillo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...823L..12G/abstract), [Goesaert et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251005199G/abstract).
