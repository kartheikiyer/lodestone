## Article

**AGN Classification and Unified Models**

[[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active galactic nuclei (AGN)]] are compact, highly luminous regions at the centers of galaxies powered by the accretion of matter onto a supermassive black hole. Emitting powerful non-thermal radiation across the entire electromagnetic spectrum, AGN are categorized into a complex taxonomy based on their multi-wavelength observational signatures. Historically, this classification has relied primarily on optical spectral emission line widths and radio luminosity. In the optical regime, objects such as [[seyfert-galaxies|Seyfert galaxies]] and quasars are divided into [[optically-classified-type-1-active-galactic-nuclei|Type 1 sources]]—which exhibit both a high-velocity broad-line region (BLR) and a lower-density, kiloparsec-scale [[narrow-line-regions-and-seyferts|narrow-line region (NLR)]]—and Type 2 sources, which display only the narrow forbidden lines. Similarly, [[radio-source-populations|Radio source populations]] are bifurcated into radio-quiet and radio-loud objects, with the latter further subdivided by their accretion efficiency into [[multiple-radio-galaxy-populations|high-excitation and low-excitation radio galaxies]].

To explain this immense phenomenological diversity, the Standard [[seyfert-unification|Unified Model]] was developed, proposing that the variations among major AGN classes are primarily geometric orientation effects rather than intrinsic physical differences [Lawrence et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987PASP...99..309L/abstract) [Wills et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..162..101W/abstract). In this paradigm, all AGN possess a central engine surrounded by an optically and geometrically thick, parsec-scale [[clumpy-torus-models|dusty torus]]. A face-on viewing angle allows direct observation of the central accretion disk and BLR (yielding a Type 1 classification), whereas an edge-on perspective obscures these inner regions, producing a spectrum dominated by the extended, unobscured NLR (yielding a Type 2 classification) [Bianchi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AdAst2012E..17B/abstract). Beyond simple orientation, the underlying physical state of the accretion flow also dictates classification; radiatively efficient thin disks drive high-excitation optical sources and powerful [[fanaroff-riley-radio-galaxies|Fanaroff-Riley Radio Galaxies]] (FR II), while radiatively inefficient, advection-dominated flows characterize low-excitation systems frequently found in massive, red-sequence elliptical galaxies.

While the classic Unified Model remains a foundational concept in AGN physics, contemporary research increasingly embeds AGN classification within a dynamic, evolutionary framework. Statistical disparities in host galaxy properties—such as Type 1 hosts having systematically lower stellar masses and older local environments than Type 2 hosts—suggest that the taxonomy frequently reflects distinct phases of galactic co-evolution rather than purely static viewing angles. In this evolutionary sequence, gas-rich galaxy mergers or secular instabilities trigger a heavily obscured starburst and accretion phase. This active nucleus eventually drives powerful kinetic and radiative AGN feedback, launching parsec-to-kiloparsec scale [[extranuclear-regions|outflows]] and relativistic jets that subsequently clear the circumnuclear dust, regulate host star formation, and transition the system into an unobscured Type 1 phase.

Modern multi-wavelength demographic studies, driven by facilities such as the [[swift-bat-agn-spectroscopic-survey|Swift-BAT AGN Spectroscopic Survey]] and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), continue to challenge and extend strict orientation-based unification. Observations indicate that host-galaxy scale dust frequently contributes to nuclear obscuration, while the existence of [[non-hblr-seyfert-2-galaxies|"True Seyfert 2"]] galaxies—a class of AGN that intrinsically lack a BLR due to exceptionally low accretion rates—demonstrates that physical deficits, not just geometry, can define spectral classes. Current open questions in the field center on the physical triggers of rapid, transient spectral shifts in [[changing-look-active-galactic-nuclei|Changing-look active galactic nuclei]], the precise role of AGN feedback in abruptly quenching versus merely maintaining galactic quiescence, and the nature of the heavily obscured, high-Eddington ratio "Little Red Dots" recently identified among [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]].

## Taxonomy of Active Galactic Nuclei

The taxonomy of active galactic nuclei relies fundamentally on observational signatures across the electromagnetic spectrum, with historical classifications driven primarily by optical spectral line widths and the relative power of non-thermal radio emission. 

**Optical Spectroscopic Classification**
In the optical regime, [[seyfert-galaxies|Seyfert galaxies]] and quasars are divided into two primary phenomenological categories: [[optically-classified-type-1-active-galactic-nuclei|Type 1]] and Type 2. Type 1 AGN exhibit both a permitted broad-line region (BLR)—characterized by velocity widths $\Delta v \gtrsim 10^3$ km s$^{-1}$—and a [[narrow-line-regions-and-seyferts|narrow-line region (NLR)]] representing forbidden transitions from lower-density, kiloparsec-scale gas. Type 2 AGN, conversely, exhibit only the narrow emission lines. To better characterize the physical conditions of this gas, modern theoretical diagnostics have been developed to accurately measure NLR metallicity, ionization parameter, and gas pressure across both ultraviolet and optical wavelengths [Zhu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..187Z/abstract). In addition to these standard lines, high-ionization "coronal emission lines" (with ionization potentials $\ge 100$ eV), particularly [Ne V], serve as reliable tracers for identifying high-luminosity AGN that might otherwise appear ambiguous on standard [[multiple-diagnostic-active-galactic-nuclei|BPT diagrams]] [Negus et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945..127N/abstract). Extending to the infrared, high-ionization mid-IR fine structure lines like [Ne V] and [O IV] act as robust, linear bolometric power indicators across all Seyfert types, including heavily obscured Compton-thick sources [Spinoglio et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...46S/abstract).

Under the classic [[seyfert-unification|Unified Model]], this spectral dichotomy is governed entirely by the observer's viewing angle relative to an optically thick, parsec-scale [[clumpy-torus-models|dusty torus]] that physically obscures the BLR in Type 2 sources [Lawrence et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987PASP...99..309L/abstract) [Wills et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..162..101W/abstract) [Bianchi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AdAst2012E..17B/abstract). However, as discussed later, the universality of this purely geometrical unification is strongly debated, with phenomena such as [[non-hblr-seyfert-2-galaxies|"True Seyfert 2"]] galaxies and [[changing-look-active-galactic-nuclei|Changing-look active galactic nuclei]] demonstrating that optical classifications can also reflect transient accretion states and intrinsic physical deficits [Pons et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...594A..72P/abstract) [Hon et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497..192H/abstract).

Extending to lower luminosities, [[liners|LINERs]] (Low-Ionization Nuclear Emission-line Regions) are identified by their characteristic low-ionization forbidden line ratios (e.g., strong [O I] and [N II] relative to [O III]). LINERs are generally considered to be a subclass of [[low-luminosity-active-galactic-nuclei|Low-Luminosity Active Galactic Nuclei]] operating in radiatively inefficient accretion states, though stellar photoionization can contaminate these signatures in some host galaxies.

**Radio Morphology and Accretion States**
The taxonomy further bifurcates AGN into "radio-quiet" and "radio-loud" populations based on the ratio of their radio to optical fluxes. Among radio-loud sources, the [[radio-source-populations|Radio source populations]] are subdivided by their optical excitation and underlying accretion efficiency into two distinct evolutionary states. High-Excitation Radio AGN (HERAGN) feature radiatively efficient accretion disks and typically reside in lower-mass, gas-rich host galaxies in the "green valley." In contrast, Low-Excitation Radio AGN (LERAGN) operate via inefficient, advection-dominated accretion flows and are overwhelmingly hosted by [[luminous-red-galaxies|massive, red-sequence elliptical galaxies]] [Smolčić et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...699L..43S/abstract) [Smolčić et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...730...64S/abstract). 

**Multi-Wavelength Synthesis**
Because standard optical diagnostic tools (such as BPT diagrams) suffer from host-galaxy dilution and dust obscuration, no single waveband provides a complete census of the AGN population. Constructing a unified taxonomy requires multi-wavelength selections—combining [[x-ray-agn|X-ray]], mid-infrared color, and radio continuum criteria—to identify heavily obscured and low-mass systems that traditional optical selections systematically miss [Mingo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.2631M/abstract).

## The Standard Unified Model

The Standard [[seyfert-unification|Unified Model]] of active galactic nuclei posits that the observed phenomenological diversity among AGN classes is primarily an orientation effect rather than an intrinsic physical difference [Lawrence et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987PASP...99..309L/abstract) [Wills et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..162..101W/abstract). In this paradigm, all AGN are fundamentally powered by the same central engine—a supermassive black hole accreting matter via a hot, radiatively efficient disk—surrounded by a high-velocity [[narrow-line-regions-and-seyferts|broad-line region (BLR)]] and a more distant, slower-moving narrow-line region (NLR). The cornerstone of this model is the presence of an optically and geometrically thick circumnuclear [[clumpy-torus-models|dusty structure]], historically approximated as a parsec-scale "torus."

According to the model, an observer viewing the system at a low inclination angle (face-on) has a direct line of sight to the central engine and the BLR, resulting in a Type 1 classification with a strong non-stellar continuum and broad optical emission lines. Conversely, if the system is viewed edge-on, the dusty torus obscures the central region, producing a Type 2 AGN (e.g., Type 2 [[seyfert-galaxies|Seyfert galaxies]]) characterized exclusively by narrow emission lines originating from the extended, unobscured NLR [Lawrence et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987PASP...99..309L/abstract). The extent of this region is intimately tied to the central engine; for instance, the [[cars-survey|Close AGN Reference Survey (CARS)]] has demonstrated that the maximum projected size of the extended narrow-line region (ENLR) correlates directly with the black hole mass ($R \propto M_{BH}^{0.5}$) [Husemann et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...659A.124H/abstract). In the X-ray regime, observations reveal that the narrow Fe K$\alpha$ emission line in nearby AGN predominantly originates from regions smaller than the dust sublimation radius, intimately linking it to the outer BLR or the accretion disk rather than the more distant torus [Andonie et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...664A..46A/abstract). In these [[hidden-agn|Hidden AGN]], the central continuum is severely attenuated in the optical and ultraviolet, though it may be detectable via scattered polarized light or hard X-ray emission.

Observations of infrared spectral energy distributions have heavily constrained the physical properties of this obscuring medium [Lyu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022Univ....8..304L/abstract). Mid-infrared spectroscopy reveals that the dust within the AGN torus differs significantly from standard Galactic interstellar dust. Silicate emission features at $9.7 \mu$m and $18 \mu$m suggest that the torus is populated by micron-sized grains (typically $\sim 1.5 \pm 0.1 \mu$m), which are substantially larger than submicron interstellar grains. This large grain size leads to a flatter, "gray" extinction law in the nuclear region [Xie et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJS..228....6X/abstract).

While the classical Unified Model elegantly explains the fundamental Type 1/Type 2 dichotomy, it has required modifications to account for luminosity-dependent effects. The "receding torus" modification historically proposed that the inner radius of the dust distribution is dictated by the dust sublimation temperature, which scales with the AGN bolometric luminosity ($L_{\rm bol}$), thereby increasing the torus opening angle for highly luminous quasars. However, recent studies suggest that the torus opening angle is actually determined by a more complex interplay between bolometric luminosity, black hole mass, and Eddington ratio [Oh et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJS..219....1O/abstract).

Despite its success, the strict orientation-only postulate remains a subject of active debate in the literature. High-resolution multi-wavelength data increasingly demonstrate that obscuration cannot be exclusively attributed to a parsec-scale torus; cold gas and dust on host-galaxy scales frequently contribute to line-of-sight extinction [Bianchi et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012AdAst2012E..17B/abstract). Furthermore, the classification of intermediate Seyfert types (e.g., 1.8 and 1.9) is complex, often driven by a combination of line-of-sight extinction and a low intrinsic AGN-to-host galaxy luminosity contrast that effectively masks the broad lines [Barquín-González et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...687A.159B/abstract). As explored in subsequent sections, statistical demographic studies and the discovery of unobscured populations lacking broad lines further indicate that the traditional Unified Model is incomplete.

## Accretion Modes and Radio Populations

Beyond the viewing angle parameters defined by standard [[seyfert-unification|Seyfert unification]], radio-loud active galactic nuclei exhibit a fundamental dichotomy based on the physical state of their accretion flows. This division separates the population into radiatively efficient [[multiple-radio-galaxy-populations|high-excitation radio AGN (HERAGN)]] and radiatively inefficient low-excitation radio AGN (LERAGN). These two classes represent distinct fueling mechanisms and evolutionary stages rather than simple geometric orientation effects [Smolčić et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...699L..43S/abstract).

**Accretion Modes and Energy Output**
LERAGN (also referred to as jet-mode or weak-line radio galaxies) are characterized by radiatively inefficient accretion flows. In these systems, the bulk of the liberated gravitational energy is channeled into mechanical or kinetic power in the form of relativistic jets, rather than emerging as radiated light [Delvecchio et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...602A...3D/abstract). This inefficient accretion mode is commonly fueled by the gradual accretion of hot ambient gas from the galaxy halo at low accretion rates [Tadhunter et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&ARv..24...10T/abstract). Morphologically, LERAGN are frequently associated with lower-power class I [[fanaroff-riley-radio-galaxies|Fanaroff-Riley Radio Galaxies]] (FR I).

In contrast, HERAGN (radiative-mode or strong-line radio galaxies) are powered by a classic, optically thick, geometrically thin accretion disk. These systems are highly efficient at converting gravitational energy into radiation, yielding an output that is predominantly radiative [Delvecchio et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...602A...3D/abstract). The optical emission line strengths in HERAGN are tightly correlated with their radio fluxes, indicating coupled growth between the jet and the radiatively efficient accretion disk. HERAGN typically correspond to the more powerful FR II radio galaxies. The development of such powerful sources requires an abundant supply of cold gas [Tadhunter et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&ARv..24...10T/abstract). 

**Host Galaxy Properties and Evolution**
The physical differences in accretion modes are strongly mirrored in the properties of the host galaxies. LERAGN typically reside in [[luminous-red-galaxies|massive, red sequence, quiescent elliptical galaxies]] hosting high-mass supermassive black holes accreting at very low Eddington ratios [Smolčić et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...699L..43S/abstract). Conversely, HERAGN are hosted by galaxies with lower stellar masses, younger stellar populations, and bluer optical colors, often placing them in the intermediate "green valley" [Smolčić et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...699L..43S/abstract). Direct measurements of the molecular gas content confirm this divide: HERAGN host galaxies contain, on average, a factor of $\sim 7$ more cold molecular gas mass than LERAGN hosts [Smolčić et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...730...64S/abstract), providing the requisite fuel reservoir for efficient accretion. Notably, the fundamental dichotomy in host galaxy properties between these radiatively efficient and mechanically dominated accretion modes has remained remarkably stable over cosmic time, persisting for at least the last 9 Gyr [Goulding et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...783...40G/abstract). Furthermore, examining their X-ray emission reveals distinct evolutionary pathways: while [[radio-source-populations|radio-quiet AGN]] generally show joint growth with their host galaxies, radio-loud AGN exhibit a statistically significant correlation between X-ray luminosity ($L_X$) and star formation rate (SFR) that may be fundamentally independent of stellar mass [Shehata et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250900612S/abstract).

These distinct populations trace different pathways in galaxy evolution, representing different phases of massive galaxy buildup [Smolčić et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...699L..43S/abstract) [Smolčić et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...730...64S/abstract). Because galaxies were generally more gas-rich in the early Universe, the space density of radiatively efficient FR II sources increases strongly with increasing redshift. At lower redshifts and lower luminosities, however, LERAGN dominate the [[radio-luminosity-function|Radio luminosity function]] [Prescott et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.457..730P/abstract). Observations extending out to $z \lesssim 6$ suggest a downsizing scenario, wherein the most massive galaxies undergo an efficient, high-luminosity AGN phase early in their history, subsequently exhausting their cold gas supplies and fading into a mechanically dominated, radiatively inefficient state at later epochs [Delvecchio et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...602A...3D/abstract).

## AGN Host Galaxies and Co-evolution

The paradigm of [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] places supermassive black holes (SMBHs) at the centers of most massive galaxies. Observations reveal that SMBH mass is tightly correlated with the properties of the host galaxy's spheroidal component, often parametrized by the $M-\sigma$ relation. This tight coupling suggests that the growth of the SMBH and its host galaxy are intimately linked through shared cold gas supplies and regulatory feedback mechanisms.

Studies of AGN demographics consistently show a strong stellar mass dependence, where the fraction of galaxies hosting an AGN increases with stellar mass. However, some evidence indicates that the probability of a galaxy hosting an AGN can be described by a universal Eddington ratio distribution that is intrinsically independent of the host stellar mass, implying that the higher observed AGN fractions in massive galaxies may be driven by selection effects rather than distinct fuelling physics [Aird et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...746...90A/abstract). In the radio regime, however, a "radio-AGN main sequence" has been discovered, demonstrating a tight, super-linear relationship between average radio-AGN power and galaxy stellar mass that persists out to $z \sim 3$ [Delvecchio et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...668A..81D/abstract). 

The star formation history of a host galaxy is deeply intertwined with its nuclear activity. Optically selected and [[x-ray-agn|X-ray AGN]] are preferentially found in host galaxies with intermediate optical colors, placing them in the "green valley" between the blue, star-forming cloud and the red, quiescent sequence [Schawinski et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...692L..19S/abstract) [Pović et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...541A.118P/abstract) [Klesman et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442..314K/abstract). This localization implies that AGN often reside in galaxies transitioning toward quiescence, potentially following a $\sim 100$ Myr delay between the initial cessation of star formation and the peak of observable black hole accretion [Schawinski et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...692L..19S/abstract). Specifically, quasars hosting extended emission-line regions (EELRs) preferentially occupy the "massive blue corner" of the green valley, suggesting that AGN feedback eventually blows away surrounding gas to initiate the quenching process [Matsuoka et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...750...54M/abstract). 

The connection between AGN activity and SFR is highly dependent on the accretion mode and feedback mechanisms. For instance, radio-loud AGN are distinctly bifurcated. [[multiple-radio-galaxy-populations|High-excitation radio AGN (HERAGN)]] typically reside in lower-mass, gas-rich "green valley" galaxies with ongoing star formation, while low-excitation radio AGN (LERAGN) inhabit massive, red ellipticals with inefficient accretion [Smolčić et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...699L..43S/abstract) [Smolčić et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...730...64S/abstract). AGN feedback operates in both positive and negative regimes: radio jets may enhance star formation by compressing gas, or suppress it by driving kiloparsec-scale ionized outflows with velocities up to $1700 \text{ km s}^{-1}$, depending on specific jet power thresholds and galaxy masses [Harrison et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441.3306H/abstract) [Kalfountzou et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471...28K/abstract).

Crucially, host galaxy properties expose tensions within classical unified models, which postulate that Type 1 and Type 2 AGN differ solely due to observer orientation relative to a dusty torus. Recent studies report significant differences in the host environments of these classes; for example, [[optically-classified-type-1-active-galactic-nuclei|Type 1]] hosts tend to have slightly lower stellar masses (by $\approx 0.2$ dex) than Type 2 hosts [Zou et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...878...11Z/abstract). Furthermore, [[non-hblr-seyfert-2-galaxies|Type 2 AGN]] often have bluer, more highly star-forming neighbors than Type 1 AGN [Villarroel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014NatPh..10..417V/abstract). Recent analyses reveal a bimodal behavior among Type II hosts specifically: lower-mass systems tend to reside on the star-forming main sequence, while massive hosts exhibit suppressed star formation, potentially directly linked to powerful AGN feedback [Vietri et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...659A.129V/abstract). These discrepancies support an evolutionary sequence wherein a heavily obscured, merger-driven starburst phase (Type 2) eventually clears its surrounding dust through feedback to become an unobscured phase (Type 1), indicating that host-scale physics contributes fundamentally to AGN classification [Dultzin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0809.1680D/abstract). This is further corroborated by recent results from the [[swift-bat-agn-spectroscopic-survey|Swift-BAT AGN Spectroscopic Survey]] (BASS), which show a roughly 400% excess of mergers and morphologically disturbed systems among AGN hosts compared to inactive control samples, alongside a significantly higher galactic bar fraction of around 50% [Parra Tello et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250621800P/abstract). Morphology is also intimately tied to the accretion rate, as rapidly accreting broad-line AGN tend to favor spheroid-dominated hosts, whereas obscured AGN are more likely to exhibit disturbed morphologies [Trump et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASPC..477..227T/abstract). Furthermore, there is a direct physical link between X-ray absorption and the specific star formation rate of the host galaxy, suggesting that the host's own gas content frequently contributes to the observed nuclear obscuration [Juneau et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..304..319J/abstract).

## AGN Feedback and Galactic Outflows

Energy injection from an active galactic nucleus (AGN) into its host galaxy is a fundamental mechanism invoked to regulate star formation and curb the assembly of massive galaxies. This energy transfer operates via two primary channels: radiation-driven winds and radio jets, which manifest across vastly different physical scales. 

On parsec scales, X-ray observations reveal Ultra-Fast Outflows (UFOs). These mildly relativistic, highly ionized winds exhibit positive correlations between outflow velocity, line width, and equivalent width, pointing to clumpy, multi-component winds accelerating through a thermally unstable multiphase medium [Laurenti et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251206077L/abstract). On macroscopic scales, these winds can expand into galaxy-wide **ionized outflows**. Integral field spectroscopy of luminous, low-redshift ($z < 0.2$) Type 2 AGN demonstrates that such [[extranuclear-regions|outflows]] are ubiquitous. High-velocity gas—with maximum velocities reaching $\le 1700$ km/s and velocity widths between $600$ and $1500$ km/s—is observed extending over $\gtrsim 6-16$ kpc in the majority ($\ge 70\%$) of these systems [Harrison et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441.3306H/abstract).

In systems hosting powerful relativistic jets, **kinetic feedback** plays a dominant role. The interaction between radio jets and the interstellar medium is complex and can yield both positive and negative feedback. Current research suggests a jet power threshold dictates this behavior: below a certain power, jets may compress surrounding gas and trigger star formation, whereas above this threshold, they efficiently eject gas and suppress it [Kalfountzou et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471...28K/abstract). The destructive impact of these outflows on **molecular gas reservoirs** is directly observable in some local [[seyfert-galaxies|Seyfert galaxies]] and [[liners|LINERs]]. For example, radio and molecular observations of the active galaxy [[notable-ngc-galaxies-n-o-sector|NGC 2639]] reveal a $\sim 6$ kpc central cavity in its CO gas distribution, coincident with multiple epochs of localized jet activity [Kharb et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Galax..11...27K/abstract). The multi-phase impact of these distinct feedback channels can even be probed chemically; a recent JWST discovery indicates that polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) band ratios can be utilized as diagnostics to effectively distinguish between kinetic-mode and radiative-mode AGN feedback [Zhang et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107990Z/abstract). The legacy of these massive outflows persists long after the active phase subsides, with "fossil outflows" identified in non-active galaxies representing the remnants of past periods of vigorous AGN activity [Albán et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240816831A/abstract).

Despite the energetic viability of these outflows, the literature remains explicitly conflicted on the direct role of AGN in the **quenching** of star formation. Direct observational evidence of abrupt, feedback-driven quenching (often termed "quasar-mode" feedback) remains elusive in the local universe [Harrison et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..304..284H/abstract). Some studies suggest a temporal offset rather than a simultaneous process, observing the aforementioned $\sim 100$ Myr delay between the cessation of star formation and the peak of observable black hole accretion [Schawinski et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...692L..19S/abstract). 

Conversely, extensive radio and optical integral-field studies—such as those combining LOFAR and MaNGA data—indicate that local radio-detected AGN typically reside in galaxies that already lie below the star-forming main sequence [Mulcahey et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...665A.144M/abstract). These findings strongly support a [[galactic-nuclear-activity-sequence|"maintenance mode"]] scenario. In this framework, AGN feedback does not necessarily cause the initial quenching event but rather injects sufficient mechanical and thermal energy to keep hot halo gas from cooling, thereby maintaining quiescence and preventing future star formation episodes over cosmological timescales [Harrison et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IAUS..304..284H/abstract) [Mulcahey et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...665A.144M/abstract) [Jin et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A&A...694A.309J/abstract). This feedback process operates continuously across the luminosity scale; even in normal elliptical galaxies, morphological evidence shows that hot gas asymmetry correlates tightly with both radio and X-ray AGN luminosities, indicating that feedback operates robustly even at the weakest activity levels [Diehl et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...680..897D/abstract).

## Challenges and Extensions to Unification

While the classic [[seyfert-unification|unified model]] successfully explains the zeroth-order dichotomy between Type 1 and Type 2 active galactic nuclei primarily through the orientation of a parsec-scale dusty torus [Lawrence et al. (1987)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1987PASP...99..309L/abstract) [Wills et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ASPC..162..101W/abstract), a growing body of multi-wavelength evidence reveals significant complexities. Modern observations indicate that simple line-of-sight obscuration is insufficient to explain all observed spectral classes, pointing instead to intrinsic physical differences and evolutionary sequences.

A major challenge to strict unification is the existence of [[non-hblr-seyfert-2-galaxies|**True Seyfert 2**]] (or "naked") galaxies. According to standard unification, all Type 2 AGN possess a hidden Broad Line Region (BLR) obscured by dust. However, X-ray and optical surveys have identified a subset of Seyfert 2s that intrinsically lack a BLR. These systems exhibit no broad optical emission lines yet show little to no X-ray obscuration, typically characterized by hydrogen column densities $N_{\rm H} < 10^{21.5} \ \rm cm^{-2}$ [Panessa et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.398.1951P/abstract) [Pons et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...594A..72P/abstract). After accounting for contaminants, approximately 47% of Seyfert 2s are found to be unobscured in X-rays. Depending on the diagnostic method used, between 20% and 90% of these unobscured sources may be True Seyfert 2 candidates [Pons et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...594A..72P/abstract). The absence of a BLR in these unabsorbed sources is widely attributed to low accretion rates or low Eddington ratios, which fail to support the requisite disk-wind structures, thereby challenging the universality of the obscuring torus. 

At the opposite extreme, astronomers have identified a subpopulation of "optically quiescent" quasars (such as SDSS J0751+4028). These objects exhibit strong infrared AGN signatures but lack even narrow optical emission lines, potentially due to a $4\pi$-covering "cocoon" of dust that completely traps the optical line emission [Greenwell et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503L..80G/abstract).

Furthermore, traditional **Sub-type determination** (e.g., intermediate classifications such as Types 1.8 and 1.9) is not solely a function of torus inclination. Recent spectral energy distribution modeling demonstrates that sub-type classification is heavily influenced by the luminosity contrast between the AGN and the host galaxy. In many Type 1.8 and 1.9 sources, the host galaxy is significantly more luminous than the intrinsic AGN continuum, effectively diluting the broad emission lines even in the absence of severe dust extinction [Barquín-González et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...687A.159B/abstract). 

The postulate that [[optically-classified-type-1-active-galactic-nuclei|Type 1]] and [[non-hblr-seyfert-2-galaxies|Type 2]] AGN differ *only* by viewing angle is also contested by the statistical differences in their host galaxies and local environments discussed previously. If orientation were the sole variable, the host properties of both types should be identical. However, Type 1 AGN hosts are found to have lower stellar masses on average, by $\Delta \log M_\star \approx 0.2$ dex, compared to Type 2 hosts [Zou et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...878...11Z/abstract). Additionally, significant disparities exist in the colors of their neighboring galaxies and local supernova rates [Villarroel et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014NatPh..10..417V/abstract) [Villarroel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...837..110V/abstract). These structural and environmental differences strongly support an **Evolutionary connection** between the classifications. In this scenario, galaxy interactions trigger a heavily obscured (Type 2) phase, which subsequently clears its surrounding gas and dust via AGN feedback to reveal an unobscured (Type 1) quasar [Dultzin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0809.1680D/abstract) [Florian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251010376F/abstract). 

Finally, the discovery of [[changing-look-active-galactic-nuclei|**Changing-look AGN**]] directly breaks the assumption of static obscuration. These sources undergo dramatic spectral transitions—appearing or losing their broad emission lines entirely—over timescales of months to years [Raimundo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..123R/abstract) [Hon et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.511...54H/abstract). These rapid transitions are primarily driven by intrinsic instabilities or sudden changes in the accretion rate, rather than variable line-of-sight obscuration. For example, newly discovered "turn-on" changing-look active galactic nuclei exhibit lower accretion rates compared to typical quasars [Yang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...91Y/abstract). Furthermore, their host galaxies are biased against young stellar populations, favoring intermediate-age populations, which implies these objects may occupy a specific transitional "feast-to-famine" stage in the co-evolution of supermassive black holes and their host galaxies [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956..137W/abstract).

## Environmental Triggering and Large-Scale Structure

The triggering of active galactic nuclei (AGN) is intimately linked to the availability of cold gas in the nuclear region, a parameter heavily influenced by the host galaxy's local environment and large-scale structure. The physical mechanisms driving this gas inflow span a continuum from secular instabilities to violent galaxy interactions, with the dominant process often depending on the host galaxy mass and the specific accretion mode.

**Galaxy Mergers and Interactions**
Galaxy mergers and tidal interactions are classically invoked to explain the rapid funneling of gas toward the central supermassive black hole, often triggering a concurrent starburst. In isolated mixed-morphology (E+S) galaxy pairs, optical AGN activity is found in approximately $40\%$ of the spiral components [Dultzin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0809.1680D/abstract). Notably, this interaction-driven activity is overwhelmingly biased toward obscured, Type 2 systems; broad-line Type 1 AGN are nearly absent in these pairs. This challenges strictly orientation-based unified models and supports an evolutionary sequence where interactions trigger heavily obscured accretion phases [Dultzin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0809.1680D/abstract) [Hernández-Ibarra et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459..291H/abstract).

Furthermore, major mergers can lead to the formation of [[dual-active-galactic-nuclei|Dual active galactic nuclei]]. While theoretical models predict such systems to be exceedingly rare at high redshift, JWST has recently identified a spectroscopically confirmed dual AGN candidate at $z \sim 5.4$, exhibiting a projected separation of $\sim 10.4$ pkpc and residing at the center of a cosmic filament [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250809749L/abstract). Despite the clear role of mergers in highly luminous systems, the necessity of interactions for triggering general AGN activity remains actively debated. When controlling for stellar mass and central star formation rate, the large-scale environment and one-on-one interactions exhibit a minimal direct effect on the overall prevalence of optical AGN, suggesting that secular processes and stochastic cold gas accretion drive the majority of low-to-moderate luminosity activity [Sabater et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.447..110S/abstract).

**Cluster Environment**
The dense intracluster medium presents a complex environment for AGN triggering. In massive clusters at $0.5 < z < 0.9$, the overall fraction of galaxies hosting an AGN is roughly $2.5\%$, comparable to the field galaxy fraction. This indicates that galaxies can still efficiently fuel their black holes despite environmental effects like ram-pressure stripping [Klesman et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.425.1215K/abstract). However, the spatial distribution of these AGN differs from the quiescent population: [[x-ray-agn|X-ray selected AGN]] are highly concentrated within the inner $20\%$ of the cluster virial radius, whereas mid-infrared selected AGN are less centrally concentrated [Klesman et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.442..314K/abstract). The AGN fraction in cluster environments also exhibits strong cosmological evolution, increasing by a factor of $\sim 8$ from $z \sim 0.1$ to $z \sim 0.9$, closely mirroring the redshift evolution of star formation in cluster members [Bufanda et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.465.2531B/abstract).

**Void Galaxies and the Cosmic Web**
At the opposite extreme of local density, void galaxies offer insights into isolated black hole growth. Observations indicate that the fraction of optical AGN in massive, red void galaxies is significantly higher than for equivalent galaxies in dense environments. This enhancement may be driven by a higher frequency of prolonged, one-on-one interactions in voids, or fundamental differences in isolated host galaxy evolution [Miraghaei et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....160..227M/abstract). Conversely, radio-mode AGN—characterized by inefficient accretion in massive ellipticals—show a higher prevalence in dense cluster and group environments, highlighting a dichotomy in how large-scale structure supplies and regulates the gas available for accretion [Miraghaei et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AJ....160..227M/abstract).

## Multi-wavelength Demographics and Modern Surveys

A complete census of the active galactic nucleus (AGN) population requires multi-wavelength observations, as no single photometric band or diagnostic technique can successfully identify all AGN classes [Lyu et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941..191L/abstract) [Radcliffe et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...649A..27R/abstract). Traditional optical and soft X-ray selections suffer from severe biases against heavily obscured sources, high-redshift populations, and [[low-luminosity-active-galactic-nuclei|Low-Luminosity Active Galactic Nuclei]] residing in highly star-forming or low-mass host galaxies [Azadi et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...835...27A/abstract) [Mingo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.2631M/abstract). In the near-infrared, new emission line diagnostics—utilizing ratios of [SIII], [CI], [PII], and [FeII] to Paschen lines—have proven highly effective, identifying approximately 60% more AGN than optical [[multiple-diagnostic-active-galactic-nuclei|BPT diagrams]] in JWST CEERS data [Calabrò et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...679A..80C/abstract). Furthermore, time-domain observations such as UV-variability selection have demonstrated great efficacy in identifying low-mass AGN candidates that are systematically missed by traditional spectroscopic diagnostics [Wasleske et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933...37W/abstract).

To overcome obscuration biases in the local Universe, hard X-ray ($>10$ keV) surveys have been instrumental. The [[swift-bat-agn-spectroscopic-survey|Swift-BAT AGN Spectroscopic Survey]] (BASS) provides a highly complete, obscuration-unbiased census of nearby AGN up to Compton-thick limits. This catalog spans black hole masses of $M_{\rm BH} = 10^5 - 10^{10} M_\odot$ and Eddington ratios of $L/L_{\rm Edd} = 10^{-5} - 100$, serving as a fundamental benchmark for modern demographic studies [Koss et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJS..261....2K/abstract). Concurrently, deep radio continuum surveys such as the VLA-COSMOS [[3ghz-large-project|3GHz Large Project]] have mapped AGN populations out to $z \lesssim 6$. The VLA-COSMOS data explicitly differentiate between high-radiative-luminosity and low-radiative-luminosity AGN, demonstrating that they occupy distinct host galaxy stellar mass distributions and trace different accretion modes over cosmic time [Delvecchio et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017A&A...602A...3D/abstract). To further disentangle these sources at high resolution, recent sub-arcsecond international LOFAR telescope observations utilize brightness temperature ($T_b$) as a robust diagnostic to unambiguously identify radio AGN and isolate their non-thermal energy contributions from concurrent star formation [Morabito et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.515.5758M/abstract).

In the mid-infrared and optical regimes, traditional color-color cuts and BPT diagrams frequently miss AGN in low-mass galaxies ($M_* \leq 10^{10} M_\odot$) where star formation dominates the integrated emission [Prathap et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024PASA...41...16P/abstract). To address this, modern demographic studies increasingly rely on panchromatic SED fitting. Using codes like PROSPECT and [[cigale|CIGALE]], SED fitting decomposes the galactic and nuclear components, identifying AGN fractions as low as 10% of the total bolometric output [Siudek et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609143S/abstract) [Thorne et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.4940T/abstract). Multi-wavelength modeling has been further advanced by Bayesian tools such as AGNFITTER-RX, which seamlessly models the complete radio-to-X-ray SED to simultaneously characterize the accretion disk, hot torus, relativistic jets, and hot corona [Martínez-Ramírez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...688A..46M/abstract). While the addition of AGN templates is necessary to capture accurate properties, it is worth noting that despite differences based on luminosity and accretion rates, the overall diversity in the intrinsic SED shapes of Type 1 AGN is relatively small . Crucially, failing to properly include these AGN templates in SED fitting can lead to severe overestimations of host stellar masses and star formation rates by factors of 5 to 10 [Florez et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497.3273F/abstract). While highly effective at recovering hidden AGN, the literature remains somewhat conflicting regarding the precise contamination rates; SED-based AGN candidates can sometimes be confused with retired galaxies, LINERs, or star-forming composites, necessitating further multi-wavelength validation [Siudek et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250609143S/abstract) [Mingo et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.462.2631M/abstract).

The advent of the James Webb Space Telescope has profoundly shifted the demographic landscape at high redshifts. Recent JWST/MIRI surveys have revealed that roughly 80% of mid-infrared selected AGN candidates at $z \sim 0-4$ were completely missed by pre-JWST multi-wavelength searches [Lyu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966..229L/abstract). These data confirm that the fraction of obscured AGN is highly dependent on both luminosity and redshift: the obscured fraction peaks at bolometric luminosities of $L_{\rm bol} \sim 10^{11} L_\odot$ and steadily increases from the local Universe out to $z \sim 4$, where the vast majority of AGN are enshrouded in dust [Lyu et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...966..229L/abstract) [Vijarnwannaluk et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...97V/abstract). Furthermore, JWST has identified a novel demographic of heavily obscured, high-redshift systems known as [[extreme-agn-and-quasar-populations|"Little Red Dots."]] These compact sources exhibit high Eddington ratios and red spectral energy distributions that can be successfully modeled as luminous AGN, where the nuclear emission is heavily attenuated by complex gas and dust geometries [Volonteri et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240812854V/abstract).

## Evolution, Open Questions, and Future Directions

While the classic [[seyfert-unification|Unified Model]] successfully attributes many observational differences in active galactic nuclei to the observer's viewing angle, contemporary research increasingly embeds this static framework within a dynamic, evolutionary context. A growing body of literature supports a sequence where gas-rich mergers or secular instabilities funnel gas to the nucleus, triggering an obscured starburst and active phase. As the central supermassive black hole grows, it eventually clears its surrounding dust via feedback to become an unobscured AGN [Dultzin et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008arXiv0809.1680D/abstract) [Florian et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251010376F/abstract). This evolutionary picture is reflected in the dichotomy of radio-loud populations: [[multiple-radio-galaxy-populations|high-excitation radio AGN (HERAGN)]] typically reside in lower-mass, gas-rich "green valley" hosts with efficient accretion, whereas low-excitation radio AGN (LERAGN) are found in massive, quiescent ellipticals with inefficient accretion rates [Smolčić et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...699L..43S/abstract) [Smolčić et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...730...64S/abstract).

The classic orientation-based unification is also challenged by unique sub-populations and evolutionary shifts. For instance, while Type 1 and Type 2 hosts possess similar stellar populations at low redshift ($z < 1$), they become intrinsically different at $z > 1$, suggesting a fundamental breakdown of simple orientation-based unification in the early Universe [Draper et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...740...57D/abstract). As previously discussed, observations of "true" [[seyfert-galaxies|Seyfert galaxies]]—Type 2 sources that intrinsically lack a broad-line region due to low accretion rates rather than dust obscuration—demonstrate that accretion state dictates spectral appearance in some regimes [Pons et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...594A..72P/abstract). The dynamic nature of the AGN duty cycle—highlighted by [[changing-look-active-galactic-nuclei|Changing-look active galactic nuclei]]—poses ongoing questions regarding the precise timescales and triggers of extreme, stochastic variations in the accretion flow [Hon et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497..192H/abstract) [Wang et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...956..137W/abstract). To help constrain these episodic events, astronomers have utilized giant AGN-ionized clouds, or [[extranuclear-regions|extended emission-line regions (EELRs)]], to estimate AGN duty cycles, inferring that individual bright phases typically last on the order of tens to hundreds of thousands of years [Keel et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.420..878K/abstract). Complementary to this, systematic searches have identified populations of "AGN ghosts"—candidates exhibiting severe discrepancies between the high luminosity inferred from extended ionizing regions and the much lower luminosity of their current dust echoes, suggesting that a rapidly fading phase is a dominant feature of the AGN life cycle in the local Universe [Esparza-Arredondo et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...905...29E/abstract).

A major focus of current demographic studies is [[x-ray-luminosity-function|X-ray luminosity function]] evolution and the redshift dependence of the obscured AGN fraction. Synthesis of the X-ray background and deep multi-wavelength surveys indicate that the obscured fraction increases significantly with redshift [Ballantyne et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...653.1070B/abstract) [Avirett-Mackenzie et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486.3488A/abstract). At $z > 2$, approximately $76\%$ of highly luminous quasars ($\log L_X > 44.5$ erg s$^{-1}$) are heavily obscured ($\log N_H > 22$ cm$^{-2}$), suggesting that the bulk of early supermassive black hole growth occurs behind thick columns of gas and dust [Vijarnwannaluk et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...97V/abstract). The nature of this obscuration is complex; deeply buried Compton-thick AGN frequently show extended hard X-ray emission on kiloparsec scales aligned with their ionization cones, suggesting an intrinsic connection between nuclear obscuration and host-scale molecular clouds [Jones et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...910...19J/abstract).

Despite these advances, the fundamental physics of the accretion-outflow connection and its impact on host galaxies remain highly debated. Kiloparsec-scale ionized [[extranuclear-regions|outflows]] with velocities up to $1700$ km s$^{-1}$ are prevalent among luminous AGN regardless of radio loudness [Harrison et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.441.3306H/abstract). However, whether these outflows actively quench star formation or merely prevent future gas cooling ("maintenance mode") is unresolved. While some studies find that AGN hosts exhibit reduced specific star formation rates, suggesting negative feedback [Comerford et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...901..159C/abstract), others find concurrent star formation and AGN growth, implying a more complex co-evolution where jet feedback might even locally compress gas and enhance star formation [Kalfountzou et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.471...28K/abstract) [Jackson et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.498.2323J/abstract).

Future directions are heavily focused on pushing the observational frontier to Redshift z > 6. Recent James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observations have discovered populations of heavily obscured, high-Eddington ratio AGN candidates at high redshift, often termed [[extreme-agn-and-quasar-populations|"Little Red Dots"]] [Volonteri et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240812854V/abstract). Theoretical models fitted to these [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] estimate that AGN contribute roughly $23\%$ of the total ionizing photon budget for cosmic reionization, with low-mass star-forming galaxies dominating the remainder [Dayal et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240111242D/abstract). Looking ahead, next-generation radio observatories, particularly the SKA and ngVLA, will be pivotal. By resolving sub-kiloparsec radio jets and mapping cold molecular gas unhindered by dust obscuration, the SKA and ngVLA will directly trace the lifecycle of young radio AGN, constrain the AGN duty cycle across cosmic time, and definitively test the mechanics of kinetic feedback in the multi-phase interstellar medium [McAlpine et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015aska.confE..83M/abstract) [Patil et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ASPC..517..595P/abstract).
