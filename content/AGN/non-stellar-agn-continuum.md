The non-stellar AGN continuum is the broad-band electromagnetic radiation generated by the central engine of an active galaxy, fundamentally distinct from the integrated [[stellar-continuum|Stellar continuum]] of its host. Powered by the highly efficient conversion of gravitational potential energy as matter accretes onto a supermassive black hole, this [[non-stellar-emission|non-thermal and thermal emission]] spans an immense frequency range from radio waves to gamma rays [Beckmann et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012agn..book.....B/abstract). Characterizing this [[non-stellar-continuum|Non-stellar continuum]] is essential to [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]], as it dictates the intrinsic energetics, ionization capacity, and radiative feedback of the active nucleus, and serves as the primary observational tracer of black hole growth across cosmic time.

Physically, the continuum is composed of several distinct emission components. The primary energy reservoir is an optically thick, geometrically thin accretion disk, whose multi-temperature thermal emission peaks in the ultraviolet and optical bands, observationally recognized as the "Big Blue Bump" [Puchnarewicz et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.276...20P/abstract), [Beckmann et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012agn..book.....B/abstract). Above this disk, a hot, compact electron corona generates a hard X-ray power-law continuum via the inverse Compton scattering of the disk's thermal UV photons [George et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990MNRAS.242P..28G/abstract), [Nandra et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991MNRAS.248..760N/abstract). This inner-engine radiation is extensively reprocessed by its environment: circumnuclear dust absorbs the short-wavelength emission and re-radiates it as an infrared bump, while magnetized relativistic jets can produce a non-thermal synchrotron continuum dominating the radio band. Together, the interplay and viewing geometry of these components form the empirical basis of [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|AGN Classification and Unified Models]].

Analyzing the AGN continuum requires robust spectral decomposition to separate the intrinsic central engine from host galaxy starlight, a challenge that is particularly acute in weakly accreting systems like [[liners|LINERs]] and [[low-ionization-emission-regions|Low-Ionization Emission Regions]]. Detailed temporal monitoring has revealed that the continuum is highly dynamic. High-amplitude "changing-look" events, where the continuum luminosity shifts drastically, can cause the [[agn-broad-line-region|AGN Broad Line Region]] to disappear or reappear on timescales of mere months or years. These rapid transitions present a severe tension with the standard viscous timescales of thin accretion disk theory, indicating that the physical mechanisms driving accretion state transitions remain incompletely understood.

Current frontiers in AGN continuum research focus on the extremes of cosmic history and the physics of the innermost accretion flow. In the early universe, the recent James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) discovery of high-redshift "Little Red Dots"—compact objects with a flat ultraviolet slope but a heavily reddened optical continuum—has sparked intense debate over whether their unique V-shaped spectra are dominated by dust-obscured AGN or extremely dense stellar populations. In the local universe, establishing the true contribution of the AGN continuum to the far-infrared band, identifying the drivers of rapid continuum variability, and explaining the apparent truncation of accretion disks in certain luminous Seyferts remain major open questions. Resolving these theoretical tensions, alongside utilizing next-generation arrays like the ngVLA to map the sub-galactic radio continuum and quantify AGN feedback, drive the ongoing evolution of the central engine paradigm.

## Physical Origin and the Central Engine Paradigm

The immense luminosity of an active galactic nucleus (AGN) is powered by a compact central engine, universally understood to be a supermassive black hole (SMBH) with a mass typically in the range of $10^6 - 10^9 M_{\odot}$. As matter accretes into the deep gravitational potential well of the SMBH, gravitational potential energy is converted into radiation with remarkable efficiency. This mechanism generates a luminous [[non-stellar-continuum|Non-stellar continuum]] consisting of both thermal and non-thermal emission that spans from radio to gamma-ray wavelengths, frequently outshining the [[stellar-continuum|Stellar continuum]] of the entire host galaxy [Beckmann et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012agn..book.....B/abstract). 

The primary thermal component of the central engine is emitted by an optically thick, geometrically thin accretion disk. Viscous heating within the disk produces a continuum that peaks in the ultraviolet (UV) and extreme-UV (EUV), observationally manifesting as the "Big Blue Bump" (BBB). In extreme EUV-bright Seyfert galaxies, the BBB temperature can reach $kT \sim 100$ eV [Puchnarewicz et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.276...20P/abstract). By applying standard disk models to high-resolution UV and near-IR data, observers have constrained the inner emitting radii of the accretion disk to $80-150$ gravitational radii ($r_g$) in powerful Seyfert 1s [Dewangan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.504.4015D/abstract). Further supporting this disk paradigm, an empirical correlation has been established between the optical spectral index and the dimensionless accretion rate, which provides a complementary method for estimating black hole masses [Zhang et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008MNRAS.385.1087Z/abstract). Above the disk, inverse Compton scattering of these thermal UV photons by a hot, compact corona generates the primary X-ray power-law continuum, completing the inner engine's high-energy emission profile [Nandra et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991MNRAS.248..760N/abstract).

However, the standard thin disk paradigm is not without conflict. Some sources exhibit a pronounced deficit in UV flux relative to standard models, indicating that the inner accretion disk may be truncated [Dewangan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.504.4015D/abstract). Other objects, such as NGC 1068, show evidence for a deep absorption trough in the ionizing continuum rather than the expected BBB [Spinoglio et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...623..123S/abstract). Further challenging the standard, stable disk paradigm are observations of chaotic accretion; for instance, in the luminous Seyfert PG 1211+143, the detection of ultrafast inflows with velocities of $v \sim 0.3c$ challenges standard disk alignment models and points to highly dynamic inner accretion flows [Pounds et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.481.1832P/abstract).

Furthermore, the central engine is highly dynamic. The recently identified class of "changing-look" AGN undergo dramatic transitions in their continuum and spectral type on human timescales (years to decades). These events are driven by intrinsic changes in the bolometric luminosity and accretion rate rather than moving obscuring clouds [Denney et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...796..134D/abstract). The [[agn-broad-line-region|AGN Broad Line Region]] is physically stratified and highly responsive; detailed global modeling of sources like Mrk 509 indicates a structured emitting region where higher-ionization emission originates significantly closer to the SMBH [Costantini et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A&A...595A.106C/abstract). This entire broad-line structure has been observed to disappear or reappear when the Eddington ratio ($\lambda_{\rm Edd}$) crosses a critical threshold of approximately $1\%$ [Guolo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508..144G/abstract). The rapidity of these continuum transitions presents a severe tension with standard thin accretion disk models, as the observed variability occurs much faster than predicted viscous timescales [Kynoch et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.2573K/abstract).

At the lowest accretion rates, determining the nature of the central engine becomes increasingly complex. While many low-luminosity sources classified as [[liners|LINERs]] are now thought to be powered by evolved stellar populations (such as post-AGB stars) rather than a central SMBH [Singh et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...558A..43S/abstract), [Bremer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...558A..34B/abstract), bona fide low-luminosity AGNs (LLAGNs) persist. The accretion flow geometry in these LLAGNs remains heavily debated. While standard models predict a transition to a radiatively inefficient, geometrically thick flow at very low Eddington ratios, high-spatial-resolution observations of the candidate "true Type 2" AGN NGC 3147 have detected the kinematic signature of a mildly relativistic thin accretion disk extending down to $<100\, r_g$, despite an extremely low accretion rate ($L/L_{\rm Edd} \sim 10^{-4}$) [Bianchi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488L...1B/abstract). Additionally, in many LLAGNs, nuclear near-infrared emission can be surprisingly strong, outshining the X-ray continuum at the lowest Eddington ratios [Dumont et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888...19D/abstract).

## Thermal Components: The Big Blue Bump

The Big Blue Bump (BBB) represents the primary thermal component of the [[non-stellar-continuum|Non-stellar continuum]] in Type 1 active galactic nuclei. Peaking in the ultraviolet (UV) to extreme ultraviolet (EUV) bands, the BBB is widely interpreted as the integrated thermal blackbody emission originating from a geometrically thin, optically thick accretion disk surrounding a supermassive black hole. Because the disk spans a range of radii, the resulting spectrum is a superposition of multi-temperature blackbody components, with the hottest material residing in the innermost stable circular orbit.

In standard models, the BBB rises steeply through the optical and UV bands and turns over in the EUV or soft X-ray regime. The exact peak and high-energy extension depend strongly on the accretion rate and black hole mass. In extreme cases, such as the EUV-bright Seyfert galaxy RE J1034+396, the BBB reaches an effective temperature of $kT_{\rm eff} \sim 100$ eV, accompanied by an exceptionally high soft X-ray to UV flux ratio ($L_{0.2\rm keV}/L_{1200\text{\AA}} \sim 10$) [Puchnarewicz et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.276...20P/abstract). Systematic studies of the optical-to-X-ray continuum show a strong behavioral link between the BBB and the [[x-ray-agn-excess|X-ray AGN excess]] at soft X-ray energies, suggesting that both features may be part of the same underlying spectral component, modified by varying degrees of cold gas and dust absorption [Puchnarewicz et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996MNRAS.281.1243P/abstract). Furthermore, as AGNs brighten, their optical continua frequently appear to become bluer (harder). While some studies conclude this is merely the variable accretion disk outshining the constant, redder [[stellar-continuum|Stellar continuum]] of the host galaxy [Sakata et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711..461S/abstract), others have demonstrated that even after accounting for the non-varying host galaxy light, the spectral index changes fundamentally with luminosity, representing a true intrinsic variation in the disk's continuum emission [Romano et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998astro.ph..6190R/abstract). This continuum luminosity also governs emission line properties; for instance, observations reveal a mid-infrared narrow-line Baldwin effect, where the equivalent widths of lines such as [S IV] 10.51 $\mu\text{m}$ anticorrelate with the underlying mid-IR continuum luminosity [Keremedjiev et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...690.1105K/abstract).

At the low-energy end, the accretion disk emission extends well into the near-infrared, dominating the continuum flux at $\sim 1\ \mu\text{m}$ before hot dust emission takes over. Isolating this 1-micron disk continuum allows for reliable black hole mass estimations using the near-infrared virial product, typically employing the unblended Pa$\alpha$ or Pa$\beta$ lines from the [[agn-broad-line-region|AGN Broad Line Region]] [Landt et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414..218L/abstract), [Landt et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.413L.106L/abstract), [Landt et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.432..113L/abstract). The disk's thermal profile is also physically linked to the surrounding environment; for instance, it has been proposed that the low-ionization portion of the [[agn-broad-line-region|AGN Broad Line Region]] originates as a dusty wind launched from the disk precisely at the radius where the effective temperature drops to a universal value of $\sim 1000$ K [Czerny et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...525L...8C/abstract).

Despite the general success of the thin disk paradigm, the literature notes significant tensions. High-resolution UV observations of certain powerful Seyfert 1 galaxies, such as IC 4329A, reveal a marked deficit in UV flux compared to standard models. This implies that in some sources, the optically thick accretion disk does not extend to the innermost stable circular orbit, but is instead truncated at $80-150$ gravitational radii ($r_g$) [Dewangan et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.504.4015D/abstract). Additionally, "changing-look" AGNs (e.g., Mrk 590 and SDSS J2232-0806) exhibit dramatic, order-of-magnitude changes in their optical/UV continua on timescales of months to years [Denney et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...796..134D/abstract), [Kynoch et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.2573K/abstract). These rapid transitions in the bolometric luminosity and accretion rate are much shorter than the standard viscous timescales predicted by thin disk theory, remaining an open challenge for disk physics. Finally, the assumption of a ubiquitous BBB is challenged by heavily obscured sources like NGC 1068, where models of the ionizing continuum suggest a deep absorption trough rather than a classic UV bump [Spinoglio et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...623..123S/abstract).

## Non-Thermal Emission: X-ray Coronae and Relativistic Jets

The non-thermal components of the [[non-stellar-continuum|Non-stellar continuum]] span from the radio to the hard X-ray and $\gamma$-ray regimes. This broad-band emission is driven primarily by two distinct physical mechanisms: inverse Compton scattering in a hot, compact corona, and synchrotron radiation from relativistic particles accelerated in magnetized jets.

**The X-ray Corona**
Hard X-ray emission (typically $2-100$ keV) in [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] originates from a hot, optically thin electron corona located near the supermassive black hole. This emission is generated via the inverse Compton scattering of UV and optical thermal photons from the underlying accretion disk [Nandra et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991MNRAS.248..760N/abstract), [George et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990MNRAS.242P..28G/abstract). The intrinsic coronal continuum is well-described by a power law, $F_\nu \propto \nu^{-\alpha}$, or in terms of photon index, $N(E) \propto E^{-\Gamma}$ (where $\Gamma = \alpha + 1$). Typical photon indices lie in the range of $\Gamma \approx 1.7 - 2.0$ [Turner et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989MNRAS.240..833T/abstract), [Nandra et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991MNRAS.248..760N/abstract). 

At high energies, the power law exhibits an exponential cutoff ($E_c \sim kT$), reflecting the thermal distribution of the coronal electrons. Observations have revealed extreme high-energy cutoffs as low as $kT \sim 15$ keV in some luminous Seyferts (e.g., 1H 0419-577) [Turner et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.1258T/abstract). These measurements suggest that X-ray coronae are often radiatively compact, with their electron temperatures tightly regulated by electron-positron pair production [Reynolds et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016AN....337..404R/abstract). In some sources, the thermal and non-thermal components are deeply intertwined; global modeling of the broadband spectral energy distribution in NGC 5548 suggests that its intrinsic near-infrared/UV continuum acts as a single Comptonised component directly linked to a warm accretion disk corona [Mehdipour et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015A&A...575A..22M/abstract).

The primary power-law continuum is frequently modified by photoelectric absorption and Compton reflection from surrounding cold or ionized material, producing a "Compton hump" at $\sim 20-30$ keV and a prominent Fe K$\alpha$ fluorescence line at $6.4$ keV [Nandra et al. (1991)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1991MNRAS.248..760N/abstract). Constraints from X-ray spectral and timing analyses indicate that the narrow core of the Fe K$\alpha$ line predominantly arises from regions within or near the dust sublimation radius, linking the reprocessor to the outer accretion disk or the outer [[agn-broad-line-region|AGN Broad Line Region]] [Andonie et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...664A..46A/abstract). However, the literature remains conflicted regarding the prevalence of relativistically broadened Fe K$\alpha$ lines. Some "weak" AGN, such as NGC 5548, lack broad iron lines entirely. This absence is actively debated and may indicate either a physically truncated inner accretion disk or strong light-bending effects where the hard X-ray source is elevated at radii $r_s \gtrsim 100 r_g$ [Brenneman et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...744...13B/abstract). Further ambiguity exists in obscuration models, where the global column density of the reprocessor may be significantly lower than the line-of-sight column density, complicating the distinction between Compton-thick and Compton-thin sources [Yaqoob et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454..973Y/abstract). Additionally, recent spatial analyses using Chandra have identified extended, kiloparsec-scale hard X-ray continuum emission in heavily obscured and Compton-thick AGN, indicating the presence of large-scale scattering clouds beyond the central nucleus [Ma et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...948...61M/abstract).

**Relativistic Jets and Synchrotron Emission**
In the radio band, the non-stellar continuum is dominated by non-thermal synchrotron radiation originating from relativistic electrons spiraling in the magnetized environments of jets and radio lobes [Arshakian et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.12393A/abstract), [Israel et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...483..741I/abstract). The radio spectrum follows a power law, $S_\nu \propto \nu^\alpha$, where the spectral index $\alpha$ depends on the optical depth and the electron energy distribution. For example, as illustrated by dedicated case studies of specific radio galaxies like Centaurus A, flat or inverted spectra ($\alpha \approx -0.3$) are observed in the optically thick, self-absorbed core, while its extended, optically thin lobes exhibit steeper indices of $\alpha \approx -0.6$ to $-1.0$ [Israel et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...483..741I/abstract). At the lowest frequencies, observations leveraging arrays like LOFAR reveal that these [[radiosed|radio SEDs]] are characteristically curved, becoming notably steeper at low frequencies specifically in AGN populations compared to typical star-forming galaxies [Calistro Rivera et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.469.3468C/abstract).

In powerful radio-loud AGN, this non-thermal synchrotron emission can extend into the sub-millimeter and far-infrared bands. Studies of 2 Jy radio AGN reveal that non-thermal contamination can account for $30-72\%$ of the continuum flux at wavelengths $>200$ $\mu$m, which can severely bias star formation rate estimates if not properly decoupled from thermal dust emission [Dicken et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.5807D/abstract). In the millimeter regime, findings from the BASS survey demonstrate that nuclear mm-wave continuum emission, constrained to scales of $\le 100-200$ pc in hard X-ray selected AGNs, arises from a diverse mix of components including jets, blobs, and outflows [Kawamuro et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJS..269...24K/abstract). Furthermore, observations of associated HI 21cm absorption against the radio continuum indicate that radio AGN can drive clumpy, fast, and massive atomic outflows on parsec scales in their nuclear regions [Morganti et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241206988M/abstract).

In "radio-quiet" AGN, the physical origin of the radio continuum remains a subject of active debate. While some models attribute this emission to scaled-down, low-level jet activity, others suggest it arises from shocks driven by wide-angle winds and outflows interacting with the interstellar medium [Albán et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240816831A/abstract), [Jin et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240901279J/abstract). Such shock-driven continuum emission is observed in objects like NGC 7212, where radio synchrotron emission is generated by shocks at the boundary of the AGN ionization cone [Contini et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...545A..72C/abstract). The interaction between outflows and the host galaxy is complex; for instance, observations of NGC 4051 provide evidence for high-velocity winds that lose kinetic energy through shocks with the interstellar medium, creating localized momentum-driven feedback [Pounds et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.413.1251P/abstract). Similarly, high-resolution X-ray spectroscopy of NGC 1275 reveals a composite source model where a distinct portion of the nuclear X-ray continuum arises directly from a jet cocoon interacting with surrounding molecular gas [Reynolds et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.507.5613R/abstract). Despite these morphological differences, multi-wavelength monitoring confirms that radio core variability often correlates with fluctuations in the optical and X-ray continua, underscoring a direct physical link between the accretion disk, the X-ray corona, and the launching region of the jet [Arshakian et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005astro.ph.12393A/abstract), [Israel et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...483..741I/abstract).

## The Infrared Bump and Dust Reprocessing

The infrared bump in the [[non-stellar-continuum|Non-stellar continuum]] of active galactic nuclei arises primarily from the thermal reprocessing of the central engine's intense ultraviolet and X-ray radiation by circumnuclear dust. This dusty structure is traditionally conceptualized as a toroidal geometry (the "dust torus") required by unified models of AGN (see [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|AGN Classification and Unified Models]]). Validating this geometrical premise, an exhaustive analysis of 50 years of broadband polarimetric data for the archetypal Seyfert 2 galaxy NGC 1068 confirms that Thomson scattering is the dominant optical polarization mechanism, fully disclosing its hidden Type 1 core and cementing the foundational framework of unification [Marin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.479.3142M/abstract). However, recent high-resolution mid-infrared and polarimetric observations have evolved this model, revealing that the dust distribution is often clumpy and may include a significant polar component extending outward from the nucleus [Bernhard et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.2598B/abstract). Complementing infrared models, molecular observations provide structural constraints; for example, OH maser emission in Mrk 231 traces a rotating, molecular torus located at radii of 30-100 pc [Klöckner et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003Natur.421..821K/abstract).

**Near-Infrared: Hot Graphite Dust and the Sublimation Radius**
The near-infrared continuum ($1$--$2\ \mu\mathrm{m}$) in Type 1 AGNs represents a transition zone. It is heavily influenced by thermal emission from the hottest surviving dust grains. Spectral decomposition studies show that this dust component is well-approximated by a blackbody with a temperature of $T \approx 1400\ \mathrm{K}$, corresponding closely to the sublimation temperature of the dust [Landt et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414..218L/abstract). Detailed modeling suggests that this near-IR excess is primarily driven by hot, pure-graphite dust located in the outermost reaches of the [[agn-broad-line-region|AGN Broad Line Region]], possessing a covering factor of $0.15$ to $0.35$ [Mor et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.420..526M/abstract). However, the primary accretion disk spectrum can still dominate the continuum at $\sim 1\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, an overlap that provides a crucial anchor for estimating black hole masses based on the $1\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ continuum virial product [Landt et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414..218L/abstract).

**Mid-Infrared: Silicate Features and Anomalous Grain Sizes**
At mid-infrared wavelengths ($5$--$38\ \mu\mathrm{m}$), the continuum is dominated by cooler dust emission. Analyses of the $9.7\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ and $18\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ silicate features—which typically appear in emission in Type 1 AGNs and absorption in obscured sources—indicate that the responsible silicate dust operates at temperatures around $T \approx 270\ \mathrm{K}$, while the underlying $5$--$8\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ continuum is primarily supplied by carbonaceous dust at $T \approx 640\ \mathrm{K}$ [Xie et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJS..228....6X/abstract). Strikingly, the mid-infrared spectra of AGNs demand dust grains that are systematically larger than those found in the Galactic interstellar medium. Typical AGN dust grains are roughly micron-sized (radii $a \approx 1.5\ \mu\mathrm{m}$), leading to a relatively flat or "gray" extinction law in the ultraviolet and optical regimes [Xie et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJS..228....6X/abstract). Modern empirical libraries categorize the intrinsic AGN mid-IR output into a small set of fundamental templates, differentiating between continuum shapes driven by nuclear obscuration or extended polar dust, and the silicate emission profile [Bernhard et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.2598B/abstract). A study of the complete 12 $\mu$m AGN sample further underscores these spectral differences, showing that the 12 $\mu$m nuclear luminosity provides a robust bolometric correction; while Type 1 SEDs show characteristic blue bumps, the SEDs of Type 2 and hidden broad-line (HBL) AGN are heavily contaminated by host starlight [Spinoglio et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...964..117S/abstract).

**Far-Infrared: The Star Formation Controversy**
The extent to which the AGN contributes to the far-infrared (FIR) and sub-millimeter continuum ($\lambda > 50\ \mu\mathrm{m}$) remains a subject of active debate. Some studies assert that the AGN contribution to the FIR is generally small and has been historically overestimated, with star formation dominating the bulk of the FIR luminosity in local luminous infrared galaxies [Alonso-Herrero et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012ApJ...744....2A/abstract), [Bernhard et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.2598B/abstract). Conversely, observations of highly luminous quasars suggest that the intrinsic AGN emission can outshine the host galaxy's stellar-powered dust emission across all wavelengths from the optical out to the sub-millimeter [Symeonidis et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459..257S/abstract). Furthermore, in radio-loud systems, non-thermal synchrotron emission from jets or lobes can heavily contaminate the FIR/sub-millimeter continuum, accounting for up to $30$--$72\%$ of the flux at wavelengths $\lambda > 200\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ [Dicken et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.519.5807D/abstract). Separating the intrinsic AGN thermal dust emission from host galaxy starlight (see [[stellar-continuum|Stellar continuum]]) and non-thermal components thus requires sophisticated, multi-component spectral energy distribution modeling.

## Spectral Decomposition and Host Galaxy Separation

The observed spectrum of an active galaxy is a complex superposition of the intrinsic [[non-stellar-continuum|Non-stellar continuum]] and the host galaxy's integrated [[stellar-continuum|Stellar continuum]], nebular emission, and dust reprocessing. In low-luminosity systems, such as [[low-ionization-emission-regions|Low-Ionization Emission Regions]] and Seyfert 2 galaxies, host galaxy contamination often dominates the integrated light. Failure to properly isolate the AGN emission leads to artificially redder apparent spectra and systematic overestimations of the AGN bolometric luminosity [Jalan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521L..11J/abstract). Consequently, robust spectral decomposition and stellar population subtraction are required to extract the intrinsic physical properties of the central engine.

In the optical and ultraviolet regimes, isolating the AGN involves modeling the power-law continuum and the Balmer continuum, while simultaneously subtracting the host galaxy's starlight and the complex Fe II pseudo-continuum. To systematically assess the factors modifying these spectra, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of red 2MASS AGN SEDs reveals that spectral variance is primarily driven by the Eddington ratio ($L/L_{\rm Edd}$) and intrinsic absorption, followed closely by host-galaxy contamination [Kuraszkiewicz et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...692.1180K/abstract). Software packages such as QSFIT have been developed to automate this decomposition process for large spectroscopic datasets. QSFIT performs a simultaneous multicomponent fit across the entire available optical spectrum, modeling the AGN continuum, iron blends, host galaxy starlight, and emission lines [Calderone et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.472.4051C/abstract). By analyzing large samples like the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS), it has been shown that the host galaxy fraction strongly anti-correlates with the AGN continuum luminosity, enabling the derivation of empirical relations to correct for stellar light when high-resolution spatial decomposition is unavailable [Jalan et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.521L..11J/abstract).

At infrared (IR) wavelengths, disentangling the hot dust emission of the AGN torus from the cooler dust heated by stellar populations and starbursts presents a distinct challenge. To extract the intrinsic IR AGN continuum, decomposition methods often rely on star-formation templates anchored to specific spectral features, such as the $11.3\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) emission line, which traces star formation but is generally destroyed or absent in the harsh radiation field of the AGN [Symeonidis et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.459..257S/abstract). Tools like IRAGNSEP utilize such diagnostics to separate the 1-1000 $\mu\mathrm{m}$ emission into robust AGN and star-forming templates [Bernhard et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.2598B/abstract).

For holistic, multi-wavelength analysis, broad-band SED fitting has become the standard methodology. Codes such as AGNFITTER (and its extended version, AGNFITTER-RX) employ Bayesian inference to model the complete radio-to-X-ray SED. AGNFITTER-RX accounts for up to seven physical components, simultaneously constraining the AGN accretion disk, hot dusty torus, relativistic jets, and X-ray corona, alongside the host galaxy's stellar populations, cold dust, and [[stellar-radio-emission|star-forming radio emission]] [Martínez-Ramírez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...688A..46M/abstract). Implementing clumpy torus models and semi-empirical accretion disk templates in these fits has been shown to significantly reduce residuals in the $1.5-5\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ and $0.7\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ regimes [Martínez-Ramírez et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...688A..46M/abstract). Similarly, versatile SED fitting tools like PROSPECT demonstrate that incorporating an AGN component is crucial not only for determining the AGN luminosity but also for obtaining unbiased estimates of the host galaxy's stellar mass and star formation history [Thorne et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.509.4940T/abstract).

## Temporal Variability and Changing-Look Phenomena

The non-stellar continuum of active galactic nuclei exhibits stochastic variability across the electromagnetic spectrum on timescales ranging from hours to decades. In the optical and UV bands, the variable AGN continuum component maintains a relatively constant spectral shape; the apparent "bluer-when-brighter" trend frequently observed in AGN is largely due to the brightening continuum swamping the redder, non-variable light of the host galaxy [Sakata et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...711..461S/abstract). Even at millimeter wavelengths, rapid variability provides extreme physical constraints; for example, the dual-frequency millimeter variability of M 81* implies an emitting region size of only $\sim 25$ Schwarzschild radii, pinpointing the very base of the compact nuclear outflow [Schödel et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007A&A...463..551S/abstract). Rapid variability can also unveil intrinsic disk instabilities. For example, Chandra observations of the luminous Narrow-Line Seyfert 1 (NLS1) galaxy 1H 0707-495 reveal a bimodal flux distribution, which may be a distinct signature of radiation pressure instabilities operating within the inner accretion disk [Leighly et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002xsac.conf..259L/abstract).

This temporal variability serves as a powerful diagnostic tool. Through reverberation mapping, the time delay $\tau$ between fluctuations in the ionizing continuum and the response of the surrounding gas or dust provides a direct measurement of the physical size of those reprocessing regions via $R = c\tau$. Such studies have successfully mapped the [[agn-broad-line-region|AGN Broad Line Region]] (BLR) and established scaling relations such as $R \propto L^{0.5}$ [Pozo Nuñez et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...552A...1P/abstract), [Landt et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.413L.106L/abstract). As an alternative to standard BLR cloud models, the "bloated stars scenario" posits that the broad-line clouds are the extended envelopes of bloated stars, offering unique predictions for emission-line reverberation and response to continuum variations in bright Seyferts [Alexander et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1997MNRAS.285..891A/abstract). Furthermore, the near-infrared continuum responds to accretion disk flux changes with a characteristic time lag, tracing the emission of hot dust at temperatures near the sublimation threshold of $\sim 1400$ K [Landt et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011MNRAS.414..218L/abstract). Extensive multi-wavelength campaigns highlight these tight physical connections; a 100-day multi-satellite campaign on Mrk 509 demonstrated a strong correlation between soft X-ray and UV flux enhancements, indicative of deeply coupled thermal and non-thermal reprocessing across the central engine [Kaastra et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011A&A...534A..36K/abstract). Rapid variability can also trace the kinematics of the immediate environment; for example, evidence for disk winds in sources like NGC 3516 comes from rapid X-ray continuum eclipses, which are interpreted as the passage of individual wind clouds across the line of sight [Turner et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A&A...483..161T/abstract).

While low-amplitude continuum variability is ubiquitous, a recently identified class of objects known as 'changing-look' AGN (CL-AGN) undergo extreme, high-amplitude transitions that fundamentally alter their spectral classification. A classic example is the former Seyfert 1 galaxy Mrk 590, which experienced a factor of 100 decrease in its UV/optical continuum luminosity, leading to the complete disappearance of its broad emission lines and transitioning it to a Type 1.9-2 state [Denney et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...796..134D/abstract), before the lines reappeared a decade later [Raimundo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.486..123R/abstract). 

Historically, such transitions were often debated and sometimes attributed to variable line-of-sight obscuration by dusty clouds moving across the nucleus. However, contemporary multi-wavelength evidence strongly favors intrinsic changes in the central engine's mass accretion rate and bolometric luminosity as the primary driver [Denney et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...796..134D/abstract), [Guolo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508..144G/abstract). In sources like NGC 2992, the appearance and disappearance of broad $H\alpha$ emission is directly correlated with the $2-10$ keV X-ray luminosity, occurring when the Eddington ratio crosses a critical threshold of $\lambda_{\rm Edd} \sim 1\%$ [Guolo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508..144G/abstract). This suggests that CL-AGN events may be governed by a global state transition between a standard radiatively efficient thin accretion disk and a radiatively inefficient accretion flow (RIAF).

The rapidity of these continuum variations presents a severe challenge to standard theoretical models in [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]]. In a standard geometrically thin, optically thick accretion disk, the viscous timescale—the time required for a change in the global accretion rate to propagate through the UV/optical emitting region—is on the order of $10^4$ to $10^5$ years. The observation of massive luminosity changes on timescales of merely a few years (e.g., the 'Big Dipper' SDSS J2232-0806; [Kynoch et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.2573K/abstract)) or the formation of a BLR just $\sim 100$ days after a nuclear outburst (e.g., 1ES 1927+654; [Li et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...933...70L/abstract)) is fundamentally inconsistent with standard viscous timescales. This glaring discrepancy indicates that the physical mechanisms driving CL-AGN remain unresolved, prompting alternative theories involving localized disk instabilities, magnetic torques, or tidal disruption events to explain the rapid depletion and refilling of the inner accretion flow.

## Low-Luminosity AGN (LLAGN) and Obscured Growth

At the lowest accretion rates, the non-stellar continuum of Active Galactic Nuclei undergoes significant structural changes. Low-Luminosity AGN (LLAGN) typically operate at an Eddington ratio of $\lambda_{\rm Edd} \lesssim 10^{-2}$ to $10^{-4}$ [Yuk et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...930..110Y/abstract). Below a critical threshold of $\lambda_{\rm Edd} \sim 0.01$, the standard optically thick accretion disk is thought to transition into a radiatively inefficient accretion flow (RIAF). This transition can precipitate dramatic "changing-look" events wherein the optical continuum dims and the broad emission lines vanish entirely [Guolo et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508..144G/abstract). However, this RIAF paradigm faces challenges; high-resolution observations of some extreme LLAGNs (e.g., NGC 3147 at $\lambda_{\rm Edd} \sim 10^{-4}$) reveal signatures of a mildly relativistic thin disk extending inward to $\lesssim 100 r_g$, contradicting the expectation of a completely truncated disk [Bianchi et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.488L...1B/abstract). Further kinematic evidence for organized nuclear structures in low-luminosity states comes from the discovery of double-peaked near-infrared Ca II triplet lines in NGC 4593, which serves as a definitive signature of a relativistic BLR disk [Ochmann et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250321994O/abstract).

In the near-infrared, the continuum of LLAGNs exhibits an unexpectedly strong thermal dust component. In sources with $\lambda_{\rm Edd} < 10^{-4}$, the nuclear $K$-band luminosity can rival or exceed the 2-10 keV X-ray luminosity [Dumont et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...888...19D/abstract). Characterizing the intrinsic LLAGN continuum is further complicated by host galaxy contamination. In many [[liners|LINERs]] and [[low-ionization-emission-regions|Low-Ionization Emission Regions]], the optical and ultraviolet continuum is not driven by a central supermassive black hole, but rather by evolved post-AGB stellar populations [Singh et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...558A..43S/abstract), [Bremer et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013A&A...558A..34B/abstract). 

While LLAGNs represent systems starved of fuel, actively growing black holes are often hidden behind dense columns of gas and dust. In Compton-thick AGN, where the line-of-sight column density exceeds $N_{\rm H} \gtrsim 10^{24} \text{ cm}^{-2}$, the primary X-ray continuum is entirely suppressed below $\sim 10$ keV. The observed X-ray spectrum is instead dominated by reflection from cold material and strong fluorescent Fe K$\alpha$ emission [Arévalo et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...791...81A/abstract), [Yaqoob et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.454..973Y/abstract). In addition to standard X-ray or infrared selections, an 'invisible AGN' selection method has been developed utilizing radio continuum emission to successfully identify optically faint, heavily obscured sources that are systematically missed by traditional mid-IR colour cuts [Truebenbach et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017MNRAS.468..196T/abstract). Similarly, recent discoveries have unveiled 'optically quiescent quasars' (such as SDSS J0751+4028), which exhibit quasar-like mid-infrared luminosities but completely lack narrow emission lines, potentially due to a sky-covering cocoon of obscuring material [Greenwell et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503L..80G/abstract). Tracing this obscured growth in dynamically complex systems such as major galaxy mergers has also been enhanced by introducing the millimeter-X-ray luminosity correlation as a novel diagnostic for identifying heavily obscured dual AGN [Droguett-Callejas et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260115186D/abstract).

This obscured growth phase is dramatically highlighted in the early universe ($z > 4$) by the recent JWST discovery of "Little Red Dots" (LRDs). These extremely compact, dust-reddened sources exhibit striking V-shaped spectral energy distributions that blend obscured AGN characteristics with potential extreme starbursts. Disentangling the stellar and non-stellar continuum in these early objects represents one of the major current frontiers in AGN physics and is driving a deep theoretical debate (see Section 10 for a detailed discussion).

## Historical Evolution of the Continuum Model

The historical development of the AGN continuum model traces its origins to the realization that a small fraction of galaxies emit a broad spectral energy distribution that cannot be explained by a standard [[stellar-continuum|Stellar continuum]]. Observations extending from early radio detections to the X-ray regime indicated that this non-thermal emission arises from an unresolved, parsec-scale central engine powered by [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|accretion onto a supermassive black hole]].

In the 1980s, dedicated X-ray surveys provided some of the first systematic constraints on the high-energy continuum. A cornerstone of this era was the EXOSAT spectral survey of the Piccinotti sample, a complete set of 30 X-ray-selected emission-line AGNs [Turner et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989MNRAS.240..833T/abstract). This survey established that the $2\text{--}10\text{ keV}$ spectrum is typically well-described by a simple power law with a mean energy index of $\alpha \approx 0.70$. Furthermore, the EXOSAT data revealed soft X-ray excesses in many Seyfert galaxies, providing early quantitative support for an optically thick accretion disk contributing to the lower-energy continuum.

Direct kinematic confirmation of the disk model proved challenging until the discovery of distinct line profile signatures. The observation of Arp 102B marked a watershed moment, as its broad H$\alpha$ emission lines displayed highly asymmetric, double-peaked profiles [Chen et al. (1989)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989ApJ...339..742C/abstract). These features were successfully modeled as emission from a Relativistic Keplerian disk, where Doppler boosting and gravitational redshift dictate the observed line shape. For Arp 102B, the disk was constrained to have inner and outer radii between $\sim 250$ and $\sim 1000 \, GM/c^2$. The subsequent discovery of similar relativistic profiles in other sources, such as 3C 332 [Halpern et al. (1990)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ApJ...365L..51H/abstract), firmly established the relativistic accretion disk as the physical origin of both the UV/optical continuum and broad-line kinematics.

The integration of these multi-wavelength continuum components led to the textbook [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|Unified Model]] [Beckmann et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012agn..book.....B/abstract). For decades, the Unified Model posited that the apparent phenomenological differences between AGN types (e.g., Type 1 versus Type 2) were strictly geometric, dictated by the observer's viewing angle relative to a dusty obscuring torus. However, the historical paradigm of a purely orientation-based model has recently been challenged by the discovery of "changing-look" AGNs, such as Mrk 590 [Denney et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...796..134D/abstract). These sources undergo dramatic transitions in their continuum luminosity and spectral type on timescales of years—much faster than the standard viscous timescales predicted by thin accretion disk theory [Kynoch et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.485.2573K/abstract). The consensus has thus shifted toward a dynamic model wherein extreme continuum variability and the appearance or disappearance of the [[agn-broad-line-region|AGN Broad Line Region]] are fundamentally driven by intrinsic changes in the black hole accretion rate, rather than variable line-of-sight obscuration alone.

## Open Questions and Future Frontiers in the ngVLA/JWST Era

The advent of next-generation observatories has shifted the study of the non-stellar AGN continuum toward characterizing elusive accretion states, mapping circum-nuclear environments, and resolving the mechanics of feedback at sub-galactic scales.

**The Nature of High-Redshift "Little Red Dots"**
Recent James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observations have unveiled a population of compact, red sources at $z > 4$ colloquially known as "Little Red Dots" (LRDs). These objects exhibit a distinctive V-shaped spectral energy distribution characterized by a [[very-blue-optical-continuum-spectrum|very blue rest-frame ultraviolet slope]] and a steeply rising, red rest-frame optical continuum [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240710760L/abstract), [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...36L/abstract). The physical origin of this continuum is currently a subject of intense debate. 

One leading hypothesis posits that LRDs are rapidly accreting, dust-reddened AGN. In this model, the red optical continuum ($\lambda > 4000$ Å) is shaped by extended dusty flows with characteristic gas densities of $10^1 - 10^3 \text{ cm}^{-3}$, while the flat UV continuum ($\lambda < 3000$ Å) requires a gray extinction law lacking small dust grains [Li et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240710760L/abstract), [Li et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...980...36L/abstract). Support for the AGN interpretation comes from the detection of non-stellar Balmer jumps and broad Balmer emission lines (e.g., H$\alpha$ and H$\beta$) originating in the [[agn-broad-line-region|AGN Broad Line Region]] with full-width at half-maximum velocities of $\sim 2000$ km s$^{-1}$, confirming the presence of an AGN-dominated optical continuum [Akins et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241000949A/abstract), [Ji et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250113082J/abstract). Detailed spatially resolved studies (e.g., of GS9422 at $z \approx 5.9$) suggest that an obscured Type 2 AGN, consistent with [[agn-classification-and-unified-models|AGN Classification and Unified Models]], coupled with dense damped Ly$\alpha$ clouds can reproduce both the continuum shape and emission line morphologies without invoking exotic stellar populations [Tacchella et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.540..851T/abstract).

Conversely, alternative models argue that the V-shaped continuum could be dominated by extreme, compact starbursts. Under this interpretation, the red optical flux is driven by strong nebular continuum emission, potentially powered by top-heavy initial mass functions and hot, massive stars [Katz et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025OJAp....8E.104K/abstract). Determining the exact fraction of [[stellar-continuum|Stellar continuum]] versus AGN light remains challenging, with some hybrid models suggesting that LRDs may host both a dusty, luminous AGN and an unobscured, low-mass stellar population, which points to the existence of highly overmassive black holes [Leung et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241112005L/abstract). To better interpret these distant sources, researchers have introduced the "doppelgänger" AGN concept, systematically identifying low-redshift sources that serve as detailed spectral proxies for highly debated high-redshift counterparts [Sareen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250501642S/abstract).

**Isolating Low-Luminosity and Elusive AGN**
At lower redshifts, characterizing the intrinsic continuum of low-luminosity AGN and [[liners|LINERs]] is frequently complicated by severe contamination from host galaxy starlight. High-resolution infrared spectroscopy with JWST enables the isolation of the point-source continuum and the detection of infrared high-ionization coronal lines, which are powerful diagnostics of the AGN ionizing continuum and are much less sensitive to dust obscuration than optical lines [Satyapal et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...906...35S/abstract). In contrast, large optical surveys like SDSS MaNGA demonstrate that optical coronal lines (such as [Ne V] $\lambda3347$ and $\lambda3427$) remain highly sensitive to dust; these studies find that the galaxies with the least dust extinction yield the most optical coronal line detections [Negus et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...945..127N/abstract). This high-resolution capability is essential for uncovering elusive, low-accretion-rate black holes in late-stage merger systems and bulgeless galaxies, thereby placing tighter constraints on black hole seed formation models. JWST spectroscopy has also uncovered critical evidence of feedback effects in these low-luminosity regimes, showing that AGN feedback preferentially destroys small PAH molecules, strongly suggesting kinetic-mode feedback is actively at work even in LLAGN [Zhang et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260107990Z/abstract).

**AGN Feedback and Extended Emission**
In the broader context of extended emission, next-generation science is mapping how the central engine's energy regulates its environment. Evidence of strong regulation is striking in objects like NGC 1266, where a compact AGN core drives a massive molecular gas outflow that actively quenches star formation in the host galaxy [Alatalo et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013IAUS..290..175A/abstract). On larger scales, mapping extended ionized gas provides crucial constraints on black hole influence. The Close AGN Reference Survey (CARS) has demonstrated a fundamental correlation between the maximum size of the Extended Narrow Line Region (ENLR) and the black hole mass ($R_{\rm ENLR} \propto M_{\rm BH}^{0.5}$) [Husemann et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...659A.124H/abstract). At longer wavelengths, this negative feedback can also be probed via extended millimeter-wave thermal bremsstrahlung, as recently detected in 3C 273, which traces gas directly ionized by the AGN far beyond the central engine [Komugi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023atyp.confE..71K/abstract).

**Sub-Galactic Radio Jets**
In the radio regime, the proposed next-generation Very Large Array (ngVLA) is poised to revolutionize our understanding of AGN feedback. While powerful radio galaxies have well-studied large-scale jets, the origin of the radio continuum in "radio-quiet" AGN is heavily debated, with contributions expected from low-level jet activity, wide-angle outflow shocks, and star formation. The broad frequency coverage (1 to 116 GHz) and superior angular resolution (yielding maximum baselines of hundreds of kilometers) of the ngVLA will map these continuum components on parsec to kiloparsec scales [Nyland et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...859...23N/abstract). By resolving the non-thermal synchrotron and thermal free-free continuum emission in the innermost regions of active galaxies, the ngVLA will directly observe the interfaces where radio jets and winds interact with the host's interstellar medium. This will provide quantitative measurements of the energetics of jet-driven AGN feedback and its role in regulating or quenching star formation, particularly in low-mass galaxies, young radio AGN, and high-redshift systems [Nyland et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...859...23N/abstract).
