The **Eddington ratio** ($\lambda_{\rm Edd}$ or simply $\lambda$) is a fundamental [[dimensionless-parameters|dimensionless parameter]] in astrophysics that quantifies the physical balance between outward [[radiation-pressure|Radiation pressure]] and inward gravitational attraction for an accreting body. It is formally defined as the ratio of an object's bolometric luminosity ($L_{\rm bol}$) to its theoretical [[eddington-limit|Eddington limit]] ($L_{\rm Edd}$). Serving as a critical diagnostic of the [[dimensionless-accretion-rate|Dimensionless accretion rate]], the Eddington ratio governs the geometric structure, radiative efficiency, and spectral state of compact accreting systems, most notably [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]] in X-ray binaries and [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] powering [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) Physics]] [Krolik et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999agnf.book.....K/abstract). 

The magnitude of the Eddington ratio dictates distinct physical accretion regimes. At moderate to high ratios ($0.01 \lesssim \lambda \lesssim 1$), accretion flows typically manifest as geometrically thin, radiatively efficient [[shakura-sunyaev-disks|Shakura-Sunyaev disks]]. Conversely, when the accretion rate drops below a critical threshold ($\lambda \lesssim 0.01$), the system transitions into a radiatively inefficient, advection-dominated accretion flow (ADAF), wherein the bulk of the viscously generated thermal energy is carried across the event horizon rather than radiated away [Lasota et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..142L/abstract), [Narayan et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002luml.conf..405N/abstract). These state transitions are tightly linked to multi-wavelength observables, such as X-ray coronal geometry and the [[radio-x-ray-correlation|Radio-X-ray correlation]]. Empirically, systems accreting at highly sub-Eddington ratios are strongly associated with the efficient launching of relativistic jets and heightened radio loudness [Ho et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...564..120H/abstract), [Sikora et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...658..815S/abstract).

On cosmological scales, the statistical characterization of accretion rates via the [[eddington-ratio-distribution|Eddington ratio distribution]] (ERDF) is essential for understanding black hole demographics and their co-evolution with host galaxies. The ERDF tracks the "downsizing" of active galactic nuclei across cosmic time, revealing that the most massive black holes accreted at near-Eddington rates in the early universe, whereas lower-mass systems dominate contemporary accretion activity. Furthermore, the Eddington ratio mediates the crucial feedback mechanisms that couple central engine growth to the host galaxy's star formation rate. Intense radiation pressure at high $\lambda$ can drive powerful wide-aperture outflows and kinetic winds, which regulate galactic gas reservoirs and help establish universal [[astrophysical-mass-scaling-relations|Astrophysical Mass Scaling Relations]] [Fabian et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999MNRAS.308L..39F/abstract), [van den Bosch et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..134V/abstract).

Although textbook models historically treated the Eddington limit as a strict maximum luminosity ceiling ($\lambda \le 1$) [Krolik et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999agnf.book.....K/abstract), recent observations have upended this paradigm. It is now widely recognized that periods of [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion and Feedback]] ($\lambda \gg 1$) play a critical role in the rapid assembly of early supermassive black holes. Current research is heavily focused on interpreting high-redshift discoveries from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), which has revealed unexpected populations of early [[dormant-black-holes|Dormant black holes]] and [[overmassive-black-hole|Overmassive black hole]] candidates. 

## Fundamental Principles and Theoretical Definition

The Eddington ratio, denoted as $\lambda_{\rm Edd}$, quantifies the physical balance between outward [[radiation-pressure|Radiation pressure]] and inward gravitational attraction. It is theoretically defined as the dimensionless ratio of an object's bolometric luminosity, $L_{\rm bol}$, to its theoretical [[eddington-limit|Eddington limit]], $L_{\rm Edd}$:

$$ \lambda_{\rm Edd} = \frac{L_{\rm bol}}{L_{\rm Edd}} $$

Classically, the [[eddington-limit|Eddington limit]] represents the maximum isotropic luminosity a gravitating body can achieve before the outward momentum imparted by radiation onto the surrounding gas halts further mass inflow [Krolik et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999agnf.book.....K/abstract). Furthermore, the Eddington limit is not restricted to black holes; it also describes the physical boundaries of extremely luminous stars, such as late-type WN stars, which can exist near the stability threshold [Gräfener et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006IAUJD...5E...7G/abstract). Observationally, for a given black hole mass, the Eddington ratio can exhibit a range of up to three orders of magnitude, highlighting significant diversity in accretion states [Woo et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...579..530W/abstract).

The Eddington ratio is intimately tied to the [[dimensionless-accretion-rate|Dimensionless accretion rate]], $\dot{m} = \dot{M} / \dot{M}_{\rm Edd}$, through the radiative efficiency $\epsilon$, such that $\lambda_{\rm Edd} \approx \epsilon \dot{m}$. For standard geometrically thin, optically thick [[shakura-sunyaev-disks|Shakura-Sunyaev disks]], the efficiency is typically assumed to be $\epsilon \sim 0.1$, dictated by the binding energy of the innermost stable circular orbit [Krolik et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999agnf.book.....K/abstract). However, theoretical and observational consensus demonstrates that $\epsilon$ is not a universal constant; rather, the physical state and radiative efficiency of an accretion flow are fundamentally governed by $\lambda_{\rm Edd}$ itself. When attempting to estimate accretion rates in low-Eddington systems via analytical means, classical Bondi accretion models must be corrected for the host galaxy's [[galactic-potential|gravitational potential]] and electron scattering to accurately capture realistic mass inflow estimates [Korol et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460.1188K/abstract). Further refinements from parsec-scale simulations provide a specific formula for the dimensionless accretion rate based on local gas density and temperature, which is essential for accurate subgrid modeling in cosmological simulations [Bu et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.484.1724B/abstract). In brightest cluster galaxies, empirical correlations show that Bondi accretion rates are generally sufficient to account for the total power of relativistic jets, even for radiatively inefficient systems [Fujita et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014arXiv1406.6366F/abstract).

While standard textbook models often define the Eddington luminosity as a strict maximum ceiling ($\lambda_{\rm Edd} \le 1$) based on the assumption of spherical symmetry [Krolik et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999agnf.book.....K/abstract), contemporary literature explicitly contradicts this limitation. Observations of ultraluminous X-ray sources (ULXs) and rapidly growing black holes in the early universe provide strong evidence for [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion and Feedback]] phases, where the effective $\lambda_{\rm Edd}$ can reach values of 10 to 100 [King et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.393L..41K/abstract), [King et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.536L...1K/abstract). In such systems, the classical spherical assumption breaks down; radiation is likely channeled through geometrically thick funnels or coupled with powerful collimated outflows, allowing the apparent luminosity to significantly exceed the isotropic limit [King et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.393L..41K/abstract). Still, extreme growth cannot continue boundlessly; theoretical models suggest a physical limit to supermassive black hole masses at approximately $5 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$, above which luminous accretion of gas becomes impossible due to disk instabilities [King et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456L.109K/abstract). 

## Accretion Regimes

Across both stellar-mass and supermassive systems, varying $\lambda$ drives transitions between distinct physical accretion regimes. Transitions between these regimes span all mass scales. The [[supermassive-black-holes|intermediate-mass black hole]] candidate HLX-1, for instance, exhibits spectral state transitions and variability consistent with sub- and near-Eddington accretion cycles, linking stellar and supermassive behaviors [Webb et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012sf2a.conf..631W/abstract).

At low accretion rates ($\lambda \lesssim 0.01$), the accretion flow generally transitions from a thin disk to a hot, optically thin, geometrically thick structure [Lasota et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..142L/abstract), [Narayan et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002luml.conf..405N/abstract). In this regime, the local cooling timescale exceeds the accretion timescale, resulting in an Advection-dominated accretion flow (ADAF) where the bulk of the viscously generated thermal energy is advected across the event horizon rather than being radiated away [Lasota et al. (1996)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1996ApJ...462..142L/abstract), [Yuan et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006JPhCS..54..427Y/abstract). Consequently, the radiative efficiency $\epsilon$ drops significantly below the canonical $\sim 0.1$ assumed for standard disks. Sgr A*, the supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way, is the prototypical example of an ADAF, operating at an extreme $\lambda \sim 10^{-8}$ [Narayan et al. (2002)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002luml.conf..405N/abstract), [Aharonian et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...619..306A/abstract). These highly sub-Eddington states are robustly associated with increased radio activity, suggesting that ADAF-like flows efficiently channel energy into relativistic jets [Ho et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...564..120H/abstract), [Sikora et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...658..815S/abstract), [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240217991W/abstract).

For intermediate ratios ($0.01 \lesssim \lambda \lesssim 1$), the flow condenses into a geometrically thin, optically thick configuration famously described as [[shakura-sunyaev-disks|Shakura-Sunyaev disks]]. Here, the accretion process is highly radiatively efficient ($\epsilon \sim 0.1$). In the context of stellar-mass X-ray binaries, this regime corresponds to the luminous, thermally dominated [[soft-states|Soft states]] [Fender et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016LNP...905...65F/abstract). Comparing black hole feedback to neutron star systems, it is notable that neutron stars are generally less radio-loud at a given [[x-ray-luminosity|X-ray luminosity]] and do not show the same strong radio suppression in these soft states [Migliari et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006MNRAS.366...79M/abstract).

At high accretion rates ($\lambda \gtrsim 1$), the flow enters a super-critical regime. Radiation pressure heavily inflates the inner disk, leading to severe photon trapping and the launching of powerful, wide-aperture winds [King et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SSRv..183..427K/abstract), [Yang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.2620Y/abstract). [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion and Feedback]] is critical for explaining the demographics of ULXs—which are often powered by stellar-mass black holes accreting at $\lambda \sim 10-30$ [King et al. (2008)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.393L..41K/abstract), [Motch et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Natur.514..198M/abstract). The population of these ULXs is observed to be anti-correlated with host metallicity, as metal-poor massive stars are more likely to collapse into massive stellar black holes that can sustain higher luminosities [Mapelli et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013arXiv1302.2238M/abstract). Highly accreting modes are also essential to explaining the rapid buildup of billion-solar-mass black holes in the early universe [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.2777Z/abstract). Furthermore, the mechanical power of such extremes can be staggering; the microquasar S26 in NGC 7793 serves as an example of a system where jet mechanical power at super-Eddington rates can exceed the core's X-ray luminosity by four orders of magnitude [Pakull et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Natur.466..209P/abstract).

On a cosmological scale, semi-analytic models predict that at $z < 2$, the AGN luminosity function at lower power is dominated by advection-dominated accretion flows, whereas thin-disk or super-Eddington rates dominate at higher redshifts [Griffin et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487..198G/abstract).

## Eddington Ratio Distribution Functions (ERDF)

The [[eddington-ratio-distribution|Eddington ratio distribution]] function (ERDF) provides a statistical description of black hole accretion rates across cosmological volumes. It serves as a vital tool for constraining the duty cycle of [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] and linking their accretion history to the evolutionary state of their host galaxies. In the local universe, observations indicate that the ERDF exhibits distinct regimes governed by the availability of cold gas and the stellar population of the host [Kauffmann et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.397..135K/abstract).

In star-forming galaxies, where gas is relatively plentiful, the ERDF is frequently modeled as a broad log-normal distribution that peaks at a few percent of the Eddington limit ($\lambda \sim 0.01 - 0.05$) [Kauffmann et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.397..135K/abstract). This regime is dominated by radiatively efficient accretion. Conversely, in quiescent, gas-starved galaxies with older stellar populations, black hole growth is primarily fueled by stellar mass loss. The ERDF for these quiescent systems is best described by a steep [[power-law-distribution|Power-law distribution]] or a [[broken-power-law|Broken power law]] that heavily favors highly sub-Eddington, radiatively inefficient accretion states [Kauffmann et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.397..135K/abstract), [Weigel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...845..134W/abstract). Indeed, Monte Carlo simulations of galaxy assembly suggest a bimodal Eddington parameter distribution, with distinct branches for [[low-luminosity-galaxies|low-luminosity AGNs]] (< 0.01) and bright quasars (0.3-1.0) [Cavaliere et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...664...47C/abstract).

A subject of active debate in the literature is whether these ERDFs are universal (i.e., independent of black hole or host galaxy mass) or if they feature strong mass dependencies. Some empirical models successfully reproduce the local [[x-ray-luminosity-function|X-ray luminosity function]] by assuming that the ERDFs for both radiatively efficient and inefficient populations are mass-independent [Weigel et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...845..134W/abstract). However, other studies argue that to simultaneously match the observed X-ray luminosity function and the flat relationship between host star formation rate and X-ray luminosity, the ERDF must be explicitly mass-dependent [Bernhard et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476..436B/abstract). Specifically, these models require a relative suppression of low Eddington ratios ($\lambda \lesssim 0.1$) in lower-mass host galaxies ($M_* \lesssim 10^{10} - 10^{11} M_\odot$) [Bernhard et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476..436B/abstract), [Blanton et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251114575B/abstract). Population models suggest that the mean Eddington ratio decreases not only with cosmic time but also with host galaxy stellar mass at fixed redshift [Carraro et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022MNRAS.512.1185C/abstract).

## Historical Development and Cosmic Evolution

Historically, models of quasar evolution assumed that [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] grew predominantly via radiatively efficient accretion at or near the [[eddington-limit|Eddington limit]] ($\lambda \approx 1$), eventually tapering off to intermittent activity [Small et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992MNRAS.259..725S/abstract). However, contemporary population models and deep surveys have established that the ERDF undergoes significant redshift evolution, an effect closely tied to the phenomenon of AGN downsizing.

At high redshifts ($z > 4$), higher cosmic gas fractions drive the average Eddington ratio upwards, with populations frequently approaching or exceeding the Eddington limit ($\lambda \gtrsim 1$) [Shirakata et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019MNRAS.487..409S/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240916347Z/abstract). Hierarchical merging models show that rapid protogalaxy growth in the early universe destabilizes large gas reservoirs, fueling black hole growth at their full Eddington rates at high redshifts [Menci et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...587L..63M/abstract). As the universe ages, the characteristic Eddington ratio declines, and the ERDF broadens to encompass a wider range of sub-Eddington states [Shankar et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..421S/abstract). The slope of the low-Eddington accretion rate distribution appears to increase with cosmic time, a trend consistent with the declining gas fractions of host galaxies [Jones et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...881..110J/abstract). This evolutionary downsizing manifests as a shift in dominant accretion activity: while the most massive black holes accreted at high $\lambda$ during the epoch of peak quasar activity ($z \sim 2-4$), their specific growth rates systematically declined at earlier cosmic times than those of lower-mass black holes, meaning that low-mass objects dominate contemporary accretion activity [Shankar et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013MNRAS.428..421S/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.518.2123Z/abstract).

Recent observations from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have profoundly challenged classical limits on $\lambda$. These observations reveal billion-solar-mass black holes fully formed by $z \sim 6$, appearing significantly more massive than local [[mass-scaling-relations|Mass scaling relations]] would predict for their host halos. To explain the rapid assembly of these massive black hole seeds, recent theoretical frameworks require periods of extreme [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion and Feedback]], marking a paradigm shift from traditional textbook treatments that enforce $\lambda \leq 1$ as a strict [[maximal-growth-rates|maximal growth rate]] [King et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025MNRAS.536L...1K/abstract). However, not all early objects are extreme; recent JWST IFU surveys of z=5-6 luminous quasars find Eddington ratios ranging from 0.1 to 2.6, largely consistent with lower-redshift populations [Liu et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251106085L/abstract). High-resolution cosmological simulations, such as the BLUETIDES simulation, complement these findings by indicating that fundamental black hole-galaxy [[fundamental-scaling-relations|scaling relations]] are established as early as $z=8$, with intrinsic scatter primarily driven by the host's gas fraction [Huang et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.478.5063H/abstract).

## Co-evolution with Host Galaxies

[[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] (SMBHs) and their host galaxies exhibit tightly coupled evolutionary histories, a phenomenon fundamentally mediated by the Eddington ratio. This co-evolution is evidenced by tight [[astrophysical-mass-scaling-relations|Astrophysical Mass Scaling Relations]], such as the correlation between black hole mass and the host spheroid's velocity dispersion ($M_\bullet - \sigma$). The universal nature of these correlations—with scaling slopes commonly measured as $M_\bullet \propto \sigma_e^{5.4}$ [van den Bosch et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016ApJ...831..134V/abstract)—suggests a feedback-regulated mechanism where the central engine and the galactic bulge grow contemporaneously. However, the discovery of black holes exceeding $9 \times 10^9 M_\odot$ in the centers of giant elliptical galaxies like NGC 3842 and NGC 4889 suggests that the growth processes of the most massive black holes may diverge from standard linear mass scaling relations [McConnell et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Natur.480..215M/abstract). Conversely, at the low-mass extreme, the discovery of a $\sim 50,000 M_\odot$ solar mass black hole in the nucleus of the dwarf galaxy RGG 118 extends these universal scaling relations down to the lowest mass regimes, while operating at a $\sim 1\%$ Eddington fraction [Baldassare et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ApJ...809L..14B/abstract). Similarly, the intermediate-mass black hole candidate in the bulgeless spiral NGC 3319 has been estimated to have a mass of approximately 30,000 solar masses, assuming an Eddington ratio between 0.003 and 3 [Davis et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PASA...38...30D/abstract).

A crucial metric in studying this interplay is the [[specific-black-hole-accretion-rate|Specific black hole accretion rate]] and its correlation with the host galaxy's star formation rate (SFR). Cosmologically, the ratio of the SMBH accretion rate to the galaxy SFR remains remarkably constant at roughly $2 \times 10^{-3}$ from $z = 0$ to $z = 4$ [Zhang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240916347Z/abstract). However, the exact relationship between the Eddington ratio and the SFR of individual host galaxies remains a subject of active debate. Some observations suggest that the Eddington ratio is largely uncorrelated with host star formation or molecular gas fractions [Molina et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...950...60M/abstract), [Suresh et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240404780S/abstract), implying that accretion and star formation may frequently proceed independent of one another. The role of dynamic environment is also nuanced; findings indicate that major mergers increase the probability of a galaxy hosting an AGN by a factor of $\sim 10$, though they do not necessarily drive the Eddington ratio higher than secular processes [Weigel et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476.2308W/abstract). Additionally, a systematic finding demonstrates that when matching for black hole mass and Eddington ratio, radio-loud AGNs are preferentially hosted by elliptical galaxies compared to radio-quiet counterparts [Kozieł-Wierzbowska et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017FrASS...4...39K/abstract). Conversely, some studies find an enhanced fraction of starbursting hosts among high-Eddington-ratio AGNs, suggesting a shared gas reservoir rather than immediate suppression [Bernhard et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.460..902B/abstract). Recent integral field spectroscopy of local active galaxies finds a moderately strong correlation between the Eddington ratio and the star formation rate, particularly with young nuclear star formation (<100 Myr) [Chopra et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260114542C/abstract).

When suppression does occur, it is generally attributed to AGN feedback driven by high Eddington ratios. As systems approach or exceed the [[eddington-limit|Eddington limit]], intense radiation pressure drives wide-aperture winds and collimated jets. Theoretical models propose that near-Eddington winds can reach velocities of $v \sim 0.1c$ and carry mechanical energies of $L_{\rm mech} \simeq 0.05 L_{\rm Edd}$ [King et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SSRv..183..427K/abstract). This energy is sufficient to sweep up the interstellar medium and potentially quench star formation, moving the host galaxy toward a "red and dead" state [Cattaneo et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009Natur.460..213C/abstract). Beyond local feedback, these dynamically active systems also leave cosmological signatures; the stochastic gravitational wave background detected by pulsar timing arrays can be modeled using quasar luminosity functions, assuming that bright activity is triggered by mergers with characteristic Eddington ratios [Kis-Tóth et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025CQGra..42g5007K/abstract).

## Multi-wavelength Diagnostics and Spectral States

Empirical correlations between the Eddington ratio and multi-wavelength observables provide critical diagnostics for estimating accretion states across cosmic time. Because the geometry and radiative efficiency of the accretion flow depend strongly on $\lambda$, its value dynamically dictates the spectral energy distribution of the central engine.

In the X-ray regime, coronal properties and spectral slopes vary distinctly with the accretion rate. Observations of local active galactic nuclei demonstrate that as $\lambda$ increases, the X-ray corona undergoes geometric changes, likely transitioning from a compact, optically thick state close to the inner disk to an optically thin configuration that extends vertically [Keek et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.2722K/abstract). As the Eddington ratio increases, the high-energy cut-off of the X-ray power-law spectrum increases in energy and the coronal reflection fraction decreases [Keek et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016MNRAS.456.2722K/abstract). While analogies with Galactic black hole binaries suggest a V-shaped correlation between X-ray spectral hardness and the Eddington ratio, studies of broad- and narrow-line quasars reveal that this trend exhibits significant intrinsic dispersion in supermassive systems and may deviate from simple binary analogues [Trichas et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...778..188T/abstract). At the lower extreme, an observational trend highlights that X-ray power-law slopes become steeper (softer) at smaller Eddington fractions ($10^{-8}$ to $10^{-6}$), suggesting distinct changes in emission physics at very low accretion rates. Furthermore, because the peak frequency of the accretion disk's extreme-ultraviolet emission shifts sensitively with $\lambda$, deriving accurate [[hard-x-ray-bolometric-corrections|Hard X-ray bolometric corrections]] requires explicit parametrization of the Eddington ratio to avoid double-counting reprocessed emission [Su et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250110793S/abstract), [Azadi et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250919666A/abstract).

In the optical and ultraviolet regimes, specific emission line ratios serve as robust indicators of the accretion state. The Fe II/Mg II line flux ratio has been proposed as a significant diagnostic that correlates strongly with the Eddington ratio in type 1 AGNs, reflecting a link between accretion activity and broad-line region metallicity [Shin et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJ...917..107S/abstract). Beyond spectral lines, continuum variability provides additional constraints; ensemble continuum variability structure functions and characteristic timescales depend primarily on AGN luminosity and rest-frame wavelength, and are explicitly found to be independent of the Eddington ratio [Kim et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251108933K/abstract).

At radio wavelengths, radio loudness is robustly observed to be inversely correlated with the Eddington ratio [Ho et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2002ApJ...564..120H/abstract). Systems accreting at low Eddington fractions ($\lambda \lesssim 0.01$) host radiatively inefficient, advection-dominated accretion flows that efficiently power collimated jets, rendering them highly radio-loud. Conversely, efficiently accreting thin disks at $\lambda \gtrsim 0.01$ are predominantly radio-quiet [Sikora et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...658..815S/abstract). In blazars, the accretion rate in Eddington units appears to follow a bimodal distribution, where broad-line systems typically exhibit higher accretion rates ($\lambda > 0.01$) and higher Compton dominance compared to featureless objects [Paliya et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021ApJS..253...46P/abstract). This physical dichotomy is codified by the [[x-ray-radio-fundamental-plane-relation|Fundamental plane of black hole activity]], a non-linear scaling relation linking radio luminosity, [[x-ray-luminosity|X-ray luminosity]], and black hole mass. The exact projection of this plane depends on $\lambda$, tracking the transition of the X-ray emission's physical origin from a pure jet-synchrotron and advection-dominated flow at $\lambda < 0.01$ to a composite of a standard thin disk and jet corona at higher accretion rates [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240217991W/abstract). The fundamental plane demonstrates remarkable universality, scaling continuously from [[stellar-mass-black-holes|Stellar mass black holes]] in X-ray binaries up to [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]] [Elford et al. (2026)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026arXiv260104007E/abstract).

## Feedback Mechanisms and Outflows

High Eddington ratios ($\lambda$) strongly influence host galaxy evolution by triggering powerful winds and outflows from [[supermassive-black-holes|Supermassive black holes]]. These feedback mechanisms can expel vast quantities of interstellar gas, potentially quenching star formation and leaving the host galaxy "red and dead" [King et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SSRv..183..427K/abstract). 

In systems accreting near or above the [[eddington-limit|Eddington limit]], intense [[radiation-pressure|Radiation pressure]] drives wide-aperture, highly ionized winds. The most extreme of these manifest as Ultrafast outflows (UFOs), which can reach bulk velocities of $v \sim 0.1c$ to $0.3c$ [King et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SSRv..183..427K/abstract), [Xrism Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025Natur.641.1132X/abstract). High-resolution X-ray spectroscopy of the luminous quasar PDS 456, for instance, has revealed discrete wind components outflowing at $20\% - 30\%$ of the speed of light. These outflows carry mass at rates of $60 - 300 M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, yielding a kinetic power that can exceed the Eddington luminosity itself [Xrism Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025Natur.641.1132X/abstract). Furthermore, at high redshifts, galaxies may undergo super-Eddington phases that trigger radiation-driven dusty outflows, effectively displacing dust to large radii and enhancing the system's UV visibility [Nakazato et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241207598N/abstract).

The coupling of these outflows to the host galaxy's interstellar medium governs the efficacy of kinetic feedback. Traditional theoretical models suggest that as near-Eddington winds shock against the interstellar gas, the subsequent thermal expansion drives massive, galaxy-scale outflows ($v_{\mathrm{out}} \simeq 1200\ \mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}$), yielding a characteristic [[wind-mechanical-energy|Wind Mechanical Energy]] of $L_{\mathrm{mech}} \simeq 0.05 L_{\mathrm{Edd}}$ [King et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SSRv..183..427K/abstract). However, recent observations introduce tension regarding this coupling efficiency. The discovery that UFOs can be highly inhomogeneous—comprising up to a million individual clumps—suggests that energy and momentum may not be efficiently transferred to galaxy-scale scales, thereby disfavoring purely energy- or momentum-driven feedback models [Xrism Collaboration et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025Natur.641.1132X/abstract). In some extreme near-Eddington systems, these uncoupled high-speed winds may instead form diffuse shocks by impacting high-density star-forming regions directly [Yang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.500.2620Y/abstract).

The relationship between the accretion rate and jet production also dictates the [[eddington-scaled-jet-power|Eddington-scaled jet power]]. While powerful relativistic jets are predominantly associated with low-Eddington, radiatively inefficient accretion states in elliptical hosts [Sikora et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007ApJ...658..815S/abstract), [Wang et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240217991W/abstract), this dichotomy is not absolute. Parsec-scale bipolar jets have been detected in highly accreting ($\lambda \sim 1$) radio-quiet sources, such as the narrow-line Seyfert 1 galaxy Mrk 335 [Yao et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.508.1305Y/abstract). However, the broader trend is strongly supported by demographics; giant radio galaxies (GRGs) with megaparsec-scale structures are statistically found to have lower Eddington ratios than normal-sized radio galaxies, typically operating in radiatively inefficient accretion states [Dabhade et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...642A.153D/abstract).

Furthermore, kinetic feedback is not strictly exclusive to high-$\lambda$ quasars. In low-luminosity active galactic nuclei operating far below the Eddington limit (e.g., M81*), hot accretion flows launch collisionless winds reaching velocities of $\pm 2.8 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}$ and temperatures of $1.3 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{K}$. These outflows propagate to $\gtrsim 10^6$ gravitational radii, carrying sufficient kinetic energy to influence circumnuclear evolution despite the absence of a near-Eddington photon flux [Shi et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021NatAs...5..928S/abstract).

Beyond radiation and mechanical winds, it has been proposed that starburst galaxies themselves may be subject to a "cosmic ray [[eddington-limit|Eddington limit]]" [Socrates et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ApJ...687..202S/abstract). However, recent analyses of representative star-forming galaxies indicate that this theoretical limit occurs at star formation rates much higher, or gas densities much lower, than those observed. This implies that cosmic ray pressure is likely not the primary factor regulating the luminosity and star formation rates of these environments [Heintz et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...941...78H/abstract).

## Open Questions and Future Directions

A major open question in the literature is whether the early, high-redshift [[overmassive-black-hole|Overmassive black hole]] population detected by JWST represents genuine physical outliers—requiring heavy initial seeding or prolonged bursts of [[super-eddington-accretion-and-feedback|Super-Eddington Accretion and Feedback]]—or if their mass estimates are systematically biased. Some theoretical models suggest that the spectral features of systems accreting near or above the Eddington limit deviate substantially from local virial assumptions, potentially inflating the derived black hole masses by up to an order of magnitude [Lupi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240617847L/abstract), [Lupi et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...689A.128L/abstract). 

Adding complexity to the high-redshift accretion landscape is the detection of [[dormant-black-holes|Dormant black holes]] in the early universe. For instance, observations at $z = 6.68$ have identified a $\sim 4 \times 10^8 M_\odot$ black hole accreting at a highly sub-Eddington ratio of $\lambda \sim 0.02$, with a [[mass-ratios|black hole-to-stellar mass ratio]] roughly 1,000 times above local scaling relations [Juodžbalis et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240303872J/abstract). This suggests an evolutionary duty cycle characterized by short, violent bursts of super-Eddington accretion followed by gas expulsion and long periods of dormancy. Understanding the triggers, durations, and frequency of these rapid accretion bursts remains a critical area for future research.

In the local and intermediate-redshift universe, empirical studies frequently observe a flat or weak correlation between AGN X-ray luminosity and the star formation rate of their host galaxies. This decoupling has been widely attributed to stochastic variability; because AGN accretion rates can fluctuate by orders of magnitude on timescales much shorter than star formation timescales, instantaneous Eddington ratios may not reflect the long-term average growth of the black hole [Bernhard et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018MNRAS.476..436B/abstract). Future theoretical work must disentangle whether this implies that accretion and star formation are completely physically decoupled, or if their underlying connection is masked by stochastic variability.

Finally, researchers are exploring whether highly accreting systems can be utilized for cosmology. By standardizing the radius-luminosity relationship for quasars using the Eddington ratio, [[active-galactic-nuclei-agn-physics|AGN]] could potentially serve as standardizable cosmological probes. This offers a promising new pathway to constrain cosmological parameters at high redshifts and may help clarify the ongoing Hubble tension [Panda et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv251001486P/abstract).
