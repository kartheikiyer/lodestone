## Article

In extragalactic astrophysics, **deeply buried nuclei** represent an extreme, transient phase of galaxy evolution where intense energy production from an active galactic nucleus (AGN) or a nuclear starburst is completely enshrouded by massive columns of gas and dust. Defined by hydrogen column densities exceeding $N_H \gtrsim 10^{24} \text{ cm}^{-2}$, these compact regions (typically $r < 100 \text{ pc}$) are Compton-thick and virtually opaque to optical, ultraviolet, and soft X-ray radiation [Iwasawa et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.297.1219I/abstract). Buried nuclei are intrinsically linked to the late stages of gas-rich galaxy mergers, where rapid angular momentum loss triggers "wet compaction." This process funnels enormous reservoirs of interstellar material into the galactic center, creating the dense environments necessary to fuel both Eddington-limited starbursts and the rapid mass assembly of supermassive black holes (SMBHs). Theoretical models suggest that this heavily obscured phase accounts for the majority of a supermassive black hole's growth over its lifetime, lasting until energetic feedback eventually clears the enshrouding medium [Hopkins et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...630..705H/abstract). The most extreme and compact variants of these environments are often classified as [[compact-obscured-nuclei|Compact Obscured Nuclei]].

Because traditional optical and soft X-ray diagnostics cannot penetrate such immense optical depths, characterizing deeply buried nuclei requires specialized multiwavelength techniques. Mid-infrared spectroscopy is critical for bypassing dust extinction, utilizing deep $9.8 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$ silicate absorption features, high-ionization fine-structure lines (such as [Ne V]), and specific polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) equivalent width ratios to identify the underlying power source [Voit et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...399..495V/abstract), [Armus et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...640..204A/abstract). At submillimeter and millimeter wavelengths, high-resolution interferometry traces the complex chemistry and kinematics of the obscuring material using dense gas tracers like vibrationally excited HCN (HCN-vib) and diagnostic HCN/HCO$^+$ flux ratios. These observations reveal that buried nuclei generally host massive, warm, and gravitationally unstable [[molecular-circumnuclear-disks|Molecular circumnuclear disks]] or [[compact-circumnuclear-disks|Compact circumnuclear disks]]. These parsec- to hundred-parsec-scale structures drive prodigious mass accretion toward the central engine [Scoville et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.266..149S/abstract) while simultaneously launching powerful, multiphase outflows that regulate star formation in the host galaxy [Page et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.485..213P/abstract), [Izumi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Sci...382..554I/abstract).

Current research into deeply buried nuclei is rapidly expanding into the early universe, where facilities like the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and ALMA are uncovering the obscured progenitors of modern massive galaxies. This article explores the historical development of the buried AGN paradigm, the physics of their feeding and feedback mechanisms, and the demographics of obscured dual AGNs. It also highlights several pressing open questions in the field, such as whether extreme dust extinction artificially inflates the observed velocities of AGN-driven outflows. Furthermore, it examines the controversial nature of newly discovered [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]] at high redshift; for these sources, astronomers face a "dust budget crisis," actively debating whether they represent genuinely dust-shrouded [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]] or unobscured supermassive black holes surrounded by anomalous, gravitationally unstable accretion disks.

## Physical Characteristics and Classification

Deeply buried nuclei are characterized by extreme gas and dust column densities, typically $N_H \gtrsim 10^{24} \text{ cm}^{-2}$, rendering them Compton-thick and virtually opaque at optical and soft X-ray wavelengths [Iwasawa et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.297.1219I/abstract). High-resolution interferometry reveals that these systems host massive, rotating molecular disks that are highly compact, typically restricted to radii $r < 100 \text{ pc}$ [Scoville et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.266..149S/abstract). The gas within these structures is remarkably dense ($n \sim 10^7 \text{ cm}^{-3}$) and warm ($T > 200 \text{ K}$), generating exceptional luminosity densities [Baba et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..184B/abstract), [Sakamoto et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASPC..477...21S/abstract). A substantial subset of these systems are classified as [[compact-obscured-nuclei|Compact Obscured Nuclei]] (CONs), representing an extreme phase of galaxy evolution where rapid supermassive black hole growth or compact star-forming activity is completely enshrouded [Donnan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...669A..87D/abstract), [Falstad et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...649A.105F/abstract). 

The traditional view of this obscuring material has evolved over time; modern models describe it not as a static, homogeneous torus, but rather as a complex, clumpy, and dynamic transition zone consisting of an equatorial disk or torus coupled with a polar dust component [Ramos Almeida et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017NatAs...1..679R/abstract). Furthermore, while typically associated with massive major mergers, deeply buried AGNs can also reside in bulgeless late-type disk galaxies. In such cases, they remain completely undetectable in optical spectra while clearly revealing themselves in X-ray and mid-infrared bands [Secrest et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...777..139S/abstract).

Classifying the primary power source—whether an active galactic nucleus (AGN) or an extreme nuclear starburst—is observationally challenging due to the severe attenuation. Robust classification relies on diagnostics that can penetrate high optical depths. In the mid-infrared, the depth of the $9.8 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$ silicate absorption feature ($\tau_N$) and the equivalent width ratios of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) features (such as the $11.3 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$ band) serve as reliable indicators of CONs [Donnan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...669A..87D/abstract), [García-Bernete et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...663A..46G/abstract). At millimeter wavelengths, the rotational transitions of vibrationally excited HCN (HCN-vib) exhibit a strong correlation (Pearson coefficient of $0.91$) with the nuclear optical depth $\tau_N$ [Donnan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...669A..87D/abstract). Furthermore, high $J=1-0$ and $J=4-3$ flux ratios of HCN to HCO$^+$ are frequently used to identify X-ray dominated regions associated with buried AGNs [Imanishi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....148....9I/abstract), [Imanishi et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AJ....134.2366I/abstract). However, this diagnostic is not without ambiguity; the literature notes that high-density molecular gas chemistry driven by turbulence in intense starbursts can occasionally mimic these AGN-like ratios [Imanishi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....148....9I/abstract). Conversely, shocks and starbursts within an AGN-hosting nucleus can cause anomalously low, starburst-like ratios, effectively hiding the AGN [Imanishi et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PASJ...58..813I/abstract).

The internal kinematics of deeply buried nuclei are governed by complex inflow and outflow mechanisms. Sub-parsec molecular inflows have been directly observed, though in some measured cases $<3\%$ of the infalling gas reaches the central black hole, with the remainder expelled via multiphase feedback [Izumi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Sci...382..554I/abstract). The interpretation of these outflows is currently subject to debate. While many observed high-velocity outflows (often $>1000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$) are attributed to luminosity-driven AGN feedback, recent simulations suggest that extreme dust extinction in gas-rich mergers can create an "apparent" blue-shift tilt in velocity dispersion diagrams, artificially inflating the observed outflow velocities of ionized gas [Yutani et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961...68Y/abstract).

At high redshifts ($z \sim 3-8.5$), the classification of heavily obscured systems faces new theoretical tensions, particularly regarding the newly discovered population of [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]] (LRDs). These compact, red sources exhibit characteristic V-shaped spectral energy distributions. While some models classify them as Compton-thick, heavily dust-reddened AGNs with visual extinctions $A_V > 3$ [Chen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...994L..42C/abstract), competing frameworks suggest a "dust budget crisis." This alternative view posits that LRDs might actually possess much lower extinction ($A_V \lesssim 1.5$) and that their unique red colors arise not from extreme obscuration, but from the emission of a gravitationally unstable outer accretion disk with a Toomre parameter $Q \sim 1$ [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250512719Z/abstract), [Chen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...994L..42C/abstract). This remains a highly active area of debate in the classification and physical understanding of early-universe buried nuclei.

## Historical Development of the Buried Paradigm

Historically, the obscuration of active galactic nuclei (AGNs) was largely understood through the unified model of Seyfert galaxies, wherein a classical Seyfert 2 is simply a Seyfert 1 obscured by a geometrically thick, line-of-sight-dependent dusty torus. Early detections of the 3.3 $\mu$m emission feature in obscured Seyfert nuclei provided some of the first evidence for small dust grains at temperatures around 300 K residing near the central engine [Moorwood et al. (1981)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1981A&A...100L..16M/abstract). However, the discovery of luminous and ultraluminous infrared galaxies (LIRGs and ULIRGs) introduced a distinct population where the central engine is deeply embedded and obscured along virtually all lines of sight. Early radio studies established that the radio core power could be used to predict the bolometric luminosity of these buried quasars, suggesting that they account for essentially all of the observed infrared luminosity in some ULIRGs [Lonsdale et al. (1995)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...438..632L/abstract).

The prototype for this "buried" paradigm was the late-stage merger NGC 6240. The critical observational breakthrough came from ASCA spectroscopy. While soft X-ray observations of NGC 6240 indicated a thermal plasma characteristic of a powerful starburst, the hard X-ray spectrum above 3 keV was extraordinarily flat and featured a prominent iron K$\alpha$ complex [Iwasawa et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1998MNRAS.297.1219I/abstract). This signature required an intrinsically highly luminous AGN hidden behind a Compton-thick screen of gas and dust with a column density $N_H > 2 \times 10^{24}$ cm$^{-2}$, rendering the central engine completely invisible in the optical and soft X-ray bands. Later detailed studies of NGC 6240's southern nucleus using advanced spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting with clumpy torus models suggested that its already high-covering-factor torus is further obscured by foreground dust residing within the host galaxy itself [Mori et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PASJ...66...93M/abstract).

Because such immense dust columns absorb and reprocess the intrinsic nuclear emission into the thermal infrared, long-wavelength observations became essential to unpack the energetics of these systems. Early theoretical work demonstrated that mid-infrared fine-structure lines, such as [Ne V], could penetrate the obscuration to differentiate a buried AGN from a [[deeply-embedded-star-formation|deeply embedded starburst]] [Voit et al. (1992)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1992ApJ...399..495V/abstract). Subsequently, ISO imaging and spectroscopy of massive merging systems like Arp 299 confirmed that their intense nuclear infrared sources were frequently composites of starburst and AGN activity [Mirabel et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.269..349M/abstract). These multiwavelength approaches initiated a paradigm shift regarding ULIRGs optically classified as low-ionization nuclear emission-line regions (LINERs). Specifically, near-infrared 3--4 $\mu$m spectroscopy and hard X-ray data from sources like UGC 5101 demonstrated that a LINER-type optical spectrum often served as a screen masking a powerful, heavily obscured AGN [Imanishi et al. (2001)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2001ApJ...558L..93I/abstract), [Imanishi et al. (2003)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003ApJ...596L.167I/abstract). High-resolution tip/tilt near-infrared imaging of 'warm' ULIGs further revealed compact nuclei with extreme colors identical to 'far-infrared-loud' quasars mixed with hot ($\sim 800$ K) dust [Surace et al. (1998)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...512..162S/abstract). High-resolution studies further mapped out this evolutionary sequence; for example, ULIRGs with double nuclei (such as Arp 220) represent earlier stages characterized by lower near-infrared contributions, whereas more evolved single-nucleus systems exhibit clearer AGN signatures and strong 25 $\mu$m excesses [Majewski et al. (1993)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...402..125M/abstract). Similarly, millimeter observations showed that 'warm' ULIRGs (with high 25/60 $\mu$m flux ratios) typically exhibit CO emission associated only with the AGN-hosting nucleus, whereas 'cool' ULIRGs exhibit starburst-related CO emission in both nuclei [Evans et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999alma.confE..16E/abstract).

The paradigm matured from individual case studies to robust statistical demographics with the launch of the *Spitzer Space Telescope*. Spitzer's Infrared Spectrograph (IRS) enabled systematic searches for optically elusive AGNs using polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) equivalent widths, deep 9.8 $\mu$m silicate absorption features, and high-ionization lines. Notably, Spitzer provided the first direct mid-infrared detection of the buried AGN in NGC 6240 via the [Ne V] 14.3 $\mu$m emission line, estimating that the AGN intrinsically contributes $\sim$20--24% of the system's total bolometric luminosity [Armus et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...640..204A/abstract).

Crucially, systematic Spitzer surveys of local ULIRG populations firmly established that the prevalence and energetic dominance of buried AGNs scale directly with the total infrared luminosity ($L_{\rm IR}$) of the host galaxy [Imanishi et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ApJ...694..751I/abstract), [Imanishi et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010ApJ...709..801I/abstract). These large-scale studies cemented the concept of a buried AGN phase within the broader context of ULIRG evolution: as gas-rich mergers progress toward coalescence, immense nuclear inflows trigger extreme starbursts and rapidly feed black hole accretion. This creates a transient, highly obscured phase before feedback mechanisms can eventually clear the surrounding material. This historical progression from isolated X-ray anomalies to a systematic evolutionary framework laid the observational foundation for the contemporary study of extreme nuclear environments, ultimately leading to the discovery and classification of [[compact-obscured-nuclei|Compact Obscured Nuclei]].

## Multiwavelength Diagnostic Tracers

The extreme column densities ($N_{\rm H} > 10^{24} \, {\rm cm}^{-2}$) characteristic of deeply buried nuclei render traditional optical and soft X-ray diagnostics ineffective. Consequently, probing the physical conditions and central power sources of these regions relies on specialized multiwavelength diagnostic tracers, primarily in the mid-infrared and submillimeter regimes, where dust extinction is minimized and unique high-density chemistry can be observed.

**Mid-Infrared Spectroscopy and Dust Properties**
Mid-infrared spectroscopy provides some of the most robust tools for identifying deeply obscured activity. A primary diagnostic is the nuclear optical depth of the $9.8 \mu m$ silicate absorption feature ($\tau_{9.8 \mu m}$), which directly traces the massive columns of obscuring dust [Donnan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...669A..87D/abstract). To distinguish genuinely compact buried nuclei from systems where high optical depth is merely the result of highly inclined host-galaxy dust lanes, astronomers utilize the equivalent width (EW) ratios of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) features. Specifically, deep nuclear silicate absorption suppresses the underlying continuum, artificially enhancing the EW contrast of the $11.3 \mu m$ PAH feature relative to other bands such as the $6.2 \mu m$ feature [García-Bernete et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...663A..46G/abstract). While these PAH EW ratios are robust against inclination effects, they can be diluted if the host galaxy exhibits significant unobscured star formation [Donnan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...669A..87D/abstract). Moreover, a '3D diagnostic diagram' developed for ULIRGs illustrates that for the most deeply buried nuclei, an 'unobscured keyhole' covering less than 10% of the sightline is enough to make the mid-infrared spectrum appear nearly unobscured [Marshall et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018ApJ...858...59M/abstract).

High-ionization fine-structure lines also serve as vital diagnostics. The detection of [Ne V] $14.3 \mu m$ emission provides direct evidence for a buried active galactic nucleus (AGN) [Armus et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...640..204A/abstract). Additionally, the luminosity ratio of the [O IV] $25.89 \mu m$ line to the sub-arcsecond $12 \mu m$ continuum has been established as an effective indicator for AGNs buried behind highly obscuring material with very small scattering fractions ($<0.5\%$) [Yamada et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...876...96Y/abstract). Alongside these high-ionization markers, strong low-ionization cooling lines, such as [Si II] $34.82 \mu m$, are highly effective discriminants for tracing the energetic X-ray-dominated regions associated with buried AGNs [Dale et al. (2016)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006ApJ...646..161D/abstract). Furthermore, near- and mid-infrared spectroscopy allows for detailed analyses of nuclear dust mineralogy and hot molecular gas. Observations reveal that the obscuring dust in these extreme nuclei consists predominantly of alumina and amorphous silicates, exhibiting significantly higher crystallinity in more luminous sources when compared to typical interstellar dust in our Galaxy [Srinivasan et al. (2017)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017P&SS..149...56S/abstract). In individual, deeply embedded sources like NGC 4418, observations showcase exceptionally strong $\text{H}_2$ emission lines indicative of a thermal origin, pointing to the heating effects of a powerful, X-ray-emitting buried AGN [Imanishi et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004AJ....128.2037I/abstract).

**Radio and Submillimeter Tracers**
At radio frequencies, neutral hydrogen (21 cm) absorption is frequently utilized to probe nuclear obscuration. Interestingly, 21 cm absorption is often suppressed toward central compact radio sources in Seyfert nuclei due to free-free absorption or excitation effects. This suppression implies that the primary obscuring gaseous disks are highly compact, typically smaller than a few tens of parsecs [Gallimore et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999ApJ...524..684G/abstract).

At submillimeter and millimeter wavelengths, the rotational transitions of dense molecular gas are utilized to probe the kinematics and excitation of the nuclear region. The flux ratios of HCN to HCO$^+$ in the $J=1-0$ and $J=4-3$ transitions are generally found to be higher in AGN-dominated buried nuclei than in starburst-powered regions, likely due to X-ray driven chemistry [Imanishi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....148....9I/abstract), [Imanishi et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AJ....134.2366I/abstract), [Imanishi et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009AJ....137.3581I/abstract). However, the literature presents some ambiguity regarding this diagnostic: extreme starbursts with high mechanical turbulence can induce heating that mimics AGN-like HCN/HCO$^+$ ratios, leading to potential false positives [Imanishi et al. (2014)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014AJ....148....9I/abstract). Conversely, shocks and intense starburst activity within a true AGN-hosting nucleus can suppress these ratios, creating false negatives that effectively hide the AGN's signature [Imanishi et al. (2006)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2006PASJ...58..813I/abstract).

An unambiguous tracer of the most extreme, dense environments—often classified as [[compact-obscured-nuclei|Compact Obscured Nuclei]] (CONs)—is the emission from vibrationally excited HCN (HCN-vib). In the central parsecs of these systems, the intense mid-infrared radiation density produced by warm ($>200$ K) dust pumps the vibrational levels of the HCN molecule, leading to emission in rotational transitions within the vibrationally excited state [Falstad et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...649A.105F/abstract). CONs are formally defined by their exceptional surface brightness in this tracer:
$$ \Sigma_{\rm HCN-vib} > 1 L_\odot \, {\rm pc}^{-2} $$
in the $J=3-2$ transition [Falstad et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...649A.105F/abstract). HCN-vib emission is tightly coupled to mid-infrared diagnostics; studies demonstrate a strong correlation (Pearson coefficient of 0.91) between the strength of the HCN-vib lines and the nuclear silicate optical depth $\tau_{9.8 \mu m}$ [Donnan et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A&A...669A..87D/abstract). 

Future far-infrared observatories, such as the proposed PRIMA mission, are expected to leverage these established mid-infrared and submillimeter diagnostics—particularly deep silicate absorption and PAH features—to detect and characterize deeply obscured galaxy nuclei out to redshifts of $z \sim 7$ [Donnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250311611D/abstract), [Donnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JATIS..11c1606D/abstract).

## The Role of Galaxy Mergers and Gas Compaction

Gravitational interactions during gas-rich galaxy mergers are a primary mechanism for driving massive quantities of gas toward the galactic center. This rapid loss of angular momentum triggers "wet compaction," an evolutionary phase characterized by intense central gas pooling and the subsequent triggering of both Eddington-limited starbursts and active galactic nucleus (AGN) activity. Observations of massive star-forming galaxies at cosmic noon, such as BX610 ($z=2.21$), show central regions highly enriched with molecular gas ($M_{\rm mol} / M_{\star} \approx 1$) experiencing rapid inflows consistent with this wet compaction phase [Arriagada-Neira et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241014781A/abstract). The compaction process frequently ignites both processes simultaneously. Indeed, mid-infrared spectroscopy reveals concurrent AGN and starburst activity in approximately $50\%$ of luminous infrared galaxies (LIRGs), many of which were previously identified only as starbursts at shorter wavelengths due to severe optical obscuration [Dixon et al. (2011)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011ApJ...740...99D/abstract). The resulting column densities are sufficient to completely obscure the central engine, creating the dense, dusty environments characteristic of [[compact-obscured-nuclei|Compact Obscured Nuclei]]. Self-regulated growth models predict that this heavily obscured, or "buried," phase accounts for the majority of supermassive black hole (SMBH) mass assembly, lasting approximately $\sim 100$ Myr before feedback clears the surrounding material [Hopkins et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...630..705H/abstract).

Multiwavelength surveys demonstrate that the energetic importance of buried AGNs scales directly with the total infrared luminosity ($L_{\rm IR}$) of the host galaxy and the progression of the merger. Buried AGNs are intrinsically more common in the late stages of coalescence than in early-stage interactions [Yamada et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...876...96Y/abstract). As the nuclei approach coalescence, the fraction of systems hosting heavily obscured AGNs rises significantly, eventually dominating the bolometric output in many luminous and ultraluminous infrared galaxies [Yamada et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...876...96Y/abstract), [Hopkins et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...630..705H/abstract), [Alonso-Herrero et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...779L..14A/abstract).

Given that deeply buried nuclei are strongly associated with major mergers, theoretical models predict a high incidence of kiloparsec and sub-kiloparsec dual AGN during the late stages of orbital decay. However, there is an ongoing tension between numerical simulations and observations: luminous dual AGNs are surprisingly rare, with detection fractions remaining below $20\%$ even in near-infrared and hard X-ray searches optimized to find obscured sources [Imanishi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...891..140I/abstract), [Imanishi et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...780..106I/abstract). This discrepancy is likely due to non-synchronous mass accretion onto the two SMBHs. Observations suggest that accretion is dominated by highly localized environmental conditions rather than global host properties, with the more massive primary SMBH typically exhibiting higher mass-normalized accretion rates than the secondary [Imanishi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...891..140I/abstract), [Imanishi et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...780..106I/abstract). To overcome these observational hurdles, very long baseline interferometry (VLBI) provides milli-arcsecond resolution to directly image compact radio structures, greatly aiding the detection of elusive dual AGNs and supermassive black hole binaries in merging galaxies [An et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018RaSc...53.1211A/abstract). Despite their general rarity, notable sub-kiloparsec dual AGN candidates have been identified, such as the radio-quiet source SDSS J1431+4358 [Severgnini et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...646A.153S/abstract). Furthermore, unique merger geometries can trigger entirely different black hole fueling modes; for instance, the "Infinity Galaxy" at $z=1.14$ hosts a candidate direct-collapse SMBH residing between two massive nuclei following a head-on collision, rather than a standard dual AGN [van Dokkum et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250615618V/abstract).

The gas compaction process ultimately feeds dense molecular structures on parsec and subparsec scales, forming [[molecular-circumnuclear-disks|Molecular circumnuclear disks]] or [[compact-circumnuclear-disks|Compact circumnuclear disks]]. High-resolution interferometry indicates that these inner structures are often gravitationally unstable, driving rapid mass accretion into the central parsec. However, not all compacted gas reaches the event horizon. Direct observations of subparsec molecular inflows in the Circinus galaxy, for example, reveal that less than $3\%$ of the inflowing gas is actually accreted by the black hole; the remaining material is ejected via multiphase outflows, providing significant feedback to the host galaxy and regulating the lifespan of the deeply buried phase [Izumi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Sci...382..554I/abstract).

## Feeding and Feedback Mechanisms

Deeply buried nuclei represent a pivotal, transitional phase in galaxy evolution, defined by a complex interplay between extremely efficient supermassive black hole accretion and violent AGN feedback. During the late stages of gas-rich mergers, massive inflows of gas and dust are channeled into the central kiloparsec, feeding the central engine while completely enshrouding it.

**Accretion and Sub-parsec Inflows**
High-resolution interferometry reveals that feeding in these environments is mediated by massive, rotating [[molecular-circumnuclear-disks|Molecular circumnuclear disks]]. These structures are exceptionally dense ($n \sim 10^7 \text{ cm}^{-3}$) and warm ($T > 200 \text{ K}$), typically extending over radii of $\sim 100 \text{ pc}$ with immense column densities ($N_{\text{H}_2} > 10^{23} \text{ cm}^{-2}$) [Baba et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...928..184B/abstract), [Ohyama et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023ApJ...951...87O/abstract). Infrared interferometry reveals that dust tori in galaxies like Circinus consist of complex, multi-component structures, including an equatorial disk and a geometrically thick warm dust distribution [Tristram et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012JPhCS.372a2035T/abstract). Because these massive disks are frequently gravitationally unstable, they are highly effective at shedding angular momentum and driving prodigious mass accretion rates into the central parsec [Scoville et al. (1999)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1999Ap&SS.266..149S/abstract), [Izumi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Sci...382..554I/abstract). However, not all inflowing material reaches the event horizon. Direct sub-parsec observations of the Circinus galaxy demonstrate that while a gravitationally unstable gas disk continuously drives material inward, less than $3\%$ of this gas is ultimately accreted by the black hole; the remaining $97\%$ is expelled via multiphase outflows [Izumi et al. (2023)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023Sci...382..554I/abstract).

**Multiphase Feedback and Outflows**
The expulsion of this gas constitutes a profound source of feedback. Many deeply buried systems, particularly [[compact-obscured-nuclei|Compact Obscured Nuclei]] (CONs), exhibit concurrent molecular inflows and highly collimated molecular outflows [Sakamoto et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ASPC..477...21S/abstract), [Falstad et al. (2021)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021A&A...649A.105F/abstract). In extreme cases, such as the lenticular galaxy NGC 1377, ALMA observations have revealed highly collimated, jet-like reversed molecular outflows driven by the obscured central engine [Aalto et al. (2015)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015ASPC..499...85A/abstract). This mechanical feedback is often augmented by parsec-scale radio jets, although disentangling the radio continuum of an AGN jet from the synchrotron emission of a dense nuclear "supernova factory" can be challenging in starburst-dominated mergers like Arp 299 [Pérez-Torres et al. (2009)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009A&A...507L..17P/abstract) and IC 883 [Romero-Cañizales et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012A&A...543A..72R/abstract).

As the buried supermassive black hole approaches its peak accretion rate, the resulting thermal and mechanical feedback—driven by both radiation pressure and jets—can violently expel the obscuring medium. Observations indicate that once the intrinsic X-ray luminosity exceeds $L_{\text{X}} \sim 10^{44} \text{ erg s}^{-1}$, the AGN-driven outflows become powerful enough to sweep the host galaxy clear of its interstellar medium, effectively quenching large-scale star formation and leaving behind an unobscured quasar [Hopkins et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...630..705H/abstract), [Page et al. (2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Natur.485..213P/abstract).

**Observational Ambiguities in Kinematics**
While the presence of powerful AGN feedback is well-established, interpreting the exact kinematics of these outflows remains controversial due to the extreme dust columns involved. It is an active matter of debate whether some of the highest-velocity ionized outflows observed in buried systems are purely luminosity-driven. Recent hydrodynamical simulations of gas-rich mergers suggest that severe dust extinction can preferentially obscure the redshifted (receding) component of an outflow. This extinction effect can create an "apparent" blue-shifted tilt in velocity-dispersion diagrams, mimicking exceptionally high-velocity gas (e.g., [O III] $\lambda5007$ line velocities $> 1000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$) even when the intrinsic outflow velocities are more modest [Yutani et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961...68Y/abstract). Consequently, isolating the true mechanical feedback energy from dust-induced optical illusions remains a persistent challenge in the study of deeply buried nuclei.

## Buried Nuclei at High Redshift: From SMGs to Little Red Dots

The study of deeply buried nuclei extends to the early universe, where gas-rich conditions drive the rapid, concurrent growth of galaxies and supermassive black holes (SMBHs). Theoretical models of self-regulated black hole growth indicate that the heavily obscured, "buried" phase accounts for the majority of the accretion timescale ($\sim 100$ Myr) before energetic feedback expels the enshrouding gas [Hopkins et al. (2005)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005ApJ...630..705H/abstract). Indeed, demographics from deep multiwavelength surveys confirm that most AGNs are obscured across cosmic history, with the amount of obscuration varying based on both luminosity and redshift [Urry et al. (2007)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007arXiv0712.1041U/abstract).

At Cosmic Noon ($z \sim 2-4$), high-resolution interferometry has uncovered extremely compact, dust-shrouded cores in submillimeter galaxies (SMGs). For instance, ALMA observations of an [[ultrabright-lensed-submillimeter-galaxy|Ultrabright lensed submillimeter galaxy]] at $z=3.63$ detected the $448$ GHz ortho-$\text{H}_2\text{O}$ transition, revealing a $0.1$ kpc nuclear core characterized by warm dust ($T_{\rm dust} \approx 100$ K), high far-infrared optical depths ($\tau_{100} \gtrsim 1$), and extreme surface energy densities of $\Sigma_{\rm IR} = 8 \times 10^{12} L_\odot \text{ kpc}^{-2}$ [Yang et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020A&A...634L...3Y/abstract). Similar sub-kiloparsec resolution studies of a lensed SMG at $z=2.78$ reveal a nuclear spiral structure and a super-Eddington central starburst [Stacey et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241203644S/abstract). This compact, highly obscured activity is widespread at early epochs. For example, observations of submillimeter-bright galaxies at $z=4.3$ provide strong evidence that their central kiloparsec regions host massive, gravitationally unstable gas disks where extreme starburst activity occurs on very short timescales of roughly $100$ Myr [Tadaki et al. (2018)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018Natur.560..613T/abstract). In such extreme environments, such as the $z=4.05$ [[heavily-dust-obscured-starbursts|heavily dust-obscured starburst]] GN20, visual extinctions can reach $A_V \approx 17$ mag, entirely masking the underlying intense star-formation or AGN activity at rest-frame optical wavelengths [Bik et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024A&A...686A...3B/abstract). Similar extreme conditions are found in high-redshift dusty Lyman break galaxies; for instance, Westphal-MD11 serves as a prime example where a compact molecular gas reservoir fuels an intense, deeply buried nuclear starburst [Baker et al. (2004)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...613L.113B/abstract).

At even higher redshifts ($z \sim 3-8.5$), observations from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have revealed a distinct population of extremely compact, broad-line sources known as [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]] (LRDs). These sources exhibit a pronounced V-shaped spectral energy distribution (SED) that breaks at a rest-frame frequency of $\nu_b \approx 10^{15}$ Hz ($\lambda \approx 3300$ \AA) [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250512719Z/abstract). Initially, LRDs were widely interpreted as highly dust-obscured AGNs with visual extinctions $A_V > 3$ [Chen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...994L..42C/abstract), representing the primordial counterparts to local [[compact-obscured-nuclei|Compact Obscured Nuclei]]. Cosmological simulations support the existence of such a phase, predicting that early-stage dusty quasars are Compton-thick and dominated by highly anisotropic, torus-like dust structures formed from infalling interstellar medium streams [Bardati et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250909770B/abstract). Under this paradigm, future far-infrared missions like PRIMA are expected to detect these early SMBHs via the deep $9.8$ $\mu$m silicate absorption feature out to $z \sim 7$ [Donnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250311611D/abstract), [Donnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JATIS..11c1606D/abstract).

However, the physical nature of LRDs remains a subject of intense debate, and the literature is currently conflicting regarding their actual degree of obscuration. The traditional dust-reddened AGN scenario is challenged by a "dust budget crisis"; constraints from ALMA and MIRI indicate that the expected mid-to-far-infrared thermal dust emission is anomalously faint or absent, implying much lower visual extinctions of $A_V \lesssim 1.5$ mag [Chen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...994L..42C/abstract). To explain the V-shaped SED without invoking extreme dust columns, alternative structural models have been proposed. One leading model suggests that the red continuum arises not from dust extinction, but from the unique accretion disk physics of the central engine: a composite structure consisting of an inner standard accretion disk and a gravitationally unstable outer disk (Toomre $Q \sim 1$) radiating at $T \sim 2000-4000$ K [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250512719Z/abstract). Resolving whether LRDs are truly [[deeply-obscured-nuclei|Deeply obscured nuclei]], compact star clusters, or unobscured AGNs with anomalous accretion disks is critical for understanding the demographics and redshift evolution of [[high-redshift-active-galactic-nuclei|High-redshift active galactic nuclei]].

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The study of deeply buried nuclei is rapidly evolving, driven by high-resolution multiwavelength observations that push the boundaries of current theoretical models. Several critical questions and tensions remain unresolved, particularly regarding the high-redshift universe, the demographics of dual active galactic nuclei (AGNs), and the interpretation of feedback mechanisms.

**High-Redshift Nuclei and the "Dust Budget Crisis"**
The nature of newly discovered high-redshift sources, such as [[little-red-dots-jwst|Little Red Dots (JWST)]] (LRDs), poses a significant theoretical challenge. As discussed previously, a "dust budget crisis" has emerged because the host galaxies often lack the requisite mass to produce the extreme dust columns originally assumed for these red sources. This tension questions whether LRDs are genuinely obscured systems, or whether our models of early supermassive black hole environments—such as those invoking hybrid, gravitationally unstable accretion disks—need fundamental revision [Chen et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025ApJ...994L..42C/abstract), [Zhang et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250512719Z/abstract). Resolving this will determine if a Compton-thick, buried phase is a universal requirement for early black hole growth.

**Dual AGNs and Direct-Collapse Black Holes**
While the late stages of gas-rich mergers are expected to trigger dual AGNs, their observed scarcity (detection rates remain below $20\%$) suggests that highly localized environmental factors govern black hole fueling rather than global merger dynamics [Imanishi et al. (2013)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...780..106I/abstract), [Imanishi et al. (2020)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ApJ...891..140I/abstract). Additionally, extreme merger environments might occasionally bypass standard accretion entirely, forming direct-collapse black holes at the collision interface of merging systems, as potentially observed in the "Infinity Galaxy" [van Dokkum et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250615618V/abstract). Constraining the rates and mechanisms of these exotic formation channels remains a key observational frontier.

**Feedback, Kinematics, and Mass Measurement**
Quantifying the true energetic impact of AGN feedback is complicated by the extreme optical depths of buried nuclei. Because severe dust extinction can artificially shift velocity-dispersion profiles—potentially creating the illusion of $>1000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ outflows—new kinematic diagnostics are needed to accurately distinguish between genuine radiation-driven feedback and apparent geometric effects in late-stage mergers [Yutani et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...961...68Y/abstract). Beyond conventional spectroscopy, astronomers are developing advanced observational techniques to probe unresolved kinematics. Specifically, spectroastrometry has been identified as a highly promising method to constrain the kinematics of rotating gas disks and directly measure supermassive black hole masses on spatial scales significantly smaller than the telescope's diffraction limit [Gnerucci et al. (2010)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010A&A...511A..19G/abstract).

**Future Observational Facilities**
Overcoming these ambiguities requires multiwavelength facilities capable of penetrating immense columns of gas and dust. While JWST has extended the search for buried nuclei out to cosmic noon [García-Bernete et al. (2022)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022A&A...663A..46G/abstract), the proposed PRIMA far-infrared mission will be essential for reaching the epoch of reionization. By targeting mid-infrared PAH features and silicate absorption up to $z \sim 7$ [Donnan et al. (2025)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JATIS..11c1606D/abstract), upcoming far-infrared observatories will definitively test whether the earliest obscured sources represent extreme starbursts or the critical assembly phases of the first supermassive black holes. Furthermore, future mid-to-far infrared spectroscopy, as envisioned for missions like SPICA, is designed to provide direct spectroscopic measurements of star formation and black hole accretion rates during the obscured peak of galaxy evolution [Spinoglio et al. (2019)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021IAUS..356...17S/abstract). In addition, future far-infrared missions with true subarcsecond resolution will be absolutely necessary to resolve severe source crowding in deep cosmological fields like GOODS and COSMOS, finally enabling precise, statistically significant studies of black hole-galaxy co-evolution across cosmic time [Bonato et al. (2024)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024ApJ...977..208B/abstract).
